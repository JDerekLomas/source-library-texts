# PREFACE

Simplicius’s commentaries on Aristotle’s *Physics* <note>Aristotle's "Physica," the foundational work on the nature of change and motion</note> hold the leading position among this class of writings, not only due to the wisdom and learning of the interpreter, but also because of the sheer magnitude of the work itself. Indeed, the only book that could be compared to it in scope—Alexander’s commentary on the same *Physics*—has perished.¹ Consequently, this nearly infinite mass of material has been divided into two admittedly unequal parts: the larger comprising the first four books, and the smaller comprising the latter four. We have been compelled by necessity to embrace this same ancient division preserved in the best manuscripts. For this reason, as I prepare the preface for the first part, I may omit those codices <note>Handwritten manuscript books</note> which contain only the later books, except that I mention for honor’s sake the *Codex Marcianus 226* (A). This is the oldest and finest specimen, and the only one to retain the ancient title of the work, which we have not hesitated to reconstruct in the earlier books as well, even though we lacked the help of the other manuscripts.²

---

¹ If the Arabs are to be believed (A. Müller, *The Greek Philosophers in the Arabic Tradition*, Halle 1873, p. 17; Casiri, *Arabic Library*, Vol. I, p. 243), at least eleven books of Alexander corresponded to Aristotle’s work as follows: Book A [I], 2 books (Book I and part of II were extant); Book B [II], 1 book; Book Γ [III] (had perished); Book Δ [IV], 3 books (extant except for a portion of the third regarding time); Book E [V], 1 book; Book Z [VI], 1 book (more than half was preserved); Book H [VII], 1 book; Book Θ [VIII], 1 book (almost entirely perished). Averroes <note>The 12th-century Andalusian philosopher Ibn Rushd</note> had a similar copy, as he himself says at the beginning [Venice edition, 1550, Vol. IV, p. 2, column 1, line 7]: *“Neither was an explanation of the words known from Alexander except for part of the first, and upon the second, fourth, fifth, sixth, and seventh; and that which is held regarding part of the eighth is not by Alexander.”* His excerpts from Alexander confirm the same, which I shall discuss elsewhere [cf. p. XI, 9]. It is no wonder that Alexander’s work perished early among the Greeks. The Byzantine era could not support two such massive bodies of work, especially since Simplicius had, by a sort of traditional right, transcribed all the best parts from Alexander. For it must be observed that the substance of the chapters is almost everywhere Alexandrian, though his name usually does not appear unless Simplicius wished to disagree. Not infrequently, Simplicius indicated that the preceding material was borrowed—that is, repeated from Alexander—by setting his own interpretation against it, beginning with that characteristic phrase "perhaps" <note>original: "μή-ποτε" (mē-pote), a Greek term used to introduce a tentative or alternative philosophical suggestion</note>. In the same manner, though less frequently, he makes use of Themistius.

² Manuscript A provides the inscription (to use this as an example): *Simplicius the Philosopher on the Zeta [6th] of the Lectures on Natural Philosophy, the first [volume], which is the sixth [book].* The addition of "which is the sixth" was added, though it might seem redundant, so that it would be clear that the numbering of Aristotle and the common numbering of Simplicius’s commentary were different. But it is possible that this custom was carried over from antiquity, where it was not unusual to attach several volumes to a single book of Aristotle (cf. note 1).

# VI PREFACE

<margin>The Archetype <note>original: "Archetypus"</note></margin>
Therefore, all the codices of Books I, II, III, and IV that have come to our attention¹ flowed from an archetype written some time after Simplicius. This is evident both from the disturbances appearing on page 238, line 5 in all available books, and from the gaps and "windows" <note>blank spaces left by a scribe when the source was illegible</note> left on page 518. From that archetype, however, two copies were derived: the better one was contained in the four books the archetype held; the other, of poorer quality, embraced the entire body of work by summoning the later books from elsewhere.

Out of the vast number of codices of both types, which Adolf Torstrik² examined with tireless labor in Italy, France, Spain, and Britain, three seemed worthy to serve as the foundation for this volume: the better ones, **D** and **E**, and the inferior **F**. For us, **D** and **E** represent that better copy; although its scribe did not entirely refrain from guessing <note>conjectural emendation of the text</note> (see p. 22, 25), he nevertheless preserved a much more intact and full memory from the archetype. From the same source are the scholia <note>marginal explanations or comments</note> transcribed by **D** and **E**, which, though originating from a Christian man (see p. 29, 2 note), are not lacking in a certain sharpness. That one inscribed at p. 201, 28 note is indeed charming.

<margin>D</margin>
Although the authority of these codices is balanced with almost equal weight, **D** stands slightly above the rest,³ not only because it is more learned (see p. 220, 1)—which is usually turned into a fault rather than a praise for scribes <note>learned scribes often "corrected" texts they should have copied exactly</note>—but because it follows the faith of the original more scrupulously even in minor matters. For in adding the <term>movable nu <note>original: "ν paragogico"; a grammatical feature in Greek where an 'n' is added to the end of a word to avoid two vowels clashing</note></term>, in tolerating the hiatus after *de*, *alla* and similar words, and in preserving *houtos* before consonants, **E** preferred to obey its own habit rather than that of the original. However, **D**, which fails at p. 347, 11, already begins to lose some of its former virtue before then; for example, at p. 318, 4 it contains gaps just like **F**, and from p. 304, 19 it follows the bad habit of the later tradition by writing out the lemmas <note>the snippets of the original text being commented upon</note> in full, of which—

² I have brought together under one view in Supplement I all the books examined either by Torstrik or, after his death, by us and others. Brandis spoke thus of the codices used in his *Scholia* and books (Schol. p. 321): *For the scholia excerpted from Simplicius's commentaries, the Paris Royal codices 1908, 1907, 1906 and the Oxford New College codex 244 were collated here and there.* In his *Handbook of Philosophical History* (III 1 p. 222), he reports that he also consulted some Venetian manuscripts. In Brandis’s own copy of Simplicius, preserved in our Academy, the following are distinguished: **A** = Paris codex 1908 [**P** in our list], **C** = Oxford New College 244 [**I** in our list], **D** unknown [similar to our **D** or **T**], **O** = Laurentian Oceanus 85,1 [i.e., our **B**]. **C** was collated by the hand of Bekker, which I recognize with certainty. Otherwise, Brandis collated only fragments, such as those of the Ionics and Eudemus, and not even all of those with their respective codices.

From this, at least some better memory flowed into Spengel’s *Eudemus* (see p. 8, 21, 27, 28, 29, 31). Advised, I believe, by Victorius, Lorenzo Giacomini transcribed several variant readings from **D** into his own copy now preserved in the Marucellian Library.

# PREFACE VII

Manuscript **E** has consistently preserved the original form, while **F** has shortened it. A corrector, designated as **D²** (whom one can only with difficulty distinguish from the first hand, **D¹**), has fixed certain gaps or errors in the first hand, for example on page 191, line 10 and page 162, line 3.

<margin>E</margin>
<margin>Eᵇ</margin>
<margin>Eᵃ</margin>
**E** was produced by a rather unrefined scribe, as evidenced by the fact that he confused shorthand signs—for instance, <term>peri</term> <note>original: "περί", meaning 'about' or 'around'</note> and <term>para</term> <note>original: "παρά", meaning 'beside' or 'beyond'</note>—with strange consistency; however, he refrained from arbitrary changes (if you except those minor points I mentioned). Here too, I have distinguished the first hand from a female corrector <note>The editor uses "correctrice," suggesting a woman performed these specific emendations</note> using the marks **E¹** and **E²**. A section originally omitted (p. 52,17—72,11) was supplied below from a poorly written book, the readings of which I have marked **Eᵇ**. However, those repeated passages (p. 20,1—30,16 and likewise 35,30—44,19) marked **Eᵃ**, which ended up near the supplied section (**Eᵇ**), were drawn from a single source—the best of all—which undoubtedly followed a tradition older than the archetype itself.¹ For instance, against all other books, **Eᵃ** alone correctly holds the word <term>synapodeiknytai</term> <note>original: "συναποδείκνυται", meaning 'is demonstrated along with'</note> on p. 21,21, and this same source alone restored the teaching of Anaximander <note>A pre-Socratic Greek philosopher</note> on p. 41,19 (reading <term>ouranon</term> <note>original: "οὐρανῶν", meaning 'of the heavens'</note> instead of <term>anthropon</term> <note>original: "ἀνθρώπων", meaning 'of men'</note>), to say nothing of smaller instances like p. 38,21; 29; 40,25 (<term>pros</term> <note>original: "πρός", meaning 'toward'</note>). Another trace of this ancient tradition is the phrase <term>em peirasi</term> <note>original: "ἐμ πείρασι", an archaic form meaning 'in the ends/limits'</note> on p. 39,27 and other slight matters of that kind.

<margin>F</margin>
The offspring of an inferior copy, **F** is nearly equal in age to codices **D** and **E**, yet of much less value. For its model had been transcribed rather carelessly from the archetype and was not only filled with gaps but also with interpolations <note>Unauthorized insertions into a text</note> taken either from the scribe's own imagination or from authors cited by Simplicius, such as Aristotle (cf. p. 55,25 note) or Plato (cf. p. 26,16). Furthermore, the scribe of **F** himself, whose name was Gregory, called upon a copy of a different class in the margin to make emendations (cf. p. 56,3 note). Indeed, it is clear from p. 556,8 note that several copies were at hand (cf. p. 304,1 note). Regarding Gregory's remaining diligence in the margins, see the description of the codex below. I have distinguished corrections later than Gregory with the mark **F²**.

<margin>a</margin>
From **F**, whose related copies dominate the 14th and 15th centuries, the Aldine edition (**a**) <note>The famous first printed edition by Aldus Manutius in Venice</note> was derived in the earlier books. I have noted every discrepancy in this edition except for the most trivial typesetting errors, so that a comparison between the common version and our own revision can be made immediately. The editor of the Aldine copy introduced quite a few innovations—most of them unfortunately—but some were approved by us as well. Although he devised most of them himself, he was nevertheless able to draw many things from older manuscripts. For just as codex **F** began to be emended here and there from better books, so most of the common books later underwent a continuous <term>diorthosin</term> <note>original: "διόρθωσιν", meaning 'correction' or 'revision'</note>.

# PREFACE

<note>original: "PRAEFATIO"</note>

Of this kind, I have described below at greater length manuscript P as a specimen; just as it was copied from manuscript F, so it provided a convenient treasury of variant readings for the Aldine edition, from which that edition drew heavily (see page XVIII). In the preface, it also appears that some conjectures preceded the Vatican manuscripts L^I, L^II, L^III, and L^IV, such as on page 1, line 19, "being brought to a conclusion" <note>original Greek: συμπεραινομένην</note> and page 3, line 5 and following.

<margin>The Corrector of the Ambrosian manuscript</margin>
Of the same sort are the "Emendations" <note>original Greek: Ἐπιδιορθώσεις</note> inscribed in the Ambrosian manuscript Q 114 inf., which the scribe claims to have sought from a manuscript dated to the year 1145 (?). I strongly doubt whether that manuscript was truly ancient or whether he sought all his readings from it. On the contrary, while a few things may be owed to the manuscript (such as page 192, line 30 "they might be" <note>original Greek: ὦσι</note> and page 364, line 22, where these corrupt words "someone might reproach this" <note>original Greek: τατῳ δ’ ἄν τις ἐνίσαιτο</note> stand in place of the true "in this way one might explain" <note>original Greek: οὕτως μὲν ἄν τις ἐξηγήσαιτο</note>), most things are nevertheless owed to the talent of a man reasonably well-versed in Plato and Aristotle. We clearly detect an interpolator on page 351, line 10, where, since the Aldine edition omits "and" <note>original Greek: καὶ</note> in the phrase "and to speak at greater length," he restored the participle "speaking" <note>original Greek: λέγων</note>. He corrected similarly at "it is unchangeable and immovable" <note>original Greek: ἀμετάβλῃτός ἐστι καὶ ἀκίνητος</note> on page 414, line 3, where the note should be consulted. His greatest care was in restoring the <term>lemmata</term> <note>The "lemmata" are the specific excerpts of Aristotle's text that Simplicius is commenting upon.</note>; since he provides these in full, it is manifest that he could not have sought at least these from an ancient manuscript (see page X below). For the rest, whatever of these readings did not seem entirely worthless, I have recorded in the critical notes.

<margin>The Editor's Method</margin>
Relying on the authority of these books, therefore, we have edited the words of Simplicius, with very few things altered by conjecture, as our purpose required. In matters of spelling <note>original: "orthographicis"</note> as well, we preferred to vary along with the manuscripts rather than remain consistent with a rule of our own making. Furthermore, if we comply with our manuscripts in these matters (which I am able to do thanks to the diligence and skill of Hugo Vitelli, who performed the collation), one can sometimes observe a certain pattern undoubtedly passed down from the author himself, such as the almost constant use of double-tau <note>original: "ττ"</note>.¹ In other matters they vary, such as in eliding or writing out in full the words "but," "nor," and "and" <note>original: "δέ οὐδέ ἀλλά"</note> before vowels, and likewise in placing the "movable nu" <note>A grammatical feature of Greek where an 'n' is added to the end of a word for sound.</note> or using the forms "the same," "such," "thus," and "until" <note>original: "ταὐτόν τοιοῦτον οὕτως μέχρις"</note> before consonants. Nevertheless, the fuller form prevails quite clearly, especially in the Aristotelian passages. Often, even in verses, they write out superfluous vowels in full, which I have nonetheless declined to imitate. Where the manuscripts disagree, I have followed DE or DF or EF when they agree; in that part where only EF stand in opposition, I have preferred F as being more accurately written in these matters. It was appropriate that we apply accents—which were not yet added by Simplicius—according to our own rules, not the often foolish rules of the Byzantines. Therefore, those perpetual forms like "equal," "to stand," and "to show" <note>original Greek: ἶσος ἑστᾶναι δεικνῦναι</note> I have not even [recorded] in the notes...

---

¹ Not even here, where there can be no doubt about the habit of Simplicius, have I made everything uniform. For if any instance of the contrary custom of writing with double-sigma <note>original: "σσ"</note> appeared, I often observed that it was handed down from Alexandria, whether the name was mentioned or hidden. In the single number "four" <note>original: "τέτταρες"</note> I have maintained consistency even against the manuscripts, because scribes vary at their own discretion when interpreting the symbol <note>original: "δ̅"</note>.
