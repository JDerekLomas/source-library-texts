*The diligent youth <note>Hippomenes.</note> carried away the prize of the Hesperian garden,*
*As the goddess Cypris <note>original: "Cypride." An epithet for Venus, who was famously born from the sea foam near Cyprus.</note> gave him the triple apple:*
*Following the fleeing maiden, he rolled it from the ground before her face,*
*And by this means, she draws out her slow delays:*
*Soon he shines, and soon she shines—she who was once swifter than the East Winds—*
*As he scatters the golden gifts upon the soil.*
*He delayed the slow footsteps of the girl,*
*But she, in turn, turns her back in flight,*
*Until the lover repeated the third weight; then yielded*
*ATALANTA to her victor as his reward.*

*Hippomenes is the power of sulfur; she is that of fleeing*
*Mercury <note>original: "Mercurii." In alchemy, "Philosophical Mercury" represents the volatile, feminine principle, while "Philosophical Sulfur" represents the fixed, masculine principle.</note>; in the race, the woman is overcome by the man.*
*After they embrace each other with greedy love,*
*Anger reproves the Goddess <note>Cybele, the Mother Goddess.</note> in her temple;*
*The avenger clothed them both in a lion’s skin,*
*Thereafter they are red of body, and they are fierce.*

*So that my Muse might express the most faithful images of this race,*
*She gives you the flights <term>Fugues</term> in three voices:*

*But the second is fleeing, and the third is duly following.*

# DISCOURSE IV.

Divine and civil law prohibits those joined closely by nature through blood from being coupled in marriage, such as those directly ascending and descending in the **genealogical tree** and those meeting at the sides <note>referring to collateral relatives like siblings or cousins</note>, and this for very certain reasons. But when the **philosophers** <note>In this context, "philosophers" almost always refers to alchemists.</note> speak of joining in marriage a mother with a son, or a father with a daughter, or a brother with a sister, they do not say or do anything against the aforementioned law: because the subjects distinguish the attributes, and the causes distinguish the effects. For the "persons" of the Philosophers differ from human persons; they are as free as the sons and daughters of Adam, who took each other in marriage without the imputation of any crime. 

The most powerful reason for human laws seems to be so that the human race might coalesce and be associated more firmly through affinity and friendship, and not be divided into hostile and hereditary family factions. Since this same cause remained intact for the first Adamic brothers and sisters, nothing prohibited them from being joined in marriage. For in them alone did the human race then exist, nor did others live besides them and their parents; therefore, as they were connected by blood, they were necessarily to be connected by **affinity** <note>Marriage-based relationship.</note>. 

But as the multitude of men grew, distributed into innumerable families, this true and equitable cause was found: that brothers should not marry sisters. Among the alchemists, there is another reason why sisters marry brothers: namely, the **likeness of substance**, so that "like may be joined to like." There are, however, in this genus at least two things most similar to each other in species, yet different in sex, one of whom is greeted by the name of **brother**, the other of **sister**. 

These, therefore, with the same liberty, condition, and even inevitable necessity by which the first blood-related humans were joined, must be legitimately committed to a single marriage. The **brother** is hot and dry, and therefore very **choleric**; the **sister** is cold and moist, having much **phlegmatic** matter in herself. <note>Maier is using the theory of the four humors: the "brother" represents the active, fiery principle (Sulfur), while the "sister" represents the passive, watery principle (Mercury).</note> These natures, though so different in temperament, are accustomed to agree best in love and fecundity, and in the propagation of offspring.

# DISCOURSE VII.

What Hippocrates, the leader of physicians, asserts concerning the humors <note>original: "humoribus" — In ancient medicine, these were the four vital fluids (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile) believed to govern health and temperament.</note>—that they are diverse and several in number within a person, and not merely one, otherwise various diseases could not arise—is exactly what we notice to be true concerning the Elements of the world. If there were but one single element, no change of one into another would occur, nor would any generation or corruption take place; indeed, all things would be one unchanging mass, and no meteors, minerals, plants, or animals could be produced from it by nature. Hence, the Supreme Creator harmonized this entire System of the world out of diverse and contrary natures—namely, from the light and the heavy, the hot and the cold, the moist and the dry—so that one might pass into another through affinity <note>The idea that elements can transform because they share "bridge" qualities (e.g., Fire and Air are both hot).</note>. In this way, the composition of bodies is achieved, which differ greatly among themselves in essence, qualities, powers, and effects. For in imperfectly mixed bodies <term>inperfectè miſtis</term> <note>A term for substances like vapors or stones that are not yet fully "perfected" or balanced.</note>, there are light Elements such as fire and air, as well as heavy ones like earth and water; these are so tempered by balances among themselves that one does not flee from the other. Instead, each individual part is easily caught and held by its neighbor.

Earth and air are naturally opposed to each other, as are fire and water; nevertheless, fire fosters a friendship with air through the heat common to both, and with earth through dryness. Likewise, air shares a bond with water, and water with earth. From this, all things are connected by bonds of affinity—or rather, of blood-relation—and they remain together in a single composition. If this composition abounds in light things, it carries the heavy ones up with it; if it abounds in heavy things, it presses the light ones down with it. 

This is illustrated by the two **Eagles**, the winged and the featherless <note>In alchemy, the winged eagle represents the volatile spirit that evaporates, while the featherless eagle is the "fixed" matter that remains at the bottom of the flask.</note>, of which the former, attempting to fly, is held back by the latter. An evident example of this matter is seen in the battle between the Falcon and the Heron: the Falcon, having become superior in the air through swift flight and rapid wings, seizes the Heron with its talons; then, by the weight of the Heron, both fall together to the ground. 

The opposite appeared in the artificial pigeon (or automaton) of Archytas <note>Archytas of Tarentum, a Greek philosopher, was said to have built a wooden pigeon that could fly using compressed air or steam.</note>, where heavy things were lifted by light ones—that is, its wooden body was snatched into the air by the spirit <note>original: "ſpiritu" — In this context, it refers to the air or steam powering the device.</note> enclosed within it. In the "philosophical subject" <note>The raw material of the Alchemical Work.</note>, the light parts at first predominate over the heavy in quantity; however, they are eventually overcome by the power and virtue of the heavy parts. In the course of time, the eagle's wings are clipped, and from the two birds, one massive bird is made—namely, the **Ostrich** <note>original: "Strutio camelus"</note>—which is able to consume iron, and which, being hindered by its weight, runs upon the ground more than it [flies]...

# DISCOURSE VIII,

Many and varied are the kinds of birds; as they are indefinite in number, so they remain unknown to us by name. They tell of a certain very large bird called the Ruc <note>The Roc, a legendary giant bird of prey from Middle Eastern mythology, famously described by Marco Polo as being capable of carrying off elephants.</note> appearing at a certain time of year on a small island of the ocean; they say it can lift an elephant into the air with it. India and America provide parrots of various colors, crows, and others like them. But to seek out the eggs of these birds is not the Philosophical intention. The Egyptians annually attack the eggs of crocodiles with iron and pursue them as if by a publicly declared war; Philosophers strike their egg with fire, not so that it might be destroyed and perish, but so that it might receive life and grow. 

For while the chick is produced from there, animated and living, it should not be called its corruption <note>original: "corruptio"; in natural philosophy, this refers to the breakdown of one form to allow for the generation of another.</note> but its generation. Indeed, it ceases to be an egg through the loss of its egg-like form, and begins to be a two-legged and winged animal through the introduction of a more noble form. There are, moreover, in the egg the seeds of the male and female joined together under one roof or shell. The yolk constitutes the chick and its fundamental limbs and internal organs, with the seed of the male acting as the internal former and shaper. The white <note>original: "Albumen"</note> provides the matter or the foundation and growth for the first sketch or thread of the chick. 

External heat is the "first mover," which by a certain circulation of the elements and the transformation of one into another, introduces a new form, by the instinct or guidance of nature. For water passes into air, and air into fire, and fire into earth; when all these are joined, and a specific form is sent down from the stars, an individual of a certain bird species is made—namely, of that species whose egg and infused seed it is. 

Truly, the egg is said to be struck by a fiery sword while Vulcan <note>The Roman god of fire and blacksmiths, representing the alchemist's furnace.</note> provides an exit for the chick with his axe (just as he did from the brain of Jupiter for Pallas <note>Referring to the myth of Athena (Pallas) being born fully formed from the head of Zeus (Jupiter) after it was split open by Vulcan.</note>), performing the duty of a midwife. This is what Basil Valentine <note>A legendary 15th-century alchemist whose writings were highly influential in the 17th century.</note> asserts: that Mercury was shut in a prison by Vulcan at the command of Mars, and was not released until he had been completely putrefied and died. But this death exists for him as the beginning of a new life, just as the corruption or death of the egg brings a new generation and life to the chick. Thus for the human fetus, once the "vegetative" life (which it enjoyed alone in the mother's womb) has ended, another more perfect life is added through the passage into this worldly light, or rather, through birth. Indeed, for us also, when we are deprived of this present life which we lead, another most perfect and eternal life looms,

### DISCOURSE VIII.

Thus Lullius <note>Ramon Lull (c. 1232–1315), a Majorcan philosopher and polymath to whom many later alchemical works were attributed.</note> calls this fiery sword <note>original: "gladium igneum"</note> a "sharp lance" in many places. This is because fire, like a lance or a sharp sword, pierces bodies and makes them porous and permeable, so that water can penetrate them to dissolve them and bring them from a state of hardness to softness and flexibility. In the stomach of the sea-raven <note>The cormorant, a bird known for its intense appetite and powerful digestion.</note>, which is the most voracious of all birds, there are found long, slender living worms. These serve as his instruments of heat; as soon as he captures eels or fish, they attack them and pierce them like the sharpest needles (as we ourselves have observed on occasion). In this way, they consume the prey in a very short time through a wonderful operation of nature. Therefore, just as heat stings, so too does a stinging thing sometimes play the role of heat. For this reason, it is not without merit that we might speak of a "fiery sword" with which the philosophical egg <note>original: "ovum philosophorum"; the sealed vessel containing the alchemical matter, or the matter itself.</note> must be struck or bound.

However, the Philosophers would rather have this understood as a "temperate heat," by which the egg is fostered, as Morfoleus declares in the *Assembly of Philosophers* <note>original: "Turba Philosophorum," one of the earliest and most influential Latin alchemical texts, written as a transcript of a debate between ancient philosophers.</note> when he says: *“It is necessary, wise men, that the humidity first be burned away by a slow fire, as is shown to us by the example of the generation of a chick; and when the fire is increased, the vessel must be sealed on all sides, lest the body of the air and its fleeing spirit be drawn out.”* But to which bird does this egg belong? Moscus <note>Another speaker featured in the "Turba Philosophorum."</note> says in the same place: *“I, however, say that no instruments are made except from our white, starry, and splendid powder, and from the white stone; from this powder, suitable instruments are made for the egg; yet they have not named the egg, nor stated which bird’s egg it might be.”*

# DISCOURSE IX.

Whatever grows in length, breadth, and depth—that is, whatever arises, is nourished, increases, reaches its peak, and is propagated—likewise decreases; which is to say, it diminishes in strength, dies, and falls away completely, as is evident in all plants and animals. Hence, for man too, when he has reached his greatest growth, a decline—that is, old age—occurs, in which his strength is gradually diminished until death arrives.

Indeed, the cause of old age is the same as that of a lamp flickering and shining dimly when its oil is almost consumed. For just as in a lamp there are three things—the **wick**, the **fat** (oil), and the **flame**—so in man, the **wick** represents the vital members, the internal organs, and the limbs; the **fat** is the <term>radical moisture</term> <note>Latin: *humidum radicale*. In medieval medicine, this was the fundamental life-fluid gifted at birth that sustained the body's internal heat; its exhaustion led to aging and death.</note>; the **flame** is the <term>native heat</term> <note>Latin: *calor nativus*. The innate warmth that drives digestion and life functions.</note>. The only difference is that the flame of a lamp shines, while the native heat does not at all, since it is not fire but merely heat; and that the fat is oily, while the radical moisture is viscous, being of a seminal principle.

Just as a lamp is extinguished from a lack of oil, so man, from old age and without any other disease, falls into wasting and senile decay, and finally death. They say of the eagle that in old age, hindered by a curved beak, it would die of hunger unless, by nature casting off its beak, it were restored to a kind of youth. So too deer by shedding their antlers, snakes by their skins or sloughs, and crabs by their shells, seem to be rejuvenated—not truly, of course, because their consumed radical moisture is not restored to them, but in appearance.

There is nothing that can make a man rejuvenate except death itself and the beginning of the eternal life that follows. However, as for the restoration of the external form and strength in some way, and the removal of wrinkles and grey hairs, there are those who say a remedy can be found, as <term>Lullus</term> <note>Ramon Llull (c. 1232–1315), a philosopher to whom many alchemical works regarding the "Fifth Essence" or alcohol were attributed.</note> affirms in his *On the Fifth Essence*, and <term>Arnoldus</term> <note>Arnaldus de Villa Nova (c. 1240–1311), a famed physician and alchemist who wrote on the medicinal properties of "potable gold."</note> in his work *On Prepared Gold*. 

But here the Philosophers <note>Meaning the alchemists.</note> say that for an old man to become a youth, he must be enclosed with a certain tree in a dewy house, and then he will eat of the fruit of the tree and thus recover his youth. That such trees exist in the nature of things is now scarcely believed by the common people. Regarding <term>myrobalans</term> <note>Astringent, plum-like fruits used in Ayurvedic and medieval medicine to balance the humors.</note>—fruits of a tree—physicians write wonderful things, as if they could perform such a feat, removing grey hairs, purifying the blood, and prolonging life. But these claims are called into doubt by many, unless they are said to achieve this by accident, like other things that purge the mass of blood from the dregs mixed into it, or those that dye grey hairs with the blackness with which myrobalans are said to color the hair and the pupils of the eyes.
