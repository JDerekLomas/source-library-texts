# To the most Reverend father in Christ and Lord, Ulrich von Frosberg, Bishop of Trent, Erhard Ratdolt of Augsburg, printer, sends his greatest greetings.

I am encouraged, most respected prelate, by your singular and inestimable kindness toward me, so that I do not fear to offer you something from the exquisite industry of the printing art <note>original: "impreſſorię artis"</note>, to which I am—as they say—entirely devoted by the stroke of fortune. I have no doubt that this gift will be either suited to your most illustrious virtue or at the very least not unpleasant to you. For a long time, revolving this within the secrets of my heart, I was much at odds with myself, because nature has distinguished you and fortune has increased you with many and most brilliant gifts. Moreover, you have persisted with such great effort in your studies toward the highest peak that, lacking no praise, you deserve the summit of all good things. For by the gifts of nature, with God as the author, you have become such a cultivator of wisdom that you have kept watch over the knowledge of both divine and human things above all others. You have grown so much in the study of the best arts that you have left it difficult to judge whether you gave more splendor to them or they to you. From this, you are recognized to have come into such high esteem among mortals that you have adorned the title of noble blood with the pontifical fillets <note>The "infulis" or fillets refer to the ceremonial headbands of a bishop's mitre, symbolizing his office.</note>.

Therefore, greatly admiring the sharpness of your perceptive intellect, I began to hesitate, drawn in different directions; for I was not unaware that your incredible wisdom demanded the greatest monuments of literature. However, since for some time now I have brought forth geometric disciplines—which had been almost abolished by some mortal neglect—out of the blind darkness of oblivion into the clarity of light, and have turned my mind's purpose toward the recovery of the science of the stars <note>original: "ſideralis ſcientię." In the 15th century, "astrology" and "astronomy" were often treated as a single discipline.</note>, it did not seem discordant to me to dedicate a certain principal work of that same divine science to your most excellent name. Truly, this is because such a work is most fitting for a man who is no less learned than he is exalted by the height of his governance. 

And so, most magnificent Bishop, as many works of this faculty offered themselves to me from every side, I decided to give you as a gift "Leopold, Son of the Duchy of Austria, on the Science of the Stars" <note>Leopold of Austria was a 13th-century astronomer; Ratdolt printed his work in 1489.</note> and to dedicate it to your infallible wisdom. This is a book precious not so much for the physical size of the work as for its utility. Indeed, it has so consummately compressed the whole system of astrology into a compendium that it has left nothing untouched that pertains to that faculty. Therefore, most venerable prelate, may you admit with your usual kindness the smallness of this gift; may the greatness of my spirit make it worth more to you and compensate for whatever it lacks in scale. Farewell.

# ¶ Here begins the compilation of Leopold, son of the Duchy of Austria, on the science of the stars.

Glorious and sublime God: who created all things by His word and wisely placed the Earth in the middle of heaven, so that the celestial bodies might find a vessel upon which to impress the effects of those virtues they received from their Creator and Ordainer. Only he who is obstinate in mind, or softened by the luxuries of a carnal life, remains ignorant that the Lord of the ages makes unstable and falling impressions upon worldly things <note>The author is describing how the heavens influence the changing, "falling" world of Earth</note> through the stable and perpetual higher bodies which are designated by the name of "heaven" from the Moon upward. Such a person does not consider or observe the works of the higher world and the passions of the lower world. There are also some who wish to be called astrologers <note>original: "astrologi"</note> by the simple folk—usurping a vain name from foolish judges—when they depict and boastfully show the movements of the stars based on the positions established by philosophers, but they do not reach the science of the effects or the "fruits" <note>original: "fructuū." In medieval science, "fruits" refers to the practical application or predictive power of astrology</note>. When such people truly turn their attention to these matters, they find themselves not wise, but wandering astray. For who but a madman would labor on a path and yet neglect to be in his homeland? Who but one captured in mind would plow a field and commit seeds to the earth, yet by his own fault neither gather nor taste the fruit? Let them say whether the movement of the heavens was sought by philosophers for the sake of its effects, or if the effects were sought for the sake of the movement. With the answer to this question established as certain: I do not reject the science of movements—indeed, I approve of it as entirely necessary—yet, by a just judgment of reason, I place the "end" of good things <note>The ultimate goal or purpose</note> before those things which merely lead toward that end. I am, I say, Leopold, son of the Duchy of Austria: who, after long and continuous study of astronomy, have the steadfast intention, for the honor of God, to reduce all that I have grasped concerning the science of the stars into one volume. As for the movements, because many have written copiously and spoken of them before, I shall pass through them summarily, so that I may dwell more fully and usefully upon the *effects*. Although philosophers have written sufficiently about these, I have found none among them who collected the knowledge of various books into one, and thus created a compendium <note>A concise summary or handbook</note> for students.

¶ For this great and weighty purpose, I humbly invoke the power of God, that He may confirm me in His service; and His wisdom, that it may illuminate my intellect; and His goodness, by which His grace may preserve me. I do not hesitate to believe that God will provide grace and favor for the completion of this labor. For while I stir men to the consideration of His miraculous works, I consequently provoke them to His love and to His praise. And so that no one may doubt that I have reverently, and not rashly, undertaken this present work by His virtue and not by my own merit, behold: I bring it forth into the light on the day of His Nativity <note>Christmas Day</note>, in whose name it was conceived and begun.

# ¶ The title of the work shall be this: Here begins the compilation of Leopold, son of the Duchy of Austria, on the science of the stars. 

Let the name of a single author not be sought, for there was not just one author, but many authors who existed. I have been a faithful observer of them and a diligent compiler. Whatever is said in this work of ours is subject to wisdom, for it deals with theory <note>original: "theorica"</note>; the practice of this science, however, is explained in the precepts of the rules <note>original: "canons," referring to the mathematical rules for calculation</note>.

¶ It is divided into ten treatises:

¶ The first is on the spheres, their circles, and their motions.
¶ The second is on the spheres of the planets, their circles, and their motions.
¶ The third is on the proof of the science of impressions <note>The "impressions" or influences the stars leave upon the earthly realm</note> and what this science is.
¶ The fourth is on the introductions to judgments <note>The basics of judicial astrology, or making predictions based on charts</note>.
¶ The fifth is on the revolutions of the years.
¶ The sixth is on the changes of the air <note>Meteorology and weather prediction</note>.
¶ The seventh is on nativities <note>Individual birth charts</note>.
¶ The eighth is on questions <note>Horary astrology, where a chart is cast to answer a specific question</note>.
¶ The ninth is on elections <note>Choosing the most auspicious time to begin an endeavor</note>, to which I shall annex the natural science of images, a daughter of astronomy; I utterly despise and reject that diabolical and profane necromancy <note>original: "nigromãticã." Leopold distinguishes "natural" magic/talismans from "black" or demonic magic</note>.
¶ The tenth is on intentions, the science of which is admirable and stupendous.

**P**resenting, therefore, my intention and the order of the treatises, I ask three things of those to whom this present compilation may come. ¶ The first is that the headings of all propositions be placed at the beginnings of the lines, because I intend to extend them to a count of one thousand: so that they may be numbered more conveniently, found more promptly, and comprehended more easily the more distinctly they are arranged. ¶ The second is that if anyone finds anything superfluous, diminished, or disordered, they should forgive me. For in such a vast sea, any multitude of things could be submerged. ¶ The third is that, considering my labor and their own benefit, they should let me have many thanks. Lastly, because an executor is necessary in this business to provide an abundance of this science to its lovers and not to its detractors, I have chosen above all others Raymond of Laudun, a captain of Orleans <note>original: "capitaneũ aurelianũ"</note>. This is not because he shines with the dignity of high blood according to the way of the world, but because he shines with a laudable honesty of character, being a lover of philosophy and a diligent seeker of truth. Wishing this, and granting it to him as a reward for his labor, I decree that the execution of the work should be considered of no less merit than that of the collector. 

¶ Astrology <term>Astrologia: in this period, the term often referred to what we now call astronomy—the mathematical study of celestial motions</term> is the science that examines and demonstrates the motions of the celestial bodies. 

¶ Astronomy <term>Astronomia: here used to mean the study of celestial influences on Earth, or what we now call astrology</term> is the science that observes the effects, works, and changes of the celestial bodies in these lower realms. Because the effects of things follow upon their motions and positions, it was necessary for the astronomer to receive instruction from the astrologer, through which he might know the motions and positions of the heavens in respect to the Earth upon which he stands, and from there he might surround his judgment regarding their effects.

This circle is called the "Great Sphere," and it is upright, more spacious, and faster than all the others; it contains all the others within itself and revolves in a day-and-night cycle of 360 parts <note>degrees</note> along with the circle of signs. It carries the circle of fixed stars with it from the east to the west. However, the circle of those stars revolves back toward the east by one degree every 100 years, according to Ptolemy <note>The famous Greco-Roman astronomer Claudius Ptolemy; this describes the slow phenomenon known as the precession of the equinoxes</note>.

Then follow the other spheres according to the degree of their contraction and expansion. Because of this [the Great Sphere], there are day and night, and the various seasons of spring, summer, autumn, and winter. It also directs the movement of the seven planets. The Earth is fixed in its center; for if it were not, day and night would never be equal. This sphere is unstarred, and some say that it is spiritual <note>Likely referring to the Primum Mobile or the Empyrean, which was often considered the abode of spiritual beings or the "unmoved mover."</note>.

**O**n the circle of signs <note>The Zodiac</note>: it is the reference against which a star is compared and thus said to be in a particular sign. Its northern pole is distant from the pole of the Great Sphere by nearly 24 parts <note>degrees</note>, as is the southern pole. This sphere is also unstarred; therefore, the physical figures of the signs cannot be placed within it, but they are referred to the twelve divisions of the circle of signs.

Indeed, the Heart of the Lion <term>Heart of the Lion: the star Regulus, the brightest star in the constellation Leo</term> and other fixed stars are now at more advanced degrees in the circle of signs than they were at the time of the Flood <note>A common medieval chronological marker; here it refers to the phenomenon of precession, where star positions appear to shift over long periods of time.</note>. The stars we see are nothing other than the fixed stars. They are not called "fixed" because they do not move at all, but because they do not change their constellations as they move. They mark off sections of the circle of signs according to the measure of their progress in their own sphere. The twelve parts of the circle of signs are named according to the shapes formed by the stars positioned beneath those parts. Thus, the "forms" and "images" of the signs exist in the starry heaven, but the signs themselves are located in the unstarred sphere, where each specific form is matched with its sign. The significance of these images and objects stems from the inherent properties of the signs. The movement of the fixed stars is the recession of the eighth or ninth sphere by one degree every hundred years. The "mean motion" of the eighth sphere is the movement of a small circle beginning from the starting point of Aries or Libra. Its equation <term>equation: in medieval astronomy, a correction applied to a "mean" or average motion to find the true, observable position of a celestial body</term> is the arc of the zodiac stretching from the starting point of the "starry" Aries to the intersection of that same zodiac and the celestial equator.

# The Sizes of the Bodies of the World
There are 14 <note>referring to the 14 categories or specific measurements that follow</note>. The **Sun** has a volume 166 times that of the earth, plus one-fourth of one-eighth <note>original: "quarta vnius octaue"; this equals 1/32, though medieval sources like Al-Farghani typically cite the Sun as 166 and 3/8 times the size of Earth</note>. The **Largest Stars** <note>stars of the first magnitude</note> are 115 times the size of the Earth. **Jupiter** is 95. **Saturn** is 91. **Fixed stars** of the second magnitude are 90. Third magnitude: 70. Fourth: 50. Fifth: 36. Sixth: 18 and 3 <note>original: "18z3"; likely meaning 18 and 1/3</note>. **Mars** is one time [the size of Earth] plus its half and half of an eighth of a time <note>equivalent to 1 and 9/16 times the size of Earth</note>. **Venus** with respect to the Earth is a 1/37 portion. The **Moon** is 1/39 and a little more. **Mercury** is the seventh part of 22—or of 22,000—parts of the Earth <note>medieval calculations for Mercury varied wildly due to its small size and proximity to the Sun</note>.

### Distances of the Celestial Bodies from the Earth
There are 8 distances. The **Diameter of the Earth** contains 6,500 miles. The **Circumference of the Earth** is 20,420 miles and 1,714 cubits, with two cubits remaining undivided <note>the text is using a rough approximation of Pi (approx. 3.1415) to calculate circumference from diameter</note>. From the Earth to the nearest point of the **Moon** is 33 radii of the Earth <note>original: "medietates dyametri terre", literally "halves of the diameter"</note>. To **Mercury**: 64. To **Venus**: 166. To the **Sun**: 1,079 or alternatively 1,979. To **Mars**: 1,260. To **Jupiter**: 8,820 (gloss: 4). To **Saturn**: 10,187. To the **Fixed Stars**: 17,360. **Albumasar** <note>Abu Ma'shar al-Balkhi, a 9th-century Persian astronomer</note> places the distance from the Earth to the nearest point of the Moon at 128,094 miles. He says a mile consists of 3,000 cubits.

The <term>Arc of the Day</term> <note>the portion of the sky the sun crosses between sunrise and sunset</note> is the arc of a circle equidistant from the <term>equator</term> through which the sun moves on that day. The <term>Arc of the Night</term> is what remains uncrossed of that circle on that day. An **Equal Hour** is the time in which 15 degrees are raised from the equator <note>since 15 degrees times 24 hours equals a full 360-degree rotation</note>. A **Temporal Hour** <note>also called "unequal hours" or "Jewish hours"</note> is one in which there are more or fewer [degrees]: and it is the twelfth part of the daylight period. The habitable world between East and West does not span 4 hours <note>this likely refers to the width of a specific geographical "clime" rather than the whole known world</note>.

The **Earth** with respect to the heavens has no perceptible size <note>it is treated as a mathematical point at the center of the universe</note>. All <term>Horizons</term> divide the equator in half; but they divide the other circles equidistant from the equator differently. The **Natural Day** <note>the full 24-hour cycle</note> varies under the equator because of the slant of the **Zodiac** and because of the slant of the horizon. Those living under the equator have two summers and two winters, and four shadows <note>referring to how the sun passes directly overhead twice a year, changing the direction of shadows</note>. The poles of the horizon are inseparable from the horizon under the equator.
