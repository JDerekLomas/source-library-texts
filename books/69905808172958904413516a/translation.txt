I am certain that anyone who knows from their own experience how many difficulties confront those who wish to observe nature on untrodden paths at every step will excuse the imperfection of my remarks.

Every sounding body can produce various tones and adopts a different type of vibratory motion for each of them; the curvature caused by this motion either does not intersect the axis—that is, the shape of the body when it is at rest—at all, or intersects it in 1, 2, 3, or more places. The points where the serpentine lines of vibration intersect the axis are called <term>vibration nodes<note>original: "Schwingungsknoten"</note></term> by Sauveur, de la Hire, and others; they remain at rest while the remaining parts of the sounding body move. One can touch the body or apply dampening at one or more such points without the sound being inhibited, which, however, happens immediately if one touches or otherwise dampens a point between two vibration nodes.

It is well known that in the <term>fundamental tone<note>original: "Grundtone"; the lowest frequency of a vibrating object</note></term> of a string, the axis is not intersected anywhere, but in the other tones, which are called <term>harmonic tones<note>also known as overtones or partials</note></term>, it is intersected 1, 2, 3, or more times; also, if one considers the fundamental tone as 1, the other tones follow the progression 2, 3, 4, and so on. a) Any desired harmonic tone of a string can be easily produced if one touches a point where a vibration node is located—

In the third case, with the simplest sound of a rod whose both ends are free, the axis is intersected twice (fig. 151); in the following cases, it is intersected three (fig. 152), four, or more times. *Euler* <note>Leonhard Euler (1707–1783), a pioneering Swiss mathematician and physicist.</note> denies in his *Method for Finding Curved Lines, etc.* <note>original: "methodo inveniendi curvas etc."</note> the possibility of sounds in which the axis is intersected at an odd number of points; he assumes that this would result in a rotating motion <note>original: "motus rotatorius"</note> around the center of the rod. I had also already discovered this through experiments before I became aware of *Riccati’s* writing and *Euler’s* essay in the *Acts* for 1779. Moreover, Count *Riccati* has added much that is new to *Euler’s* calculations, both regarding the sounds of free-floating cylinders and the vibrations of cylinders in general.

A difference is revealed in the way the vibrating motion is produced; whereas a bell that is struck or played with a violin bow always has the point of contact at approximately the center of a vibrating section—so that the distance to the nearest stationary line <note>The "stationary line" refers to a node, a point in a vibrating body that remains still while other parts move.</note> from this spot is about 45 degrees—on a glass harmonica bell that rotates on its axis, there is a stationary line at the point where it is rubbed. Two areas, which are quite wide and extend from the ends of the inner circle to a short distance from the edge of the disc, produce either no vibrations at all or only extremely weak ones. They connect with one another in various ways, so that no regular curvatures of the circles can be distinguished in these places; instead, all circles terminate in straight or slightly curved lines.

Furthermore, I intend at another time to further explain and correct much of what has been said so far, as well as to provide remarks on the vibrations of some differently shaped elastic bodies, and on various other subjects; especially if, in the future, a more favorable fate should permit me to devote more to physical and mechanical experiments than I am currently able to devote to them.
