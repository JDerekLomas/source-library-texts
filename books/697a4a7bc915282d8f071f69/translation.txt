# TO DISCERNING <note>original: "DISRCETI," a typographical error for "DISCRETI" (discerning or discreet)</note>
## Readers.

<image-desc>A large historiated woodcut initial 'P' depicting a rural landscape with a building, trees, and a seated figure in the foreground.</image-desc>

IT WILL PERHAPS seem a new thing, and entirely out of place, to see the Dialogue on the universality of love by Leone Ebreo <note>Judah Leon Abravanel (c. 1460–1530), a Jewish philosopher, physician, and poet. His *Dialoghi d'amore* was one of the most influential philosophical works of the 16th century.</note>—a man who, as far as can be gathered from his writings, was of the greatest genius, most learned, and highly practiced in <term>Philosophy</term> <note>Here referring to the study of the nature of reality and existence, particularly Neo-Platonism.</note>—published alone and without the usual company of the other Dialogues. Perchance we shall not earn praise (as our good intentions deserve) or the favor of wise readers, but instead their blame and disfavor, as if we had spoiled and maimed such a beautiful body by tearing away the most beautiful and best limb within it. This could undoubtedly be attributed to us as a failing if, by removing this part, the rest of the work remained lacking and imperfect, or if this Dialogue, separated from the others, lost that splendor which the entire work receives from the profound wisdom introduced there by the Author under the name of **Sophia** (which means nothing other than <note>original: "Sophia," from the Greek Σοφία</note> **wisdom**). 

But since the other dialogues do not lose their accustomed beauty when this one is removed, nor does this one appear any less learned and pleasant when separated from them, it did not seem inappropriate—given that it treats the universality of Love—to give it to the press once again, emended and most diligently corrected for the common benefit of everyone. We were induced to do so chiefly for the reasons written below. First, because we were earnestly requested to do so by virtuous and rare <note>The word "rade" (rare) here implies individuals of exceptional or distinguished character.</note> persons, whom we neither can nor wish to fail for many respects. Next, so that being by itself capable of leading the human intellect to that happiness which is possible to achieve on earth, it may be impressed upon virtuous minds with greater ease than it would in the company of the others, leaving them full of its health-giving fruits. Lastly, being taught by that common love which the book itself teaches us, we did this so that—costing much less by itself than the whole work is worth—a greater number of spirits who love the good sciences may participate in it. Accept it...
