THE Sâmaveda <note>The "Veda of Melodies" or Holy Songs</note>, or Veda of Holy Songs, third in the usual order of enumeration of the three Vedas <note>The foundational scriptures of Hinduism</note>, ranks next in sanctity and liturgical <term>liturgical: relating to the formal rites of public worship</term> importance to the Rigveda or Veda of Recited Praise. Its Sanhitâ <note>The "Collection" or metrical portion of the text</note>, or metrical portion, consists chiefly of hymns to be chanted by the Udgâtar <term>Udgâtar: the specific priest responsible for chanting during sacrifices</term> priests at the performance of those important sacrifices in which the juice of the Soma <note>A sacred plant whose juice was used in Vedic rituals to induce a state of inspiration or immortality</term> plant, clarified and mixed with milk and other ingredients, was offered in libation <note>A ritual pouring of a liquid as an offering to a deity</note> to various deities. The Collection is made up of hymns, portions of hymns, and detached verses, taken mainly from the Rigveda, transposed and re-arranged, without reference to their original order, to suit the religious ceremonies in which they were to be employed. In these compiled hymns there are frequent variations, of more or less importance, from the text of the Rigveda as we now possess it, which variations, although in some cases they are apparently explanatory, seem in others to be older and more original than the readings of the Rigveda. In singing, the verses are still further altered by prolongation, repetition and insertion of syllables, and various modulations, rests, and other modifications prescribed, for the guidance of the officiating priests, in the Gânas or Song-books. Two of these manuals, the Grâmageyagâna, or Congregational <note>Literally "songs to be sung in the village"</note>, and the Âranyagâna or Forest Song-book <note>Songs intended for the ascetic or secret study in the wilderness</note>, follow the order of the verses of Part I. of the Sanhitâ, and two others,

There is no clue to the date of the compilation of the <term>Sâmaveda</term> <note>The "Veda of Melodies," the third of the four sacred Hindu scriptures</note> Hymns, nor has the compiler's name been handed down to us. Such a manual was unnecessary in the early times when the <term>Âryans</term> <note>The Indo-European people who migrated into northern India</note> first came into India, but was required for guidance and use in the complicated ritual elaborated by the invaders after their expansion and settlement in their new homes.

For help in translating the non-Ṛigvedic hymns of the Sâmaveda <note>The Veda of Melodies or Chants; while most of its text is drawn from the Ṛigveda, it contains unique verses intended for singing during sacrifices.</note> I am additionally indebted to the late Professor Benfey and to Professor Ludwig whose version will be found in his *The Ṛigveda* <note>original: "Der Ṛigveda"</note>, vol. iii, pp. 19—25.
