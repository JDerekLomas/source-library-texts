# ORATION ON THE DIGNITY OF MAN

<image-desc>A large, ornate decorative initial 'P' at the beginning of the first paragraph, surrounded by leafy, vine-like scrollwork in green ink.</image-desc>

I HAVE READ, venerable fathers, in the records of the Arabians, that when Abdala the Saracen <note>Likely referring to a medieval scholar or a character from Islamic literature used as a rhetorical authority</note> was asked what seemed to him most wonderful in this world, he replied that there is nothing more remarkable than man. To this sentiment agree the words of Hermes: "O Asclepius, what a great miracle is man!" <note>original: "Magnum, o Asclepi, miraculum est homo" — a quote from the Hermetic corpus, a collection of mystical and philosophical texts attributed to the legendary Hermes Trismegistus</note>. When I meditated on the meaning of these sayings, I was not satisfied by the numerous arguments brought forward by many in favor of the superiority of human nature: that man is the mediator between all creatures, close to the higher beings and lord over the lower; the interpreter of nature by virtue of the keenness of his mind, the clarity of his thinking, and the inquisitiveness of his intellect; the interval between unchanging eternity and flowing time; the bond of the world <note>original: "copula mundi"</note>, as the Persians say; a marriage-song <term>Hymenaeus: a Greek wedding hymn or the god of marriage, symbolizing the union of different parts of the universe</term> standing a little lower than the angels, according to the testimony of David <note>Referring to Psalm 8:5</note>.

All this is significant, but it is not the main thing that deserves the greatest admiration. For why should we not admire to a greater degree the angels and the beautiful heavenly choirs? In the end, it seemed to me that I understood why man is the happiest of all living beings and worthy of universal admiration, and what lot was prepared for him among all other destinies—a lot to be envied not only by animals, but even by the stars and the souls beyond this world. It is incredible and wonderful! And how could it be otherwise? For it is precisely because of this that man is rightly called and considered a great miracle, a living being truly worthy of admiration. But whatever it may be, listen, fathers, and graciously forgive me this speech.

Now the Supreme Father, God the Creator, had already fashioned this worldly home according to the laws of wisdom, which to us appears as the most august temple of divinity. He adorned the super-celestial sphere with intelligence, and animated the heavenly bodies with eternal souls. He filled the filthy, excremental parts of the lower world with a diverse mass...

Then he will direct us to feed the cock—that is, to nourish the divine part of our soul with the knowledge of divine things as its true food and heavenly ambrosia. When this cock crows, the straying man comes to his senses. This cock crows daily at the dawn of morning, when the stars praise God.

**Plato**, when he wrote to **Dionysius** about certain higher substances, asserted that it was necessary to speak in riddles so that if the letter fell into the wrong hands, its content would not be revealed.

**Aristotle** declared that his work *Metaphysics*, in which he speaks of the divine, was "published and at the same time not published." What else is there to add?

**Jesus Christ**, the teacher of life, as Origen noted, revealed much to his disciples, who did not wish, however, to write it all down, so as not to make the secret the property of the crowd.

After the Jews were saved by Cyrus from the Babylonian captivity and the temple was restored under **Zerubbabel**, they turned to the restoration of the law. **Ezra**, who was then the head of the church, having corrected the book of Moses and clearly understanding that due to exiles, murders, flight, and the captivity of the Israelite people, the custom established by the ancestors of passing the teaching orally could not be maintained—and so that the secrets of the highest teaching revealed to Moses by God would not be lost in the future, as it was no longer possible to keep the commentaries on it in memory—decreed at a meeting of the most prominent scholars of the time that each should report to all what he had preserved in memory regarding the secrets of the law, and those invited were to write it all down in seventy books (according to the number of scholars in the **Sanhedrin** <note>The supreme council and tribunal of the Jews.</note> at that time).

And so that you, fathers, might believe not only me in this matter, listen to Ezra himself, who spoke thus:

That which you wrote first, make public, so that the worthy and unworthy may read it, but the following seventy books preserve for transmission to the most learned men of your people. In them is the **vein of understanding**, the **source of wisdom**, and the **river of knowledge**. And so I did."⁷² <note>Pico is quoting from the apocryphal text 2 Esdras 14:44–47.</note>
