## OF MARCUS AURELIUS ANTONINUS the Philosopher
# THE LIFE;
->FROM<-
## ANDRÉ DACIER,
->AND<-
->Excerpts from other authors, confirmed by the testimonies of the Ancients.<-

<margin>Book 9, Section 24 <note>Likely a reference to Marcus Aurelius's own 'Meditations' or a specific historical compendium.</note></margin>
For a long time now, Roman affairs had experienced the various shifts of fortune since they had been brought under the dominion of a single ruler. Specifically, after the murder of Julius Caesar, the Roman state endured the cruelty, insolence, and tyranny of the Triumvirs <note>The political alliance of Octavian, Mark Antony, and Lepidus.</note>, and of Nero and Domitian—an era infamous for fatal calamities and the most grievous injuries. Yet, the state was also enriched and refreshed by the most ample and pleasant fruits of Justice, Clemency, and Piety through the benefit of the best Princes, during the reigns of Augustus, Vespasian, Trajan, Hadrian, and Antoninus Pius. Whether you look at virtue or vice, you might say that this City, in such diverse states of affairs, had before its eyes the most perfect examples of both dispositions.

But God, who so arranges both the succession and the characters of rulers according as it seems good to Him—either to wear down the republic with misfortunes or to raise it up when broken and fallen—reserved the perfect image of virtue to be exhibited at last in Marcus Aurelius. Before his radiant brightness, whatever of the Caesars was previously considered illustrious easily gave way to him in praise and admiration of Virtue, just as a shadow or twilight yields to the Sun. To put everything into a single word, divine counsel seems to have composed the Wisdom of this Prince so that he alone might be equal to those most mournful calamities which awaited him during his reign; he who, of almost all who have ever reigned anywhere, was the only one who could be their match.

For never had so many and such fierce storms brooded over the Roman Empire at once, arising from almost every quarter. By these, the Empire would not only have been broken, but utterly submerged, destroyed, and perished, had it been without him as its pilot. Therefore, I would wish those who devote their efforts to reading these accounts to be warned of this one thing: let them not hope to find in this Prince the clever schemes of politicians, nor the solemn tricks of the court, nor the complex deceits and simulations of insolent men, nor again the "arts" (as they call them) of governing a kingdom with cunning. He was indeed wise, but with a wisdom far different from those. We are depicting an Emperor, to be sure, but a philosophizing Emperor—a Prince most adorned with simplicity of character, truth, piety, modesty, and candor, and every sanctity of life. A King who first learned to rule himself, and to desire nothing except what justice willed and right reason persuaded, and who held this as the first of all his duties.
