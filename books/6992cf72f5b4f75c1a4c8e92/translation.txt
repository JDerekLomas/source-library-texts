# General Introduction to the Historical Evolution of the South and West Oceans and the Five Indias

Navigation at sea relies on the compass to determine direction, just as the armillary sphere <note>A model of objects in the sky, consisting of a spherical framework of rings, used here to represent global coordinates.</note> is used to distinguish the cardinal points on the globe. The "Five Indias" <note>Historically, Chinese texts divided the Indian subcontinent into five regions: North, South, East, West, and Central India.</note> are located to the southwest of China <note>original: "震旦" (Zhendan), an ancient Buddhist name for China derived from the Sanskrit *Cinasthana*.</note>. When calculated from China's orientation—which leans toward the east—they occupy the southern and southwestern sectors <note>original: "丙午丁未" (Bing, Wu, Ding, Wei), terms from the traditional 24-point compass denoting bearings between South and Southwest.</note>. According to the twenty-four horizontal compass points, they are located in the southeast-to-south range <note>original: "巽巳" (Xun, Si), bearings roughly corresponding to Southeast and South-Southeast.</note>.

Prior to the Tang dynasty, the tribute route from the Western Regions passed through the Pamir Mountains <note>original: "蔥嶺" (Congling), literally "Onion Range."</note> to reach the Jade Gate Pass <note>The *Yumen Guan*, a vital frontier pass in modern Gansu province that served as the gateway to the Silk Road.</note>. Although the path was long and winding, generations of monks seeking Buddhist scriptures and the Dharma traveled it personally; what they witnessed with their eyes was recorded in maps and descriptions as clear as the palm of one's hand.

During the Tiansheng era of the Song dynasty [1023–1032 AD], to avoid the plundering raids of the Western Xia <note>A Tangut empire that controlled the Hexi Corridor, effectively blocking the overland Silk Road to the Song heartland.</note>, the tribute routes for nations such as the *Dashi* <term>Dashi: A historical Chinese name for the Arab Caliphate or the Persian regions.</term> were changed to sea routes arriving at Guangzhou. From that point on, people became accustomed to the convenience of maritime navigation. Consequently, the various coastal nations of the ancient Western Regions and the Southwest have generally been referred to as the "South and West Oceans."

Of the ancient names for these countries, sixty percent have been lost. When the Yuan dynasty [1271–1368 AD] held sway over the world, they annexed the Western Regions and enfeoffed the sons-in-law and younger members of the imperial clan as kings, completely changing the names of the countries into the Mongolian language.

They were said to be conquering a land "never before reached since ancient times." <gloss>This is found in the Biography of Shi Bi.</gloss> However, when examining its actual territory, the distance from both Fujian and Guangdong is less than 10,000 *li*.

### General Investigation into the History of the Five Indias <gloss>Originally missing from the source; now supplemented and compiled</gloss>

According to the *Book of the Later Han* <note>The *Hou Hanshu*, an official history of the Eastern Han Dynasty (25–220 CE).</note>, the kingdom of **Tianzhu** <note>A historical Chinese name for India.</note>—also known as **Shendu** <note>An early phonetic transcription of "Sindhu" (the Indus), later used to refer to the entire Indian subcontinent.</note>—is located several thousand *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters.</note> southeast of the **Yuezhi** <note>The Yuezhi, or Kushans, were an ancient Central Asian people who established a vast empire covering parts of modern-day Afghanistan and Northern India.</note> territory. The customs of the people are similar to those of the Yuezhi, but the land is low-lying, damp, and intensely hot. 

The kingdom borders a great river <note>Literally "Great Water," likely referring to the Indus or the sea.</note>. The inhabitants ride elephants into battle. The people are described as weaker than the Yuezhi. They practice the way of **the Buddha** <note>Original: *Futu* (浮圖), an early Chinese transliteration for Buddha.</note> and refrain from killing or waging war; this has consequently become their established custom. 

From the kingdom of **Gaofu** <note>Modern-day Kabul, Afghanistan.</note> in the Yuezhi territory, extending southwest to the **Western Sea** <note>Likely the Arabian Sea.</note> and east to the kingdom of **Panqi** <note>An ancient kingdom located in either eastern India or Northern Southeast Asia.</note>, all is considered the land of Shendu. Shendu contains several hundred distinct cities, each with its own appointed governor. There are dozens of distinct sub-kingdoms, each with its own king. Although they differ slightly from one another, they all go by the general name of Shendu. 

At that time <note>Referring to the Han Dynasty period.</note>, they were all subjects of the Yuezhi. The Yuezhi killed their local kings and appointed generals to govern the people. The land produces elephants.

The King of **Zhangye** acted as a **patron** <term>Patron (danayue): From the Sanskrit *danapati*, meaning a layperson who provides food and support to the monastic community.</term>. After staying there for one month, he again traveled northwest for fifteen days to reach the Kingdom of **Wuyi**. Traveling southwest for one month and five days, he reached the Kingdom of **Khotan** <note>Original: *Yutian*, a major Buddhist center in the Tarim Basin.</note>. In front of every house, the people built small pagodas, the tallest of which reached about twenty feet. In that country, there were fourteen large **monasteries** <term>Monastery (sangharama): A traditional Buddhist monastic compound.</term>, not counting the smaller ones. The river is named the **Indus River** <note>Original: *Xintou He*</note>. In ancient times, people carved paths through the rock and established seven hundred ladders to facilitate the passage. Having climbed the ladders, they crossed the **River of Sand** <note>Referring here to the Indus River, likely due to its silt or the surrounding arid terrain.</note> by way of suspended ropes. The two banks are roughly eighty paces apart. Even the famous explorers **Zhang Qian** and **Gan Ying** of the Han Dynasty never reached this place. 

Once across the river, one arrives at the Kingdom of **Udyāna** <note>Original: *Wuchang Guo*, located in the Swat Valley of modern-day Pakistan.</note>. This is the northernmost part of India. All the people there speak the language of **Central India**. Here, there are the Buddha’s remaining footprints and the stone where he once dried his robes. 

From here, traveling five days to the east, one reaches the Kingdom of **Gandhara** <note>Original: *Jietuowei*.</note>. This is the land once governed by **Fayi** <note>Likely referring to Kunala, the son of King Ashoka.</note>, the son of **King Ashoka** <term>King Ashoka: The 3rd-century BCE Mauryan Emperor who famously converted to Buddhism and spread the faith across Asia.</term>. 

From here, traveling four days to the south, one reaches **Purushapura** <note>Original: *Tita*, modern-day Peshawar.</note>. This place is considered most supreme, for the **Buddha’s Alms Bowl** is located in this kingdom. # Record of the Northern Wei Monk Huisheng’s Mission to the Western Regions

The monk **Huisheng** of the **Northern Wei** <note>The Northern Wei (386–535 AD) was a dynasty that famously promoted Buddhism; this mission took place during the reign of Emperor Xiaoming.</note> was sent on a mission to the Western Regions. Along with **Song Yun**, a native of **Dunhuang**, he journeyed to the West to retrieve sacred Buddhist scriptures. In total, they obtained 170 texts, all of which were profound works of the **Mahayana** <term>Mahayana: One of the two main branches of Buddhism, emphasizing the path of the Bodhisattva and the salvation of all beings.</term> tradition. 

They first departed from the capital <note>Luoyang, the capital of Northern Wei.</note> and traveled west for forty days to reach **Chiling** <note>Literally "Red Ridge," modern-day Ri’yue Mountains in Qinghai Province.</note>, which marks the western border of the state. These mountains are devoid of grass or trees. Here, one finds the "birds and rats sharing the same burrows" <note>A phenomenon often noted in ancient Chinese texts regarding the high plateaus, likely referring to ground-dwelling birds and pikas living in close proximity.</note>. 

Continuing west for twenty-three days, they reached the Kingdom of **Tuyuhun**. Traveling west again for 3,500 *li* <term>li: A traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters.</term>, they arrived at the city of **Shanshan** <note>An ancient kingdom located near the Lop Nur salt lake.</note>. Another 1,640 *li* to the west brought them to the city of **Qiemo** <note>Cherchen.</note>, where there stands a statue of a Buddha and Bodhisattva commissioned by **Lü Guang** <note>A general of the 4th century who founded the Later Liang state.</note> during his campaign against the Western barbarians. 

Traveling west for another 375 *li*, they reached **Mo City**, and 22 *li* further west, they came to **Hanmi City**. Within this city is a pagoda dedicated to the Buddha by the Kingdom of **Khotan** <note>original: *Yutian*. An important oasis kingdom and center of Buddhist learning on the southern edge of the Taklamakan Desert.</note>. Surrounding it are thousands of smaller pagodas and tens of thousands of hanging ceremonial banners. 

Further west by 878 *li* lies the Kingdom of Khotan itself. There, one finds a stupa shaped like an inverted bowl built by the King. Within it is preserved the boot of a **Pratyekabuddha** <term>Pratyekabuddha: A "solitary realizer" who attains enlightenment through their own efforts without the guidance of a teacher.</term>, which to this day has not decayed. The territory of Khotan spans over 3,000 *li* from east to west. 

On the twenty-ninth day of the seventh month, in the second year of the **Shengui** era <note>519 AD.</note>, they entered the Kingdom of **Zhuqubo** <note>Likely modern Karghalik.</note>. The people there live in the mountains and do not practice slaughter; they only eat the meat of animals that have died naturally. Their customs and language are the same as those of Khotan, but their writing system is the same as that of the **Brahmans** <note>Refers to Indic scripts, likely Brahmi.</note>. The borders of this kingdom can be traversed in five days. 

In the eighth month, they entered the territory of the Kingdom of **Kabandha** <note>Likely modern Tashkurgan in the Pamir Mountains.</note>. After six days of westward travel, they ascended the **Congling** Mountains <note>The Pamir Mountains.</note>. After three more days, they reached the city of **Bameng**. Since crossing the Congling Mountains, they reached **Shoulong Lake** <note>Sometimes identified with Lake Victoria or Zorkul in the Pamirs.</note>. It is said that in ancient times, the King of Kabandha used **Brahman spells** <note>Mantras or Sanskrit incantations.</note> to move a dragon from here; the dragon moved more than 2,000 *li* west of the Congling Mountains. From the moment they set out into the Congling range, the path grew higher with every step; it took four days of such climbing before they reached the peaks.

# The Crossing of the Pamirs and the Kingdom of the Hephthalites

The mountain ridges hang halfway to the heavens. The Kingdom of **Kepantuo** <note>Modern-day Tashkurgan in Xinjiang.</note> sits right upon the mountain peaks. From the **Pamir Mountains** <term>Congling: Historically known as the "Onion Range," these mountains form the high-altitude hub of Central Asia.</term> westward, all waters flow west into the West Sea <note>Likely referring to the Aral Sea or the Caspian Sea.</note>. People of the time say that this place is the center of Heaven and Earth.

In mid-September, the mission entered the Kingdom of **Bohuo** <note>Located in the Wakhan Corridor of modern Afghanistan.</note>. Here, high mountains, deep valleys, and treacherous paths are the constant reality. The people use the natural mountainsides as their city walls; they wear simple clothing and dwell in caves, with humans and livestock living in close proximity. The wind and snow are piercingly cold. There is a "Great Snowy Mountain" that, when viewed from afar, resembles a peak of pure jade.

In early October, they entered the Kingdom of the **Hephthalites** <note>Transliterated as *Yeda*, a powerful nomadic empire often called the White Huns.</note>. These people possess no walled cities but move their camps to follow water and pasture. They have no knowledge of writing and their calendar lacks leap months, counting exactly twelve months as one year. They receive tribute from many surrounding nations. 

To the south, their power reaches **Diela**; to the north, it extends to **Chile**; to the east, it covers **Khotan**; and to the west, it reaches **Persia**. More than forty countries come to pay them tribute, as they are the most powerful nation in the region. The King’s tent is forty paces in circumference, and his utensils are crafted from the **Seven Treasures** <term>Seven Treasures: A traditional Buddhist list of precious substances, including gold, silver, lapis lazuli, and carnelian.</term>. They do not believe in the **Dharma** <note>The teachings of the Buddha.</note>; instead, they practice animal sacrifice and eat raw meat. When they met the envoys of the **Wei** dynasty, they bowed to receive the imperial decree. This land is over 20,000 *li* <note>An ancient Chinese unit of distance; 20,000 li is roughly 6,000 miles.</note> from the capital.

In November, they entered the borders of **Persia**. The territory there is quite narrow. It took seven days to pass through the lands of the Hephthalites. Adjacent to these are several smaller states such as **Niji**, **Fuhan**, and **Shan**. The people live in mountain valleys where the glare of the snow is so bright it dazzles the eyes.

In mid-November, they entered the Kingdom of **Shemi** <note>Likely the Chitral region in modern Pakistan.</note>. They were gradually emerging from the Pamir Mountains. The terrain was jagged and dangerous; the paths were so narrow that a person and a horse could barely pass. They crossed chasms using suspended iron chains, where the depths below were so great the bottom could not be seen.

At the beginning of December, they entered the Kingdom of **Udyana** <note>Transliterated as *Wuchang*, located in the Swat Valley.</note>. To its north lie the Pamirs and to its south lies **India** <note>Original: *Tianzhu*.</note>. The climate is mild and warm, the fields are lush and fertile, and the people and their goods are prosperous. 

The King follows a vegetarian diet and maintains a permanent fast. He worships the Buddha day and night, and only begins to handle the affairs of state after noon. The sound of temple bells echoes throughout the land, and exotic flowers are offered in devotion. Upon hearing that the Wei envoys had arrived, the King prostrated himself to receive the imperial decree. Within this land is the site where the **Tathagata** <term>Tathagata: An honorific title for the Buddha, meaning "One who has thus come."</term> once dried his robes upon a flat stone. Other sacred traces of the Buddha’s presence are found everywhere; each holy site is covered by a temple or a stupa <term>Stupa: A dome-shaped Buddhist monument used to house relics or mark sacred sites.</term>. The monks here are extremely disciplined and strict in their practice. This continued until the middle of the fourth month of the first year of the **Guangyuan** era <note>Approximately 519–520 AD; likely referring to the *Zhengguang* era of the Northern Wei.</note>...
