I HAVE NO DOUBT, most merciful Francesco, Great Duke of the Tuscans, and always the most fierce defender of the Catholic faith, that your Serenity knows very well those things which were said by the ancient seekers of wisdom concerning justice; and not only that you know them, but also that you prove them through the most open and effective works in the ruling of your subjects, and in the orderly conduct of a Christian life. Those ancients clearly understood that justice, offended by the vices of men, was absent from human affairs and had migrated to heaven: for they wished to teach, through certain metaphors, what it means to live justly. For after Saturn was driven out by his son <note>original: "Saturnus à filio pulsus est"; refers to the mythological transition from the Golden Age of Saturn to the Silver Age of Jupiter/Jove</note>, fleeing the weapons of Jove, the most just virgin <note>The goddess Astraea, the personification of Justice, who was said to be the last immortal to leave the earth as it became corrupt</note> quickly abandoned the lands. From this, he <note>Jove/Jupiter</note> added deadly venom to black serpents and commanded wolves to hunt. Then the madness of war and the love of possession followed. <note>This passage paraphrases Virgil's *Georgics* and Ovid's *Metamorphoses*, describing the moral decay of humanity.</note> But because God is rich in mercy, and is the most indulgent parent, he sent his only-begotten son to us from heaven to bring back the exiled justice, so that the appearance of that Golden Age might return—namely, the pious and religious worship of the one God. Moreover, this worship, most merciful Francesco, although it embraces all virtues at once, has two that are the most principal of all: piety and equity. These are like the veins of this pious and holy Christian religion—

...of religion. Furthermore, piety is the worship of God, the essence of which is that you revere Him. Therefore, anyone who does not hold to the religion of God is proven to be ignorant of justice. For how can one hold to justice itself if they are ignorant of its origin? The other part of justice is called equity, namely equality. For God willed all men to be equal: He imposed the same condition of living upon all; He brought all forth for wisdom; He promised immortality to all. Therefore, those who have held men to be unequal by many degrees could by no means maintain justice.

Rightly, therefore, was it said by the Philosopher <note>original: "Philosopho"; a standard medieval and Renaissance reference to Aristotle</note> in Book 1 of his *Metaphysics*: Justice is the virtue by which individuals possess what is theirs according to the laws. And in Book 6 of his *Ethics*, he calls justice the most illustrious of virtues; for justice is a great splendor, from which good men receive their name. Indeed, from justice arise innocence, friendship, harmony, piety, religion, and humanity—those things which make a man good. In truth, justice claims nothing belonging to another; it neglects its own advantage so that it may preserve the common good.

Valerius Maximus <note>A 1st-century Roman author known for his collection of historical anecdotes</note> relates in Book 6, under the title "On Justice," that remarkable example of justice: Zaleucus <note>original: "Zeleucus"; a famous lawgiver of the Locrians in Italy known for his strict but fair laws</note>, having fortified the city of the Locrians with beneficial and most useful laws, found his own son condemned for the crime of adultery. According to the law Zaleucus himself had established, the guilty party was to lose both eyes. Although the whole city, out of respect for the father, wished to waive the penalty for the youth, Zaleucus resisted for some time. At last, overcome by the prayers of the people, he plucked out one of his own eyes first, and then one of his son’s, leaving the use of sight to both. In this way, with an admirable balance, he showed himself to be both a merciful father and a just lawmaker. 

Moreover, Cambyses <note>Cambyses II, King of Persia, whose "Judgment of Cambyses" became a popular subject in European art as a warning against judicial corruption</note> showed an example of unusual severity: he had the skin flayed from the body of a most wicked judge and stretched it over the judicial seat, then commanded the judge's son to sit upon it to deliver his own judgments. Truly, that Barbarian King, through this atrocious and novel punishment of a judge, ensured that no judge could thereafter be corrupted.

Holy Scripture has placed before our eyes the clearest and most effective example of justice of all: *Mercy and truth have met together,* it says, *justice and peace have kissed each other* <note>original: "Misericordia & veritas obuiauerunt sibi... iustitia & pax osculatæ sunt"; Psalm 85:10</note>—specifically in our Lord Jesus. To uphold justice, He endured the shame of the cross and the punishment of a most ignominious death, which was owed to our own transgressions. *In whatever hour you eat,* it says, *you shall die the death* <note>original: "In quacunq; hora comederis, morte morieris"; a reference to Genesis 2:17</note>. The sentence of death had been passed, and by taking it upon Himself, the innocent Lamb found for us the entrance to life. For this reason, He is rightly called the most Just King, and He has attained a kingdom that has no end. *Love justice, therefore, you who judge the earth* <note>original: "Diligite ergo iustitiam, qui iudicatis terram"; the opening line of the Book of Wisdom</note>; for justice, placed in a certain lofty retreat, sees and examines...

...all of which searches not so much for its own advantage as for public benefit. For justice will not do to another what it would not wish to happen to itself, nor will it deny to another what it would want for itself; in these principles, certainly, the full and complete state of justice consists. And truly, as the wise man says in the Book of Wisdom, chapter 1: <note>original: "Sap: 1"</note> "Justice is perpetual and immortal, but injustice is the acquisition of death." A kingdom is transferred from one people to another because of injustices, injuries, insults, and various deceits—just as we read that the kingdom of the Chaldeans <note>The Neo-Babylonian Empire</note> was transferred to the Persians, the Persian to the Greeks, and the Greek to the Roman Empire.

And what do you think, most merciful Francis <note>Likely King Francis I of France, a common dedicatee for scholars of this era</note>, is the justice of Christians? Surely nothing other than not sinning; for not to sin is to keep the commandments of the law. Observation of the law’s commandments is maintained in two ways: that you do none of those things which are forbidden, and that you fulfill everything that is commanded. 

Truly, for a wise and just man, the whole world is a treasury of riches; for he holds common things as his own, and his own as common, sparing neither himself nor others, and he does not allow his secrets to remain hidden. Indeed, where there is prudence, there is often also malice; where there is fortitude <note>Physical or moral courage</note>, there is anger; where there is temperance, there is impatience, and frequently other vices; but where there is justice, there is the harmony of all the other virtues. 

Therefore, the highest good in human affairs is to cultivate justice and to preserve for each person their own rights; for to obey justice is the highest freedom. The justice of a prince is the peace of the people, the defense of the fatherland, the immunity of the common folk, the protection of the nation, the care for the suffering, the joy of men, the balance of the air, the serenity of the sea, the fertility of the earth, the solace of the poor, and the hope of future blessedness. Justice is the freedom of the soul, which makes a man admired and respected by everyone; finally, man is the best of animals while he practices justice, but the worst of animals when separated from it. 

Well, then, did the Philosopher <note>Aristotle</note> say in the second book of his *Topics*: "The justice of a ruler is more useful to his subjects than the fertility of the season"; for what is done justly is done wisely, but what is done unjustly is done wickedly and dishonorably. Therefore, justice is better than strength; for if all were just, strength would not be useful. 

Plutarch <note>A famous Greek biographer and philosopher</note> relates that when Agesilaus <note>A king of Sparta known for his austerity and justice</note> was asked whether strength or justice was better, he replied: "We have no need of strength if we are just." Justice is better than great wealth, because justice delivers from death, which riches cannot achieve. ...[better] than prudence; because justice without prudence will be able to do much: but without justice, prudence will be worth nothing. For this reason, justice is not ineptly called by the same Author <note>Likely referring to Cicero, a favorite of Renaissance humanists</note> the foundation of perpetual commendation and fame: without which nothing can be praiseworthy, nothing honorable, nothing more excellent, and finally, nothing more powerful and strong.

For this [justice] could not be drowned in Noah, nor burned in Abraham; it was not crushed in Isaac, nor broken in Jacob; in Joseph, it could be bound, but it could not be conquered; in Moses, it crossed the sea with a dry footprint; in Joshua, it held the sun firm; in David, it overcame persecutions; in Elijah, it slew the unfaithful; in the Prophets, it brought labors to an end; in Christ, it despoiled the strong armed man <note>original: "fortem armatum spoliauit" — a reference to Christ’s victory over the devil in the Gospel of Luke 11:22</note>; in the Apostles, it triumphed over Tyrants; in the Martyrs, it overcame the world; in the Confessors, it received the robe of joy; in the Virgins, it conquered itself.

O admirable Justice, a virgin born of Jove <note>The Roman name for Zeus; the author is blending classical mythology with Christian virtue</note>, chaste and venerable to all the heavenly dwellers who inhabit Olympus: through you kings reign, through you princes command, and the powerful judge with righteousness. For what are kingdoms without you but open robberies? <note>original: "aperta latrocinia" — a famous phrase from St. Augustine’s "City of God" regarding the legitimacy of governments</note> Therefore, love justice, you who judge the earth: if you truly speak justice, judge rightly, you sons of men. This [justice] shut the mouths of lions in Daniel; in those three boys, it extinguished the violence of the fire; in war, it overthrew the camps of foreigners.

Finally, to conclude at once, justice cannot be conquered by death, nor overcome by the devil; on the contrary, it restrains and curbs demons, while it crushes and puts death to flight. Therefore, take up justice, you who judge the earth, and place it in the dwelling-place of your heart. Be equitable and good, and the justice you seek will follow you of its own accord.

Since these things are so, most clement Lord, as I was reflecting and reading again of the grandfathers, great-grandfathers, great-great-grandfathers, and ancestors <note>original: "auos, proauos, abauos, atauos"</note> of your Highness, and the Supreme Pontiffs <note>Refers to the Medici Popes, Leo X and Clement VII</note> arisen from the most noble and ancient Medici family; it came to mind that it has perpetually cultivated justice, and could be called the "family of the just."

And though I am silent about many others, I do not think this one should be kept quiet: the distinguished works of your Most Serene father, who brought back justice when it was abolished and driven away, so that in his times some appearance of that Golden Age <note>In classical mythology, a primordial era of peace and harmony</note> shone forth.
