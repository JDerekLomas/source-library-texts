# INTRODUCTION

THE general Introduction to the reduced photographic facsimile of the Codex Alexandrinus <note>A famous 5th-century manuscript of the Greek Bible, one of the four great uncial codices.</note> is prefixed to the part containing the New Testament and the Clementine Epistles, issued in 1909. In that Introduction is given the history of the manuscript, so far as it is known, and of the various editions of it, a statement of its contents, and an analysis of the various hands of the scribes and correctors employed upon it. To this there is little that is material to be added here.<sup>1</sup>

The present part (the first of four that will contain the Old Testament) comprises the Octateuch <note>The first eight books of the Old Testament: Genesis through Ruth.</note>. These eight books form a single unit, written by a single scribe. They are also the part of the manuscript which has been most extensively corrected. As has been stated in the general Introduction, the original readings of the MS. <note>Manuscript</note>, and the work of the various correctors, are indicated as follows:

->A = the reading of the MS. when there has been no alteration.<-
->A* = the original reading of the MS. when there has been an alteration.<-
->A<sup>1</sup> = the reading of the correction, when it has been made by the original scribe.<-
->A<sup>a</sup>, A<sup>b</sup>, A<sup>c</sup>, &c. = readings due to subsequent correctors.<-

The attributions of the various corrections to their several authors, so far as the present editor has been able to satisfy himself on the subject, are recorded in the larger Cambridge edition of the Septuagint <note>The ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament.</note>.<sup>2</sup> Most of them are due either to the original scribe or to the first corrector (A<sup>a</sup>), whose hand (visible on an un-erased portion of the vellum) is smoother and rounder than that of his predecessors, but does not appear to be materially later in date. This corrector worked very drastically on the earlier books of the Octateuch, frequently erasing whole passages of two, three, or four lines, and substituting a fresh text. The textual relation of the corrector to the original scribe does not appear to have been studied as yet, but the material is now available in the edition of Messrs. Brooke and McLean, and (substantially) in the earlier edition of Dr. Swete. It is possible that a study of this subject would assist in determining the authorship of certain of the corrections, where the palaeographical <note>The study of ancient handwriting.</note> indications are doubtful. The work of the other correctors (of whom three may be discerned) is of very minor importance.

F. G. KENYON.

BRITISH MUSEUM,
*15 Jan., 1915.*

<sup>1</sup> Prof. Burkitt, in a review of the New Testament volume (*Journal of Theological Studies*, xi. 603, 1910), has some interesting remarks, favouring the tradition recorded by Wetstein (see N. T., Introduction, p. 7, note 3) to the effect that the manuscript was found at Mt. Athos. In his opinion, the Arabic note by “Athanasius the humble” may be contemporary with Cyril, and the work of one of his staff; the MS. may never have been in Egypt at all until Cyril brought it there, but may be of Constantinopolitan origin, which would account for the Constantinopolitan character of its text in the Gospels. If an Arabic scholar and palaeographer states that it is not possible to be certain whether a note was written in the fourteenth or the seventeenth century, it is not for those who have no knowledge of Oriental palaeography to dispute the statement; but it does seem absolutely impossible to believe that Cyril <note>Cyril Lucaris (1572–1638), Patriarch of Alexandria and later of Constantinople, who gave the Codex to King Charles I.</note> had himself brought the MS. from Athos to Alexandria. If Cyril had himself found it at Athos, and presented it to the patriarchate at Alexandria with an anathema on its removal, it is not very likely that he would have transferred it to Constantinople, and still less likely that he would have impressed its Egyptian origin on Sir Thomas Roe (as he evidently did), and suppressed its Athoan derivation and his own merit in discovering it. But even if these difficulties are ignored, it is plainly incredible that he should with his own hand have stated its Egyptian origin, as he does in the autograph note prefixed to the manuscript, and should have attributed the mutilation of the end of the MS. to the ill-treatment which the Christians and their books suffered after the Mahomedan conquest of Egypt. This note makes it certain that Cyril himself believed in the Egyptian origin of the MS.; and in face of this evidence, the statement of his deacon, Matthaeus Muttis, falls to the ground. One is tempted to say that Prof. Burkitt’s suggestion has only the attractiveness of the improbable.
