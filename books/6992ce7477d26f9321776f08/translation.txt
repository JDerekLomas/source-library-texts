### Horses, Part I

Master Mao <note>The author, Mao Yuanyi, refers to himself here in the third person as "Maozi," a title of respect similar to "Master Mao."</note> says: 

"For travel in the heavens, nothing compares to the dragon; for use on the earth, nothing compares to the horse. Truly this is so! Truly this is so! 

The *Book of Odes* <note>original: 詩 (Shī) — Referring to the Shijing, the oldest existing collection of Chinese poetry (11th to 7th centuries BC).</note> says: 'A lucky day was that *wu* day; we sacrificed to the Father of Horses <note>original: 伯 (Bó) — The "Celestial Horse" or "Father of Horses," a deity associated with the protection of horses.</note> and prayed.' This shows how much importance was placed upon them. 

The *Rites of Zhou* <note>original: 周禮 (Zhōulǐ) — A classic text describing the idealized organizational structure of the Zhou Dynasty government.</note> says: 'Regulate their food and drink, inspect their six joints <note>Refers to the physical health and mobility of the horse's limbs.</note>, manage the timing of hitching and unhitching, and distinguish their housing according to the four seasons.' This shows the great care with which they were treated. 

After the Three Dynasties <note>The Xia, Shang, and Zhou, considered the "Golden Age" of antiquity.</note>, in the **Han Dynasty**, horses were raised by the people and utilized by the government. In the **Tang Dynasty**, they were raised by the government and provided to the people. In the **Song Dynasty**, the government raised them while the people also kept them, but ultimately, the state resorted to purchasing them from the border tribes <note>original: 戎 (Róng) — A general term for non-Han ethnic groups to the west and north, renowned for their superior horse breeds.</note>."

<note>This introduction establishes the horse not just as a tool of war, but as a sacred and essential element of statecraft, tracing the evolution of horse management from ancient rituals to the practical logistics of later dynasties.</note>

# Physical Appraisal

The **Classic of Judging Horses** <note>original: 相馬經 (Xiàngmǎjīng) — an ancient Chinese manual on equine physiognomy, traditionally attributed to the legendary judge of horses, Bole.</note> states: Among the **thirty-two physical traits** <term>sānshí'èr xiàng: a standardized set of anatomical markers used to evaluate a horse's quality</term>, the **eyes** are of primary importance.

Next, observe the **head and face**, which should be square and round in form. The eyes should resemble **hanging bells** and possess a vibrant purple luster. They should fill the sockets and protrude slightly, yet the horse should not be easily startled. 

If **white threads** <note>likely referring to specific vascular patterns or the clarity of the sclera</note> run through the pupil, the horse is capable of traveling five hundred *li* <note>roughly 150 miles or 250 kilometers</note> in a single journey. However, if the eyes are spotted like **scattered beans**, they must be viewed in a different <insert>negative</insert> light.

The profile of the face should be curved like the **back of a sickle**. The nostrils should be wide, like **golden cups**, and large enough to hide a fist <note>broad nostrils were prized as a sign of superior respiratory capacity for galloping</note>. 

The mouth must be deep, and the teeth should be set far apart. The tongue should be shaped like a **heavy sword** and have the pinkish color of a **lotus**. A mouth free of **black spots** <note>original: 黑靨 (hēiyè) — moles or dark pigmentations on the tongue or gums</note> indicates a long-lived animal. 

The lips should fit together like the **lid of a hanging chest** <term>chuíxiānggài: a metaphor for firm, well-aligned lips that indicate a strong constitution</term>. Finally, the **jawbone** <term>shícáo: literally "feeding trough," the space between the lower jawbones</term> must be wide and expansive, appearing clean and without excess flesh.

The **swallow bones** <note>referring to the zygomatic arches or jawbones</note> should be high and well-arched.

There are **eight arches** that should be curved, and the **left and right dragon-meetings** <note>original: 龍會 (lónghuì) — likely referring to the junctions of major muscle groups or meridians</note> must be high and arched, according to the teachings passed down from **antiquity**. 

The **neck** should be long, like that of a **phoenix**, and must be naturally curved. The **mane** should be soft and luxuriant. The **waist** should be as slender and flexible as **silk** <note>original: 綿 (mián)</note>. The **crest** should be long and the **shoulders** broad. The **wind-catchers** <term>qiǎngfēng: the area of the chest that meets the wind while running</term> should be small, while the **chest** itself is wide.

The **flanks** should be high and the **feet** broad. The **front knees** must be wide and have a high arch. The structure should be as round as "restrained bones." The **tendons** should be fine while the **joints** are thick. The **hooves** must be **gathered** <term>cuántí: hooves that are compact, upright, and well-formed rather than splayed</term>; they must be round, solid, and stand perfectly upright. 

The **body** should be level, full, and expansive. The frame must be flat and wide. The **tendons and joints** should be curved and must be tightly knit. The flesh where the **saddle** sits must be thick and stable. The "two peaks of the golden saddle" <note>likely the withers and the croup</note> should press down as if concealing the bone. 

When the horse **lies down**, it should be as agile as a **falling ape**; when it **stands**, it should be as steady as a **mountain**. The **nose** should be like that of a **goose**, possessing both curve and straightness, appearing stable. The **tail** should be like a **shooting star**, flowing and not clumped together.

The size of the **fat and tendons** must be even and strong. The tendons in the **lower joints** should be gathered tightly, with a precision of "one qian" <note>a small unit of weight, implying extreme tightness and lack of slack</note>. The **chin hair** <note>original: 羊髭 (yángzī) — "sheep's beard"</note> should have a **spur** resembling that of a **rooster**. 

A horse with these traits can gallop and run with great speed, traveling a **thousand li** <note>approximately 310 miles or 500 kilometers</note> in a single day. The aforementioned are the **thirty-two noble traits**; such a horse is difficult to find among ten thousand.

### The Canon of the White Planet

The **Canon of the White Planet** <note>original: 太白陰經 (Tàibái Yīnjīng) — an influential Tang Dynasty military manual by Li Quan</note> states:

In all methods of **judging horses** <term>xiàngmǎ: the ancient art of equine physiognomy</term>, one must first evaluate the shape of the head. The **ears** should be like **shaved bamboo tubes** <note>meaning upright, sharp, and alert</note>. The eyes should be intelligent like a **human’s eyes** and sharp like a **bird’s eyes**. The **spine** should be like a **deer’s**, the **belly** like a **Qilin’s** <note>original: 麟腹 (lín fù) — the Qilin is a mythical chimerical beast; this implies a firm, tucked-up abdomen</note>, the **chest** like a **tiger’s**, and the **tail** like a **hanging broom**.

Next, appraise the ridges and angles of the **skull bones**. When looking from the front to the back, and from the back to the side, or from the side itself, one should see primarily the bone structure <note>implying a lean, powerful frame without excess fat</note> through the **thin skin**.

The **nostrils** should be open and the **bridge of the nose** <note>original: 衡柱 (héng zhù)</note> should be high, narrow, and long. The **forehead** should have a **flat, platform-like bone** <note>original: 伏臺骨 (fútái gǔ)</note> that is clearly defined. All structural segments of the body should rise prominently together.

The horse’s **gaze** should be far-reaching, and its **spirit** and **vitality** should be high and noble. When standing, observe its movement and stillness. Its **walk** should be energetic, its **gallop** swift, and its **coat** and **breathing** steady.

It should carry its **head high**, like a **majestic dragon** <note>original: 膺龙 (yīng lóng)</note>, looking into the distance with deep vision and hearing from afar. When viewed from the **front**, it should look like a **crowing rooster** <note>referring to a proud, arched neck</note>. When viewed from the **back**, it should look like it is **squatting** <note>referring to powerful, stable, and low-set hindquarters</note>. When **standing**, it should look like a **lion**, capable of **repelling an army** <term>pì bīng: a spiritual quality where the horse's majesty alone is said to ward off enemy soldiers</term> for ten thousand miles. The **crest of the neck** should be level and centered.

# A Section on Meticulous Care
<note>original: 一段細心那可草 (Yī duàn xì xīn nà kě cǎo); likely a chapter heading regarding the meticulous requirements for fodder and daily attention</note>

During a long rest, add extra feed; when riding only a little, add extra feed; during the scorching heat of summer, add extra feed. This is what is called the **Three Drinkings and Three Feedings**. If one observes these three principles when watering and feeding, the horse can gallop through the four seasons without illness, allowing it to be ridden and trained at will.

The **Inner Classic** <term>內經 (Nèi Jīng): Likely a reference to a foundational veterinary manual, modeled after the famous medical "Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic"</term> states: For all riders, walk on the first day, trot on the second, canter on the third, gallop on the fourth, and sprint on the fifth. By following this cycle and returning to the start, one can travel a thousand *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance, approximately 500 meters or 1/3 of a mile</note> without any health problems.

When arriving from a long distance and the horse is sweating, lead it by the hand <term>捧行 (pěng xíng): leading a horse gently and attentively by the bit or halter to cool it down</term> until its breathing has steadied and the sweat has subsided. Only then should you remove the saddle. Immediately after removing the saddle, let the horse move about; tie it in a place that catches the breeze, but do not place it near the eaves of a building <note>Keeping the horse away from eaves prevented exposure to dampness or stagnant air trapped under roofs</note>. Wait for some time to pass before feeding.

If the horse has been fed but has already been led on foot for a long time, then this restriction does not apply. Soldiers should be encouraged to use their own judgment in these matters. 

When cavalry units are on the march or heading into battle—regardless of whether the pace is fast or slow, or whether the ranks are closed up or spread out, dense or sparse—allow the horses to move naturally <note>original: *zizai* (自在), meaning at ease or following their own natural rhythm</note> as they follow the path. This ensures that when the army wheels about or changes formations, the horses will not collide with one another. Furthermore, if the unit encounters enemies on a dangerous or narrow road, it will be much easier to maneuver for mutual protection. 

When a horse is at a full, vigorous gallop and the rider wishes to stop, he should observe the distance and slowly pull back on the **reins** <term>le (勒)</term> <note>refers to the bit and the act of restraining the horse's head</note>. One must never pull back with a sudden, sharp jerk. One must always exercise moderation and foresight so as not to cause the horse to pant to the point of injury. 

The **Art of War** <note>A general term for military canonical texts; here likely referring back to the *Wujing Zongyao*</note> states: "For all warhorses, one must ensure their quarters are secure, their water and grass are appropriate, and their hunger and fullness are regulated..."
