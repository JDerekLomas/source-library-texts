These three allegories are the stories of **Saturn**, who is the same person as **Osiris**, of **Thoth** or **Mercury**, and of the 12 labors of **Hercules**.

Closely connected to one another, they form a whole, where all individual parts mutually explain each other and are made for one another.

**Saturn**, the devourer of children <note>Referring to the myth of Saturn/Cronus swallowing his offspring to prevent being overthrown</note>, opens the procession with his scythe; he is followed by the messenger of the gods, **Mercury**, with his caduceus <note>original: "Schlangenstabe," literally "snake-staff"</note>: and the entire scene is concluded by the lion-conqueror, **Hercules**, victorious in twelve battles with his club, as he burns himself on a funeral pyre and ascends to heaven.

If one observes these pictures in this very order, and overlooks none of the symbols <note>original: "Sinnbilder"</note> by which they are characterized, they appear to be what they truly are: very ingeniously devised and most fitting allegories, as they depict the most necessary arts with the greatest truth.

Thus, the story of **Saturn** is an allegorical tale of the invention of agriculture <term>Ackerbau: the cultivation of land for crops</term>, which had to lay the foundation for states and wealth, and lead to the measurement of time.

**Mercury** will offer us an allegory about the invention of astronomy and the calendar, according to which the farmer guides himself in his field work.

And in the story and the 12 labors of **Hercules**, we will see the plowing of the earth and the distribution of rural tasks for every month of the year.

Everything that can be said about Cronus, whom the Latins call Saturn, has already been said by both the ancients and the moderns; indeed, there is perhaps no subject that is more complicated, and one might almost say that through the effort spent on explaining him, he has become even more obscure.
