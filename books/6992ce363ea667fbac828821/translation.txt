## Article 1: Establishing the Fort

Regarding the establishment of a fort: the difficulty lies not in the lack of a place to build, but in the difficulty of placing it in the *right* location. With hills and mountains to the left and rear, and rivers or marshes to the right and front—in ancient times, no army would march without first selecting the terrain. How much more important is this for a fortified settlement where people must live? Therefore, the first regulation concerns the placement of the fort. It contains four sub-points.

First: Relying on Heights. "Heights" refers to landforms such as mounds, hills, and mountain ridges. ## The Disposition of the Fortress

The challenge regarding the **disposition of a fortress** <term>堡勢 (bǎo shì): The strategic posture, morale, and physical configuration of a fortification that creates an aura of strength.</term> is not that it is difficult to find a position of power, but rather that it is difficult to **preserve the integrity** of that power. 

When a tiger dwells in the mountains, even the wild herbs are left unpicked <note>The tiger’s mere presence acts as a deterrent, protecting the entire mountain.</note>. To resist an insult or attack, one must act like the *Shuairan* <note>original: "率然" (shuài rán). A mythical snake mentioned in Sun Tzu's *Art of War* that responds instantly to an attack on any part of its body: if you strike the head, the tail attacks; if you strike the tail, the head attacks.</note>, where the head and tail assist one another in close kinship.

In the past, **Emperor Wen of the Han** <note>Han Wendi (reigned 180–157 BCE), known for his frugal and defensive policies.</note> mandated the establishment of walled cities and towns that consisted of no less than a thousand households. Today, however, when fortresses are constructed, neighborhoods hatch their own private plans, and individuals seek only their own personal profit. Consequently, we see cases where a single village is split into several forts, or a single fort contains only a few families. 

In the worst instances, **arrogant commoners** <note>original: "武斷匹夫" (wǔ duàn pǐ fū), referring to local bullies or self-important men who use force to seize control of a small area.</note> occupy the city walls for themselves, while **wealthy, elite clans** <term>膏梁 (gāo liáng): Literally "fat meat and fine grain," a metaphor for the rich who live in luxury.</term> set up empty **parapets** <term>陴倪 (pí ní): Low walls or crenelations atop a main wall used for observation and protection.</term> for show. 

There is no one to guard the gates, and the **halberdiers** <note>original: "執戟" (zhí jǐ), a term for guards or soldiers armed with halberds.</note> have all abandoned their duties. When the invaders enter and the defense is lost, these people turn and lay the blame upon the government officials. Is this truly the fault of the fortress itself? No, it is the fault of failing to preserve the collective strength of its position.

Therefore, we next outline the agreements regarding the **disposition of the fortress**, which consists of four main articles.

If dozens of people try to lift it without coordination, their strength will be insufficient. Yet, if just a few people lift it with perfect timing and unity, they will have strength to spare. The weight of the stone has not increased or decreased; it is simply a matter of whether the strength is united or fragmented. Therefore...

### 41. Enclosure Walls

Once the site for the fort has been secured, the design of the **enclosure walls** must be discussed. # Subterranean Passages

**Subterranean passages** <note>original: 地道 *didao*</note> are tunnels bored through the foundations of the fortress walls to provide a connection for the personnel stationed within the **moat-walls** <note>the outer defensive perimeter described on the previous page</note>. While placing men at the moat-walls is an excellent strategy for a solid defense, a problem arises when enemy cavalry surrounds the position. If the main gates are bolted shut and these men are left alone outside the primary fortifications, it is rare that they do not become paralyzed with fear. Therefore, one must construct a subterranean passage on each side of the fortress to allow movement back and forth. This ensures the men's minds remain steady and their courage remains resolute.

The design is as follows: dig at the center of each wall, or perhaps near a **watchtower** <note>original: 敵臺 *ditai*; a platform for observation and flanking fire</note>. First, sink a pit over one <term>zhang</term> <note>approximately 3.3 meters or 11 feet</note> deep inside the fortress wall, wide enough to accommodate a person. Next, tunnel horizontally through the base of the wall. Once you reach the outside of the wall, sink another pit and tunnel upward to the surface. These pits are dug vertically; seven steps should be carved into the side of the shaft to serve as a ladder, similar to what is commonly called a "reversing well." 

In ordinary times, these passages are kept locked or covered with timber and stone. In times of emergency, they are opened and guarded by **auxiliary troops** <note>original: 餘丁 *yuding*; adult males in a household not currently serving as primary soldiers</note> or **strong women** <note>original: 健婦 *jianfu*; the text highlights a total-defense strategy involving the whole community</note>. This system is convenient for both routine movements and urgent retreats.
