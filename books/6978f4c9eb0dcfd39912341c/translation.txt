# Nineteenth Explanation.

The poets write of **Geryon**, a king of the Spaniards, that he had three bodies and various purple-colored oxen, which were protected by a dog with two heads and a dragon with seven. This Geryon was the natural son of **Chrysaor** (who descended from the blood of **Medusa** <note>original: "Medusæ"</note>). The seven-headed dragon, however, descends from **Typhon** and **Echidna**. Since this account is not recorded as truth in any historical record, no one is burdened to believe it; rather, one should give approval only to the **chemists** and **students of nature** who, according to the opinion of the wise **Hermes** <note>original: "Hermetis," referring to Hermes Trismegistus, the legendary founder of alchemy</note>, propose that by the three-bodied Geryon, the "three heads" visible in one "father" must be understood.

Or, as others prefer, the number four is to be taken as the **four elements** <note>original: "Elementa"</note>. For from the **square** <note>original: "Quadrat"</note> or quadrangle, a **triangle** <note>original: "Dreyeck"</note> must be prepared—just as the square came from a **circle** <note>original: "Circul"</note>—and this is then turned back into a circle again. <note>This describes a central alchemical theorem: reducing the four elements (square) to the three principles of Salt, Sulfur, and Mercury (triangle), and finally to the unity of the Philosopher's Stone (circle).</note> The four elements are found so precisely united with the bodies of Geryon that if only one were killed and defeated, the others would immediately fall and rot with it, without any further force being required.

It is well known regarding two-bodied births that where one dies, the other also dies at the same time. Such a two-bodied boy was seen a few years ago in **Italy**; he had a brother hanging from him, whose head was enclosed within his body, and only the torso reached out from the middle of his belly at the navel. <note>This likely refers to the famous 17th-century conjoined twins Lazarus and Joannes Baptista Colloredo.</note> Now, when the smaller body <note>original: "Corpus"</note>, whose head was enclosed in the larger one, was touched on the foot or hand by a healthy person, the larger one felt the pain and became hungry, just as if the smaller body had hunger.

**Nature** conducts such a **sympathy** and union that the limbs of a body—or those connected to another body—move in concert; where one is sick, the other also feels weakness, and indeed, if one were mortally wounded, the other is lost along with it. When one neighbor has all the benefit for himself, the other will suffer harm, and when his house catches fire, he suffers even greater loss. Your own situation is no different than your neighbor's house being in flames.

For through the mortal departure of one of these **four brothers** (whom others consider to be only three), the others also die. # Twentieth Explanation.

It is a common *axiom* <note>original: "Axioma"; a self-evident truth</note> or *symbol* <note>original: "Symbolum"; here meaning a guiding principle or creed</note> of the philosophers, that in those things which are related to one another, one nature draws, governs, instructs, and rules the other—just as art is guided and ruled by the master teacher, the maid by the lady, the subjects by the queen, yes, even the daughter by the mother, and the relative by the friend. The truth of this is demonstrated to us by the daily example of the upbringing of youth and other matters, such as *instruction* <note>original: "Information"</note>, guidance, and government, etc. *Pliny* the Naturalist <note>Pliny the Elder, a Roman author of "Natural History"</note> writes of the nightingale that one instructs the other in singing; they listen and mimic one another’s melody, even striving to surpass each other with a bright song. If one cannot force this victory, she laments it mournfully, and sometimes, through such competition in singing, her throat bursts so that she falls dead to the earth. Now, we also find among poultry that they accustom their young to flight; from which it is evident that Art performs its work simultaneously with Nature. Even if Nature alone had imprinted in them a power for flight, Art must nevertheless provide the guidance for it. Young mules are led to the run by horses, young dogs are led to barking by the old ones, and the little fox is led to deceit by its parents; indeed, nothing is to be found among all animals that does not point out the way, lead, guide, and instruct its young. Among *vegetables* <note>original: "Vegetabilien"; referring to the plant kingdom</note> we cannot perceive the like, yet one finds that the hand of man can practice and achieve great things with them. When the herb begins to ripen, it must be cleansed of its weeds and canker; and when the tree is still young, one is accustomed to bend and train it according to one's pleasure. The nature of metals and philosophical *subjects* <note>original: "Subjectorum"; the raw materials used in alchemical work</note> sustains the other in the fire, nourishing and protecting it, as is all too well known to the smelters, naturalists, and other artists. For silver and gold, which still lie raw and un-melted, locked in their *ore* <note>original: "Minera"</note> and heavily surrounded by *antimony* <note>original: "Antimonio"</note> and *arsenic*, iron serves to no small advantage when it comes into the fire and is melted. Likewise, iron itself, when it is to become steel, must be *preserved* <note>original: "conserviret"</note> by the white pebbles <note>likely flint or quartz</note> that lie by the water, so that it cannot be burned away. The philosophers likewise have their own additive, which *Morienus* <note>original: "Movienus"; referring to Morienus Romanus, a legendary 7th-century alchemist</note> calls a glass-gall, and commands that it be sought in the glasses. # Twentieth Explanation.

If an addition is given, it is preserved: for all bodies that have lost their soul are subject to the destruction of fire. The addition or **glass gall** <note>original: "Glaßgall"; sandiver or the scum of molten glass, used here as a flux to protect the "body" of the metal from being consumed by heat</note> is useful to all bodies to resist the power of the fire, as **Morienus Romanus** <note>a legendary alchemist, purportedly the teacher of King Khalid ibn Yazid</note> also writes. Thus, this is the nature that instructs others to fight against the fire and to adopt its nature: this is the teacher who leads her school: and if you consider it rightly, she is the **queen** who rules her subordinate subjects, and the **daughter** who is elevated through the virtues of her mother. This is the **red servant**, who is given in marriage to his fragrant mother, and from whom a far nobler fruit springs forth than the parents were. It is **Pyrrhus**, the son of **Achilles**, and the red-headed youth who carries a golden piece, has black eyes, and white feet. This is the **equipped knight** who, armed with shield and sword, steps against the dragon to tear the uninjured white maiden from its maw. This is the beast-slayer **Hercules**, who frees **Hesione**, the daughter of **Laomedon**, from the monstrous beast. This is **Perseus** who rescued **Andromeda**, daughter of King **Cepheus** and **Cassiopeia**, in Egypt from the sea monster by presenting the head of **Medusa**, and after he freed her from the chains, took her as his wife. This is a defender of liberty, and a sacrifice of his own body, like **Marcus Curtius**, **L. Scævola**, **Horatius Cocles**, and **Manlius Capitolinus**, who saved the city of Rome (as their mother) from danger through the sacrifice of their own bodies. For if a thing is to reach its true perfection, then according to the required nature, something else and higher must be prepared from such a thing—from an imperfect thing a perfect one; indeed, from **potentiality** <note>original: "Potenz"</note> and power, the **actuality** <note>original: "Actus"</note> or the real deed must be prepared. And this does not happen all at once, but rather gradually: not alone, but it carries with it its regent, to whom death and life are given into his hand—that is, he who has the power to work upon another. For example: in the **generation of a human**, ten months are counted before he comes to birth. His **heart** grows first (as **Aristotle** also states) as the regent and the most eminent part of the entire inner body; and this further forms, shapes, and completes the life, the senses, and the growth for nourishment, and through such presentation and decision, communicates the **vital spirit** <note>original: "Lebens-Geist"; the animating force of life</note> to the newly forming birth; that is, it restricts and encompasses the same with veins as long as it remains free from hindrance and violence. Therefore, one nature teaches the other; which has been presented to us as an example for the preparation of the **Philosophical Work** <note>original: "Philosophisches Werck"; the alchemical process of creating the Philosopher's Stone</note>.

# Twenty-first Explanation.

<image-desc>A decorative woodcut initial 'E' featuring ornamental foliage and scrolling vines within a square frame.</image-desc>The highly renowned philosopher **Plato** establishes that all wisdom and the knowledge of the arts lie truly imprinted within the human soul <note>original: "Gemüth"; refers to the mind, spirit, or inner disposition</note>. He argues that these may be awakened and brought to light by anyone through frequent repetition and recollection. To prove this, he presents a small, raw, and uneducated boy to whom he poses various geometric questions. The boy did not fail to answer them correctly, demonstrating—against all expectations—that he was already well-versed in the innermost secrets of such high arts. According to this view, no art would be acquired or learned from the beginning; rather, it would be unearthed solely through reflection and recollection within the soul, guided into the memory of the boy from his earliest childhood. 

He further adds the concept of his **Great Year** <note>The "Platonic Year," a cosmic cycle after which the celestial bodies return to their original positions; here cited as 48,000 years</note>, suggesting that 48,000 years ago, the very same persons, things, and deeds existed in this form and manner before the course of the stars, just as they are seen to be today. 

All of this, however, is without foundation and is a mere dream. It is true, as is well known, that some sparks of the sciences still cling to our souls; however, these are more a <term>potentiality (potenz)<gloss>the capacity or possibility to learn</gloss></term> to be able to learn the arts, rather than an actual possession of knowledge to be brought to light through mere repetition, reflection, or memory. It cannot be proven that the sciences and arts are, as it were, sown into the boy from the start. 

Now some may ask: from where then did the arts and sciences come, if men did not invent them? Do they then descend from the gods in heaven or from the rulers of the world? 

I answer briefly: it is one thing to say that a glowing coal lies hidden beneath the ashes, so that when one scrapes it out, it is enough to cook food or warm our cold limbs. It is quite another thing to claim that only a tiny, hidden spark lies within, which, if it is to be used, must first be blown upon, <term>cultivated (excoliret)<gloss>refined or improved through labor</gloss></term>, and increased through diligence, effort, and art; otherwise, it will go out entirely and turn into cold ash. This latter view is the opinion of the <term>Aristotelians (Aristotelicorum)<gloss>followers of Aristotle, who believed the mind starts as a blank slate</gloss></term>, just as the former is maintained by **Plato**. 

Reason and <term>experience (experienß)</term> stand by the side of the latter view, while the former springs alone from fantasy and imagination. Because of this, one might still ask: why did **Plato** write above the door of his school: *“Let no one ignorant of the geometric sciences enter here”*? <note>original: "Den ohnerfahrenen in der Geometrischen Wissenschafften ist dieser Eingang nicht erlaubet"; a famous inscription over Plato's Academy</note>. Whether they are people with less understanding than children, or equal to children, or whether they have forgotten such things due to age, is not possible to <note>original catchword: "messen"; to measure or fathom</note> determine.

Just as three **digestions** <note>original: "Digestiones"; in early modern medicine, these are the stages of processing food and nutrients into the body's substance.</note> or "cookings" are found within a human being—one performed by the stomach, the second by the liver, and the third by the veins—so too are there three **universal purifications** which, in harmony with these "cookings," daily expel their excess. The first is through the bowel movement; the second is through the urine, for which the liver serves as the site of **digestion**; and the third is through sweat, which is an evaporation of the body's internal parts through the sweat-pores.

In the first digestion, the **chyle** <note>original: "Chylus"; the milky fluid of digested food.</note> and life-sap are prepared; in the second, the **chyme** <note>original: "Chymus"; the pulpy acidic fluid passing from the stomach to the small intestine.</note> is prepared; and in the third, the moisture or the "dewing substance" is prepared and sent to every part of the body. # Thirty-fifth Explanation.

A Spartan legislator, *Lycurgus* <note>Lycurgus (c. 800 BCE–730 BCE) was the legendary lawgiver of Sparta, credited with the city's military-oriented social system.</note>, presented a proof of bad and good habits at a public assembly using two young dogs born of the same mother. He then addressed them, saying: "See what upbringing can do; though these dogs come from the same mother, their habits are entirely different." In this same way, one must also improve nature and lead it toward higher things, because nature allows itself to be bent and turned toward good or evil, just like soft wax. Under such a rule of state, the *philosophers* also tend to approve and authorize the same principle from the study of *physics* <note>In this context, "physics" refers to the broader study of natural philosophy and the laws of the physical world.</note>; for daily experience shows us everywhere what habits have crept in among humans and animals. > Behold the nursing child, and do not hinder it.

# Forty-second Explanation.

Travelers are subject to many great dangers, especially when they wander upon dangerous, slippery paths at night. Such travelers, in addition to their provisions and physical strength, have need of four main things: first, the path upon which they wish to travel, so that they may explore it well and carry a faithful guide with them. For if an ignorant man were to lead another, it would be as if one blind man showed the way to another—not only might they both fall into the pits, but they would surely go astray. Secondly, they must apply themselves to a good walking staff to steady and support themselves on the journey and in other slippery places. Thirdly, they must have good eyes; for no blind or dim-sighted person can travel without danger. Fourthly, they must have a good, brightly shining lantern or a lit torch.

A traveler eager for the pursuit of Alchemy <term>Alchemy: original "Chymie." In the 17th century, the terms chemistry and alchemy were often used interchangeably to describe the study of natural substances and their transformations.</term> must, besides physical strength and the required funds, take into close consideration <note>original: "Consideration"</note> and observe <note>original: "observiren"</note> four points: namely, to bring Nature, Reason, Experience, and the Philosophical books or writings into a harmony <note>original: "Harmonie"</note>. For if even one of these is missing, the entire labor is in vain and useless. With these, as if with four wheels, the Philosophical Wagon must be equipped. 

Nature presents <note>original: "prätendiret"</note> natural bodies and spirits <note>original: "Spiritus"; in alchemical terms, these are the volatile essences or "breath" of a substance.</note> as those subjects <note>original: "subjecta"; the raw materials or "matters" chosen for the work.</note> which have been worked upon by her from their beginning. This is done so that Art <note>The "Art" refers to the skill and intervention of the Alchemist.</note> may proceed with the preparing, purifying, and the final elaboration, bringing to a conclusion that which is due to the diligent seeker as a reward. 

The potter makes use of earth (clay) and water in all his work. The glassmaker uses ash and sand. The blacksmith uses iron, copper, tin, lead, silver, and gold. The tanner uses raw hides, and so forth, and other artists use other things. In the same way, the Alchemist <note>original: "Chymicus"</note> must take his material in hand. To some, this material is already known; to others, however, it becomes recognizable only after much effort; and to yet another, perhaps not at all. And although kind Nature points to it with her fingers, most are yet so blinded that they cannot understand or recognize it. Therefore, first and foremost, Nature must be considered <note>original: "consideriret"</note> and contemplated in her center <note>original: "Centro"</note> or her innermost essence: namely, how she works so that the true subjects (materials) of the Alchemist may be chosen without deficiency or excess. For on this journey, Nature is the guide whose footsteps one must follow. 

Reason is the walking staff which makes the practitioner’s <note>original: "Artisten"</note> step sure and gives help to the feet so that they may not easily stumble. For this reason, the Philosophers <note>original: "Philosophi"</note> have written: whatever you hear, ponder it well to see if it could truly happen in such a way.
