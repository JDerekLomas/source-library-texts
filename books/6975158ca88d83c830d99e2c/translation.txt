## LETTER I.

->To the most Reverend and<-
->Distinguished Sir,<-

Time truly glides away so gradually and unexpectedly in its course that one can scarcely notice its paths. You act rightly, however, in rousing me from this lethargy and calling me forth toward the set goal of our endeavor. It would have been more fitting to prick me with spurs than to soothe me with some small gift; thus, with shame, I finally see how reckless and thoughtless a procrastinator <term>cunctator: "one who delays," often used in reference to the Roman general Fabius Cunctator, though here used self-deprecatingly</term> I have been. 

Not long ago, I received from our Most Merciful Lady Mother <note>original: "Clem: Dn. Matre nostrâ"; likely referring to a high-ranking patroness or noblewoman</note> that cleverly drawn page regarding the resurrection of the dead <note>original: "resurrectione mortuorum"</note> which you mention. I do not know, however, where it came from. I have for some time possessed the same thing in a smaller format and of the most perfect craftsmanship; so much so that unless the Author himself signs his name, another's might deservedly be substituted. "Diligence produced it" <note>original: "Diligentia faciebat"; possibly an artist's signature or a variation on the classical artist Apelles' motto</note>. The imitation is so elegantly deceptive that it presents to the eyes the blessed enduring here in eternal delights, while the damned truly suffer the most exquisite tortures of hell. 

I have not yet been able to see the works—of great skill, as they say—by your blind organ-builder <term>Organarii: a builder or player of organs</term>. However, I easily place my faith in you and in those things which we have heard from public report—that is, common rumor—and I marvel at the distribution and compensation of Nature <note>original: "Naturæ"</note>, who has abundantly restored to this man in other ways what she took from his eyes...

### THE YEAR 1649.

*To Anton Ulrich <note>Anton Ulrich (1633–1714) was the son of Duke August the Younger. He later became a prominent Duke of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel, a patron of the arts, and a famous novelist.</note>, I have received your news with rejoicing. There were indeed reasons for me to congratulate you when you departed from home, which I did not hide; but many more, and more compelling reasons for applauding your desired return have followed. I would rather you infer these for yourself, given your remarkable wisdom and accurate judgment, than for me to announce them openly. Nevertheless, I will not deny that while you were away for so long, I was never without some care and fear. In your companion Krams <note>original: "Kramio"; likely a reference to a tutor or a noble official who accompanied the prince on his "Grand Tour."</note>, a truly noble man, I had much confidence—next to the favor of* GOD *and the angelic vigils—but what does a most tender love not fear even in safe circumstances? What dangers does it not imagine for itself? Such is my love for you, joined with humble respect.*

*Therefore, I congratulate you from the heart, and I truly and sincerely rejoice for myself that you have returned to your own home, your own family, your own people, your own possessions, and to your very self—by which terms I wish to express both the embrace of your happiness and the mystery of my own feelings. For you see, hear, and possess everything at home that can be sought abroad, and which perhaps are not found elsewhere, nor anywhere else. O happy are you, while you have your Father, the solid foundation of future happiness. Your own land and fatherland will hold you; your people will love, cherish, and respect you. You yourself will adorn and increase your possessions, and you will belong to yourself with a sound mind in a vigorous and healthy body <note>original: "sanâ mente in viridi ac corpore sano"; an adaptation of the classical Roman ideal of "a sound mind in a sound body."</note>. Your wise intuition will suggest more to you than I can point out; and unless I am mistaken, the very comparison of your domestic happiness with exotic inconveniences will supply more reasons for my congratulation. Truly, I owe thanks to* GOD *the Preserver <note>original: "Sospitatori DEO"</note>, under whose shadow you completed this journey safe in mind and body, not without growth in judgment, actions, and studies. It will now be a pleasure to digest these things at home and to convert them into "juice and blood" <note>original: "in succum sanguinemque convertere"; a common early modern metaphor for fully internalizing and masterfully applying knowledge rather than just memorizing it.</note>, while your* Most Serene Lord Father <note>original: "Sereniss. D. Pater"; Duke August the Younger of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel, known for his massive library and scholarly interests.</note> *according to his incredible prudence, sets forth other matters. In the meantime, unless it be unwelcome, return to my literary correspondence <term>literarium commercium: the formal exchange of letters and ideas between scholars and nobility, seen as a vital part of a prince's education.</term>, to which perhaps this* Little Book of Letters <note>original: "Libellus Epistolica"</note> *may incite you...*

## LETTER III.

->To the Very Reverend and Most Distinguished<-
->Lord Doctor,<-

Whether it has yet become known to you that I have now returned to my ancestral hearth from the lands of Cleves <note>original: "ex Clivensibus terris"; Cleves (Kleve) was a territory on the lower Rhine, then under the influence of the Elector of Brandenburg.</note>, I truly do not know. However, I judged it right that you should learn this from my own letters rather than those of others—all the more so since I can say this of myself to you (which others might not). For I do not wish you to be unaware that I have spent a quite happy year abroad. Most notably, I was treated with great honor and much favor by that most excellent Prince, the Elector of Brandenburg <note>Frederick William (1620–1688), known as the Great Elector.</note>, who is a mirror of virtue. Having returned to my native home, I have received the pinnacle of that happiness; for I found my parents and other kinsmen alive and still enjoying good health. Indeed, I have experienced my father’s spirit in all matters to such a degree that I almost doubt whether I should more admire his love or his care for me. I am well aware that I cannot offer sufficient praise to God and to such a parent for such great benefits, nor [adequately thank] you and the prayers of others...
