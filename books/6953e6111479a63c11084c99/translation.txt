### Usage guidelines

Google is proud to partner with libraries to digitize public domain materials and make them widely accessible. Public domain books belong to the public and we are merely their custodians. Nevertheless, this work is expensive, so in order to keep providing this resource, we have taken steps to prevent abuse by commercial parties, including placing technical restrictions on automated querying <note>The use of software or "bots" to automatically download or search large amounts of data.</note>.

We also ask that you:

+ **Make non-commercial use of the files** We designed Google Book Search for use by individuals, and we request that you use these files for personal, non-commercial purposes.
+ **Refrain from automated querying** Do not send automated queries of any sort to Google’s system: If you are conducting research on machine translation, optical character recognition <note>term: "optical character recognition" (OCR); the electronic conversion of images of typed, handwritten or printed text into machine-encoded text.</note> or other areas where access to a large amount of text is helpful, please contact us. We encourage the use of public domain materials for these purposes and may be able to help.
+ **Maintain attribution** The Google “watermark” you see on each file is essential for informing people about this project and helping them find additional materials through Google Book Search. Please do not remove it.
+ **Keep it legal** Whatever your use, remember that you are responsible for ensuring that what you are doing is legal. Do not assume that just because we believe a book is in the public domain for users in the United States, that the work is also in the public domain for users in other countries. Whether a book is still in copyright varies from country to country, and we can’t offer guidance on whether any specific use of any specific book is allowed. Please do not assume that a book’s appearance in Google Book Search means it can be used in any manner anywhere in the world. Copyright infringement <note>The unauthorized use of works under copyright, violating the owner's exclusive rights.</note> liability can be quite severe.

I BELIEVE you are aware, most learned Claude, why—although I previously seemed inflamed with a passion for medical matters—I have nevertheless for some time now almost ceased to occupy myself in that kind of study and literature. For this happened partly through the advice of others with whom I have a very long-standing friendship, but most especially by your own judgment regarding *the caretakers of the sick* <note>original Greek: περὶ τῶν νοσοκόμων</note>. It was by your will that I did not follow my own original purpose. When I compared [the text] primarily with the Aldine <note>The Aldine Press was a famous Venetian printing house known for its Greek editions.</note> and German copies, I discovered it was mutilated in several places, corrupted in many, with much changed and a great deal added. Indeed, who can be found with such keen eyesight <note>original: "lynceus" - referring to Lynceus of Greek myth, famous for his sharp vision.</note> that he would stumble over nothing in such great darkness, or never run into an obstacle? Therefore, I thought it most consistent with my duty—and most beneficial for those unacquainted with Greek literature and medicine (for whom alone these works were prepared)—to emend that version as far as possible. This was done both by faithfully rendering the Greek passages that lacked their own interpretation word-for-word, and by adding annotations where the subject matter itself seemed to require it. 

Furthermore, we have employed a slightly different arrangement in certain parts, and we have seen to it that the version be renewed in a new and somewhat more convenient format. We have done all these things with such zeal so that the work might come into the sight and hands of men both more adorned and more accessible. Whether I have achieved this or not, I would prefer the judgment to be that of others rather than my own. 

I certainly undertook this labor all the more willingly because I seemed to understand that this particular kind of study was greatly approved by you. In these studies, I myself have undoubtedly found—not what "boys find in a bean" <note>A Latin proverb referring to finding a trivial prize or a hidden trifle.</note>—but what is commonly called a sea of blessings <note>original: "ἀγαθῶν θάλασσαν" (agathōn thalassan)</note>; I mean a most rich treasury of all things. For I ask you, have you ever read or heard of anyone more diligent in investigating matters than Galen <note>Claudius Galenus, the most influential physician of the Roman Empire.</note> himself, or anyone better instructed in every branch of learning? What necessary species of animal, what part of those very creatures, what kind of earth, what genus of metals, or what stone [did he not investigate] by the method of experience...

*and has he not handed down to us the knowledge of these things? Indeed, to me at least, Galen <note>Galen of Pergamon (129–216 AD) was the most influential physician and medical philosopher of antiquity.</note> alone seems to have expressed for us not only the powers of simple medicines—both those arising from their primary qualities and those hidden powers which their entire substance conceals—but also the very nature of things itself.
