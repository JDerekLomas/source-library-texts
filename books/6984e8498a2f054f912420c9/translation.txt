# THE ARGUMENTS OF THE BOOKS
### On the Harmony of the World <note>original: "De Orbis Concordia"</note>.

First, the tenets of the Christian religion <note>original: "religionis Christianæ"</note> are taught through philosophical reasoning.

Second, the life, upbringing, and customs of Muhammad <note>original: "Muhamedi"</note>, the lawgiver of the Arabs <note>original: "Arabum"</note>, and his followers are related; finally, the Quran <note>original: "Alcoranum"</note> is examined and refuted from beginning to end <note>A Latin idiom, "from head to heel"</note> from the original Arabic.

Third, what the whole world holds in common, both by human law and divine law.

Fourth, by what skill false beliefs about God or the gods may be drawn toward the truth without civil unrest.

Although something consistent with reason could be brought against this axiom, nevertheless, so that we may receive it as proven, it will be enough to show that I do not withdraw from the common opinion received in the Church of Christ to this day. To those who, imbued with the sacred mysteries, are moved by the Spirit of God (although they ought to persuade themselves of this one thing above all: that God, the author of reason, commanded or taught nothing, however arduous or obscure, without reason), faith is not to be confirmed by reasons, as they already hold it sufficiently persuaded. Therefore, I have not undertaken this work so that I might play the Christian philosopher among you. A window behind him shows a hilly landscape.</image-desc> Although it is difficult and beyond human capacity to treat anything that does not fall under the senses—since even things widely admitted find a place in many people's minds with difficulty, and many withhold their agreement from the most obvious truths, while things usually perceived by the senses are known only on the surface—reason is ineffective for weighing all things, and especially divine matters separated from this filthy matter <note>The author refers to the physical, material world as "filthy matter" (*feculenta materia*), contrasting it with the purity of the divine.</note>. Nevertheless, driven by a zeal for trying and a desire to achieve the highest things, I judge it better to test what human powers can do than to leave unattempted, through laziness and dejection of spirit, that which brings no small benefit to this mortal life even by the mere thought of achieving it. For while the mind is occupied in the contemplation of great things, even if it does not usually attain what it proposed for itself, yet while it is held by the zeal for attaining it and is entirely occupied in contemplating, it must necessarily lack many troubles. For when it is carried so completely into the thought of the matter that it is plainly snatched away by that very passion, it happens that it does not even feel those troubles by which others are weighed down. Just as someone intent on blood and vengeance does not feel the wounds he receives unless he settles down, because his passion itself carries him away; so too might he see other successes only through a lattice-window <note>The phrase *per transennam* (through a lattice or screen) implies seeing something only partially or in passing.</note> and marvel at them, like someone who, with the goal of happiness set before him, looks at or cares for nothing else at all until he has reached it.

Therefore, since I see that <term>happiness</term> <note>original: *felicitatem*. In this context, it refers to "beatitude" or the ultimate fulfillment found in God.</note> is the goal of this life, which all things naturally seek, and it has been cut into various parts by various judgments—while one person proposes one part for himself and another busies himself to obtain many parts—everyone seeks a solid happiness. By debating about that very thing, as it can be thought of as the highest and absolute goal, I have decided to treat it in such a way that, from those sensible traces, shadows, and conjectures, I may come into knowledge (as far as it is permitted for a mind closed in the prisons of the body to reach) and demonstrate the divinity of the supreme deity and the creator of all things. Thus, by this labor—which meanwhile calls me away from other things—while I strive to satisfy myself, even if the argument handed down to this age could be of benefit, I might indeed seem to have done a great service. To repeat this from a slightly higher point, I judge that one must begin from the contemplation of all nature, daring to approach this not without the favor and grace of Him whom we seek. I therefore pray and beseech Him with every vow that the knowledge concerning Him which He has deigned to open to us by His mercy through the holy scriptures and His son Jesus Christ, the <term>God-man</term> <note>original Greek: *Θεάνθρωπον* (Theanthrōpon), a theological term emphasizing Christ as being simultaneously fully God and fully man.</note>, He might allow and cause that same knowledge (as if reclaimed from His authority) to pass for a short while into the power of reason.

As long as innocence lasted, ambition was banished, deceit did not creep into sacred places, and providence—the avenger of crimes and the producer of miracles—was present and revealed itself sufficiently to the world; whatever sacred religion proclaimed in His name was accepted. But these dangerous times, which Peter predicted, receive nothing of sound doctrine; they laugh at authority, turn toward fables, and recoil from all authority of antiquity to follow their own dreams. # ON THE CONCORD OF THE WORLD

...either attack it with words and deeds, or they completely reject it by their way of life. I am moved by the error and unhappiness of so many peoples who, although they are far from the truth and follow falsehoods, nevertheless omit nothing of those things which they are convinced lead to the highest happiness, so that they might finally obtain it. For who would not weep that so many labors perish? That so many noble efforts are lost when vice is admitted and accepted in place of virtue? Who would not weep that the sweetest liquids are ruined by the mixture of wormwood <note>Wormwood is a proverbially bitter herb; the author uses it here as a metaphor for how false beliefs embitter a well-intentioned life.</note>, and that a life lived in the constant company of death never truly lives? One man claims the opinion of Moses for himself; another approves of Muhammad <note>original: *Muhamedem*</note> as the author of the law; for many others, the foul image of a demon presents itself to be worshiped. Some are terrified by ghosts <note>original: *lemuribus* — spirits or shades of the dead</note> and perform sacrifices to them; whatever else is offered to the rest is held in the place of God.

And meanwhile, the truth itself lies hidden among us; it remains concealed by our sloth, unknown by many names. Truly, it is primarily because of our way of living that even if there were some who would voluntarily submit to that law—which we prove and assert depends on the highest happiness—they would be drawn in a completely different direction. For it seems unjust that the common conduct of life should differ so much from the holy precept. The primary examples of neglected persuasion are not sought from the common people, nor are they found only in lawless military service, nor do they show themselves in the courts of princes in such a way that they are not also by far the worst among the initiates <note>original: *mystis* — referring here to the clergy or those "initiated" into the mysteries of the faith</note> themselves. 

Dire ambition and the pursuit of vain reputation <note>original: *fumorum captatio* — literally "the catching of smoke," a metaphor for seeking empty honors</note> torment those removed from the common noise of human affairs and from more splendid usage. Pride and arrogance drive the rich astray; the middle class, increased by the vices of their elders, think everything is permitted to them. In short, ignorance, vanity, and greed infect the entire social order, so that those who correspond to so holy a name—whom I wish were untouched—are very rare. Other nations leave no stone unturned to sow the seeds of their false persuasions wherever they can; they entice with gifts, lure with friendship, compel with authority, pull with force, and strive to join everyone to themselves by persuasion. But we, idle and sluggish, sleep deeply, considering it enough to possess the most noble thing like misers: having it without the use of the use itself <note>The author means that Christians possess the "treasure" of faith but do not actually put it into practice.</note>.

The third cause lies in the difficulty of the matter, which not infrequently repels the most excellent men. For although we may be well-disposed toward the things themselves, and faith itself—a gift of God devoid of reason and supported by divine authority—ought to produce belief by itself, yet while authority offers itself everywhere, the right of all seems entirely the same unless you make a choice by reason. For all either seek God while thinking they have Him, or they profess Him. Jews, Muhammadans, and Indians <note>original: *Indi* — likely referring to the peoples of the East Indies or the Americas known at the time</note> either worship or acknowledge one creator of heaven and earth; we do the same. Imagine you are a stranger who has never heard anything about God (whom you nevertheless judge should be held by nature): it is certain that you would be in doubt as to whom among all you should believe, unless reason compels you to turn toward one side rather than the other. If authority alone makes the law, the reasoning for all will be equal, since there will be none. Surely no mortal would not greatly approve of our precepts if he understood everything from the very beginning.

But since difficulty alienates many of the pagans, and all the Jews and Muhammadans from us, I judge that we must deal with them by reason, both in all things and especially in those which they cannot persuade themselves to believe. Such as these:

Indeed, we even persuade others that this—which is the basis and proof of a truly pious man—is a matter of counsel, not a command <note>The author critiques the theological view that "loving enemies" is an optional "counsel of perfection" rather than a mandatory commandment for all Christians.</note>.
