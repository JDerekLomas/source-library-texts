Son,

The calamity of these times is such that it has deprived me of the ordinary means of expressing my affection to you. Therefore, I have been searching for some other way of doing so in a manner that will bring you the most profit. In this search, I soon settled upon this consideration: that parents owe their children not only material subsistence for their bodies, but much more, spiritual contributions to their better part—their minds. I am deeply grateful to God that He has endowed you with a mind very capable of receiving the best instructions; and consequently, I feel myself obliged to do my utmost to mold it to its greatest advantage. 

If my aim in this proves successful, you will more easily digest those inconveniences and distresses which you have already begun to experience, and which daily threaten to become worse. For if an eternity remains for us after the short revolution of time we so swiftly run over here on earth, it is clear that all the happiness which can be imagined in this fleeting state is not valuable in respect to the future. I hope to prevail upon all ingenious <note>original: "ingenious"; meaning clever or open-minded</note> persons who shall read what I have written, to notify me fairly (if they judge it worth their while) of what they dislike in it. I ask this so that in another, more accurate edition, I may give them better satisfaction. For besides what failings may be in the subject matter, I do not doubt that even in the expression of it, there must often be great obscurity and brevity. I, who have my thoughts filled with the things themselves, am not aware of these flaws. So it is that what perhaps may seem very full to me—because every imperfect touch brings into my mind the entire notion and whole chain of circumstances belonging to that thing I have so often pondered—may appear very crude and mangled to a stranger, who cannot guess what I am aiming at except as my direct words lead him.

One thing more I shall wish you to desire of those who perhaps may peruse these two treatises <note>original: "Treatises"</note>, as much for their own sakes as for mine. And that is, that they will not pass their judgment upon any particular piece or broken fragment of either of them, taken by itself. Let them draw the entire thread <note>original: "thridde"</note> through their fingers, and let them examine the consistency <note>original: "consequentnesse"</note> of the whole body of the doctrine I deliver. If they find in the school's teachings many breaks <note>original: "brackes"</note> and short ends which cannot be spun into an even piece, and in mine, a fair coherence throughout, I shall promise myself a favorable judgment from them. <margin>1. Quantity is the first and most obvious property <note>original: "affection"</note> of a body.</margin>
In delivering any science, the clearest and smoothest method—and the one most agreeable to nature—is to begin with the consideration of those things that are most common and obvious. By the dissection of these, one may descend by orderly degrees and steps (as they lie in the way) unto the examination of the most particular and remote ones. Now, in our present intended survey of a body, the first thing which occurs to our senses in the study of it is its *Quantity*, bulk, or magnitude. This seems to be conceived by all mankind as inseparable from a body. For example, when a man would distinguish a corporeal <note>physical or material</note> substance from a spiritual one (which is accounted indivisible), he naturally settles upon an understanding of its having bulk, and being solid, tangible, and apt to make an impression upon our outward senses. This is according to the expression of Lucretius <note>Titus Lucretius Carus (c. 99 – c. 55 BC), a Roman poet and philosopher</note>, who, studying nature in a familiar and rational manner, tells us:

> For nothing can touch or be touched, except a body. <note>original Latin: "Tangere enim & tangi, nisi corpus nulla potest res."</note>

And therefore, in our inquiry into bodies, we will observe that plain method which nature teaches us. We will begin by examining what *Quantity* is, as it is their first and primary property <note>original: "affection"</note>, and that which makes the things we treat of be what we intend to signify by the name of "body."

# A TREATISE

<margin>
2. Words do not express things as they are in themselves, but only as they are painted in the minds of men.
</margin>

But because there is a great variety of understandings <note>original: "apprehensions"</note> framed by learned men regarding the nature of **Quantity** (though indeed nothing can be more plain and simple than it is in itself), I believe it will not be out of place, before we enter into the explanation of it, to consider how the mystery of reasoning and expressing our thoughts to one another by words (a prerogative belonging only to man) is ordered and governed among us. By doing so, we may avoid those rocks which many—and for the most part, those who think they spin the finest threads—suffer shipwreck against in their most subtle discourses. 

The most dangerous of all these errors, assuredly, is when they confound the true and real natures of things with the conceptions they frame of them in their own minds. By this fundamental miscarriage of their reasoning, they fall into great errors and absurdities; whatever they build upon such a ruinous foundation proves to be nothing but useless cobwebs or monstrous illusions <note>original: "prodigious Chymeras"</note>. It is true that words serve to express things; but if you observe the matter well, you will perceive they do so only according to the pictures we make of them in our own thoughts, and not according to how the things are in their own proper natures. 

It is very reasonable that it should be so, since the soul, which gives the names, has nothing of the things in her but these mental concepts <note>original: "notions"</note>, and knows not the things otherwise than by these concepts. Therefore, she cannot give other names except those that must signify the things through the mediation of these concepts. In the things themselves, all that belongs to them is comprised under one entire Being <note>original: "Entity"</note>; but in us, there are framed as many several distinct formal conceptions as that one thing shows itself to us with different faces. Every one of these conceptions seems to have for its object a distinct thing, because the conception itself is as much severed and distinguished from another conception or image—even one arising out of the very same thing that produced this one—as it can be from any image painted in the understanding by an absolutely different thing.

<margin>
3. The first error that may arise from this: which is a multiplying of things where no such multiplication is really found.
</margin>

It will not be out of place to illustrate this matter by some familiar example. Imagine I have an apple in my hand: the same fruit works different effects upon my several senses. My eye tells me it is green or red; my nose that it has a mellow scent; my taste that it is sweet; and my hand that it is cold and weighty. My senses, being thus affected, send messengers to my imagination <note>original: "fantaſie"</note> with news of the discoveries they have made; and there, all of them make several and distinct pictures of what enters through their doors. So it is that my **Reason** (which discourses upon what it finds in my imagination) can consider greenness by itself, or mellowness, or sweetness, or coldness, or any other quality whatsoever, singly and alone by itself, without relation to any other quality that is painted in me by the same apple. In the apple itself, none of these have any distinction at all, but are one and the same substance of the apple that makes various and different impressions.

<summary>The author argues that human language and reason describe our mental perceptions of objects rather than the unified essence of the objects themselves. He warns that confusing these mental "pictures" with reality leads to philosophical error, using an apple's various sensory qualities as an example of how we divide a single thing into many concepts.</summary>
