# Dietary Contraindications
<note>original: "Yinshi Jinji." This section lists foods that should not be eaten while taking specific medicines, as well as general dietary rules to prevent worsening a disease.</note>

## Li Dongyuan’s General Rules for Prescribing According to Symptoms
<note>original: "Li Dongyuan Suizheng Yongyao Fanlie." Li Dongyuan (1180–1251) was one of the four great masters of medicine in the Jin-Yuan period, famous for emphasizing the role of the spleen and stomach in health.</note>

## Chen Zangqi’s General Rules for Prescribing for Various Deficiencies
<note>original: "Chen Zangqi Zhuxu Yongyao Fanlie." Chen Zangqi was a Tang dynasty official and physician known for his "Supplement to the Materia Medica." This section provides guidelines for treating "deficiency" (xu)—a state where the body's vital energy or fluids are depleted.</note>

## Zhang Zihe’s Three Methods: Diaphoresis, Emesis, and Purgation
<note>original: "Zhang Zihe Han Tu Xia San Fa." Zhang Zihe (c. 1156–1228) led the "Attack and Purge" school of medicine. He believed that pathogens must be forcefully driven out of the body through sweating (diaphoresis), vomiting (emesis), or bowel movements (purgation).</note>

## The Eight Essentials, Six Failures, and Six Untreatable Conditions of Disease
<note>original: "Bing you Bayao, Liushi, Liubuzhi." A framework for diagnosis and medical ethics. The "Eight Essentials" often refer to the principles of Yin, Yang, Interior, Exterior, Cold, Heat, Deficiency, and Excess. The "Six Failures" and "Untreatable Conditions" warn physicians about cases where medicine may fail due to the patient's lifestyle or the advanced stage of the illness.</note>

## Medicinal Pairings, Seasonal Objects, and Categories of Substances
<note>original: "Yaodui Suiwu Yaopin." This likely refers to the "Yao Dui" (Medicinal Pairings), an ancient manual attributed to Lei Gong that explains how two drugs work together. "Seasonal objects" refers to harvesting materials at the correct time of year.</note>

## Table of Contents for the Divine Farmer’s Classic of Materia Medica
<note>original: "Shennong Bencao Jing Mulu." This honors the oldest known Chinese pharmaceutical text, attributed to the mythical Emperor Shennong (the Divine Farmer).</note>

<note>original: "Song Bencao Jiu Mulu." Li Shizhen includes the structure of the Materia Medica as it existed during the Song Dynasty (960–1279) to show how medical knowledge has evolved and been re-organized over the centuries.</note>

## Introductory Examples, Part Two

### Similarities and Differences in Medicinal Names
<note>In this section, Li Shizhen addresses a critical issue in traditional pharmacology: nomenclature. Because different regions or historical texts might use the same name for entirely different plants or minerals, he categorizes these "homonyms" to prevent life-threatening medicinal errors.</note>

**Five substances sharing the same name** for "Single-Swaying Grass" <gloss>Notopterygium root, Gastrodia tuber</gloss> <gloss>Chinese Mayapple, Wild Ginger</gloss> <gloss>Ghost's Bailiff</gloss>
<note>original: "Duyao Cao." These five plants were all colloquially called "Single-Swaying Grass" because their stems or leaves were said to move even when there was no wind.</note>

**Four substances sharing the same name** for "Jin" <gloss>Violet, Monkshood</gloss> <gloss>Chinese Elder</gloss> <gloss>Celery-leaved Buttercup</gloss>
<note>original: "Jin." This was a dangerous homonym, as it could refer to a harmless violet or the highly toxic Monkshood (Aconite).</note>

"Bitter Herb" <gloss>Fritillary bulb, Bitter Lettuce</gloss> <gloss>Patrinia</gloss> <gloss>Black Nightshade</gloss>
<note>original: "Kucai." While many plants are bitter, these four specifically shared the formal name in various classical texts.</note>

"Ghost's Eye" <gloss>Bittersweet Herb, Trumpet Creeper</gloss> <gloss>Dock Leaf, Deer's Eye</gloss>
<note>original: "Guimu." These plants typically share a physical characteristic, such as berries or spots, that resemble eyes.</note>

"Red Bean" <gloss>Adzuki Bean, Jequirity (Love Pea)</gloss> <gloss>Red Cardamom, Sandal Bead Tree</gloss>
<note>original: "Hongdou." Li Shizhen distinguishes between the edible adzuki bean and the decorative but highly toxic Jequirity bean.</note>

# Li Dongyuan’s General Rules for Prescribing Based on Symptoms

## Wind Hitting the Six Hollow Organs
<note>In Chinese medicine, "Wind" (fēng) is a pathogen that causes sudden changes. "Six Hollow Organs" (liù fǔ) refers to the digestive and excretory organs like the stomach and intestines.</note>
When the limbs are paralyzed or fail to function, one must first release the "exterior" <note>original: "biǎo"; the surface of the body</note>. Use **Notopterygium root** <term>Qiānghuó: an herb used for upper body pain and wind-cold</term> and **Saposhnikovia root** <term>Fángfēng: literally "Protector against Wind"</term> as the Sovereign <note>the primary or "king" herb in a prescription</note> medicines. Add further medicines according to the symptoms, and then circulate the energy through the channels. Use blood-nourishing herbs like **Angelica sinensis** <term>Dāngguī: the premier blood tonic</term>, **Large-leaf Gentian root**, and **Pubescent Angelica root**.
<gloss>Apply according to the specific channel: Pubescent Angelica, Saposhnikovia, Bupleurum, Dahurian Angelica, and Sichuan Lovage should be used depending on which meridian is affected.</gloss>

## Wind Hitting the Five Solid Organs
<note>The Five Solid Organs (wǔ zàng) are the Heart, Liver, Spleen, Lungs, and Kidneys. "Wind" hitting these organs is considered more internal and severe than the "Hollow Organs" mentioned above.</note>
For deafness and blurred vision, first dredge the "interior" <note>original: "lǐ"; the deep, internal levels of the body</note> using the **Three-Transformation Decoction** <note>Sānhuà Tāng: a formula used to purge heat and wind from the bowels to clear the head</note>, then proceed with circulation.

## Tetanus and Wind-Stroke from Injury
If the pulse is "floating" at the exterior, use sweating to release it; if the pulse is "deep" in the interior, use purgation to clear it. <unclear>Ointment</unclear> should be crushed/pounded.

## Wind-Strike and Aversion to Wind
<note>A common cold or flu-like state characterized by a dislike of drafts.</note>
**Saposhnikovia root** serves as the Sovereign herb.
<margin>
Notopterygium and Saposhnikovia for the back, crushed.
Bugbane and Dahurian Angelica for the front, crushed.
Bupleurum and Saposhnikovia for the right side, crushed; add Dahurian Angelica.
</margin>
**Licorice root** serves as the Assistant.

## Cold-Strike and Aversion to Cold
**Ephedra** <term>Máhuáng: a powerful herb for inducing sweat and opening the lungs</term> serves as the Sovereign, with **Licorice root** and **Saposhnikovia root** as Assistants.

### Chen Zangqi’s General Rules for Using Medication in Cases of Various Deficiencies
<note>Chen Zangqi (c. 681–757 AD) was a famous Tang Dynasty pharmacologist known for his "Supplement to the Materia Medica."</note>

In general, the many diseases, accumulations, and gatherings all arise from a state of **deficiency**. **Deficiency** <note>original: "xu," a state of weakness or insufficiency in the body's vital energy, blood, or fluids.</note> gives rise to a hundred diseases. **Accumulations** <note>original: "ji," referring to fixed, internal masses.</note> are what collect within the **Five Viscera** <note>The solid organs: Heart, Liver, Spleen, Lungs, and Kidneys.</note>, while **gatherings** <note>original: "ju," referring to movable or fluctuating masses.</note> are what congregate in the **Six Bowels** <note>The hollow organs: Gallbladder, Stomach, Small Intestine, Large Intestine, Bladder, and Triple Burner.</note>.

For ailments such as these, many physicians simply follow old prescriptions without daring to make any <term>additions or subtractions</term> <note>The practice of modifying a standard formula by adding or removing ingredients to fit a patient's unique symptoms.</note>. However, for those suffering from deficiency combined with **exhaustion** <note>original: "lao," referring to chronic fatigue or "consumptive" diseases like tuberculosis.</note>, the complications are myriad. One should always add or subtract ingredients according to the specific progress of the illness.

The skilled physicians of antiquity all gathered their own herbs. They carefully examined the **physical properties** and **nature** of the plants and what they governed, and they harvested them at the correct time of day and season. If harvested too early, the medicinal potency has not yet formed; if harvested too late, the peak potency has already faded.

Modern physicians do not gather their own herbs, nor do they understand the importance of the **Solar Terms** <note>original: "jieqi," the 24 divisions of the traditional calendar that track seasonal changes vital for agriculture and medicine.</note>. Furthermore, they do not understand how to balance **cold and heat** properties or the correct dosages. They possess only the reputation of treating illness but never achieve the effect of a guaranteed cure. This is truly a superficial confusion. One must again carefully examine the cold and heat properties and record the principles of modification.

Scattering pertains to **sweating**; surging pertains to **vomiting**; draining pertains to **purging**. Seeping is for releasing the exterior, which is categorized with sweating; discharging is for promoting urination, which is categorized with purging.

# Compendium of Materia Medica
## Page Thirty-Three

Grains, fruits, vegetables, and meats are all the true substances of supplementation. Yet, one must still distinguish what is appropriate for the <term>Five Viscera</term> <note>original: "Wu Zang" (五藏); the Heart, Liver, Spleen, Lungs, and Kidneys</note> to ensure that no single organ becomes over-dominant. If this balance is maintained, all is well. 

However, if one uses medicinal drugs for "supplementation"—even mild ones like <term>Licorice</term> <note>original: "Gancao" (甘草)</note> or <term>Bitter Ginseng</term> <note>original: "Kushen" (苦參)</note>—taking them for a long duration will inevitably lead to a "partial victory" <note>an imbalance where one organ's energy becomes too strong at the expense of others</note>. This artificially increases the <term>Qi</term> and risks cutting one's life short. If this is true for mild herbs, how much more dangerous are those that are highly toxic or poisonous? 

Therefore, the **Three Methods** <note>Sweating, Vomiting, and Purging</note> are like criminal punishments, while fine grains and meat are like moral education. To govern a state in chaos, one must use punishments; to maintain a state already in order, one uses virtue. This is the natural principle of things. 

When I employ the Three Methods, I often combine them with many other techniques: there is massage, therapeutic stretching, covering/compressing, guided breathing, increasing or decreasing dosages, and knowing when to continue or stop. It is a tragedy that other physicians do not grasp the breadth of my methods and instead falsely accuse me of being reckless! 

For instance, inducing salivation, draining phlegm, inducing sneezing, and drawing out tears—any method where the movement of the illness is directed **upward**—belongs to the category of the **Vomiting Method**. 

Smoking, steaming, soaking, washing, hot compresses, cauterizing, acupuncture, pricking, stone-needle therapy, douching, therapeutic exercises, and massage—any method that releases the "exterior" <note>the surface of the body or the skin</note>—belongs to the category of the **Sweating Method**. 

Hastening labor, promoting lactation, grinding down internal accumulations, expelling excess water, breaking through stagnant menstruation, and venting trapped gas—any method where the movement is directed **downward**—belongs to the category of the **Purging Method**.
