# The Wei Army Retreats <gloss>Emperor Gao</gloss> <note>Emperor Gao (Xiao Daocheng) was the founder of the Southern Qi dynasty. The "Wei" refers to the Northern Wei, the primary rival to the southern Chinese dynasties during this period of division.</note>

The ruler of Wei invaded the state of Qi, reaching Shouyang <note>A strategic city in modern-day Anhui province.</note>. Moving eastward along the Huai River, the local people remained settled and undisturbed in their homes, while the transport of tax grain filled the roads. The army eventually reached Zhongli. 

The Qi court dispatched General Cui Huijing to rescue the region. Meanwhile, the Wei commanders Liu Chang and Wang Su <note>Wang Su was a prominent defector from the south who became a high-ranking official in the Northern Wei.</note> claimed to lead a force of two hundred thousand men. They dug triple moats and erected palisades, concentrating their full strength to attack Yiyang.

At dawn, the defenders inside the city spotted the relief army on the mountain. They sent out Wang Boyu, the Chief Administrator, to attack the Wei palisades. Taking advantage of the wind, they set fires to the enemy defenses, while Xiao Yan and his men struck from the outside. The Wei army was forced to lift the siege and retreat; the Qi forces pursued and defeated them.

The people of **Qi** <note>The Southern Qi dynasty (479–502 AD), the southern rival of the Northern Wei.</note> occupied the river islets to intercept the army, cutting off the ferry routes. As the enemy reinforcements arrived in succession, the Northern Wei officers and soldiers were already exhausted. Seized with great fear, they wanted to flee. **Yuan Ying**, however, intentionally **loosened his reins and rode slowly**, his **expression remaining calm and composed**. He climbed to high ground to observe the enemy, pointing and gesturing to the east and west as if he were issuing complex tactical orders. 

Only after this display did he arrange his ranks and advance. The **Qi army** <note>The Southern Qi dynasty forces.</note> suspected an ambush was waiting for them; they hesitated and began to withdraw. **Ying** immediately pursued and defeated them, subsequently laying siege to the city of **Nanzheng** <note>A strategic city in modern-day Hanzhong, Shaanxi province.</note>.

He strictly **forbade his officers and soldiers from committing acts of aggression or violence** against the local population. As a result, people from near and far were pleased and submitted to his authority, even competing with one another to provide taxes and transport supplies to his army. 

Inside the city, however, there was great trepidation. The defending general, **Yu Yu**, resorted to a clever deception: he sealed and labeled **dozens of empty granaries**, showing them to his soldiers and officials. He told them: "These are all full of grain, enough to support us for an entire year. You need only strive to hold out firmly." Upon hearing this, the hearts of the multitude were set at ease.

It happened that the **Lord of Wei** <note>Emperor Xuanwu of Northern Wei.</note> recalled **Ying** to the capital. **Ying** ordered the old and weak soldiers to depart first, while he personally led the elite troops to serve as the rear guard. He sent an envoy to inform **Xiao Yi** <note>The Southern commander defending the region.</note> of his departure. **Yi**, suspecting it was a trap, waited until **Ying** had been gone for a full day before sending his generals to pursue him. **Ying** dismounted to face them in battle; **Yi's** soldiers did not dare to close in and eventually retreated.

**Mu Tai**, a high official of the **Northern Wei**, was sent out to serve as the Regional Governor of **Dingzhou** <note>In modern Hebei province.</note>. He personally submitted a report stating that he had suffered from a long-term illness and that the local soil was excessively damp; he begged to be moved to **Hengzhou** <note>The region surrounding the old capital, Pingcheng, in modern Shanxi.</note>. The Emperor of Wei accommodated him by moving the Regional Governor of Hengzhou, **Lu Rui**, to Dingzhou, and appointing **Tai** to replace him.

When **Tai** arrived at his new post, **Rui** had not yet departed. Consequently, the two of them conspired together to stage a rebellion. They attempted to install **Yuan Yi, the Prince of Yangping**, as their leader. **Prince Yi** feigned his consent to their plan, but secretly reported the conspiracy to the throne.

At that time, **Yuan Cheng, the Prince of Rencheng**, was ill. The Emperor summoned him for an audience and told him: "**Mu Tai** is plotting a revolt. We have only just moved the capital <note>This refers to the historic move from the northern city of Pingcheng to Luoyang in 494 CE, which many northern elites opposed.</note>, and the northerners still long for their old homeland. With disturbances now split between the north and the south, my position here in **Luoyang** is not yet stable. This is a grave matter that only you can handle. You must force yourself to travel north for me. If their faction is still weak, go directly and capture them. If they have already grown powerful and numerous, you are authorized to exercise imperial power <term>chengzhi: the authority to issue orders and mobilize troops in the Emperor's name without waiting for further permission</term> to call up the troops of **Bingzhou** and **Sizhou** to strike them."

The Emperor then granted **Cheng** the imperial tally <note>A symbolic staff of office representing the Emperor's direct authority.</note>. When **Cheng** reached **Yanmen**, the local Governor arrived under the cover of night to report that **Tai** had already led his troops westward to join the **Prince of Yangping**. **Cheng** immediately ordered his forces to advance. The Assistant Minister, **Meng Bin**, cautioned him, saying: "The outcome of this situation is still impossible to measure; we should proceed strictly according to the original imperial decree."

## 33

The **Chen** general **Wu Mingche** launched an invasion of **Northern Qi**. Many inhabitants of **Northern Qi's** **Northern Xuzhou** <note>A region roughly corresponding to parts of modern-day Shandong and Jiangsu provinces.</note> rose in arms to support the **Chen** forces, placing the provincial capital under immense pressure. The rebels, suspecting that the city had been abandoned or was a trap, failed to prepare their defenses. Suddenly, **Ting** ordered a thunderous beating of drums that shook the heavens; the rebels were so startled that they turned and fled.

Shortly after, the rebels reformed their battle lines and advanced toward the city again. **Ting** commanded his Adjutant, **Wang Junzhi**, to lead troops out to resist them. He himself mounted a horse and approached the front lines, directing his personal attendants as they rained arrows upon the enemy. The rebels had previously heard of his blindness and assumed he would be unable to leave the city; seeing him personally leading the defense, they were struck with great alarm. Meanwhile, the corrupt court favorite **Mu Tipuo** <note>Mu Tipuo was one of the "Three Nobles" who dominated the late Northern Qi court, often accused of prioritizing personal rivalries over national survival.</note> desired for the city to fall and refused to send any reinforcements. Despite this, **Ting** continued to fight while maintaining the defense, and the rebels eventually dispersed and fled.

The **Emperor of Northern Zhou** <note>Emperor Wu of Northern Zhou, known for his ambition to reunify China.</note> plotted to invade **Northern Qi** in total secrecy, consulting only with the **Prince of Qi**, **[Yuwen] Xian**, and the Palace Secretary **Wang Yi**. He further dispatched the Imperial Attendant **Lu Hu** to travel by postal relay to visit **Yu Yi**, the Commandant-General of **Anzhou**, on three separate occasions to seek his strategic counsel. No one else was aware of these movements.
