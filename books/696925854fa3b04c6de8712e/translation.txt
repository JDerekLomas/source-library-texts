<note>Marsilio Ficino (1433–1499) was the leader of the Platonic Academy in Florence; Giovanni Cavalcanti was his closest friend and patron.</note>

TRUE FRIENDSHIP, MY DEAREST GIOVANNI,
the Platonic philosophers have best defined as the stable
union of the lives of two people. Indeed, we believe that there is
only one life in those men who, by a single labor, walk toward a single end,
as if by the same path toward the same
destination of their journey. And we consider that their
partnership will finally be stable when it is not only a union,
but when that which they have proposed to pursue through a certain
mutual duty between them is also stable and firm.

Truly, since every pursuit and association of men is
always accustomed to strive toward that which is thought to be good,
and there appear to be three types of goods for mortals: namely, the goods of the soul,
of the body, and external things; either the virtue of the soul is sought by them,
or the pleasure of the body, or an abundance of riches. Of these,
the first is certainly certain and perpetual; the remaining two
are fleeting and mortal. For which reason,
a stable union of life—which is true

...nor for the sake of satisfying the lusts of the body, which are all fleeting and perishable. Instead, they should set out with a certain shared zeal and with the full fire of the mind to seize and cultivate the one stable virtue of the soul. Our Plato, that master and leader of all philosophers, wished for this virtue of the soul to be called "Wisdom." They <note>referring to the Platonic school</note> considered Wisdom to be the knowledge of divine things. Furthermore, in his books *On the Republic* <note>original: "de re p." referring to Plato's Republic</note>, he demonstrates that divine things cannot become known to our minds in any other way than by God illuminating us, just as the forms of bodies cannot be seen by the eyes unless the sun illuminates the eyes. Therefore, it is God himself whom we desire to see; He who illuminates the sharp edge of the mind so that we may be able to perceive, and who finally shows Himself more clearly to the mind. In showing Himself, He delights us. God is likewise the Way for us, the Truth, and the Life <note>A reference to John 14:6, "Ego sum via et veritas et vita," integrated here into Platonic philosophy</note>. He is the Way because He turns us toward Himself with His rays, and leads us, and carries us away. He is the Truth because He shows Himself as true to us who have turned toward Him. Finally, He is the Life because He feeds and delights our soul—the contemplator of Him—forever with that blessed vision. Therefore, all those who desire to taste the sweetest liquids of wisdom must thirst for this perennial fountain of wisdom.
