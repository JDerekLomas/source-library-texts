# Stone Catapult Carriage
<note>original: "石砲車" (shípào chē). This describes a traction trebuchet or catapult mounted on a mobile wheeled chassis, a staple of Chinese siege engineering.</note>

The **Stone Catapult Carriage** uses large timbers to form its base frame, beneath which four wheels are attached. Above this, a single vertical pole is erected. At the top of the pole, a **swivel-bracket** <note>original: "羅匡" (luó kuāng); a wooden housing or frame that allows the arm to pivot.</note> made of wood is installed, upon which the **catapult arm** <term>Catapult Arm: Known as the "pào shāo" (砲梢), this is the flexible or pivoting beam that flings the stone projectile.</term> is set. 

The height and angle are calibrated according to the height of the city walls <note>original: "邑下約城爲準"; meaning the positioning is adjusted relative to the height of the target fortifications.</note>. The carriage is pushed and moved back and forth to achieve the greatest tactical advantage. In all other respects, the methods for firing and the materials required are exactly the same as the standard catapult method.

# Single-Pole Trebuchet

The **Single-Pole Trebuchet** utilizes four front and rear support pillars: <gloss>The front pillars are 18 feet long, with a top opening of 6 inches, wrapped in iron leafing for reinforcement; the rear pillars are 16.5 feet long.</gloss>

One **Upper Comb-Frame**: <gloss>8.5 feet in length; after accounting for the angled slope, the functional length is 6 feet.</gloss> One **Lower Base Sleeper**: <gloss>13 feet in length; after accounting for the angled slope, the functional length is 9 feet.</gloss> **"Deer-Ear" Pivot Brackets**.

Two **Iron Bands**: <gloss>Each weighing 7 taels with a circumference of 7 inches.</gloss> Eighteen **"Wolf-Tooth" Spiked Nails**. Two **Tension Strings**: <gloss>25 feet long.</gloss>

One catty and eight taels of **Hemp**: <gloss>Used for the twelve secondary lines.</gloss> One **Leather Sling Pouch**: <gloss>4 inches long and 6 inches wide.</gloss> Six **Binding Ropes**: <gloss>50 feet long; each rope requires 2.5 catties of hemp to construct.</gloss>

# Rockets
<note>original: "火箭" (huǒjiàn)</note>

<vocab>火箭, huojian, rocket, fire arrow, gunpowder weapons, military technology</vocab>

The fire arrow <note>the precursor to the modern rocket</note> is a weapon that harnesses the explosive power of gunpowder to propel an arrow shaft over great distances. Unlike a traditional arrow, which depends entirely on the physical strength of the archer and the tension of the bow, the fire arrow carries its own propulsion system.

To manufacture these devices, a tube is fashioned from sturdy paper or bamboo. This tube is filled with a balanced mixture of gunpowder—typically saltpeter, sulfur, and charcoal—and then tightly sealed. The tube is securely lashed to the arrow shaft, just behind the arrowhead, using hemp cord or thin wire. 

A fuse <term>引線 (Yǐnxiàn): A "leading line" or fuse treated with flammable chemicals to carry fire into the propellant chamber.</term> is inserted into a small aperture at the base of the tube. When ignited, the rapid combustion of the powder creates a jet of flame and gas that pushes the arrow forward with great force. 

<column-break/>

As shown in the accompanying woodcut, there are various configurations for these weapons. On the left, we see the individual rocket-arrow, which can still be launched from a traditional bow to extend its effective range. On the right, the diagrams indicate the components used for volley-fire systems. 

These "fire-clusters" are often gathered into specialized launching frames or boxes. By ignition of a single master fuse, a soldier can discharge dozens of rockets simultaneously, creating a terrifying "rain of fire" <note>a common historical description of rocket volleys</note> that is particularly effective against wooden fortifications, enemy sails, or cavalry formations where the noise and flame would stampede the horses.

While less precise than the cannons described on the previous page, the rocket is valued for its portability and its ability to deliver fire across terrain where heavy artillery cannot be moved.

The **Bird-Gun** <term>Bird-Gun|the Chinese term for a matchlock musket (niǎochòng), implying it is accurate enough to hit a bird in flight</term> used by the Japanese <note>original: 倭奴 (Wōnū). A historical, often derogatory term for the Japanese used in Ming records.</note> requires the **front and rear sights** <note>original: 星門 (xīngmén), literally "Star and Gate."</note> to be aligned before firing. It is hailed as a highly effective weapon; yet, while it is accurate, it lacks volume. Once it has been discharged, it is immediately useless <note>Referring to the vulnerability of the soldier during the slow reloading process of a single-shot musket.</note>.

We have now weighed the merits of these two designs to create a new weapon. It utilizes the front and rear sights modeled after the Japanese Bird-Gun for accuracy, but adds three or five barrels. Its trigger mechanism is even easier to operate, allowing the soldier to control the timing of the discharge. When the front and rear sights are aligned and the weapon is fired level, it can hit its mark at a distance of 130 paces.
