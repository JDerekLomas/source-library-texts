He returned to the "Prison Crag" and, accompanied by three subordinates, utilized the **four modes of transport** <note>original: 四載 (*sizai*); traditional transport for different terrains: boats for water, carriages for land, sledges for mud, and spiked boots for mountains.</note> to travel along the rivers. He began at Mount Huo, lingered at the **Five Sacred Peaks** <note>The five most important mountains in Chinese geography and ritual.</note>, and followed the course of the **Four Great Rivers** <note>The Yangtze, Yellow, Huai, and Ji Rivers.</note>.

Yu consulted with **Yi** and **Kui** <note>Legendary officials; Yi (Bo Yi) is often credited as the original recorder of the Shanhaijing.</note> to plan their progress. Wherever they reached famous mountains or great marshes, they summoned the local spirits and questioned them. They inquired about the **veins and principles** <note>term: 脉理 (*maili*); refers to the "pulses" or energy flow of the land, similar to how blood flows through veins.</note> of the mountains and rivers, the locations of gold and jade, the varieties of birds, beasts, and insects, as well as the customs of the people from the eight directions and the distances to strange and exotic kingdoms. 

Yu then commanded **Yi** to organize and record these findings, naming the work the ***Classic of Mountains and Seas***. He also commanded **Da Zhang** to pace the distance from east to west, and **Shu Hai** <note>Two legendary figures renowned for their incredible speed and ability to measure the Earth.</note> to measure the world from north to south, so as to fully illuminate the vastness of the eight reaches and calculate the cycles of heaven and earth.

It is the resonance that completes the transformation, blending everything into a single phenomenon. What the world calls "strange," it does not yet understand the *reason* for that strangeness. What the world calls "not strange," it does not yet understand the reason for its *ordinariness*. 

Why is this? A thing is not strange of itself; it awaits a human observer and only *then* becomes strange. Truly, strangeness resides in us; it is not inherent to the thing. 

Therefore, a person might see a simple piece of cloth and doubt its width, or a man from the southern land of **Yue** <note>A region in ancient China whose people were known for distinct customs, such as going barefoot and tattooing their skin</note> might see a shoe and be startled. This is to fail to find strange what is truly strange, while finding strange what should not be strange. If you do not find strange what is truly strange, you have reached a state where almost nothing is strange. But if you find strange what should not be strange, then you haven't yet begun to understand what "strange" truly means. If you can accept as possible what others call impossible, and reject as impossible what others call possible, then in principle, there is nothing that cannot be true.

**Chen’s Catalogue of Books from the Prefectural Study** states that the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* was collated by the Han Palace Attendant and Commandant of the Imperial Carriages, **Liu Xin** <note>original: "臣秀" (Chen Xiu) — another name for the famous Han scholar Liu Xin (c. 50 BCE – 23 CE)</note>, who served as a Secretary in the Imperial Archives. Tradition says the book was authored by **Yu and Yi** <note>The legendary Emperor Yu the Great and his assistant Boyi</note>. The Great Historian **Sima Qian** <note>Author of the *Shiji* (Records of the Grand Historian), c. 145–86 BCE</note> said: "Regarding the mountains and rivers of the Nine Provinces, the *Book of Documents* is close to reality. But as for the monstrous creatures described in the *Basic Annals of Yu* and the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*, I dare not speak of them." This can truly be called a famous saying.

Who would say that this skepticism arises merely from a fondness for the past? **You Mao** particularly clarified that the work was not written by Yu and Boyi, yet he maintained it is undoubtedly an ancient book from the **pre-Qin period** <note>Prior to 221 BCE</note>. # Classic of Mountains and Seas

The **Classic of Mountains and Seas** <term>Shan Hai Jing: An ancient Chinese source of geography and mythology</term> was written for the purpose of following the mountain ranges, clearing timber, and carving out paths for travel. It is, in essence, an unfinished work. For this reason, it relies solely on the five directions—South, West, North, East, and Center—to define the geographical corners of the world, whereas the formal division into Provinces <note>The "Nine Provinces" (Kyushu), the traditional administrative divisions of ancient China</note> had not yet been established in this text.

It uses the regions of the "Lands within and beyond the Seas" and the "Great Wilderness" to provide a general outline of the world. It records various wonders among birds, beasts, flora, minerals, and human figures; however, the systems of tribute <note>The specific taxes or regional products owed to the central government</note> remained unsettled in its records.

The descriptions in the **Tribute of Yu** <term>Yu Gong: A chapter in the "Book of Documents" that established the geographical and tax standards for the empire</term> are detailed because that text focuses primarily on honoring the Imperial Capital. Therefore, it describes Jizhou <note>The region surrounding the ancient capital, spanning parts of modern-day Hebei and Shanxi</note> as the starting point. It then moves through Yan and Yong <note>Provinces in the north and west</note> as the highest elevations, concluding the geographic survey with Yong. Because the Kunlun Mountains are the most distant, the *Tribute of Yu* concludes its survey there with the mention of "woven skins" <note>Tribute garments from western tribes</note> and the "Three Tribes," recording no other taxes from such remote areas.

The **Classic of Mountains and Seas** serves as a guide to managing the land based on what was actually witnessed. While it is said that all mountains in the world originate from the Kunlun range, the **Classic of Mountains and Seas** actually begins its record with the Southern Mountains.

The **Tribute of Yu** was intended to define the taxes to be presented to the Son of Heaven <note>The Emperor</note>. Because Mount Kunlun lay in the remote wilderness beyond the civilized world, it was not listed among the Nine Provinces. That text only speaks of "guiding the Yellow River from Jishi," but it does not mean that Jishi is the actual source of the River. 

The **Classic of Mountains and Seas** provides a comprehensive record of everything surrounding the inner and outer seas. Consequently, it is able to trace the origins of these landmarks and determine where they end. Only in its descriptions of the "Beyond the Seas," the "Great Wilderness," the "Five Directions," and the "Within the Seas" are the accounts...

The "backward knee" is a common trope in ancient bestiaries to denote the 'otherness' of people living at the world's edge.</note> They dwell to the east of the Kingdom of One-Eye.
