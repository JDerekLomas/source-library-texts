# PREFACE.

The *Lives of the Succession of Elders* <note>original: *Sthavirâvalî-charita*</note>, i. e., the Lives of the Jaina Patriarchs, by Hemachandra <note>A celebrated 12th-century Jain scholar and polymath</note> is, as its other and more frequently used title *Appendix Section* <note>original: *Parisishṭaparvan*</note> indicates, the Appendix or continuation of the same author's great work in ten *books* <note>original: *parvans*</note> on the Lives of the Saints <term>hagiology</term> of the Jainas, the *Lives of the Sixty-Three Illustrious Persons* <note>original: *Trishashṭiśalâkâ-purusha-charita*</note>. The sixty-three *illustrious persons* <note>original: *śalâkâ-purushas*</note> are the great personages, divine or human, who, according to the belief of the Jainas, have, since the present order of things, risen in the history of the world, and directed or influenced its course; they comprise the twenty-four *Prophets* <note>original: *Tîrthakaras*, literally "Ford-makers" who provide a way across the sea of rebirth</note> or prophets, the twelve *Universal Monarchs* <note>original: *Chakravartins*</note> or universal monarchs, the nine *Vâsudevas*, the nine *Baladevas*, and the nine *Prativâsudevas* <note>These last three categories represent sets of heroic brothers and their rivals in Jaina mythology, similar to figures in the Hindu epics</note>. With the exception of the last two *Prophets* <note>original: *Tîrthakaras*</note>, *Pârśvanâtha* and *Mahâvîra* <note>The 23rd and 24th Tîrthakaras; Mahâvîra was a contemporary of the Buddha</note>, all these great men must be regarded as belonging not to history, but mythology or epical fiction. It is different with the patriarchs of which the *Appendix Section* <note>original: *Parisishṭaparvan*</note> treats. For it cannot reasonably be doubted, that patriarchs of the name and, partially at least, in the order which the unanimous tradition of the *White-Clad Sect* <note>original: *Śvetâmbaras*, one of the two main branches of Jainism</note> has preserved, have really existed. But nobody will therefore go the length of maintaining the historical character of the tales which Hemachandra has pieced together into something like a connected history of the Jaina church. For most of these legends are, on the face of it, legends, which, however, may have preserved some grains of historical truth imbedded in a ground-work of fiction and miracles. But an attempt to separate the historical facts from the legendary matter which has grown round them, would be a futile undertaking, because we have

If we regard Hemachandra's work as a poem, we shall not deny him our praise. For he is an agreeable narrator who knows how to tell a tale and to interest his readers. The king and his subjects showed great devotion during these events. The example and advice of **Samprati** inspired his subordinate rulers to also embrace and patronize the Jain creed, ensuring that monks could practice their religion not only in his own kingdom but in all the surrounding lands (66—83).

In order to spread the faith to less developed regions <note>referred to in the text as "uncivilized countries," likely meaning areas where Jainism was not yet established.</note>, **Samprati** sent messengers there disguised as **Jaina** monks. These messengers taught the local people about the types of food and other necessities that monks are permitted to accept as alms. In reality, these "revenue collectors" were to be actual **Jaina** monks. Having prepared the way in this manner, the king persuaded the monastic Superior to send monks to those regions, as it would now be possible for them to survive there. Accordingly, missionaries were sent to the **Andhras** and **Dramilas** <note>Ancient peoples of South India.</note>, who found the conditions exactly as the king had promised. In this way, these nations were brought under the influence of **Jainism** (89—102).

Remembering that he had been a starving beggar in his previous life, **Samprati** issued orders that the needy should be provided with food and drink at the city gates. He arranged for the leftover food, which the cooks usually kept for themselves, to be reserved specifically for the monks, while compensating the cooks with money. Such was the king’s religious fervor that he even commanded merchants to provide the monks with whatever they requested free of charge.
