# INTRODUCTION.

## On the Greek Text of Heron’s *Belopoiika*¹.

<margin>July 16, 1921 - A.F. Pauli</margin>
The revision of Rudolf Schneider's text², to which an introduction and a concluding chapter have been added, could—for the philologist <note>a scholar of historical texts and languages</note> who currently has no way to consult the authoritative Paris manuscript of Mynas to fill certain gaps in Wescher’s edition—only consist of avoiding the shortcomings of the last two editions. The final establishment of the text must be left to the great Heron edition of the *Bibliotheca Teubneriana* <note>a famous series of Greek and Latin biological/technical texts</note>, whose second part of Volume II will also include the *Belopoiika* <note>The Construction of Artillery</note>.

<margin>Classics 15 N20 Harrassowitz 17.33 Marks</margin>
Regarding Wescher's achievement, R. Schneider expressed himself in the *Reports of the Imperial Archaeological Institute, Rome 1905 (XX), p. 144* as follows: "This work entirely meets the requirements of a critical edition as demanded by modern philology; one only notices that the author lacks technical knowledge, which General de Reffye could easily have provided for him." We are not in a position to fully adopt this favorable judgment of the philological performance. While the <term>recensio</term> <note>the systematic examination and stemmatic grouping of manuscripts</note> of the manuscript and the priority given to the ancient Mynas manuscript are correct, the arrangement of the <term>apparatus</term> <note>the list of textual variations and corrections at the bottom of a page</note> is, aside from individual obscurities and gaps, as confusing as possible. The amateurish palaeographical notes are distracting, and for the <term>emendation</term> <note>the process of correcting errors in the manuscript</note> of difficult passages, he lacks not only technical expertise but also grammatical and critical training.

Thus, R. Schneider had a rewarding field of activity here, as in the other places where he updated Wescher’s *Poliorketika* <note>Treatises on Siegecraft</note>. His text represents a great step forward, yet the spirit of our late friend, who wrestled passionately with these problems, overshot the mark in several places. It was necessary not to immortalize these failures by mentioning them in the notes.

DIELS and E. SCHRAMM:

We received valuable help from Mr. Richard Schöne, who kindly provided us with his personal copy of Wescher’s edition. It contains not only some corrections to Wescher’s collation <note>a line-by-line comparison of manuscripts to identify differences</note> of Manuscript M, but also his own contributions to the emendation <note>the process of correcting or improving a text</note> of the work, which we have gratefully utilized. During the proofreading process, Hermann Schöne’s collation of Manuscript M also became accessible to us. We thank him from the heart as well. As a rule, variations in spelling have not been included in the critical notes.

Heron is the most useful of all the ancient military writers who have written about artillery; since the text and drawings complement one another, one obtains a fairly clear picture of the engines of war. Nevertheless, without a precise knowledge of Philon and Vitruvius <note>two other major authorities on ancient engineering and mechanics</note>, he must remain partially incomprehensible. Only the continuous comparison of all these writers, alongside the reconstruction and testing of every single part, can provide complete clarity.

The technical terms of antiquity have been translated into their correct modern technical equivalents whenever possible. Where this was not possible, the ancient terms have been retained—for example, <term>peritret</term> <note>the frame or perforated plate that held the hair-springs in a catapult</note> and so on. The term <term>diostra</term> <note>the sliding trough of a catapult</note> could also have remained, but since we have a commonly known instrument in the <term>slide rule</term> <note>original German: "Rechenschieber"</note> whose slider can be quite well compared with the <term>diostra</term>, this translation was chosen.

<margin>Wescher p. 71</margin>
The most significant and essential part of philosophical study is that which deals with tranquility <note>original Greek: ἀταραξίας (ataraxia), a state of serene calmness and freedom from fear</note>, about which the most numerous inquiries have been made by those who handle wisdom, and continue to be made to this day; and I believe that theoretical inquiry into this matter through words will never reach a conclusion.
<margin>p. 72</margin>
But Mechanics, having surpassed the verbal instruction regarding this state, has taught all men how to live without disturbance through one single and smallest part of its field—I mean that which pertains to so-called <term>belopoiia</term> <note>the construction of engines of war or artillery</note>. For through this, people will never be disturbed in a state of peace by the incursions of enemies and foes, nor, when war is imminent, will they ever be troubled by the philosophy <note>here meaning "system" or "discipline"</note> provided by it through these instruments of war. Therefore, one must always apply oneself to this part...

---

The largest and most necessary part of world-wisdom <note>original German: "Weltweisheit," an older term for philosophy</note> is that which deals with peace of mind, concerning which philosophers have conducted the most investigations and continue to do so today; and I also believe that theoretical investigations into it will never find an end. Mechanics, however, stepped beyond the theoretical teaching of peace of mind and taught all people the science: to live in peace of mind through a single, minimal part of it, which deals with so-called artillery construction. For through it, one is placed in a position to be troubled neither in a state of peace by attacks of internal or external enemies, nor at the outbreak of war, as a result of the teaching of machines communicated by it. Therefore, one must apply oneself to this part of mechanics at all times and take every precaution...

---

The ropes for bringing [the slider] forward look like a single rope on the drawing. The clear width of the <term>ladder</term> <note>original: *klimax*; the central guiding track of the catapult.</note> is 1 1/5 <term>Calibers</term> <note>original: *Kal.*; the diameter of the spring-hole, which serves as the base unit for all catapult measurements.</note>. The thickness of the ladder-beams is 1/4 Caliber each; 1 1/5 + (2 × 1/4) = 1.7 Calibers. Thus, the total width of the ladder corresponds to the clear distance between the half-frames.

In a 20-mina <term>Palintonon</term> <note>original: *palintonon*; a heavy, stone-throwing torsion catapult.</note>, this distance measures 62 cm, which is slightly more than double the length of a human elbow (forearm). It is probable that Heron had this specific engine in mind and meant the length of a human elbow, rather than the length of the catapult's throwing arm. While this measurement does not remain proportional for larger or smaller calibers, the probability is increased by the fact that the specification of 1 1/2 cubits for the column height also refers only to the 2-cubit <term>Euthytonon</term> <note>original: *euthytonon*; a "straight-spring" catapult typically used for firing arrows.</note> and likewise does not scale for different calibers.

### Scaling the Artillery Engine from a Successful Model

Once the construction of an engine has been successful, one can assemble the others based on it in the following way. Let the diameter of the (given) engine be <margin>5</margin> AB, and let the task be to construct a second engine based on this which throws, for example, a projectile three times as heavy <margin>p. 115</margin> as the one previously mentioned. Since the tension-bundle <term>tonos</term> <note>original: *tonos*. This should not be done with an arbitrary hole <term>trēma</term>, but rather such that the height (length) <margin>15</margin> of the *tonos* stands in the correct proportion to the hole, so that the cylinders <note>The rope-springs within the frame form a cylindrical shape; "similar" cylinders have the same ratio of height to diameter.</note> formed by the tension-bundles are similar. Since the volume of similar cylinders relates <margin>20</margin> to one another in the triplicate ratio <note>The "triplicate ratio" is the ancient Greek way of saying the ratio of the cubes (x³).</note> of the diameters of their bases, let the diameter of the hole that has been correctly found be ΓΔ [Gamma-Delta]. Therefore, the cylinder resulting from AB to the <margin>25</margin> cylinder from ΓΔ has a triplicate ratio, just as AB is to ΓΔ. Now, let it be constructed so that as AB is to ΓΔ, so ΓΔ is to EZ, and EZ is to ΗΘ. Therefore, AB will also have to ΗΘ [the same ratio as AB³ to ΓΔ³].
