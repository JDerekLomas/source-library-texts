At that time, Sariputra <note>One of the Buddha’s senior disciples, known for his wisdom but often used in this sutra to represent the "hearer" (Sravaka) perspective.</note>, empowered by the Buddha’s spiritual authority, thought to himself: "If the Buddha-land is pure only when the Bodhisattva’s mind is pure, then could it be that our World-Honored One's mind was impure when he was a Bodhisattva? At that moment, the Brahma King Sikhin <note>A high-ranking deity in Buddhist cosmology.</note> said to Sariputra: "Do not think this Buddha-land is impure. Why? I see the land of Shakyamuni Buddha as pure and majestic, as beautiful as the palace of the highest heavens."

Brahma King Sikhin replied: "My friend, because your mind has 'highs and lows' and does not rely on the Buddha’s wisdom, you see this land as impure. Sariputra, a Bodhisattva treats all beings with equality. With a profound and pure mind relying on Buddha-wisdom, one can see the purity of this land."

Then, the Buddha pressed his toe against the earth. Immediately, the universe of three thousand great thousand worlds was adorned with hundreds of thousands of jewels, resembling the Jewel-Adorned Land of the Buddha 'Infinite Virtue.' The entire assembly marveled at this unprecedented sight, and everyone found themselves sitting upon jeweled lotus flowers.

The Buddha told Sariputra: "Look now at the majesty and purity of this Buddha-land."
Sariputra said: "Indeed, World-Honored One. I have never seen or heard of this before. The majesty of the Buddha-land is now fully revealed."
The Buddha told Sariputra: "My Buddha-land is always pure like this. But to save those of inferior character, I show it as an impure land full of evils. Just as the gods eat from the same jeweled vessel, yet the color of the food differs according to each one's merit—so it is, Sariputra. ## Chapter 2: Expedient Means

There was an elder named Vimalakirti. He had already made offerings to immeasurable Buddhas and deeply planted the roots of virtue. He had attained the Patience of Non-arising. His eloquence was unobstructed. He played with supernatural powers and attained all forms of <term>dharanis</term><note>Sacred chants or mnemonic spells used to retain the teachings.</note>. He had achieved fearlessness, subdued the 'Mara' of afflictions, and entered the profound gates of the Dharma. He was skilled in the perfection of wisdom and well-versed in <term>expedient means</term>. He had fulfilled his great vows and clearly understood the paths of sentient beings' minds. He could distinguish the sharp or dull capacities of others. Having long practiced the Buddha-way, his mind was seasoned and refined, committed to the Great Vehicle (Mahayana). In all his actions, he acted with careful consideration. He dwelt in the dignity of the Buddha, his mind as vast as the ocean. The Buddhas praised him, and he was respected by the disciples, Indra, and the Brahma lords.

Desiring to save people, he lived in the city of Vaisali using <term>expedient means</term>. Although he possessed immeasurable wealth, he cared for the poor. He maintained pure discipline to guide those who broke the precepts. He used patience to transform the angry, and great diligence to guide the lazy. He used meditation to focus the distracted, and firm wisdom to guide the ignorant.

Though he lived as a layman, he maintained the pure discipline of a monk. Though he lived at home, he was not attached to the three realms of existence. He appeared to have a wife and children, yet he always practiced celibacy. He appeared to have a household, yet he delighted in solitude. Though he wore jewels and ornaments, he used the 'marks of a Buddha' to adorn his body. Though he ate and drank, he took the joy of meditation as his flavor. If he visited places of gambling or entertainment, he did so only to save people. He engaged with other religions but never abandoned the true faith. Though he was expert in secular texts, he always delighted in the Buddha's laws. He was respected by all as the foremost among those worthy of offerings. ## Chapter 3: The Disciples

At that time, the elder Vimalakirti thought to himself as he lay ill in bed: "The World-Honored One <note>A common title for the Buddha.</note> possesses great compassion; would he not show me his pity?"

The Buddha, knowing his thoughts, addressed <term>Sariputra</term><note>One of the Buddha’s foremost disciples, renowned for his superior wisdom.</note>: "Go to Vimalakirti and inquire about his illness."

Sariputra replied to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I am not fit to go and inquire about his illness. Why? I remember once in the past, when I was sitting in quiet meditation beneath a tree in the forest. At that time, Vimalakirti came to me and said: 'Listen, Sariputra! Sitting as you are is not necessarily <term>quiet meditation</term><note>original: "宴坐" (yànzuò); refers to zazen or seated meditation intended to reach a state of stillness.</note>. True quiet meditation means not displaying the body or mind in the <term>Three Realms</term><note>The realms of desire, form, and formlessness that comprise the cycle of rebirth.</note>. It means remaining in the state of cessation without extinguishing your majestic bearing. It means not abandoning the path of the Law while still performing the tasks of an ordinary person. It means the mind neither dwelling within nor staying without. It means remaining unmoved by various views while still practicing the Thirty-seven Aids to Enlightenment. It means entering the state of <term>Nirvana</term><note>The ultimate state of peace and liberation from suffering.</note> without first cutting off all worldly afflictions. If you can sit like this, you will receive the Buddha’s seal of approval.' At that time, World-Honored One, having heard these words, I remained silent and could not respond. Therefore, I am not fit to go and inquire about his illness."

The Buddha then addressed <term>Maudgalyayana</term><note>Also known as Maha-Maudgalyayana, he was the Buddha’s disciple most famous for his supernatural powers.</note>: "Go to Vimalakirti and inquire about his illness."

Maudgalyayana replied to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I am not fit to go and inquire about his illness. Why? I remember once in the past, I entered the great city of Vaisali and was preaching the Law to the householders in a lane. At that time, Vimalakirti came to me and said: 'Listen, Great Maudgalyayana! When preaching the Law to white-clothed householders, you should not speak as you are doing. One who preaches the Law must do so in accordance with the Law. The Law has no 'living beings,' because it is free from the defilement of beings. The Law has no 'self,' because it is free from the defilement of ego. The Law has no 'lifespan,' because it is free from birth and death. The Law has no 'person,' because it cuts through the boundaries of past and future. The Law is eternally tranquil, for it extinguishes all appearances. The Law is free from appearances, for it has no external conditions. The Law has no name, for language is cut off. The Law has nothing to say, for it is beyond thought and observation. The Law has no form, for it is like empty space. The Law has no frivolous arguments, for it is ultimately empty. The Law has no 'mine,' for it is free from possessions. The Law has no distinctions, for it is free from consciousness. The Law has no comparison, for nothing stands against it. The Law is not subject to causes, for it does not reside in conditions. The Law is identical to reality, for it enters into all things. The Law follows 'suchness,' for there is nothing to follow. The Law dwells in the limit of reality, unmoved by any extremes. The Law is unshakable, for it does not rely on the six senses. The Law has no coming or going, for it never stays. The Law accords with emptiness, follows the signless, and responds to the wishless. The Law is free from beauty and ugliness. The Law has no increase or decrease. The Law has no birth or death. The Law has no place to return. The Law transcends the eyes, ears, nose, tongue, body, and mind. The Law has no high or low. The Law is eternally dwelling and unmoving. The Law is free from all meditative practices. Listen, Great Maudgalyayana! If the appearance of the Law is like this, how can it be spoken? One who truly preaches the Law has nothing to say and nothing to show; one who truly listens to the Law hears nothing and attains nothing. It is like a phantom person preaching to another phantom person; one should establish this intent when preaching. One should understand the sharp or dull faculties of living beings and have clear, unobstructed insight. With a heart of great compassion, one should praise the <term>Great Vehicle</term><note>original: "大乗" (Mahayana); the path of the Bodhisattva seeking to save all beings.</note>, remembering to repay the Buddha's kindness and ensuring the <term>Three Jewels</term><note>The Buddha, the Law (Dharma), and the Community (Sangha).</note> are never cut off. Only then should one preach the Law.' When Vimalakirti spoke these words, eight hundred householders resolved to attain Unsurpassed Perfect Enlightenment. I lack such eloquence, and therefore, I am not fit to go and inquire about his illness."

The Buddha then addressed <term>Mahakasyapa</term><note>A primary disciple of the Buddha, known for his ascetic practices and for being the first patriarch of the Zen tradition.</note>: "Go to Vimalakirti and inquire about his illness."

Mahakasyapa replied to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, I am not fit to go and inquire about his illness. Why? I remember once in the past, I was walking through a poor neighborhood to beg for food. At that time, Vimalakirti came to me and said: 'Listen, Great Mahakasyapa! You have a heart of compassion, but it is not universal. You bypass the wealthy and powerful to beg only from the poor. Kasyapa, you should dwell in the Law of equality and beg in sequence. One should beg for food as if not eating. One should receive food as if to destroy the appearance of the union of the four elements. One should receive food with the thought of not receiving it, entering a village with the thought of an empty village...'"

At that time, the Buddha told **Manjushri** <note>The Bodhisattva of Transcendent Wisdom.</note>: "You must go to Vimalakirti to inquire after his illness." 

Manjushri said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, that superior man is difficult to confront. He has reached the profound depths of the true nature of reality and is skilled at preaching the essentials of the Dharma. His eloquence is unhindered and his wisdom is without obstruction. He understands all the protocols of the Bodhisattvas and has entered into the secret treasuries of all Buddhas. He has subdued the various demons and sports with spiritual powers; in both wisdom and skillful means, he has reached the further shore. Nevertheless, I shall obey the Buddha’s sacred command and go to inquire after his illness."

Thereupon, the Bodhisattvas, the Great Disciples, the gods Indra and Brahma, and the Four Heavenly Kings in the assembly all thought: "Now these two great beings, Manjushri and Vimalakirti, will be talking together; they will surely speak of the wondrous Dharma!" Immediately, eight thousand Bodhisattvas, five hundred Voice-Hearers <note>original: "聲聞" (Śrāvaka); disciples seeking personal liberation.</note>, and hundreds of thousands of heavenly beings all wished to follow them. 

Manjushri, surrounded and revered by the assembly of Bodhisattvas, great disciples, and heavenly beings, then entered the great city of **Vaishali** <note>original: "毗耶離".</note>. At that moment, the layman Vimalakirti thought to himself: "Manjushri is coming with a great multitude." He then used his spiritual powers to empty his room, removing everything within it and dismissing his attendants, leaving only a single couch upon which he lay as if ill. 

When Manjushri entered the house, he saw the room was empty and void of all things, with Vimalakirti lying alone on the single couch. Vimalakirti said: "Welcome, Manjushri! You come without the appearance of coming; you see without the appearance of seeing."

# Chapter on Inquiring about the Illness

After Mañjuśrī <note>original: "文殊師利"; the Bodhisattva of Transcendent Wisdom</note> entered the house, he saw that the room was empty and devoid of all belongings; Vimalakīrti lay alone on a single couch. 

Vimalakīrti spoke: "Welcome, Mañjuśrī! You come without the appearance of coming, and you see without the appearance of seeing."

Mañjuśrī replied: "Just so, Layman <note>original: "居士"; a term for a dedicated Buddhist practitioner who remains a householder rather than a monk</note>. If one has already arrived, one does not 'come' again. If one has already departed, one does not 'go' again. Why is this? That which 'comes' has nowhere from which to come; that which 'goes' has nowhere to arrive. That which is 'seen' cannot be seen again. 

"But let us set this aside. Layman, is this illness bearable? Is the treatment reducing the pain rather than it increasing? The World-Honored One <note>original: "世尊"; a title for the Buddha</note> sends his sincere regards and asks after you infinitely. Layman, what caused this illness to arise? How long has it persisted, and how shall it be extinguished?"

Vimalakīrti said: "From ignorance <note>original: "癡" (moha)</note> there is craving <note>original: "愛" (tṛṣṇā)</note>, and thus my illness is born. Because all sentient beings are sick, I am sick. If all sentient beings could attain freedom from illness, then my illness would be extinguished. 

"Why is this? A Bodhisattva <note>original: "菩薩"; an enlightened being who seeks to liberate others</note> enters the cycle of birth and death for the sake of sentient beings. Where there is birth and death, there is illness. If sentient beings can be freed from illness, then the Bodhisattva will no longer be sick. It is like an Elder who has an only son; if the son falls ill, the parents also fall ill. If the son is cured, the parents are also cured. The Bodhisattva is like this: they love all sentient beings as if they were their own children. When sentient beings are sick, the Bodhisattva is sick; when sentient beings are cured, the Bodhisattva is cured."

He spoke again: "You ask what caused this illness to arise? The illness of a Bodhisattva arises from **Great Compassion**."

Mañjuśrī asked: "Layman, why is this room empty and without any attendants?"

Vimalakīrti replied: "All Buddha-lands are likewise empty."

Mañjuśrī asked: "By what means are they empty?"

Vimalakīrti answered: "They are empty by means of **emptiness** <note>original: "空" (śūnyatā); the fundamental Buddhist concept that all things lack a permanent, independent essence</note>."

Mañjuśrī asked: "What use is emptiness for emptiness?"

Vimalakīrti answered: "Because it is empty through non-differentiation, it is empty."

Mañjuśrī asked: "Can emptiness be differentiated?"

Vimalakīrti answered: "The act of differentiation is itself empty."

Mañjuśrī asked: "Where should emptiness be sought?"

Vimalakīrti answered: "It should be sought within the **sixty-two views** <note>original: "六十二見"; a traditional list of mistaken philosophical or religious views about the soul and the world</note>."

Mañjuśrī asked: "Where should the sixty-two views be sought?"

Vimalakīrti answered: "They should be sought within the liberation of all Buddhas."

Mañjuśrī asked: "Where should the liberation of all Buddhas be sought?"

Vimalakīrti answered: "It should be sought within the mental conduct of all sentient beings. Furthermore, you ask why I have no attendants? All the **demons** <note>original: "眾魔" (māras)</note> and practitioners of **outside paths** <note>original: "外道"; those following non-Buddhist teachings</note> are my attendants. Why? The demons take pleasure in the cycle of birth and death, but the Bodhisattva does not abandon birth and death. Those of outside paths take pleasure in their various views, but the Bodhisattva remains unmoved by those views."

Mañjuśrī asked: "Layman, what are the characteristics of your illness?"

Vimalakīrti said: "My illness has no form and cannot be seen."

Mañjuśrī asked: "Is this illness connected to the body or connected to the mind?"

Vimalakīrti answered: "It is not connected to the body, for the body is separate from such characteristics. It is also not connected to the mind, for the mind is like an illusion."

Mañjuśrī asked: "Among the **four great elements**—earth, water, fire, and wind—which element’s illness is this?"

Vimalakīrti answered: "This illness is not of the earth element, yet it is not separate from it. The same is true for the elements of water, fire, and wind. However, the illnesses of sentient beings arise from these four elements; because they are sick, I am sick."

At another time, Mañjuśrī asked Vimalakīrti: "How should a Bodhisattva comfort another Bodhisattva who is ill?"

Vimalakīrti said: "One should speak of the **impermanence** of the body, but not speak of feeling revulsion for it. One should speak of the suffering of the body, but not speak of the joy of **Nirvana** <note>original: "涅槃"; the ultimate state of peace and liberation from suffering</note>. One should speak of the 'non-self' <note>original: "无我" (anātman)</note> of the body, while speaking of the duty to guide sentient beings. One should speak of the emptiness and stillness of the body, but not speak of its ultimate annihilation. 

"One should speak of repenting past sins, but not speak of entering into the past. Because of one's own illness, one should feel pity for the illness of others. One should recognize the suffering of countless eons in past lives, and resolve to benefit all sentient beings. Recall the merits one has cultivated and maintain a pure livelihood. Do not give way to sorrow, but constantly arouse diligence. One should act as a 'King of Doctors' to heal the many illnesses of others. A Bodhisattva should comfort an ailing Bodhisattva in this way, making them feel joyful."

Mañjuśrī asked: "Layman, how should a Bodhisattva who is ill discipline their own mind?"

Vimalakīrti said: "A Bodhisattva who is ill should think: 'This current illness of mine arises from the delusions, inverted thinking, and various afflictions of previous lives. There is no real, substantial entity here; who then is suffering from the illness?' 

"Why is this? The four elements combine to form what is falsely called a 'body.' The four elements have no master, and the body has no 'self.' Furthermore, this illness arises entirely from clinging to the concept of 'I.' Therefore, one should not give rise to clinging to a self. Once the root of the illness is known, one removes the idea of a self and the idea of 'sentient beings' and instead gives rise to the idea of the **Teaching** <note>original: "法" (Dharma)</note>. One should think: 'This body is merely composed of various elements coming together; when it arises, only the Teaching arises; when it ceases, only...'"

Manjushri answered: "If one sees the unconditioned and enters the 'correct status' <note>original: "正位"; the level of an Arhat who has attained a private liberation</note>, they are no longer capable of giving rise to the mind of <term>Supreme, Perfect Enlightenment<note>original: "阿耨多羅三藐三菩提"; *Anuttara-samyak-sambodhi*, the full enlightenment of a Buddha</note></term>. ### Chapter Nine: Entering the Dharma-Door of Non-Duality

At that time, Vimalakirti said to the gathered Bodhisattvas, "Virtuous ones, how does a Bodhisattva enter the <term>Dharma-door of non-duality<note>original: "不二法門"; the teaching that reality transcends all binary oppositions, such as good/evil or self/other</note></term>? Let each of you speak as you please."

In the assembly, there was a Bodhisattva named Self-Existent Speech, who said: "Virtuous ones, 'birth' and 'extinction' are two. Since all phenomena are fundamentally not 'born' <note>in the sense of having a permanent, independent beginning</note>, they now can have no 'extinction.' To attain this 'patience of the non-arising of dharmas' is to enter the Dharma-door of non-duality."

### The Teaching on the Exhaustible and the Inexhaustible

The Buddha told the Bodhisattvas: "There is a teaching called the **Exhaustible and the Inexhaustible Door to Liberation**. This is what you should study. 
*   What is the **Exhaustible**? It refers to <term>conditioned phenomena<note>original: "有爲法"; things that are created, change, and pass away—the physical and mental world we experience</note></term>. 
*   What is the **Inexhaustible**? It refers to <term>unconditioned phenomena<note>original: "無爲法"; the eternal, unchanging reality or Nirvana</note></term>. 

As a Bodhisattva, one does not 'exhaust' the conditioned, yet one does not 'dwell' in the unconditioned."

#### On Not Exhausting the Conditioned

What does it mean to "not exhaust the conditioned"? It means:
*   Not separating from great mercy and not abandoning great compassion.
*   Deeply resolving to attain the mind of all-wisdom and never forgetting it.
*   Teaching and transforming living beings without ever becoming weary.
*   Always practicing the <term>four ways of gathering<note>original: "四攝法"; giving, kind words, helpfulness, and cooperation to attract beings to the Dharma</note></term>.
*   Protecting the True Dharma even at the cost of one's own body and life.
*   Planting roots of goodness without exhaustion.
*   Keeping the will set upon the use of clever methods and <term>dedication of merit<note>original: "迴向"; directing the energy of one's good deeds toward the enlightenment of all</note></term>.
*   Seeking the Dharma without laziness and preaching it without stinginess.
*   Diligently making offerings to all Buddhas and entering the cycle of birth and death without fear.
*   Remaining unmoved by honor or disgrace, and not looking down upon those who have yet to learn.
*   Respecting those who are advanced in learning as if they were the Buddha himself.
*   Helping those mired in afflictions to develop right mindfulness.
*   Not seeking personal pleasure, but instead rejoicing in the joy of others.
*   Regarding the stillness of meditation as if it were a hell to be avoided <note>meaning: not getting stuck in solitary bliss</note>, and regarding the cycle of birth and death as if it were a beautiful garden.
*   Treating those who come seeking help as if they were great teachers.
*   Giving away all possessions with the goal of attaining all-wisdom.
*   Feeling the urge to protect and save those who have broken the precepts.
*   Regarding the <term>perfections<note>original: "波羅蜜"; the virtues (like patience and giving) practiced by Bodhisattvas</note></term> as one's own parents.
*   Regarding the factors of enlightenment as one's own family.
*   Performing good deeds without limit and adorning one's own Buddha-land with purity.
*   Practicing infinite giving and attaining the physical marks of a Buddha.
*   Cleansing away all evil through the purity of body, speech, and mind.

Though they cycle through birth and death for countless eons, their spirit remains courageous. Having heard of the Buddha’s immeasurable virtues, their will never tires. With the sword of wisdom, they strike down the thieves of affliction. They go into the realms of the five skandhas and the senses to carry the burdens of sentient beings and lead them to eternal liberation. With great persistence, they defeat the armies of <term>Mara<note>original: "魔軍"; the personification of temptation and obstacles to enlightenment</note></term>. 

#### On Not Dwelling in the Unconditioned

What does it mean for a Bodhisattva to "not dwell in the unconditioned"? 
It means:
*   They practice the realization of **Emptiness**, but do not use Emptiness as a final proof of attainment.
*   They practice **Signlessness** and **Wishlessness**, but do not use them as a final proof. 
*   They observe that things do not truly "arise," yet they do not use "non-arising" as a final proof.
*   They contemplate impermanence without becoming disgusted by the roots of goodness.
*   They contemplate the suffering of the world without hating the cycle of birth and death.
*   They contemplate the lack of a permanent self, yet they never tire of teaching others.
*   They contemplate "quiescence" (Nirvana), yet they do not enter eternal stillness.
*   They contemplate the lack of a destination, yet they find their destination in good deeds.
*   They contemplate "non-birth," yet they use the process of birth to carry the burdens of all.

Because they possess merit, they do not dwell in the unconditioned <note>they stay active in the world</note>. Because they possess wisdom, they do not exhaust the conditioned <note>they are not confused by the world</note>. Because of their great compassion, they do not dwell in the unconditioned. Because they must fulfill their original vows, they do not exhaust the conditioned.

"You noble Bodhisattvas," the Buddha concluded, "should all study this **Exhaustible and Inexhaustible Door to Liberation**."

Then, they bowed their heads at the Buddha’s feet.

# Chapter 12: Seeing the Buddha Akshobhya

At that time, the World-Honored One asked **Vimalakirti**: "You wish to see the <term>Tathagata<note>original: "如來"; a title for the Buddha meaning 'The One who has thus come'</note></term>. How exactly do you view the Tathagata?"

Vimalakirti replied: "As I view the true reality of my own body, so too do I view the Buddha. I do not view him as sensation, perception, mental formations, or consciousness <note>these are the Five Aggregates that make up a person</note>. He does not arise from the four elements; he is like the empty sky. 

The six sense-entrances have no storehouse for him. His eyes, ears, nose, tongue, body, and mind have already passed beyond and do not reside in the three realms of existence. He is free from the three stains of passion, aggression, and ignorance, and is in accord with the three gates of liberation. He possesses the three types of transcendental knowledge, which are equal to the 'Original Wisdom.'

He is not of one single appearance, nor of different appearances. He is not his own appearance, nor another's appearance. He is not without appearance, nor does he grasp at appearances. He does not belong to this shore, nor the other shore, nor the middle of the stream, yet he transforms living beings. He is viewed as being in total stillness, yet he is not eternally extinguished. He is not 'this' or 'that.' He cannot be known by worldly intelligence or perceived by ordinary consciousness. 

He is neither dark nor bright, neither named nor featured. He is neither strong nor weak, neither pure nor defiled. He does not occupy a place, yet he is not apart from any place. He is neither conditioned nor unconditioned. He has no speech and nothing to be spoken. He is not generous and not stingy; he does not sacrifice and does not withhold. He is neither patient nor angry, neither diligent nor lazy, neither composed nor distracted. He is neither wise nor foolish, neither honest nor deceptive. 

He does not come and does not go; he does not exit and does not enter. All paths of language are cut off. He is not a 'field of merit,' yet he is not *not* a field of merit. He is neither worthy of offerings nor unworthy of offerings. He neither grasps nor abandons. He is without features, yet not without the appearance of truth. He is identical to the 'Reality Limit' and the universal nature of all things. He cannot be weighed or measured; he transcends all measurement. He is neither large nor small. He is neither seen, nor heard, nor sensed, nor known. He is free from all fetters. He is equal to the highest wisdom and equal to all living beings. In all things, he makes no distinctions. He has no loss, no turbidity, no pride. He has no action, no arising, no birth, and no extinction. He has no fear, no sorrow, no joy, no loathing, and no attachment. 

He had no past, he will have no future, and he has no present. One cannot use any speech or distinctions to display him. **World-Honored One**, the body of the Tathagata is such as this, and to view him in this way is called 'Right Viewing.' To view him otherwise is called 'False Viewing.'"

At that time, **Shariputra** asked Vimalakirti: "Where did you pass away to be born here in this land?"

Shariputra replied: "There is no passing away or being born."

Vimalakirti asked: "If all things have no nature of passing away or being born, why do you ask, 'Where did you pass away to be born here?' Shariputra replied: "They do not."

Vimalakirti asked: "Have you not heard the Buddha say that all things are like an illusion?"

Shariputra replied: "I have."

Vimalakirti said: "If all things are like an illusion, why ask where I passed away to be born here? Shariputra, 'passing away' is a mark of the destruction of a deceptive phenomenon. 'Birth' is a mark of the continuation of a deceptive phenomenon. Although a Bodhisattva 'passes away,' they do not exhaust their roots of goodness; although they are 'born,' they do not grow in evil."

Then the Buddha told Shariputra: "There is a land called **Abhirati** <note>original: "妙喜"; meaning 'Exceeding Joy'</note> and a Buddha named **Akshobhya** <note>original: "元動" or "阿閦"; meaning 'The Immovable'</note>. It was from that land that Vimalakirti passed away to be born here."

Shariputra exclaimed: "This is unprecedented! World-Honored One, that this person could leave such a pure land to come and take delight in this place of so much anger and harm!"

Shariputra replied: "No. When the sun rises, the darkness vanishes."

Shariputra replied: "To use its light to illuminate and remove the darkness."

Vimalakirti said: "A Bodhisattva is like this. Although they are born in an impure Buddha-land, it is for the sake of transforming living beings. They do not join with the darkness of ignorance; they only extinguish the darkness of the afflictions of living beings."

Why? Because in future ages, there will be good men and women, as well as gods, dragons, spirits, and celestial musicians, who will seek this supreme enlightenment and take joy in the Great Dharma. If they do not hear such sutras, they will lose a great benefit. When such people hear these teachings, they will surely believe them with joy and find them rare and precious. You should receive these teachings and spread them widely for the benefit of all living beings."

"Maitreya, you should know that there are two types of Bodhisattvas. What are these two? First, those who take pleasure in superficial words and decorative phrases. Second, those who do not fear profound meanings and are able to enter into reality. If a practitioner delights in decorative words and flowery speech, know that they are a **beginner**<note>original: "新學"; literally 'new learner,' a novice on the path</note>. But if they encounter these profound, unattached, and deep scriptures without fear, and can enter into them, hear them with a pure heart, uphold, read, and practice them as taught, know that they have long cultivated the Way."

"Maitreya, there are two further reasons why beginners cannot remain firm in the profound Dharma. What are these two? First, when they hear deep sutras they have never heard before, they become terrified, develop doubts, and instead of following the teaching, they slander it, saying, 'I have never heard this; from where did it come?' Second, if there are people who protect, explain, and uphold such deep sutras, these beginners refuse to associate with them, honor them, or respect them, and sometimes even speak of their faults. Because of these two things, the beginner Bodhisattva harms themselves and cannot discipline their mind within the profound Dharma."

"Maitreya, there are two further ways a Bodhisattva—even if they believe and understand the profound Dharma—may still harm themselves and fail to attain the **Realization of the Non-Arising of All Things**<term>Anutpattika-dharma-kshanti: a high level of realization where one recognizes that nothing is truly born or destroyed</term>. What are these two? First, looking down upon beginner Bodhisattvas and failing to teach or guide them. Second, although they understand the profound Dharma, they still cling to appearances and make intellectual distinctions. These are the two ways."

When Maitreya Bodhisattva heard this, he said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, this is unprecedented. I shall follow what the Buddha has said and distance myself from such evils. I will uphold the Dharma of supreme enlightenment gathered by the **Tathagata**<note>A title for the Buddha meaning 'One who has thus come'</note> over countless eons. If in future ages there are good men and women seeking the **Great Vehicle**<term>Mahayana: the 'Greater Vehicle' path focused on the liberation of all beings</term>, I will ensure these sutras come into their hands, and through the power of mindfulness, I will enable them to receive, read, recite, and explain them to others. World-Honored One, in the final age, if there are those who can uphold, read, and explain this to others, it is through the spiritual power of Maitreya that they are established."

The Buddha said: "Excellent, excellent, Maitreya! It is as you say. I share in your joy."

Then all the Bodhisattvas in the assembly joined their palms and said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, after the Buddha’s passing, we too shall spread this teaching throughout the lands of the ten directions. We will guide those who explain the Dharma so they may obtain this sutra."

At that moment, the **Four Heavenly Kings**<note>The guardians of the four cardinal directions</note> said to the Buddha: "World-Honored One, in any place—whether city, village, mountain forest, or wilderness—where this sutra is read, recited, or explained, we will lead our followers to that place to listen to the Dharma. We will protect those people so that no one within a hundred **yojanas**<note>An ancient unit of distance, roughly 7 to 9 miles</note> can take advantage of them or find a way to harm them."

The Buddha then said to **Ananda**<note>The Buddha's personal attendant, famous for his perfect memory</note>: "Uphold this scripture and spread it widely."

Ananda replied: "I have already accepted and upheld the essentials of this teaching. World-Honored One, what should we call this scripture?"

The Buddha said: "Ananda, this sutra is called **The Teaching of Vimalakirti**<note>original: "維摩詰所說"; Vimalakīrti-nirdeśa</note>, and also **The Inconceivable Portal of Liberation**<note>original: "不可思議解脱法門"</note>. You should uphold it as such."

When the Buddha had finished speaking this sutra, the Elder Vimalakirti, **Manjushri**<note>The Bodhisattva of Wisdom</note>, **Shariputra**<note>One of the Buddha's chief disciples, known for his wisdom</note>, Ananda, and the entire assembly of gods, humans, and **asuras**<note>Demigods or titans</note> rejoiced greatly at the Buddha's words. They bowed in respect and departed.
