# FOREWORD

---

In examining this reproduction of the **SECRETS OF ALBERTUS MAGNUS** <note>original: "Secrets du Grand Albert"; a famous medieval grimoire of folk magic and medicine attributed to the 13th-century scholar Albert the Great</note>, many readers will undoubtedly be surprised by the universal popularity achieved during the Middle Ages by this curious collection of formulas, which has since been translated into every language and reprinted so many times.

However, this prodigious success is easily explained if one observes that not even a hundred years ago—at the very moment when the medical arts were reorganized by the French Revolution—the empirical Codex <note>a reference to traditional, experience-based medical knowledge rather than modern clinical science</note> was still the only one in effect.

> "If the Royal Society of Medicine," says **Jean Frollo** <margin>See the *Petit Parisien* (January 1895).</margin>, "and the Academy of Surgery, which represented the new spirit at the end of the eighteenth century, were advancing Science..."

The *Recipes of the Great Albert* <note>Referring to Albertus Magnus (c. 1200–1280), a medieval scholar to whom many books of magic and folk medicine were attributed.</note>, which were generally adopted in former times just like those mentioned above, offer to Enthusiasts the interesting study of the panaceas <note>Universal remedies or "cure-alls."</note> advocated by the ancient disciples of Asclepius, Aristotle, Galen, Hippocrates, etc. <note>These names represent the foundational figures of Greek and Roman medicine.</note>

Before laughing at them or accusing them of imposture, we advise our Sceptics to make a loyal trial of these Secrets <note>In this context, "Secrets" refers to hidden or occult medicinal formulas.</note>, so as not to criticize or reject anything without proof.

Who knows if these experiments do not reserve for them marvelous revelations, whose pleasant surprises will repay their attempts.
