# TO THE KIND READER
->The Printer sends his fondest greetings.<-

*It is not for nothing that we little humans <note>original: "Homuncionibus"</note>, who live in this world surrounded on all sides like worms in cheese, engage in such great disputes concerning its construction—specifically, whether this seat and home of ours which we call the <term>Earth</term> runs around the <term>Sun</term> on high along with other globes of its kind, or whether the Sun runs around the Earth. We are indeed such tiny creatures that, like a mouse in a ship, we know almost nothing of these matters; just as if a mouse were to look out upon a calm sea to debate the point, he could never determine whether the ship—their common home—is moving or remains fixed in one and the same place. These matters are therefore judged by many to be of deeper mystery than they might seem to the common people, especially after so many new things in the celestial globes were discovered through that optical instrument which the <term>Lyncean Philosophers</term> <note>The Academy of the Lynxes, a Roman scientific society founded in 1603, famously including Galileo Galilei, known for their "lynx-like" sharp vision in science.</note> call a <term>Telescope</term>. However, although certain presumptuous men—who wish to be addressed as "philosophers" by the public—frequently witness these discoveries, their willful ignorance <note>original: "voluntarius stupor"</note> should not be valued so highly that it turns everyone else away from a more diligent investigation of the truth. Indeed, many <term>Theologians</term> <note>scholars of religious doctrine</note> from both sides of the debate strive to weigh down this inquiry with the unyielding authority of the Holy Scriptures. Whether this is done rightly or wrongly is something that a lover of truth must discern above all else...*

# DEFENSE FOR

## Preface.

**H**aving long since completed two inquiries, necessary at this time: namely, whether it is permitted to forge a new philosophy, and whether it is permitted and expedient to diminish the <term>Peripatetic sect</term> <note>The followers of Aristotle’s philosophy, which dominated medieval and Renaissance universities.</note> and the authority of the pagan philosophers, and to introduce in their place a new philosophy according to the doctrine of the saints into Christian schools; now I am invited to another specific controversy by those who detest the method of philosophizing championed by <term>Galileo the Florentine</term> <note>Galileo Galilei (1564–1642). Scholars often identified people by their home city; Galileo was from Florence.</note>, for the reason that it seems to establish dogmas contrary to the Sacred Scriptures. I shall oblige, as far as my circumstances permit.

### THEREFORE, IT IS ASKED

->*Whether the method of philosophizing that Galileo celebrates favors the Sacred Scriptures, or truly opposes them.*<-

**I** shall conclude the whole matter in five chapters. And first, indeed, I will bring forward arguments attacking Galileo. Second, I will submit reasons defending him. Third, I will establish certain <term>hypotheses</term> <note>original: "hypotheses." In this context, foundational principles or premises upon which the later judgment will be based.</note> for the two-part decision that follows. Fourth, I will respond to the arguments attacking Galileo. Fifth, I will state what weight should be given to the reasons defending him.

->(∵)<-

# CHAPTER I.

## *The Argument against Galileo.*

**F**irst, it is argued against Galileo: that theological dogmas seem to be entirely overturned by him, as he strives to introduce novelties against the <term>physiology</term> <note>In this context, the study of natural philosophy or the physical workings of the universe.</note> and metaphysics of Aristotle, upon which theological doctrine is founded by Saint <term>Thomas</term> <note>Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), whose synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy and Christian theology became the standard for the Catholic Church.</note> and all the <term>Scholastics</term> <note>The medieval university scholars who used logic and Aristotelian philosophy to explain Christian doctrine.</note>.

2. Furthermore, he promulgates opinions that contradict all the <term>Church Fathers</term> <note>Early influential Christian theologians whose writings established foundational doctrines.</note> and the Scholastics. For he teaches that the earth moves and exists outside the center of the world, while the sun and the starry sphere stand still. However, the Fathers, the Scholastics, and the very senses attest to the contrary dogma.

3. Furthermore, he clearly contradicts Sacred Scripture. For it is said in Psalm 92 [93]: *He established the world, which shall not be moved.* <note>original: "Firmauit orbem terræ, qui non commouebitur."</note> And in Psalm 103 [104]: *Who established the earth upon its stability: it shall not be tilted forever and ever.* <note>original: "Qui fundaſti terram ſuper ſtabilitatem ſuam: non inclinabitur in ſeculum ſeculi."</note> And Solomon in Ecclesiastes 1: *But the earth stands forever.* <note>original: "Terra autem in æternum ſtat."</note>

4. Furthermore, the same is clear regarding the motion of the sun. For it is said in Ecclesiastes, in the same place: *The earth stands forever, the sun rises and sets, and returns to its place: and being reborn there, it circles through the south, and turns toward the north: the spirit goes about surveying all things in a circuit, and returns into its circles.* <note>original: "Terra in æternum ſtat, oritur ſol & occidit, & ad locum ſuum reuertitur: ibique renaſcens, gyrat per meridiem, & flectitur ad aquilonem: luſtrans uniuerſa in circuitu pergit ſpiritus, & in circulos ſuos reuertitur."</note>

5. Furthermore, in Joshua 10, it is presented as a most astounding miracle that Joshua halted the motion of the sun with a prayer. *Sun,* he said, *do not move against Gibeon, and moon against the valley of Ajalon. And the sun stood in the midst of heaven, and did not hasten to set for the space of one day.* <note>original: "Sol, ait, contra Gabaon ne mouearis, & luna contra vallem Aialon. Stetitque ſol in medio cæli, & non feſtinauit occumbere ſpacio unius diei."</note> The same is repeated in <term>Ecclesiasticus</term> <note>Also known as the Book of Sirach.</note> chapter 46.

6. Furthermore, in Isaiah 38, as a sign of recovered health, God gives Hezekiah a portent in the sundial of Ahaz. *And the sun returned ten lines by the degrees which it had gone down.* <note>original: "Et reuerſus eſt ſol decem lineis per gradus quos deſcenderat."</note> Concerning this portent, Hezekiah is questioned by the King of the Chaldeans, who, since he devoted himself to astronomy, noticed this reversal of the sun, as can be recognized from 2 Chronicles 32. Therefore, if God did not truly inhibit the motion of the sun, the miracle is not true. Consequently, the Scripture—which narrates these two events as true miracles—would be false.

7. Furthermore, we seem to marvel at the motion of the starry sky in Sacred Scripture. For it is said in the song of Deborah, Judges 5: *The stars remaining in their order and course fought against Sisera.* <note>original: "Stellæ manentes in ordine & curſu ſuo aduerſus Siſaram pugnauerunt."</note> Therefore, the stars run. Therefore, also the sky [runs]: in which they are fixed, just as knots are in a board. <note>original: "ſicut modus in tabula." This was a common medieval analogy describing stars as being physically embedded in a solid celestial sphere.</note> Likewise, Jude the apostle [calls them] wandering stars—

# CHAPTER II.

## *Arguments in favor of Galileo.*

**But** on the other hand, arguments are raised in favor of Galileo based on the authority of theologians who decreed that the books of <term>Nicolaus Copernicus</term> <note>The Polish astronomer (1473–1543) who first formulated a comprehensive heliocentric cosmology, displacing the Earth from the center of the universe.</note> regarding the revolutions of the celestial spheres—based on his observations made from the year 1525—should be committed to print, on the grounds that they contained nothing contrary to the Catholic faith. In those books, the motion of the earth and the fixed nature of the firmament (that is, the starry heaven) are discussed, as well as the sun’s residence in the center of our world. Nor does Galileo reveal anything fundamentally new beyond these other systems which were previously unknown. Therefore, if the books of Copernicus do not inconvenience the Catholic faith, neither will Galileo.

2. Likewise, Pope Paul III <note>born Alessandro Farnese, Pope from 1534 to 1549.</note>, to whom Copernicus dedicated those books, and certain Cardinals (who, before the books were even published, busied themselves with having them transcribed at their own expense, as appears in the introductory letters), approved these same books. In the time of Paul III, the most brilliant minds flourished in the Church; for that Pope, most noble in spirit, virtue, and blood, decorated men summoned from everywhere with high dignities. Thus, it would be a wonder if those men were "moles" <note>A metaphor for being blind to the truth or unable to see the light.</note> regarding Copernicus, while our contemporaries, who are not of such great name, should be far more "sharp-eyed like Argus" <note>In Greek mythology, Argus Panoptes was a giant with a hundred eyes; the author suggests modern critics are being overly scrutinizing compared to the wise leaders of the past.</note> against Galileo, who relies on even more certain observations.

But **St. Thomas** <note>St. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274), the most influential Catholic theologian of the Middle Ages.</note> rightly proves that to posit many worlds without an order toward a single unity—as **Democritus** and **Epicurus** <note>Ancient Greek "atomists" who believed the universe was made of infinite atoms forming infinite worlds by pure chance.</note> did—is an error in faith. For from this it follows that worlds are made by chance, as they claim, without God as an orderer. But to posit several small systems within one maximum [universe], all ordered toward God, is by no means against Scripture, but only against **Aristotle**. 

For even the reasoning of **St. Thomas**—that it is impossible for there to be another earth in other worlds besides this one, because those earths would travel toward this one and leave their own places <note>Aristotle's physics claimed there was only one "center" to the universe; therefore, all "earthy" matter must seek that one center. Campanella is refuting this by suggesting gravity is local to each body.</note>—is taken from **Aristotle’s** first book *On the Heavens*, and it has no value. For my heart does not go to the place of your heart. All things are stabilized in their own center and are preserved and rejoice in the likeness of their own parts. Lunar things strive toward the center of the moon; Mercurial things strive toward the center of Mercury. Outside their own circle, they do not "smell" <note>Campanella uses "smell" (*olfaciunt*) metaphorically to mean the natural inclination or "perception" of a physical body toward its proper place.</note> anything better for themselves. And why, if the stars are of the same nature according to the <term>Peripatetics</term> <note>Followers of Aristotle’s philosophy.</note>, do they not move toward one another, and the parts of this one toward the parts of that one?

Furthermore, the **University of Paris**, among the articles worthy of correction in **St. Thomas**, lists this one: namely, that there cannot be another earth. For in this way, the power of God is limited. They say that in theology, the discussion is not about natural power, but about divine power. But certainly, **St. Thomas** did not understand this (as is charged against him) as a limit on the power of God—even if he did not explain himself as fully as he ought—since elsewhere he states the contrary: namely, that God could make many worlds and earths. Rather, he spoke from ordinary Aristotelian philosophy, as **Cajetan** <note>Thomas Cajetan (1469–1534), a prominent cardinal and commentator on Aquinas.</note> also notes. Indeed, in [his commentary on] the first book *On the Heavens*, where **Aristotle** examines this, **St. Thomas** teaches that this dogma is by no means against the faith, but only against **Aristotle**. 

That other point regarding multiple species of humans, and that **Christ** died elsewhere, does not follow from **Galileo’s** words; nor is that conclusion valid against others. For it is not true that He died in the other hemisphere—the existence of which was denied by many theologians, even by **Augustine**, because of this argument—contrary to what experience shows today <note>A reference to the "Antipodes." Many early theologians thought people could not live on the other side of the Earth because they would be cut off from the preaching of the Gospel; the discovery of the Americas proved this theological assumption wrong.</note>. Nor would humans, if there were any on other stars, be infected by the sin of **Adam** (from whom they do not originate) so as to need redemption, unless they labored under some other sin. In that case, we would be forced to explain those passages in **Ephesians 1** and **Colossians 1**: *reconciling in his blood, whether those things in the heavens, or those things on the earth, etc.* <note>Campanella is grappling with the "alien" problem: if other worlds have people, did Christ save them too? He suggests they might have their own spiritual history separate from Adam.</note> But we are ignorant of these things; therefore, we stand by the ancient interpretation of the Fathers. <summary>Campanella refutes the Aristotelian idea that only one Earth can exist, arguing that gravity is a local force drawing objects to their own planetary centers. He addresses the theological "plurality of worlds" debate, noting that God's power cannot be limited by Greek philosophy and comparing the doubt about other worlds to the once-discredited doubt about the American Antipodes.</summary>
