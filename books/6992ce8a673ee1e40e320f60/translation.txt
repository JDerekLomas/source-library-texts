# Longan
<term>Longan: *Dimocarpus longan*</term>

The Longan grows in the mountain valleys of the South Seas <note>original: "南海" (Nánhǎi); referring generally to the coastal regions of Southern China and Southeast Asia</note>. The tree reaches a height of about ten feet. It resembles the Lychee tree, though its leaves are slightly smaller and do not wither even in the depths of winter. 

In late spring and early summer, the tree produces tiny white flowers; by the seventh lunar month, the fruit matures. The shell is a yellowish-green color, patterned with a texture like scale-armor, and the fruit is as round as a pellet. Its pit is similar to that of the Soapnut tree <note>original: "無患" (wúhuàn); *Sapindus mukorossi*</note> but is not as hard. The flesh is white and juicy, possessing an exceptional sweetness. 

The tree is incredibly prolific. Because the Longan ripens just as the Lychee season concludes, people of the South refer to it as the **"Slave of the Lychee."** 

It is also known by the name **"Benefit of Wisdom"** <note>original: "益智" (yìzhì)</note>. Its flavor is sweet and its medicinal nature is neutral. It is primarily used to treat "evil qi" <note>original: "邪氣" (xiéqì); a traditional term for harmful influences or pathogens</note> within the Five Organs <term>Five Organs: The Heart, Liver, Spleen, Lungs, and Kidneys</term>. It settles the mind, addresses a lack of appetite, eliminates internal parasites, and neutralizes toxins. If consumed over a long period, it is said to strengthen the soul, enhance intelligence, and make the body feel light and youthful.

# Walnut
<term>Walnut: *Juglans regia*; known in Chinese as *Hútáo*</term>

The Walnut tree originally came from the Western Regions <note>original: 西域 (Xīyù); a historical term for Central Asia and the lands reached via the Silk Road</note>. Tradition holds that the Han dynasty envoy Zhang Qian <note>A famous explorer and diplomat who opened the Silk Road (c. 164–114 BCE)</note> brought the seeds back to China, which is why it is called the **"Barbarian Peach"** <note>original: 胡桃 (Hútáo); 'Hu' was a term used for Central or Western Asian peoples, and 'Tao' means peach</note>.

The tree is substantial and tall. Its leaves are long and grow in pairs. During the third month of the lunar calendar, it produces flowers that resemble those of the chestnut tree. The fruit is protected by a green, fleshy outer husk; inside is a hard, woody shell that protects the edible kernel.

The kernel is divided into four lobes and is covered by a thin, slightly bitter skin. The meat itself is white, oily, and possesses a rich, savory flavor.

In traditional practice, the Walnut is considered sweet and warm in nature. It is valued for its ability to tonify the kidneys and moisten the lungs. It is frequently prescribed to treat chronic coughs, asthma, and lower back pain. Because the appearance of the kernel resembles the human brain, it has long been associated with nourishing the mind and enhancing memory.

# Walnut

The Walnut <term>also known as the **Kernel Peach**; original: 核桃 (Hétáo)</term> grows into a massive tree with thick foliage that provides deep shade. It produces flowers in spikes of a pale yellow color. The fruit is encased in a green outer husk, resembling a pear in size. When the fruit is ripe, the green husk is soaked away to retrieve the inner pit, which is the walnut.

The flavor is sweet, and its nature is balanced and non-toxic. Consuming it promotes a robust and healthy physique, moistens the skin, and keeps the hair dark. It is used to treat the **five types of hemorrhoids** <note>original: 五痔 (wǔzhì); a collective term in traditional Chinese medicine for various rectal ailments</note>. If eaten in large quantities, it facilitates urination, though it is said it can cause the loss of eyebrows <note>A traditional cautionary note found in historical herbals regarding the side effects of excessive consumption</note>. The bark of the tree is used to stop **watery diarrhea** <note>original: 水痢 (shuǐlì); a term for acute, thin stools</note>.

## Lychee

The fruit of the lychee grows in Lingnan, Fujian, and Sichuan <note>original: 嶺南 (Lǐngnán), 閩 (Mǐn), and 蜀 (Shǔ). These represent the tropical and subtropical regions of Southern China</note>. Regarding their quality, those from Fujian are ranked as the finest; those from Sichuan are next; and those from Lingnan are considered the lowest.

The tree reaches a height of twenty to thirty feet <note>Approximately 6 to 9 meters</note>. Its leaves are a deep green and provide thick shade; they flourish through all four seasons without withering. The flowers are a pale bluish-white. The fruit is shaped like a pine flower when it first emerges. Its shell is textured like patterned silk <term>luówén (羅文); a type of fine, whorled or gauze-like texture often found on ancient fabrics or stationary</term>.

The fruit is green at first, gradually turning red. The flesh is a translucent white, appearing like solidified fat or fine jade <note>original: 肪玉 (fángyù). In classical Chinese descriptions, "fat" (fáng) refers to a lustrous, creamy white texture, a high compliment for both jade and fruit</note>. Its flavor is sweet and very juicy; it quenches thirst and improves the complexion.

## The Pear

Pears are found in every region; their varieties are exceedingly numerous <note>original: 處處有之。其種甚多 (chùchù yǒu zhī; qí zhǒng shèn duō). The text emphasizes the pear's ubiquity compared to the more regional lychee.</note>. Among the many types available, those harvested from Xuanhu Mountain <note>A site historically famous for its high-quality fruit, likely referring to a location in the northern or northwestern frontier</note> are considered the most superior. The fruit appears in several colors, including green, white, yellow, and purple.

Regarding their flavor, they are sweet with a subtle hint of acidity. In nature, the pear is considered "cool" <term>liáng (涼); in Traditional Chinese Medicine, "cooling" foods are used to balance internal "heat" (inflammation or fever) and restore the body's equilibrium</term>. The texture of the flesh varies—some varieties are crisp, others are soft, and some are slightly astringent—with each type having its own appropriate use in diet or medicine.

Medical practitioners state that the pear is highly effective at moistening the lungs and cooling the heart. It is used to dissolve phlegm, descend internal "fire" <note>This refers to clearing excess heat or inflammation from the body's systems</note>, and neutralize toxins, particularly those resulting from alcohol or seasonal illnesses.

# The Pear

## Characteristics and General Use

The leaf of the pear tree resembles that of the wild pear <note>original: 棠葉 (táng yè). The text refers to the leaf of the *Malus baccata* or *Pyrus betulaefolia*</note> but is larger and deep green in color. It produces blossoms that are white. The flavor of the fruit is sweet with a hint of acidity; its nature is "cold" <term>hán (寒); in Traditional Chinese Medicine, "cold" foods are used to clear internal heat and reduce inflammation</term> and it is entirely non-toxic.

The pear is primarily used to treat coughs caused by "heat" and to quench thirst. Its leaves are also used medicinally to treat "sudden turmoil" <note>original: 霍亂 (huòluàn). This historically refers to acute gastrointestinal distress involving simultaneous vomiting and diarrhea, often translated as cholera-like symptoms</note> that will not stop.

## Specific Varieties

There are several distinct varieties of this fruit:

**The Melting Pear**
<note>original: 消梨 (xiāolí). Literally "dispersing" or "melting" pear, known for its high juice content and ability to dissolve phlegm.</note>
The flavor is sweet and its nature is cold. It is primarily used to treat "guest heat" <note>fevers or inflammation caused by external environmental factors</note> and instances where a patient is unable to speak following a stroke. It is also effective in treating the fevers associated with "Cold Damage" <term>shānghán (傷寒); a broad category of acute febrile illnesses including typhoid</term> and helps to facilitate both bowel movements and urination.

**Other Varieties**
There are also the **Green Pear**, the **Sprout Pear**, and the **Mulberry Pear**. Among these, the Mulberry Pear is only palatable when boiled in honey; in this form, it is primarily used to treat a dry mouth.

## The Bush Cherry
<note>original: 郁李 (yù lǐ). Commonly known as the Chinese bush cherry or *Prunus japonica*.</note>

The Bush Cherry grows in high mountains, river valleys, and upon hills. The tree reaches a height of five or six feet. Its branches, twigs, leaves, and flowers all resemble those of the plum tree <note>original: 李 (lǐ); the common plum or *Prunus salicina*</note>, though the fruit alone is small, resembling a cherry. It is red in color with a flavor that is both sweet and sour. The pit ripens at the same time as the fruit.

In the sixth month <note>roughly July in the Gregorian calendar</note>, the roots and the fruit are gathered. The kernels are then extracted from the pits for medicinal use.

In the *Commentary on the Materia Medica* by Lu Ji <note>original: 陸機 (Lù Jī), a prominent 3rd-century scholar. His *Bencao Shu* is a standard reference for identifying plants mentioned in classical poetry</note>, it is stated that the *Tangdi* is in fact the *Yuli* <note>original: 唐棣 (tángdì). This is a classical name used in the *Book of Odes*</note>. It is also known by the name "Sparrow Plum" <note>original: 雀梅 (quèméi)</note>, and is sometimes called the "Plum Under the Cart" <note>original: 車下李 (chē xià lǐ)</note>. Other names include the "Nobleman’s Plum" <note>original: 爵李 (jué lǐ)</note> and the "Tied Plum" <note>original: 棣李 (dì lǐ)</note>.

**Medicinal Properties:**
The kernels have a flavor that is sour and "neutral" <term>píng (平); in Traditional Chinese Medicine, a neutral property means the substance does not significantly alter the body's hot/cold balance</term>. They are primarily used to treat abdominal edema <note>original: 大腹水腫 (dà fù shuǐ zhǒng); a buildup of fluid in the abdomen</note> and to assist the urinary tract and water passages.

The roots are primarily used to treat swelling of the gums and tooth decay <note>original: 齲齒 (qǔchǐ)</note>. They serve to strengthen the teeth and eliminate "white worms" <note>original: 白蟲 (báichóng); historically, this could refer to intestinal parasites or the "dental worms" once believed to cause cavities</note>.

# The Plum Blossom

The plum possesses the Four Virtues: the emergence of the first leaves is "Origin" (*Yuan*); the blossoming is "Prosperity" (*Heng*); the forming of the fruit is "Benefit" (*Li*); and the ripening is "Purity" (*Zhen*). <note>These "Four Virtues" refer to the four fundamental stages of the *I Ching* (Book of Changes), mapping the life cycle of the plum onto the cosmic order of the universe.</note>

There are four qualities prized in the plum: it is valued for being sparse rather than dense; for being old and weathered rather than young and tender; for being slender and lean rather than thick and fleshy; and for being in bud rather than fully bloomed.

*   **Powder Pink Plum** <gloss>Ten-petaled variety</gloss>

# Apricot

The apricot is said to be the essence of the Year Star <note>the planet Jupiter</note> of the East. The *Records of Strange Matters* notes that "Winter Apricots" grow in the marshes. On Wugong Mountain, there are two apricot trees, each over ten *zhang* <note>approximately 33 meters</note> tall. The tree to the east flowers while the tree to the west bears fruit; the following year, they switch, with the eastern tree fruiting and the western tree flowering. In Jinan Commandery, there are **Gold Apricots**; they grow more abundantly than pears and are more yellow than oranges, possessing a flavor of peerless beauty. When Emperor Wu of the Han dynasty visited the mythical lands of Peng and Ying <note>Penglai and Yingzhou</note>, someone presented these to him. The Emperor found them so excellent that people today still call them "Emperor Han's Fruit."

The kernels <note>the seeds inside the apricot pit</note> are sweet yet bitter in flavor. Their nature is categorized as both warming and cold, and they can be sharp and toxic. They are primarily used to treat coughing, "reversing *qi*" <note>shortness of breath or wheezing</note>, thunderous rumbling in the digestive tract, and "throat closure" <note>severe sore throat or swelling</note>. Furthermore, they address "cold heart" <note>chills or anxiety</note>, <term>Running Piglet (Bentun)<note>A traditional medical syndrome where the patient feels a sensation of gas rushing from the abdomen up to the throat, often associated with intense anxiety or palpitations.</note></term>, and epilepsy. They relieve distress and <gloss>heat and wind-borne pathogens that frequently arrive during seasonal epidemics</gloss> beneath the heart, as well as headaches. They are effective for relaxing the muscles and dispelling "vile pain" in the chest and abdomen.

# The Pomegranate

The pomegranate’s original name was *Ruoliu* <note>original: 若榴 (ruòliú)</note>. It originally came from the country of Anxi <note>The Arsacid Empire or ancient Parthia, located in modern-day Iran; "Anshi" is the Chinese phonetic rendering</note>, which is why it is called the **"Anshi Pomegranate."** It is also known as "Cinnabar Ruo."

One variety with multi-petaled, deep red flowers that bears fruit is called the **"Treasure Pearl."** The single-petaled variety is known as the **"Fire Pomegranate."** It is a prolific bloomer, and there are also multi-petaled versions of this type. 

There is a variety with white flowers called the **"White Pomegranate,"** one with yellow flowers called the **"Yellow Pomegranate,"** and one with blue-tinged flowers called the **"Green Pomegranate."**

The **"Heyin Pomegranate"** is nicknamed "Thirty-Eight" because each fruit contains exactly thirty-eight seeds.

In the Kingdom of Liuchou, there is the **"Golden Thorn Pomegranate."** Its wood grain is patterned like beautiful brocade and is very fragrant.

Duan Chengshi <note>A Tang Dynasty scholar (d. 863 AD) famous for his encyclopedic writings on the exotic and the mundane</note> said: The **"Sweet Pomegranate of Baima"** <note>White Horse</note> has a fruit as large as an ox’s ear and is called **"Celestial Nectar."**

In Beijing, there is the **"Stalked Pomegranate,"** whose flowers are large and red, and its seeds are large and of excellent quality. There is also a multi-petaled **"Pink Pomegranate"** which blooms twice: once in early summer and again in mid-autumn.

The flavor of the fruit is sweet and sour, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat dryness in the throat and thirst. The rind is used to treat dysentery and to stop the involuntary loss of essence <term>Nocturnal emission <gloss>The leakage of reproductive fluids during sleep, often viewed in traditional medicine as a sign of constitutional weakness</gloss></term>. The roots that grow toward the east are used to treat roundworms, pinworms, and tapeworms <term>Cestoda <gloss>A type of parasitic flatworm; the text uses "inch-white" to describe their appearance</gloss></term>.

# The Tong Tree

There are four varieties of the **Tong tree** <note>original: 桐 (tóng). While "Tong" covers several species, it primarily refers to the Paulownia and the Chinese Parasol Tree.</note>. The **Green Tong** <note>original: 青桐 (qīngtóng); likely the Chinese Parasol Tree, *Sterculia platanifolia*</note> has branches and leaves that are both green and it bears no seeds. The **Wutong** <note>original: 梧桐 (wútóng); the Chinese Parasol Tree</note> has white bark and green leaves and bears seeds. These seeds are plump, tasty, and edible. The **White Tong** <note>original: 白桐 (báitóng); likely *Paulownia tomentosa*</note> has flowers and seeds; its flowers bloom in the second month of the lunar calendar and are a yellowish-purple color. It is also known as the **Yellow Tong**. The **Gang-tong** <note>original: 崗 (gǎng); likely a mountain variety or a species of *Catalpa*</note> resembles the White Tong but lacks seeds.

In the *Commentary on the Flora* by Lu Ji <note>original: 陸機 (Lù Jī), a famous 3rd-century scholar. His *Cao Mu Shu* is a standard reference for identifying plants mentioned in ancient Chinese poetry.</note>, it is said that the **White Tong** is suitable for making *qin* and *se* <note>original: 琴瑟 (qín sè); these are traditional plucked zithers, highly valued in scholarly culture for their refined sound</note>. The people of Zangke <note>original: 牂牁 (Zāngkē); an ancient administrative region in what is now Guizhou province</note> process the fibers of this tree to weave into cloth.

The **Wutong** tree is said to be sensitive to the sun, the moon, and the calendar, including intercalary months. It normally grows twelve leaves <note>one for each month of the year</note>, with six leaves on each side of the branch. If there is an intercalary month <note>a "leap month" in the traditional lunar calendar</note>, it grows a thirteenth leaf.

In the regions south of the Yangtze River, there is the **Red Tong** <note>original: 赬桐 (chēngtóng); likely *Clerodendrum japonicum*</note>, which blooms in autumn with red flowers but bears no fruit. There is also the **Purple Tong**, whose flowers resemble lilies and whose fruit can be boiled with sugar and eaten.

In the regions south of the mountain ranges <note>original: 嶺南 (Lǐngnán); modern-day Guangdong and Guangxi</note>, there is the **Thorny Tong** <note>original: 刺桐 (cìtóng); the Indian Coral Tree, *Erythrina variegata*</note>. Its branches and trunk are covered in thorns, and its flowers are a deep red. In Quanzhou <note>a major port city in Fujian</note>, the **Thorny Tong** flowers in early summer and are a brilliant, vibrant red. It is said that if the leaves sprout first and the flowers bloom later, the "five grains" <note>the staple food crops</note> will yield a great harvest.

# The Lotus

The lotus grows in the midst of water. Its leaves are named **he** <note>original: 荷 (hé)</note>, its stems are called **qie** <note>original: 茄 (qié)</note>, and its leaf stalks are called **jia** <note>original: 葭 (jiā)</note>. Its slender root-runners are known as **mi** <note>original: 蔤 (mì)</note>. 

Before the flower has blossomed, it is called **handan** <note>original: 菡萏 (hàndàn)</note>; once it has opened, it is called **fuqu** <note>original: 芙蕖 (fúqú)</note>. Its fruit is called the **lian** <note>original: 蓮 (lián)</note>, which refers to the seed pod. Its thick, edible root is the **ou** <note>original: 藕 (ǒu); commonly known in English as "lotus root"</note>. 

Within the pod are the lotus seeds, which consist of a green outer skin and a white inner nut. Inside the seed is a green sprout, approximately one or two-tenths of an inch long, called the **yi** <note>original: 薏 (yì); the lotus plumule or embryo</note>; the center of this sprout is bitter.

The flavor of the seeds is sweet and balanced. They are primarily used to fortify the center <note>the digestive system and internal organs</note>, nourish the spirit, and enhance vital energy and physical strength. They also serve to expel the "hundred evils" <note>a traditional term for various pathogens, ailments, or malevolent environmental influences</note>. Another name for this plant is the **Water Reishi Elixir** <note>original: 水芝丹 (shuǐ zhī dān)</note>.

They facilitate the passage of water and grains through the body and direct energy downward, effectively stopping vomiting and coughing.

They are also used to clear retained heat and stagnant fluids in the bladder, treating the "five types of urinary dysfunction" <term>wu lin|a category of traditional ailments involving painful, sandy, or frequent urination</term> and promoting healthy urination. Additionally, they are prescribed when the spleen is unable to digest food and when energy rushes toward the stomach, helping to calm vomiting and "sudden turmoil" disorders <term>huo luan|a historical term for acute gastrointestinal distress involving simultaneous vomiting and diarrhea</term>. Finally, they are used to stop diarrhea and expel tapeworms <note>original: 寸白 (cùn bái); literally "inch-white" parasites</note>.

# Jujube

The **Jujube** <note>original: 棗 (zao)</note> grows in the regions East of the River <note>Hedong; modern-day Shanxi province</note> and nearby northern prefectures. Those produced in the regions South of the River <note>Jiangnan</note> are firm and dry with very little succulence. There are many varieties of jujube:

*   Those that are large and pointed at the top are called **Pot Jujubes** <note>original: 壺棗 (huzao)</note>.
*   Those shaped like a large pot but with a narrow middle are called **Pot-waist Jujubes**.
*   Those that turn white when ripe are called **Hand-held White Jujubes**.
*   Small fruits with a sour taste are called **Hand-held Jujubes**.
*   Small fruits that are purple-black in color are called **Sheep-following Jujubes**, though they are commonly referred to as **Sheep Dropping Jujubes** due to their appearance.
*   There is also the **Xi Large Jujube**, which is as large as a chicken egg.
*   The variety known as the **Bitter Jujube** has a bitter taste and lacks a stone <note>seed</note>.
*   Jujubes that do not bear fruit are called **Returning Flavor** <note>original: 還味 (huanwei)</note>.
*   The **Ren Jujube** is known for having a flavor that is short-lived.

The medicinal properties of the **Large Jujube** are sweet in flavor and balanced in nature. It is primarily used to treat "evil qi" <term>evil qi|pathogenic factors or stagnant energy that causes illness</term> within the heart and abdomen. It stabilizes the center of the body, nourishes the spleen, and balances the qi of the stomach. Furthermore, it opens the **nine orifices** <term>nine orifices|the various openings of the human body, including the eyes, ears, nostrils, mouth, and lower passages</term>, supplements the "Middle Burner," and boosts the vital energy <note>qi</note>. 

However, one should be cautious with the **Fresh Jujube**; while it is sweet and pungent, eating too many will cause a person to suffer from alternating chills and fever.

# Chestnut

# Raspberry

**Raspberries** are found everywhere, though they are particularly abundant in the regions of **Qin and Wu** <note>These correspond to modern-day Shaanxi province and the Jiangsu/Zhejiang region respectively.</note>. The shoots are short, not exceeding a foot in height, and both the stems and leaves are covered in thorns. 

The term **penglei** <term>penglei: a classical botanical name for various brambles in the Rubus genus; here it specifically denotes the stalks and shoots of the plant.</term> refers to these very shoots and stems. The flowers are white, and the fruit is reddish-yellow, shaped like a small round pellet or half-bullet. Children often eat the fruit.

# Persimmon

<note>The Persimmon (*Diospyros kaki*) is native to China and has been cultivated for over two millennia for its fruit, timber, and medicinal properties.</note>

The **Persimmon** tree is found throughout the valleys and mountains. It is a tree of great stature, with wood that is hard, fine-grained, and often used for crafting sturdy wares. 

According to ancient lore, the persimmon tree is celebrated for possessing **Seven Virtues**: 
1.  **Longevity**: The tree can live for centuries. 
2.  **Abundant Shade**: Its broad leaves provide a cool canopy. 
3.  **Cleanliness**: Birds do not build nests within its branches. 
4.  **Resilience**: It is rarely plagued by wood-boring pests or worms. 
5.  **Beauty**: Its leaves, after the first frost, turn a vibrant red and are a delight to the eye. 
6.  **Abundance**: It yields a plentiful harvest of delicious fruit. 
7.  **Utility**: Its fallen leaves are large and thick, providing a surface upon which one may practice calligraphy. <note>original: 柿葉學書 (shiye xueshu). ### Varieties and Characteristics
There are many varieties of the fruit. Some are small and round like a bull's eye, while others are large and square. Some are bright red, while others are yellow.

# Persimmon

Persimmons are found in both the North and the South, and their varieties are numerous.

**Yellow Persimmons** can be mixed with rice flour to make parched cakes. When fed to children, they are effective at stopping dysentery. 

**Red Persimmons** and **Vermilion Persimmons** are both common, though the **Vermilion Persimmon** is particularly prized for its thin skin and delicate, fine texture. 

The **Green Persimmon** <note>original: 椑柿 (beishi)</note> is green in color and possesses a **cold nature** <term>cold nature: in traditional medicine, "cold" foods are used to clear internal heat and inflammation, but can cause digestive upset if eaten in excess</term>. It can be eaten raw, but it is not suitable for drying. This variety is capable of suppressing the "poisonous fire" caused by **alchemical elixirs** <note>original: 丹石毒 (danshi du) — refers to the toxic internal heat or poisoning resulting from the historical practice of consuming mineral-based "immortality" pills containing lead or mercury</note>.

### Dried Varieties and Medicinal Use

There are different methods for drying the fruit, which change its medicinal properties:

*   **Crow Persimmons** <note>original: 鳥柿 (niaoshi)</note>: These are dried over a fire. They are primarily used to neutralize toxins and treat **metal wounds** <term>metal wounds: injuries caused by blades, such as knives or swords</term> and burns from fire. They help promote the growth of new flesh and relieve pain.
*   **White Persimmons**: These are dried in the sun. They are cold in nature.

There is also a variety of small persimmon commonly called the **Cow-Nipple Persimmon**. It is extremely cold in nature and must not be eaten in large quantities.

# Loquat

The loquat is found in all regions. The tree grows to a height of over ten feet, and its leaves are shaped like the ears of a donkey, covered in a fine, velvety fuzz. The tree's crown is dense and shady, swaying in a lovely manner; it does not wither throughout the four seasons. 

In the heart of winter, the tree produces white flowers, and by the third or fourth lunar month, the fruit ripens. The fruits are spherical, resembling yellow plums, with very thin skin and flesh. Their flavor is sweet, and the pits inside are like small chestnuts. 

In the fourth lunar month, one should harvest the leaves and dry them in the sun. The leaves have a bitter and neutral nature <note>original: 苦平 (kǔ píng). In traditional herbology, this indicates a "temperature" and flavor profile that can treat symptoms without causing significant imbalance to the body’s internal heat or cold.</note>. They are primarily used to treat sudden, persistent retching <note>original: 啘 (yuě). This refers to dry heaving, vomiting, or stubborn hiccups.</note> and to direct the *qi* downward <note>original: 下氣 (xià qì). A term in traditional medicine meaning to soothe the flow of energy to alleviate coughing, wheezing, or reflux.</note>.

# Rice

Rice consists of both <term>non-glutinous rice<gloss>original: 秔稻 (Jingdao); typically refers to the common, non-sticky varieties of rice</gloss></term> and <term>glutinous rice<gloss>original: 糯稻 (Nuodao); sticky rice often used for specialty dishes and specific medicinal applications</gloss></term>. **Glutinous rice** has the capacity to circulate blood accumulations within the <term>nutritive and defensive systems<gloss>original: 榮衛 (Rong-Wei); in traditional medicine, the "Rong" (nutritive) flows within the vessels, while the "Wei" (defensive) flows outside them to protect the body</gloss></term>. **Non-glutinous rice** has a flavor that is sweet yet slightly bitter, and its nature is "balanced" <note>meaning it is neutral, neither heating nor cooling to the constitution</note>. It benefits the <term>vital energy<gloss>original: 氣 (Qi); the life force that animates the body</gloss></term>, soothes restlessness or irritability, and arrests diarrhea.

# Wheat

Wheat includes several varieties: Barley, Common Wheat, Hulless Barley, and Buckwheat. **Barley** <note>original: 大麥 (damai), literally "Large Wheat"</note> settles the stomach, quenches thirst, and aids digestion. When soaked in water until it sprouts, it becomes **malt** <note>original: 蘖 (nie)</note>; this is used to transform stagnant food, dispel "cold energy" <note>A traditional medical concept referring to sluggishness or chills in the digestive tract</note>, and relieve bloating and fullness in the chest and abdomen.

There are two kinds of **hulless barley** <note>original: 穬麥 (kuangmai)</note>: one resembles common wheat and the other resembles barley, though both are slightly larger than the standard varieties. 

Generally, wheat is planted in autumn, grows through the winter, flowers in the spring, and ripens in the summer. Because it possesses the balanced energy of all four seasons, it is considered the most noble of the **Five Grains** <note>The *Wugu* are the five most important staple crops in Chinese history, though the specific list varies by text.</note>.

**Common Wheat** <note>original: 小麥 (xiaomai), literally "Small Wheat"</note> is cold in nature. However, when ground into **flour**, it becomes warm and carries a slight toxicity. When processed into **leaven** <note>original: 麴 (qu); a fermentation starter used for brewing or medicine</note>, it settles the stomach and stops diarrhea. Its outer skin is the **bran**, which is slightly cold in nature; it harmonizes the center of the body and clears internal heat.

**Buckwheat** strengthens the intestines and stomach and increases physical strength. However, it should not be eaten in excess, as overconsumption can stir up "wind" <note>In traditional medicine, "wind" (feng) can refer to symptoms like dizziness or tremors</note> and cause a person to feel lightheaded or dizzy.

**Broomcorn millet** <note>original: 稷米 (jimi); *Panicum miliaceum*</note> is what is known today as *ji* millet <note>original: 穄米 (jimi); referring to the non-glutinous variety of broomcorn millet</note>. **Sesame** <note>original: 油麻 (Youma). While literally "Oil Hemp," this refers to *Sesamum indicum*.</note> is by nature **extremely cold** and **non-toxic**. It **lubricates the intestines and stomach** and **circulates wind-qi**.

# Mung Bean

Mung beans are sweet in flavor, cold in nature, and non-toxic. They are primarily used to treat irritability and heat <note>referring to a feeling of internal restlessness and feverishness</note> and wind-rashes <note>likely referring to hives or itchy skin eruptions</note>. They also treat the internal heat-energy caused by the "activation of mineral medicines" <note>this refers to the toxic side effects or "heating" reactions caused by ancient Chinese alchemical or mineral-based drugs</note> and "running piglet" syndrome <term>running piglet: a traditional Chinese medical term for a sensation of qi or energy surging violently upward from the abdomen to the throat</term>.

One may grind them raw to extract the juice for consumption. When boiled and eaten, they reduce swelling, descend the qi <note>meaning to harmonize digestion and ease breathing</note>, and suppress internal heat. When using them, do not remove the skin; doing so may cause a person to feel a slight obstruction <note>a sense of fullness or bloating in the chest or abdomen</note>. This is because the skin is cold in property while the flesh is balanced. Those that are small, round, and green are of the best quality.

There is also the Wild Bean <gloss>pronounced: lü</gloss> <note>original: 穭豆, lüdou; refers to a wild variety of bean similar to the mung bean</note> which looks similar. It is primarily used to treat "sudden turmoil disorder" <note>a term for acute vomiting and diarrhea, often associated with cholera-like symptoms</note>. To use it, take the leaves, pound them and wring out the juice, mix with a small amount of vinegar, and drink while warm. The seeds also serve to descend the qi.

# Wild Oat

<note>original: 雀麥, *quemai*; literally "Sparrow Wheat"</note>

->[Illustration: A woodblock print of the Wild Oat plant, showing its long, slender leaves and its characteristic drooping seed heads.]<-

The **Wild Oat** <gloss>likely *Bromus japonicus* or *Avena fatua*</gloss> is a wild grass found throughout the countryside. In this Ming Dynasty encyclopedia, it is categorized alongside other "Herbs and Trees." It is named "Sparrow Wheat" because its small, refined grains are frequently eaten by birds, and its delicate, hanging seed clusters are said to resemble a sparrow’s tail. 

While it resembles cultivated wheat or barley, it is typically smaller and considered a "wild" or "lesser" grain. In the botanical traditions of this era, such plants were often documented for their dual roles as potential emergency food sources during famine and for their specific, though mild, medicinal properties.

# Sparrow Wheat

In the *Materia Medica* <note>original: 本草, *Bencao*; the traditional Chinese pharmacological record</note>, **Sparrow Wheat** <term>雀麥, *quemai*; *Bromus japonicus*</term> is also known as "Swallow Wheat" <gloss>燕麥, *yanmai*</gloss> or by the name *Bei* <note>original: 藣</note>. 

The seedlings of this plant resemble those of the Swallow Wheat <gloss>wild oat</gloss>, but they are more slender and delicate in appearance. When it produces its seed heads, they look like wheat spikes, yet they are extremely fine and small. Each individual spike is further divided into ten or more smaller branching spikelets. The seeds themselves are very tiny.

Regarding its medicinal qualities:
*   **Flavor:** Sweet <note>original: 味甘, *wei gan*</note>
*   **Nature:** Neutral <note>original: 性平, *xing ping*; neither heating nor cooling</note>
*   **Toxicity:** Non-toxic <note>original: 無毒, *wu du*</note>
