# Preface by Dr. Johann von Staupitz

To the illustrious, high-born princess, Lady Kunigunde <note>Kunigunde of Austria (1465–1520), sister of Emperor Maximilian I and widow of Duke Albert IV of Bavaria.</note>, born Archduchess of Austria, Palatine Countess of the Rhine, Duchess in Upper and Lower Bavaria, etc. My most gracious lady: I, Brother Johannes von Staupitz, Augustinian, wish you—according to my poor prayers and the good that God works within me—a joyful, blessed New Year.

¶ I take to heart, high-born princess, that this miserable life is filled with such countless troubles, turmoil, persecution, as well as misery and woe, in which neither the Pope, nor the Emperor, nor the priest, nor the people, nor the noble, nor the commoner, nor the old, nor the young, nor the rich, nor the poor, nor the beautiful, nor the misshapen <note>original: "vngeschaffnē"</note>, nor the wise, nor the foolish, nor the pious, nor the wicked are spared; eventually, everyone must be troubled by suffering. Furthermore, I see that God from heaven has shown a way to soften all suffering, or to set it aside entirely through words and works—indeed, He has sealed it with His death—namely His love, which is above all things, and to which we are obligated and bound. Yet, unfortunately, few are found from time to time who walk that path. Therefore, I have set out and resolved to write about God's lovely love, and to dedicate the same to Your Princely Grace—to whom I wish all things to serve for divine joy—with the most humble intent, asking with humble diligence that Your Princely Grace might accept it with gracious pleasure and not hold my foolishness against me. For I have been sufficiently moved by the high-born Prince, the late Duke Albrecht <note>Albert IV, Duke of Bavaria (1447–1508).</note>, Your Princely Grace's husband, my most gracious lord of highly praiseworthy memory, to be a faithful servant of all the lords and ladies of Bavaria until my end. With this, I commend myself to you. In the year 1518.

## God is Lovely Above All Things. Chapter 1.

<image-desc>Large ornamental woodcut initial 'O' featuring floral and vine motifs within a rectangular frame.</image-desc>

O, no one ponders how he might show that God is lovely <note>The word "lieblich" in this period carries meanings of being both "lovely" to behold and "lovable" or "worthy of love."</note> above all things. The question is answered if one simply takes to heart that God is God, and thus is not only full of all perfection, but is perfection itself, in which, through which, and from which all perfection flows and goes out. That He is also love itself, and without His love, nothing can become lovelier; indeed, He is the love that makes everything lovely upon which it falls. If it falls upon the hideous enemy, he immediately becomes a lovely enemy. If it falls upon the cruel darkness, it somehow becomes the delightful light. If it falls upon nothing, then it must immediately become something—indeed, something good. And it is impossible that what is loved by God should be unlovely. Every heart also understands that nothing is more lovely than this love, and nothing unlovely can flow from it, because it is that same constant, essential love which is good in itself. Our love takes the measure of its goodness from the goodness of the thing loved. It is good when it loves something good. Better when it loves something better. Best of all when it loves the highest good <note>original Latin: "summum bonum"</note>, and is evil when it loves what is not good. Yet, whether it falls upon the good or the evil, love nevertheless remains lovely and sweet in itself. Moreover, it makes all things bearable and light.

# No person can learn to love God 
->above all things from another. Chapter 4.<-

Regarding things that cannot be known except through a perceptive experience <note>original: "entpfindlicher experientz oder erfarūg"</note>, one person cannot learn them from another. No one—as you know—can learn from another how to see, hear, smell, taste, try, or feel; much less how to love, rejoice, be saddened, and the like, which one experiences and feels rather than merely sees. Accordingly, David said: "Taste and see that the Lord is sweet." <note>A reference to Psalm 34:8.</note> One may well give instruction regarding the visibility of colors and the fitness of good eyes, yet one learns nothing of "seeing" through this. In the same way, one can say much about the perfect goodness of God and the demonstration of His love, which is indeed praiseworthy. Likewise, one may speak of the quality of the will, of the purity of the heart, of a good conscience, and of an unfeigned faith <note>original: "vngestifftem glauben," referring to sincere or "un-propped" faith that does not rely on external show.</note>, as Paul says: "Love is the end of the commandment, out of a pure heart, a good conscience, and an unfeigned faith." But we cannot at all teach one another how to be able to have love, much less how to love above all things. Instead, that must be reserved for Him who has the heart in His hands and the will in His power, to turn it wherever and however He wills.

## A person is never comforted in hope unless the Spirit of God himself comforts them.
->Chapter 9<-

<margin>Where our true comfort resides.</margin>
All comfort of this world is useless and empty; all trust in the creature <note>In 16th-century theology, "the creature" (die Creatur) refers to anything created—including humans, wealth, or nature—as opposed to the Creator.</note> is deceptive. Therefore, Scripture curses all those who trust in their own or others' created powers and thus seek their comfort in the creature. True comfort on earth rests and remains in this: that (as Paul says) we boast in the hope of the glory of the children of God. That is, we trust without doubt that we are children of God, predestined for eternal glory. From this alone comes the fact that we feel joyful or boast in our suffering, which will be discussed hereafter. No creature can give such comfort, nor can anyone earn it; it must be poured into our hearts by the Holy Spirit alone. For this reason, Christ calls the Holy Spirit "the Comforter," as it belongs to him alone to comfort, and it is his unique nature to give hope. This hope exists in a person before all merit, and it can have no cause other than the unique and merciful will of God and the essential, substantial love that God himself is. Accordingly, hope is in no way grounded upon the love we have for God, nor upon the works we do for God. Rather, it is grounded upon

In this, God proves his love to us: that he died for our sake while we were yet sinners.
