**B**acon’s Letter was first published by *Oronce Finé* in the year 1542, printed from the presses of *Simon de Colines*. <note>Oronce Finé (1494–1555) was a French mathematician and cartographer. Consequently, it was later attached to the second volume of the *Assembly of Philosophers* <note>original: "Turbæ," referring to the *Turba Philosophorum*, one of the earliest and most influential Latin alchemical texts, which takes the form of a debate between ancient philosophers.</note> and other alchemical writings, with the errors still preserved throughout. And although that writing of Libavius is indeed feeble and poorly cobbled together, and is most unworthy of a response—inasmuch as for the most part it either stirs up certain empty and futile suspicions about the brotherhood of the <term>R. Here, therefore, this Libavius—lest Roger Bacon should lack any stain of disgrace, from which he lets almost no philosopher escape—has breathed upon him the charge of superstition. This was not, clearly, the superstition of a demonic mage (whom people rightly condemn, believing that they lure impure spirits to impious services); for he admits that Bacon did not wish to approve of magic—meaning demonic magic—since Bacon commended the power of art and nature [on the page previously cited]. Rather, he perhaps suggests the kind of superstition by which a false worship of God is offered as true, according to the traditions and precepts of men, instead of that which is according to faith in a pure conscience. 

But Libavius went looking for this crime in Bacon much too foolishly. For we do not draw dogmas of religion from him; rather, because he searched into the forces and power of nature as befitted a philosopher, we read through his writings so that we may know nature.
