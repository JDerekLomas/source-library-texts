<image-desc>Decorative woodcut initial 'I' depicting two figures, one seated and one standing, in a landscape or room setting.</image-desc>It is even so, Kind Reader. This Organ—the subject of this Work which we have inscribed with the name *The Subterranean World*—can be spoken of with the greatest justice. It is a truly harmonic Organ, so disposed and adapted by the craftsmanship of the most provident Triune <note>original: Triunius; referring to the Holy Trinity</note> Divinity in number, weight, and measure, that although it has the instruments of its operation hidden in the innermost hiding places and secret recesses of the Earth; nevertheless, through crowded subterranean tubes and pipes of immense multitude, it gives forth such measures of sound, such a variety of most diverse voices, that nothing encountered in the circuit of the sublunary World is not imbued with a certain sympathetic harmony, its own number, its own weight, and finally its own measure. Indeed, we encompass this organ as if equipped with twelve registers in twelve Books, by which the universal Majesty and riches of Nature are explained in the greatest variety of things. In setting out to explain this, I might indeed seem to commit a bold, unusual deed, perhaps unequal even to a Herculean breast; while I attempt, somewhat rashly, to introduce myself upon a much loftier chariot than that by which the Mythologists say Proserpina <note>The daughter of Ceres, abducted to the underworld</note> was once snatched by Pluto <note>The god of the underworld</note>, into the hidden and unknown kingdoms of the subterranean world, into the innermost shrines and hidden recesses of the Earthly Realm <note>term: Geocosmic (from Geocosmus); Kircher's term for the Earth as a complex, living system</note>. I attempt a journey which, as far as I know, has hitherto not only been unattempted and untrodden by footsteps, but has not even been penetrated by the conception of the human mind. But I do not delay; I dare, I dare, I say, to enter and approach a journey undertaken not so much by my own will, as persuaded by I know not what impulse of the Divine Spirit, and also extorted by the prayers of Illustrious men.

I know that in the course of this enlightened age there has been no lack of men, illustrious in the cultivation of Geographic study, who have tirelessly toiled at great expense to explain the external face of the Earthly Realm with truly distinguished effort, and with no less praise and immortality of name; yet, there has been found no one who has—I will not say penetrated—but even reached with the mind toward that which uniquely seemed to be missing: the internal Economy of the Earth and the hidden sacraments of latent Nature. ->PREFACE.<-

so that I have, to the greatest extent possible, turned my mind's eyes toward admiring the wonderful Organ of the interior Earth-world <note>original: "Geocosmi"; a term used by Kircher to describe the Earth as a living, organized system</note>, and its structure—which until now has been generally neglected, either because of the inaccessible hiding places of Nature, or despised because of the insurmountable difficulties in penetrating it. I had for a long time conceived of this subject as one that is rare, unusual, and most excellent, and likewise most worthy of the exertion of all human industry. I confess I had read much among many writers of Natural History concerning the hidden miracles of the subterranean world, which nevertheless, due to a lack of personal experience, I believed were not so much properly and sincerely explored as they were recounted by simple report, in which much trust could not be placed; I required a first-hand viewing <note>original: "αὐτοψίαν" (autopsian); the act of seeing for oneself</note> of each thing, demanding this alone as if it were absolutely necessary for the execution of the task I had conceived.

Tossed about by this great tide of thoughts, it happened at that same time, by the command of my Superiors, that I undertook a journey to Sicily and Malta in the retinue of the Most Excellent Prince Frederick, Landgrave of Hesse <note>Friedrich of Hesse-Darmstadt (1616–1682), a German prince and later a Cardinal</note>, now a most worthy Cardinal, to whom I served as confessor; I interpreted this occasion as one provided for me by the Divine Providence of the Godhead, and wonderfully opportune for finally bringing my efforts into execution. Nor did my hope deceive me. Having entered Sicily, I attained that theater of Nature unfolding itself in a wonderful variety of things which I had already desired with so many prayers; for whatever occurs in the whole Earth-world that is wonderful, rare, unusual, and worthy of admiration, I found contracted here as if in a certain epitome of the industry of sagacious Nature. Therefore, inflamed with a great desire to explore each thing, and by the notable convenience of the Maltese galleys—the aforementioned Landgrave of Hesse, then High Admiral, ardently promoting my endeavors out of the affection he bore toward me and my studies—I pursued my purpose. And before all else, which I had most desired, I climbed Etna, the source of all the prodigious effects exerting themselves in all of Sicily, so that I might find by personal experience the first-hand viewing <note>original: "τὴν αὐτοψίαν"</note> of those wonders which the historians of all ages had written about it. Then I explored with the greatest zeal and a three-day scrutiny the Aeolian or Hephaestian Islands, and especially Stromboli <note>original: "Strongylum"</note> and the one they call Vulcano; also the Strait of Messina <note>original: "Fretum Mamertinum"</note>, no less dangerous for its incredible reciprocations of tides than infamous for shipwrecks. Furthermore, I discussed with a great tide of mind—perhaps no less than the Scyllaean tide itself—the wondrous motions of the famous Scylla and Charybdis, the successions of their boilings, and the reasons for each symptom, as being entirely consistent with my purpose. Whatever stupendous things occurred in each place, I committed to my notebook <note>original: "palimpsešto"</note> and, having returned home, I weighed and examined them with the exact scale of reason; the reader will find these described most fully in the appropriate places throughout the course of this work as proof of the undertaking.

All these things having been duly observed, it pleased the Divine Goodness to open another theater to me, one formidable and filled with utterly mournful and tragic events—certainly for no other end, as far as my part is concerned, than that having seen the ineffable power of an angry Godhead working in Nature, I might amplify my subject with a copious harvest: not only with those things which might soothe the itch of a more curious mind, but which might instill the fear of GOD and the hidden abysses of His judgments into the innermost marrow of the breast. For while I was waiting for a favorable opportunity to begin my journey back to Rome, having completed my observations by land and sea, I know not by what providence it happened that I was detained at Messina for so long against my will; despite so many and such varied opportunities for departing, I nevertheless remained, lacking all opportunity. And just as we cannot sufficiently wonder at the hidden paths of Divine Providence, by which it leads improvident mortals with more than paternal care and solicitude, so too it is by no means permitted to investigate them, as they are most remote from the limits of human genius. However, the light of Truth then first began to rise for us—

## CHAPTER II.

*Concerning the horrendous earthquakes arising in Calabria in the year 1638, in which the Author, present for the space of fourteen days at great peril to his life, learned great secrets of Nature by that occasion.*

And so, in the year 1638, together with two Religious of the Third Order of St. Francis and two other seculars, having hired a small boat, we set sail from Messina on the 24th day of March. On that same day we reached Pelorus, a promontory of Sicily, where staying for three days, we struggled with every kind of calamity; so much did all the injuries of the place and the air seem to have conspired against us. These were all, as it were, certain prologues to the future tragedy. Once and again we attempted a departure, but in vain; we were always driven back to the same place, both by the unwilling sea and by the hidden hand of God (as later became clear) delaying us. Had this not happened, a grave would have awaited us all in St. Euphemia <note>A town in Calabria later destroyed by the quake</note>, where we were hurrying and where we intended to stay for a short while for the sake of business. From this miraculous disposition of Divine providence we learned how often man knows not what he asks for, and how vain and fleeting are the arrangements of men unless they are submitted to and supported by the Divine will.

Therefore, as the day of Palm Saturday dawned, which was the 27th of March, we, weary of the long delay, began a forced departure, as it were. On that very day the sea was seething beyond its usual manner, and it drove huge whirlpools in a spinning descent, especially around the place of Scylla <note>A famous navigational hazard in the Strait of Messina</note>, infamous for so many shipwrecks, so that the unusual sight struck horror not only into us, but even into most of the sailors. Therefore, with the tide of the sea against us, we directed our light vessel toward Tyndaris or Myla <note>Modern Milazzo</note>, so that from there, with the Southeast wind <note>original: "Euronotus"</note> rushing forth, we might be propelled by a shorter and straighter passage across the Gulf of Sant'Euphemia <note>original: "Sinum Cujacium"</note> to the shores of Calabria.

But when we reached that point of the sea which is almost midway between the Lipari Islands, Milazzo, and Cape Vaticano <note>original: "Promontorium Vaticanum"</note>, I, gazing more diligently at Etna and Stromboli <note>original: "Strongylus"</note>, noted that they were belching out huge globes of smoke like mountains, beyond their usual custom. As these spread far and wide, the sight not only of the Lipari Islands but also of Sicily was completely snatched from our eyes. Certain subterranean bellows, as it were, which we perceived, increased the horror, as did crashes with a sulfurous odor; these, whispering I know not what fatal and mournful thing, seemed to have conspired with unanimous consent for the destruction of all Calabria and Sicily which they were preparing. I, terrified by these types of precursors of impending calamities, urged the sailors by all the means and prayers I could, that leaving the Liparian coast, they should head straight for Cape Vaticano, adding that we were involved in a great crisis, lest being swept away by the tide of the sea, we should prepare for a journey from which there is no return.
