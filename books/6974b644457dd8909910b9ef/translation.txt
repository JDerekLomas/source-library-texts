# TO THE MOST AUGUST PRINCE

## WILLIAM,

### KING of Great Britain, France, and 
### Ireland.

Had the demands of a grateful soul and a sense of duty not almost compelled me, I would not have dared to inscribe the sacred name of so great a Prince upon these studies of mine, whatever they may be. For to you, to whom we owe everything—our entire fortunes, our liberty, our life itself, and even that which is dearer than life, our Religion <note>This refers to the "Glorious Revolution" of 1688, where William III took the throne to protect Protestantism and English liberties</note>—it is too little to offer these small gifts of a slender wit or of literary leisure. Nor can we be equal to the task of celebrating your praises, we who are already unequal to the task of returning sufficient thanks.

<margin>Look! A comparison, not bad, or rather excellent, between two rulers <term>dynarchs; powerful lords</term> of the same age! Namely, both in the flower of their youth, both Arcadians <note>original: "Arcadas ambo"; a literary reference from Virgil's Eclogues meaning peers or equals in character</note>, both consumed by the same ambition—But you, conquered province, lament! <note>original: "At tu victria provincia ploras!"; a reference to the irony of a province "winning" a legal case but being financially ruined by it</note></margin>

Most Illustrious Prince, two virtues especially constitute a great-souled man: Fortitude <term>courage and strength of mind</term> and Clemency <term>mercy or leniency</term>. Not [only do we call] you Brave...

The author humble-brags about his own literary work while positioning the King as a paragon of the classical virtues of fortitude and mercy.</summary>

# Dedicatory Letter.

We call you brave, but we also lament it: for you are reckless with a life upon which the safety of us all and the very lives of us all depend. Nor do we merely proclaim your Clemency <term>mercy or leniency</term>, which is known to the entire world; rather, we pray as humble suppliants that the Commonwealth <term>Respublica; the state</term> suffer no harm from the excessive indulgence of Caesar <note>original: "Cæsaris indulgentiâ"; here the author uses "Caesar" as a title of supreme imperial honor for King William</note>.

Let others seek after empty triumphs without the help of Mars <term>the god of war; military struggle</term>, or claim laurels without true Valor <term>Virtus; manliness and courage</term>. But for a great Leader or a brave Man who truly deserves honors, it is necessary that he count—if not his own wounds—at least his battles: those he waged, those in which he stood in the very midst, burning with zeal amidst swords and fires. And he who wishes to attain the praise of Clemency should not display to us or to our descendants the fires and ashes of cities and towns, nor the devastation of fields, as monuments to his mercy.

Furthermore, bad faith, broken treaties, and violated public oaths are unbecoming of a Great Man—those things which both divine law <term>jus divinum</term> and human law <term>jus humanum</term> have decreed to be binding and sacred among all nations, and which [the law] has willed to... <note>The text cuts off here at the catchword "volue-", likely for the word "voluerunt" or "voluere", meaning "willed" or "desired"</note>

<summary>This page of the dedication praises King William III’s genuine military valor and mercy, contrasting his authentic leadership with rulers who seek empty glory or leave destruction in their wake. It emphasizes that a true leader must uphold sacred treaties and laws.</summary>
