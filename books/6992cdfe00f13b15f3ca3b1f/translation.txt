# General Preface to the Twelve Works of Benefit and Relief

My late father previously struggled with the lack of an heir. Once, while traveling to Jingkou, he encountered an extraordinary man <note>original: "yiren" (異人); often referring to a master of physiognomy or a spiritual ascetic</note> who remarked that, based on his facial features, my father was destined to have no descendants. However, the man added that if he performed acts of **benefit and relief** <note>original: "liji" (利濟)</note>, he might yet obtain a son. Later, while he was serving as the superintendent of the salt administration <term>鹺 (cuo): an archaic term for the salt trade and its administrative bureaucracy</term> at Xiasha, a great tidal surge occurred. Those who drowned numbered no fewer than several hundred thousand. My father devoted all his energy to devising ways to recover and bury the bodies. When a plague followed that autumn, he again donated his own salary to hire physicians and prepare medicinal formulas to treat the illnesses. Because of these efforts, tens of thousands of people survived.

He further submitted a detailed proposal for the construction of a seawall. The relevant authorities forwarded his petition, and he eventually received the <gloss>Imperial Decree</gloss> <note>original: "yuzhi" (俞旨); formal approval from the Emperor</note> granting permission to proceed. At that time, all the local salt fields were being devastated by the tides. The people "clutched the shafts of his carriage" <note>original: "panyuan jiekou" (攀轅借寇); a classical idiom describing citizens who are so moved by an official's virtue that they physically try to prevent him from leaving his post</note> to plead for his continued leadership. From the stone weirs of Xiasha and Shiyan to the regions of Chuanshan, Minghe, and Longtou—totaling seven districts in all—he personally supervised the entire project.

When the embankment was completed, it was named the **Liji Seawall** (The Seawall of Benefit and Relief). That year, when the autumn tides arrived with sudden violence, they caused no harm. Later, while my father was serving as a sub-prefectural magistrate in Yongchun and Youxi, he finally fathered my brother and me. He gave us the childhood names "Benefit" and "Relief," and upon his retirement, he used those same words to name his study hall. This is the origin from which the name "Liji" first began. ### Supplement to the Compendium of Materia Medica

One of my father’s sons followed the path of the Confucian scholar, while the other followed the path of the physician. Thus, my younger brother Kai <note>original: "Kai di" (楷弟); referring to Zhao Xuemin, whose courtesy name was Shukai</note>, in addition to reading the Confucian classics from his early childhood, was also assigned lessons in the *Divine Pivot* and *Basic Questions* <note>original: "Ling Su" (靈素); the two parts of the *Yellow Emperor’s Inner Canon*, the foundational text of Chinese medicine</note>, the *Classic of Difficulties* <note>original: "Nan Jing" (難經)</note>, and the *Treatise on Cold Damage* <note>original: "Shanghan Lun" (傷寒論)</note>.

In his spare time, he was further instructed to draw the **Anatomical Bronze Man** <note>original: "Tongren Tu" (銅人圖); a reference to the famous bronze statues used to teach acupuncture points</note> from memory as a form of play. He stored the *A-B Classic* <note>original: "Jia-Yi" (甲乙); likely referring to the *Zhenjiu Jia Yi Jing*, a fundamental acupuncture text</note> in the "Garden for Nourishing Plainness." We set aside a single plot of land to serve as a medicinal herb garden. During the spring and autumn seasons, my brother and I would often eat and sleep within it.

I encouraged my brother to focus his will on the **Arts of the Yellow Emperor** <term>岐黃 (Qihuang): a traditional term for medicine, named after the legendary Yellow Emperor and his minister Qi Bo</term> so that he might carry on our late father’s aspirations. Although he did not yet dare to practice medicine publicly, whenever relatives or friends requested a diagnosis, they invariably recovered immediately upon taking his medicine.

In his daily life, he has always delighted in writing. Among the works he has compiled are the *Mirror of a Hundred Herbs* <note>original: "Baicao Jing" (百草鏡)</note> in eight volumes and *Saving Lives from the Bitter Sea* <note>original: "Jiusheng Kuhai" (救生苦海)</note> in one hundred volumes. In all of them, his words strike the essential truth <note>original: "kenqing" (肯綮); literally the point where the blade meets the joint in butchery, meaning the crux of a matter</note> and explain the most profound mysteries. They truly provide no small benefit to this discipline.

As for myself, I am ashamed of my own meager talents—a mere puddle or a spoonful of water. How could I ever be worthy of the notice of the refined? Why should I wish to **clatter like an earthenware pot**? <note>original: "wafu ming" (瓦釜鳴); a classical idiom for a common person making a loud, unrefined noise, often used as a trope of humility regarding one's own writing</note>. Yet I remember that since my childhood, my nature has been to love broad reading. Whether it be astrology, the calendar, medicine, divination, or various technical arts, I have dabbled in them all. Whenever I gained some insight, I would joyfully forget my fatigue and transcribe my notes into volumes.

My silk-bound book chests <note>original: "Xiang zhi qie" (緗之篋); "Xiang" refers to the light yellow silk used for book covers, a metonym for a scholarly library</note> have long been so full that their contents overflow the baskets. Bundled and stored on high shelves, they have piled up into several thousand volumes. Lately, I have grown somewhat weary of this clutter. During a quiet year, I inspected the collection and burned those works that were of no use. 

Among the twelve works in this collection, the two manuscripts for **Incantation Therapy** <term>祝由 (Zhuyou): an ancient medical category involving spiritual healing, prayer, and incantations</term> and **The Itinerant’s Medical Lore** <term>串雅 (Chuanya): literally "Elegant Strings," referring to the knowledge and "calling cards" of traveling "bell-doctors"</term> were nearly lost; in the spring of the *Jichou* year <note>1769</note>, I had already thrown them into the flames. However, because the youngsters in the house snatched them away from the fire, they were preserved for the time being.

Starting from the *Jiaxu* year <note>1754</note>, I began compiling the **Gathering Fur from the Medical Forest** <note>original: "Yilin Jiye" (醫林集腋); "Gathering fur from underarms" is a metaphor for collecting small, precious fragments from many sources to create a valuable whole</note>. I examined more than ten thousand medical books held in the collection of my neighbor, Old Man Huang Fanweng, and cross-referenced them with old secret volumes from the Jiangnan and Fujian regions. I collected those formulas that were repeatedly proven effective and titled the work "Gathering Fur" to show that making a refined selection is no easy task.

Among my relatives and friends, some showed me their own proven prescriptions, and I acquired others during my travels. Over the years, these additions grew into the **Proven Prescriptions** <note>original: "Chuanxin Fang" (傳信方); literally "Prescriptions for Passing on Trust"</note>. In the spring of the *Yihai* year <note>1755</note>, Mr. Wang Zishi of Hunan rented a room at my residence. I noticed a volume on *Incantation Therapy* on his desk; Mr. Wang used these methods with consistent success. 

In my spare time, I borrowed his book to make a copy. Later, I also obtained a manuscript from the Zhang family, as well as excerpts from *The Confucian’s Duties Toward Parents* <note>original: "Rumen Shiqin" (儒門事親); a famous medical text by Zhang Congzheng</note> and the transcriptions of the two masters **Wan Quan** and **Xue Ji** <note>original: "Wan Xue er jia" (萬薛二家); two of the most influential physicians of the Ming Dynasty</note>. I selected and tested these remedies, deleting those that were absurd and retaining only those that were effective and convenient for a practitioner to use.

# Supplement to the Compendium of Materia Medica, Volume One

...I believed that while living in the mountains, one might occasionally be unable to obtain help from medicines or surgery <term>藥石 (Yaoshi): literally "drugs and stones," referring to the full spectrum of medical treatment including herbs and physical procedures like acupuncture</term>, so I compiled the **Verified Records of Incantation Therapy** <note>original: "Zhuyou Luyan" (祝由錄驗); Zhuyou is an ancient practice of spiritual healing</note>.

I have always had a "book-addiction" <note>original: "shupi" (書癖)</note>. The day was never enough for me, so I would burn the midnight oil <note>idiom: "burning fat to continue the daylight"</note> to keep reading. Fearing that the elders of the house would scold or forbid me, I used to hide a lamp within my bed curtains. I would hide my books and read at night until the soot accumulated so much that my green silk curtains were completely blackened. 

In the autumn of the *Bingzi* year <note>1756</note>, I suffered from an eye ailment that nearly blinded me. I could not see or open my eyes for six months before I was finally cured. Because of this experience, I composed the **Dew in the Satchel Collection** <note>original: "Nanglu Ji" (囊露集)</note>. I compared it against classic texts like the **Jade Casket and Silver Sea** <note>Yao Han Yin Hai</note>, the **Treatise of Longmu** <note>Longmu Lun</note>, and the **Mirror of Ophthalmology** <note>Mingjing Jian</note>, and I believed my work surpassed them. The reason I named it "Dew in the Satchel" is that I took the idea that "dew from cypress leaves gathered in a pouch can cure the eyes."

My friend Zong Zibai, known as Baiyun, possessed the medical arts of the legendary masters Hua Tuo and Bian Que <note>The most famous doctors of ancient China, used here as a high compliment</note>. He traveled through famous cities, and in the *Wuyin* year <note>1758</note>, he returned from his voyage to the Middle Mountain Kingdom <note>Zhongshan, usually referring to the Ryukyu Islands/Okinawa</note>. We had been separated for eight years. He presented his visiting card to pay his respects; at that time, I was at home observing a period of mourning <note>original: "reading the rites" (duli), a euphemism for the mandatory retirement and study period following a parent's death</note>. I hosted him for three months. 

Whenever we discussed medical matters, I would listen for any errors. Baiyun was a scholar of humble spirit and largely agreed with my assessments. He generously produced the various methods he had gathered during his travels, including "top-knot" and "string" techniques <term>頂串 (Dingchuan): the specialized medical techniques of itinerant "bell-doctors." "Ding" refers to medicines applied to the head, and "Chuan" to the medicines they carried on strings or their distinctive bell-rings</term>. I combined these with the formulas I had personally tested in my **Garden of Cultivating Simplicity** <note>Yangsu Yuan, Zhao's private study</note> and compiled them into the **Elegant Lore of the Itinerant** <note>Chuanya (串雅)</note>. I did so because I truly knew that these were not the lowly, vulgar skills of common street charlatans.

# Supplement to the Compendium of Materia Medica, Volume One

The lay practitioner **Yinyuan** and the **Venerable Master** <note>original: "Shangren" (上人); a respectful title for a Buddhist monk</note> were both highly skilled in the **Method of Refining Herbs** <note>original: "Dianmao fa" (點茆法); likely a specific alchemical or pharmaceutical technique for processing raw materials</note>. They had long been on good terms with **He Zhuli**. 

In the *Gengchen* year <note>1760</note>, I was studying at the **Huifeng Vihara** <note>a "vihara" is a secluded room or cell for study and meditation</note> of the **West Mountain Temple**. It happened that **Zhuli** arrived there to escape the summer heat, and I joined him for a **He-shuo drinking session** <term>河朔飲 (Heshuo yin): a literary allusion to the tradition of drinking heavily during the hottest days of summer to stay cool, a practice dating back to the Three Kingdoms period</term>. We stayed together for some time, and it was through him that I was able to hear various theories regarding the **Control of Fire in the Tripod** <note>original: "Zhifu dinghuo" (制伏鼎火); this refers to the precise management of heat and temperature when refining medicinal elixirs or processing potent herbs</note>.

I applied these methods to the various categories of medicines used for **Ascending and Descending** <term>升降 (Shengjiang): in Chinese medicine, this refers to the directional movement of medicinal effects—whether a drug moves energy upward to the head/lungs or downward to the lower organs</term>. By using these techniques, I found that my physical constitution was not depleted by the treatments, and the results were twice as fast. 

Consequently, I collected the various ancient formulas for "ascending and descending" and incorporated these specific preparation methods to create a book titled **Essential Secrets** <note>original: "Miyao" (秘要)</note>. Furthermore, I used the most extraordinary medicinal properties and preparations as the basis for a work called **The Primal Explanations** <note>original: "Yuanjie" (元解)</note>, intended to demonstrate that the applications of materia medica are exceptionally broad.

In the past, **Gao Lian** <note>A Ming dynasty polymath and author of "Eight Discourses on the Art of Living"</note> recorded many rare and exotic medicines, but his search for wonders was not exhaustive. **Binhu** <note>The courtesy name of Li Shizhen (1518–1593), the most famous figure in Chinese medicine</note> wrote the **Compendium of Materia Medica** <note>Bencao Gangmu</note>, but many things discovered since its publication have not been added to supplement it. I have attempted to continue all of these works. One is titled the **Reference for Extraordinary Medicines** <note>original: "Qiyao Beikao" (奇藥備考)</note> and the other is the **Supplement to the Compendium of Materia Medica** <note>original: "Gangmu Shiyi" (綱目拾遺)</note>. Each follows its own organizational principles.

As for the formalization of materia medica books, from the **Divine Farmer’s Classic** <note>Shennong Bencao Jing</note> and **Tong Jun’s Records of Medicines** <note>Tongjun Yaolu</note> downwards, there have been no fewer than several dozen successors. Certainly, nothing is lacking in these records. However, that which is valued most highly is the **Property of the Medicine** <term>藥性 (Yaoxing): the specific character or "nature" of a drug, such as cold, hot, warm, or cool</term>. # Supplement to the Compendium of Materia Medica: Preface

How can one distinguish between truth and falsehood when the local products of the earth vary so greatly? For instance, **Rehmannia** <note>original: "Dihuang" (地黃)</note> is judged by the "Three Realms" <note>Heaven, Earth, and Man; a traditional method of grading the quality of this root</note>, and **Asparagus Root** <note>original: "Tiandong" (天冬)</note> is categorized by its origin among the "Five Sacred Peaks." Even though Master Li <note>referring to Li Shizhen (1518–1593), the most famous pharmacologist in Chinese history</note> discussed these matters briefly, the regional customs differ and the paths of the past and present have diverged. 

In the hundred-plus years since his time, there is nothing that has not shifted or changed. Furthermore, names like "Old Lady" <note>original: "Ponai" (婆奶)</note> or "Old Man’s Beard" <note>original: "Gongxu" (公鬚)</note> change according to local folk customs. The techniques found in alchemical texts and medical manuals are also divided by different schools of practice. If a substance is obscure or from a remote area, one might simply admit to not knowing it; however, if one is even unfamiliar with the common names of herbs, one can hardly avoid being ridiculed. 

For this reason, I have written *Discourses on the Materia Medica*, the *List of Minor Names for Flowers and Medicines*, and even the *Leisurely Observations on Health Preservation*—which contains methods for **Guiding and Stretching** <term>導引 (Daoyin): ancient Chinese physical and breathing exercises, a precursor to modern Qigong, used to maintain health and dispel illness</term> as well as theories on the **Harmonizing of Water and Fire** <note>original: "Kan-Li caijie" (坎離裁接); "Kan" (Water/Kidneys) and "Li" (Fire/Heart) are trigrams from the I-Ching; this refers to internal alchemical balance</note>. What is the purpose of all this? 

Alas! The goal of study is practical application, and medicine is a path to understanding the profound mysteries of the universe. I am deeply ashamed of my **meager, "sock-thread" talent** <term>襪線之才 (Wàixiàn zhī cái): a humble idiom for limited ability, comparing one's skill to the short length of thread used to sew a sock</term>, yet I dare to boast of sharing these **"golden needle" secrets** <term>金針 (Jīnzhēn): a metaphor for passing on the essential, "secret" techniques of a craft to others</term>. 

I have therefore recounted the origins of this work and added this preface to the beginning of the volume. # Small Preface to the Supplement to the Compendium of Materia Medica

A guest once asked me, "I have heard that you are writing a *Supplement to the Compendium of Materia Medica* <note>original: "Bencao Gangmu Shiyi"</note>. Is this true?"

I replied, "It is."

The guest said, "Master Binhu <note>The courtesy name of Li Shizhen (1518–1593), the most famous pharmacologist in Chinese history and author of the original *Compendium*.</note> was a man of immense learning who exhausted the study of countless books. His work encompassed a hundred generations, examining texts and investigating traditions from the Classics and Histories down to the most minor records. He collected everything in detail to establish a singular school of thought. Furthermore, at that time, he spared no expense in labor or cost, inviting the empire’s finest physicians and inquiring everywhere into local customs. He traveled to the most remote and secluded regions to find their products, and braved dangerous peaks to explore their flora. Even obscure items like the Mandrake <note>original: "Yabulu" (押不蘆), a plant from the West often identified as Mandragora, mentioned in the Yuan Dynasty "Guixin Zhashi".</note> or the Mummy <note>original: "Munaishi" (木乃伊), recorded in the "Chuogeng Lu" as a medicinal substance from the Middle East.</note>—which were only known through rare miscellaneous records—were all included by Binhu. What could possibly have been left out that is waiting for you to 'gather' it? Looking at your project, does it not seem like adding an extra finger to a hand or a superfluous wart to the skin?" <note>A classical metaphor for something redundant or unnecessary.</note>

I replied, "Yes and no. It is true that Binhu’s book is indeed vast. However, as living things exist over long periods, their varieties become even more numerous. As popular tastes lean toward the curious, rare and excellent specimens are collected more than ever before. Thus, medicines like the 'Ding-vine' or 'Chen-medicine' were not seen in the *Divine Farmer's Classic* <note>original: "Benjing," the earliest Chinese materia medica.</note>. Plants like Jili and Jinu <note>Refers to *Shaoqi* (Jili) and *Siphonostegia chinensis* (Jinu), which were added to the medical canon in later eras.</note> were only passed down by later generations. The study of birds and insects was greatly expanded in the works of Simiao <note>Sun Simiao (581–682), the 'King of Medicine' from the Tang Dynasty.</note> and his writings on decoctions..."

Note: This **Jar Water** <note>original: "Guan Shui"</note> is simply **Strong Water** <note>nitric acid</note>; it is merely a case of different names being used in ancient and modern times.

### Water for Blade Wounds

This comes from the Western Oceans <note>a general term for Europe and the Americas during the Qing dynasty</note>. It is unknown what substances are combined to create it. Foreign ships bring it to the markets of **Macau** in Guangdong. To treat **metal wounds** <note>term: "jin chuang," injuries caused by blades or weapons</note>, one applies this water to the wound, and it will immediately draw together and heal as it was before.

### Water for Clearing the Nose

This is brought by Western ships. Its method of manufacture is unknown. Some say it is made of resin, others say it is plant juice, or that it is made from **urine and dew** <note>original: "sou lu," possibly an early attempt to explain the nitrogenous or ammoniacal nature of the substance</note> dried on a platform. The foreigners on the ships store it in **glass bottles**, tightly sealing the mouth so that the **vapor** <note>original: "qi," referring here to the volatile gas</note> does not escape. If the vapor does not leak, the medicinal power is not diminished. The scent is extremely sharp and pungent; it strikes a person's brain. Unless one is ill, one must not smell it. When the **island foreigners** <note>original: "dao yi," a period-specific term for Westerners; literally "island barbarians"</note> encounter **head-wind** <note>term: "tou feng," chronic headaches or migraines in Traditional Chinese Medicine</note>, **cold-damage** <note>term: "shang han," a broad category of febrile or infectious diseases</note>, or similar illnesses, they do not take internal medicine, but only use this water...
