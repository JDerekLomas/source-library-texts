<note>
This page describes one of the most famous scenes in Christian iconography: the "Majesty of God." 

The text and accompanying woodcut depict several key theological elements:
*   **The Twenty-Four Elders:** Often interpreted as representing the twelve tribes of Israel and the twelve Apostles, symbolizing the whole people of God.
*   **The Four Living Creatures:** Described as having wings and being covered in eyes; in Christian tradition, these are usually identified with the four Evangelists (Matthew as a man, Mark as a lion, Luke as an ox, and John as an eagle).
*   **The Seven Lamps:** Representing the "seven spirits of God."
*   **The Sealed Book and the Lamb:** This refers to Revelation chapter 5, where only the "Lamb that was slain" (symbolizing Jesus Christ) is found worthy to open the seven seals of the scroll containing the destiny of the world. 

The mention of "Revelation 4" in the text actually encompasses the narrative of both chapters 4 and 5, which together form the vision of the celestial liturgy.
</note>

<note>
This illustration by Hans Holbein the Younger captures the dramatic onset of the Apocalypse. As the Lamb opens the first four seals of the scroll, four riders are summoned:

*   **The First Rider (Conquest):** Mounted on a white horse and carrying a bow.
*   **The Second Rider (War):** Mounted on a red horse, receiving a "great sword" to take peace from the earth.
*   **The Third Rider (Famine):** Mounted on a black horse, holding a pair of scales (statera), symbolizing the scarcity and high cost of food.
*   **The Fourth Rider (Death):** Often depicted on a pale horse. Here, he is represented as a skeleton, with "Hell" (often depicted in this era as a monstrous mouth or a following presence) trailing behind him.

In the 16th century, these images resonated deeply with a public living through frequent warfare, plague, and religious upheaval. The woodcut emphasizes the terrifying momentum of these figures as they trample the earthly population.
</note>

In the visual language of the 16th century, these celestial signs—a darkened sun and a moon turned the color of blood—signaled the <term>Apocalypse</term> (the final uncovering of God's judgment). 

The artist depicts a literal interpretation of the biblical text: stars are shown physically falling from the sky like "untimely figs," while the landscape itself is fractured by a massive <term>earthquake</term> (referred to in the German text as "Erdbidem"). 

Crucial to this scene is the social inversion it portrays. Below the celestial chaos, people of all ranks—kings wearing crowns, nobles, and commoners—are shown fleeing in panic. They attempt to hide in caves and among rocks, crying out for the mountains to fall on them to hide them from the "wrath of the Lamb." This imagery emphasized to contemporary readers that on the day of divine reckoning, worldly power and status provide no protection.
</note>

<term>Artistic images <note>original Latin: Icones artificiosissimæ / German: künstliche Bildnuſſen</note></term>: During the Renaissance, the word "artistic" or "artful" implied great technical skill and craftsmanship. <term>Martyr <note>from the Greek 'martys,' meaning witness</note></term>: A person who suffers death rather than renounce their religious beliefs. In the 16th century, martyrologies (histories of martyrs) were popular among both Catholics and Protestants to bolster faith during times of religious conflict.
