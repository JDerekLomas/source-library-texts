# TRANSLATOR'S NOTE.

It is perhaps unnecessary to say anything regarding the difficulty of making an adequate translation of Hegel's writings. In the case of the *History of Philosophy*, that difficulty is possibly increased by the fact that the greater part of the book is compiled from the notes of different lecture courses delivered on the subject at various times. Hegel, as we learn from Michelet <note>Karl Ludwig Michelet (1801–1893), a student of Hegel and editor of his works.</note> in his preface to the first edition of this work, lectured a total of nine times on the History of Philosophy: first in Jena in 1805–1806, then in Heidelberg in 1816–1817 and 1817–1818, and the other six times in Berlin between the years 1819 and 1830. He had begun the tenth course on the subject in 1831 when death cut his labors short. 

It was only for the first course of lectures—the one delivered in Jena—that Hegel fully wrote out his lectures; this was evidently done with the intention of future publication in book form. At Heidelberg, he composed a short abstract of his subject, giving in a few terse words the main points dealt with in each system of philosophy. In the later lecture courses, Hegel relied on extempore <note>original: "extempore speaking"; speaking without preparation or from brief notes.</note> speaking, but at the same time made considerable use of the writings mentioned above, the margins of which he annotated with subsequent additions. Besides these annotations, he left behind a large number of miscellaneous notes, which have proved to be of the greatest value. 

The present translation is taken from the second and amended edition of the *History of Philosophy* <note>original: "Geschichte der Philosophie"</note>, published in 1840. I have also attempted, as far as possible, to provide the recognized symbols for words for which we have no satisfactory equivalents in English. “<term>Begriff</term>,” when used in its technical sense, is translated as “<term>Notion</term>.” “<term>Idee</term>” is translated as “<term>Idea</term>,” as distinguished from the colloquial use of the word “idea.” “<term>Vorstellung</term>” is usually rendered as “<term>popular</term>” or “<term>ordinary conception</term>.”
