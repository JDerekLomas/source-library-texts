# The Heart and Small Intestine reside at the Left Inch; the Liver and Gallbladder together are fixed at the Left Gate.

<gloss>The "Left Inch" <note>original: 左寸, 'zuo cun'</note> refers to the "Inch" position on the left hand. The Heart is categorized as Sovereign Fire <note>original: 君火, 'jun huo'</note>; the nature of Fire is to flame upward. The Small Intestine is the *fu* <term>fu: a hollow, "yang" organ that processes and transports</term> of the Heart. Therefore, the pulses of the Heart and Small Intestine are located at the "Inch" position of the left hand, which is the highest. The "Left Gate" <note>original: 左關, 'zuo guan'</note> refers to the "Gate" position on the left hand, which corresponds to the East. They "together return" because both the Liver and Gallbladder belong to the element of Wood. Consequently, the Gallbladder resides at the "Gate" position of the left hand.</gloss>

# The Kidneys reside in the Cubit section likewise.

<gloss>The Kidneys correspond to Water in the North. Its nature is to flow downward rather than upward, so it resides at the bottom. Since the Kidneys are in the *chi* <note>original: 尺, 'chi' - the "Cubit" or "Foot" position</note> section, which is the lowest pulse position, they are paired with the Bladder organ. The phrase "likewise" refers back to the logic of the previous text, indicating that the Kidney also resides in the lower position and likewise possesses a paired *fu* organ.</gloss>

# Apply focus to harmonize and examine for tranquility.

<gloss>This follows from the discussion of the three positions of the left hand. It instructs that the physician should use their own regulated breathing and the focus of their fingers to examine the pulse of the patient. <note>original: 注</note> The three positions of the left hand are primarily concerned with the influences of Warmth, Wind, and Cold. These are considered "Guest" influences <note>External pathogens or environmental factors</note> diagnosed on the left. If the "Guest" pulse is harmonious and does not "offend the Host" <note>The 'Host' refers to the body's internal, stable state</note>, the pulse will be peaceful and calm.</gloss>

# The Lungs and Large Intestine reside at the Right Inch; the Spleen and Stomach pulses are identified within the Gate.

<gloss>The "Right Inch" refers to the *cun* position of the right hand, which is the highest. The Large Intestine is the *fu* organ of the Lungs; thus, the pulses of the Lung and Large Intestine reside at the Right Inch. The Lungs are categorized as Dry Metal <note>original: 燥金, 'zao jin'</note>, and Metal is situated in the West. The "Gate" refers to the *guan* position on the right hand. The Spleen is categorized as Damp Earth <note>original: 濕土, 'shi tu'</note>; the position of Earth is in the Southwest and it resides in the center.</gloss>

# The Three Regions Must Be Clearly Understood Beneath the Fingers

<gloss>The **Three Regions** <note>original: 三部, 'Sanbu'</note> refer to the **Inch**, the **Gate**, and the **Cubit** <term>Inch, Gate, and Cubit: the three points along the radial artery used in Chinese pulse diagnosis; 'Cun', 'Guan', and 'Chi'</term>. The Inch represents the Upper Region, the Gate represents the Middle Region, and the Cubit represents the Lower Region. The medical classics state: The Upper Region follows the **Method of Heaven** and governs ailments from the chest upward to the head. The Middle Region follows the **Method of Man** and governs ailments from below the diaphragm down to the navel. The Lower Region follows the **Method of Earth** and corresponds to the Earth, governing ailments from the navel down to the feet. These three regions of the pulse are the masters of the entire body. When performing a pulse diagnosis, the practitioner must explore their source and peer into their hidden depths.</gloss>

# General Treatise on the Frequencies of the Six Pulse Positions

Jiegu <note>The pseudonym of Zhang Yuansu (c. 1151–1234), an influential physician who revolutionized Chinese medicine by correlating herbs and pulses with specific internal organs.</note> states: The left and right hands each correspond to the locations of the **five Zang and six Fu organs** <note>The Zang (solid) organs—Heart, Liver, Spleen, Lungs, and Kidneys—and the Fu (hollow) organs—Small/Large Intestines, Gallbladder, Stomach, Bladder, and Triple Burner—are the core of internal medicine.</note>. Occasionally, a pulse may have a high frequency of beats yet indicate a "Cold" condition, or it may have a low frequency yet indicate "Heat." One must deduce the **transmission and transformation** <note>original: 傳變; the process by which a disease progresses from one organ system to another.</note> and whether the progression is **adverse or favorable** <note>original: 逆順; "favorable" follows the natural cycle, while "adverse" indicates a breakdown of the natural order.</note> based on the specific pulse position. 

From this, we know that we should not be restricted by the simple rule that a "rapid" pulse always signifies Heat or a "slow" pulse always signifies Cold. A major pulse reflects the numbers of the **Five Phases** <note>original: 五行; the theory that Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water govern all natural cycles.</note>, each of which has its specific function of "generation" (creation) and "growth." Regarding the numbers of **mutual overcoming** <note>original: 相尅; the destructive or controlling cycle between elements.</note>:
*   **Wood** meets **Metal** and is felled.
*   **Fire** meets **Water** and is extinguished.
*   **Metal** meets **Fire** and is chipped or melted.
*   **Earth** meets **Wood** and is depleted.
*   **Water** meets **Earth** and is severed or blocked.

The five Zang organs correspond to these Five Phases, and each follows the logic of **mutual generation and mutual overcoming**. When the pulse indicates "generation," the patient will recover; when it indicates "overcoming," the patient will die. If one does not master this theory of how the five Zang organs exchange and transmit influence—as well as the patterns of deficiency, excess, and the direction of flow—there is no way to grasp the profound interest and logic of this principle.

# Four Beats per Breath is Called Balanced; Adding One More Means Great Health
<gloss>One breath consists of one exhalation and one inhalation in balance. During exhalation, the pulse beats twice; these correspond to the **Heart** and **Lung** pulses. During inhalation, the pulse beats twice again; these correspond to the **Kidney** and **Liver** pulses. When the upper and lower systems communicate in this way, it is considered "Balanced" <note>original: 平和 (pinghe), meaning peaceful and harmonious</note>. If an additional beat occurs between the exhalation and inhalation, totaling five beats, it indicates that the **Spleen** has received the essence of food. Since the Spleen's pulse is at the center of the system, each of the five internal organs receives one beat; therefore, there is "Great Health" <note>original: 太無病 (tai wu bing), literally "greatly without disease"</note>.</gloss>

*   Six beats indicates **life is extinguished**.

### The Fourth Difficulty: The Laws of Yin and Yang in the Pulse

**The Question:** The **Fourth Difficulty** states: The <term>pulse</term><note>original: "脉" (mai); refers to the radial pulse at the wrist used as a diagnostic tool</note> follows the laws of **Yin and Yang**. What does this mean?

**The Answer:** It is because exhalation is associated with the **Heart** and the **Lungs**, while inhalation is associated with the **Kidneys** and the **Liver**. In the interval between exhalation and inhalation, the **Spleen** receives the flavors of grain <note>This refers to the Spleen's role in extracting nutrients and "Gu Qi" (Grain Qi) from digested food</note>. 

The pulse of the Spleen is located in the center. Those qualities that are **Floating** are **Yang**, and those that are **Sinking** are **Yin**. This is why we speak of Yin and Yang.

**Distinguishing the Floating Pulses:**
Since the pulses of both the Heart and the Lungs are **Floating** <note>original: "浮" (fu); a pulse felt with very light pressure</note>, how does one tell them apart?
*   The pulse that is **Floating**, yet **large and scattered**, belongs to the **Heart**.
*   The pulse that is **Floating**, yet **short and rough** <note>original: "澀" (se); a hesitant, "choppy" quality like a knife scraping bamboo</note>, belongs to the **Lungs**.

**Distinguishing the Sinking Pulses:**
Since the pulses of both the Liver and the Kidneys are **Sinking** <note>original: "沉" (chen); a pulse felt only with deep pressure near the bone</note>, how does one tell them apart?
*   The pulse that is **firm and long** belongs to the **Liver**.
*   The pulse that feels **soft** <note>original: "濡" (ru); also translated as "soggy" or "damp"</note> when pressed down, but feels **full and firm** when the fingers are slightly lifted, belongs to the **Kidneys**.

The **Spleen** governs the <term>Middle Province</term> <note>original: "中州" (zhong zhou); a metaphorical term for the digestive center of the body</note>; therefore, its pulse is found in the middle position. This is the law of Yin and Yang.

**The Complexity of Combinations:**
It is said that the pulse may have **one Yin and one Yang**, **one Yin and two Yang**, or **one Yin and three Yang**; likewise, it may have **one Yang and one Yin**, **one Yang and two Yin**, or **one Yang and three Yin**. 

Does this mean that at the <term>Cunkou</term> <note>The "Inch Opening," the specific area of the wrist where the pulse is felt</note> there are six distinct pulses all moving at once?

No, these descriptions do not mean there are six separate pulses moving together. They refer to the various qualities of the pulse: **Floating, Sinking, Long, Short, Slippery, and Rough**. 
*   **Floating** is Yang. 
*   **Slippery** is Yang. 
*   **Long** is Yang. 
*   **Sinking** is Yin. 
*   **Short** is Yin. 
*   **Rough** is Yin.

# The Fifth Difficulty

The **Fifth Difficulty** <note>original: "五難" (Wu Nan); one of the eighty-one "difficulties" or doctrinal problems addressed in this foundational medical text</note> states: The **pulse** is said to have qualities of **lightness and heaviness**. What does this mean?

The answer is: When first taking the pulse, applying a pressure equivalent to the weight of **three beans** <note>original: "三菽" (san shu); a traditional unit of pressure where one 'shu' equals the weight of a single soybean</note> so that it corresponds with the **skin and hair** indicates the **Lung** region. 

Applying a pressure equivalent to the weight of **six beans** so that it corresponds with the **blood vessels** indicates the **Heart** region. 

Applying a pressure equivalent to the weight of **nine beans** so that it corresponds with the **muscles and flesh** indicates the **Spleen** region. 

Applying a pressure equivalent to the weight of **twelve beans** to be level with the **tendons** <note>original: "筋" (jin); refers to the connective tissues, including tendons and ligaments</note> indicates the **Liver** region. 

Pressing down until reaching the **bone**, where the pulse arrives rapidly upon lifting the fingers, indicates the **Kidney** region. 

This is what is meant by the terms **lightness and heaviness**.

### The Sixth Difficulty

The **Sixth Difficulty** states: The pulse may manifest as **Abundant Yin with Deficient Yang**, or **Abundant Yang with Deficient Yin**. What does this mean?

The answer is: When the pulse is felt at the **floating** <term>original: 浮 (fú); the superficial level felt with light pressure, corresponding to the exterior and Yang</term> level and it is **diminished and small**, but when felt at the **sinking** <term>original: 沉 (chén); the deep level felt with heavy pressure, corresponding to the interior and Yin</term> level it is **solid and large**, this is called "Abundant Yin with Deficient Yang." <note>In this state, the "Internal" (Yin) is overactive or stagnant, while the "External" (Yang) lacks strength.</note>

Conversely, when the pulse is **diminished and small** at the **sinking** level, but **solid and large** at the **floating** level, this is called "Abundant Yang with Deficient Yin." <note>This indicates the "External" (Yang) is overactive, often representing a fever or external pathogen, while the "Internal" (Yin) resources are depleted.</note>

This is the essential meaning of **deficiency and excess** <term>original: 虛實 (xū shí); "Xu" refers to a lack of vital energy, while "Shi" refers to an overabundance or a harmful blockage</term> within the **Yin and Yang**.

# The Seventh Difficulty

The **Seventh Difficulty** asks: The classic states that when the **Lesser Yang** <term>original: 少陽 (shǎo yáng); the first stage of Yang energy, associated with birth and the gall bladder/triple burner</term> arrives, the pulse is suddenly large and suddenly small, suddenly short and suddenly long. 

When the **Yang Brightness** <term>original: 陽明 (yáng míng); the stage of full, receding Yang energy</term> arrives, the pulse is **floating**, large, and short. 

When the **Greater Yang** <term>original: 太陽 (tài yáng); the peak of Yang energy</term> arrives, the pulse is **overflowing**, large, and long. 

When the **Greater Yin** <term>original: 太陰 (tài yīn); the first stage of Yin energy</term> arrives, the pulse is **tight**, large, and long. 

When the **Lesser Yin** <term>original: 少陰 (shǎo yīn); the stage of deepening Yin</term> arrives, the pulse is **tight**, thin, and faint. 

When the **Absolute Yin** <term>original: 厥陰 (jué yīn); the final stage of the Yin cycle</term> arrives, the pulse is **deep**, short, and rapid. 

Are these six types considered healthy pulses <term>original: 平脉 (píng mài); a "balanced" or normal pulse for the season</term>, or are they pulses of disease?

The answer is: These are all **Sovereign Pulses** <term>original: 王脈 (wáng mài); pulses that "rule" or dominate during a specific time</term>. In which months does their energy <note>The vital "Qi" or life force</note> rule, and for how many days does each remain sovereign?

The answer is: After the **Winter Solstice** <note>The shortest day of the year, marking the turning point where Yang energy begins to rise</note>, the arrival of the first *Jia-Zi* day <note>The first day in the traditional sixty-day cycle</note> marks the beginning of the **Lesser Yang’s** sovereignty. 

The next *Jia-Zi* marks the sovereignty of the **Yang Brightness**. 
The next *Jia-Zi* marks the sovereignty of the **Greater Yang**. 
The next *Jia-Zi* marks the sovereignty of the **Greater Yin**. 
The next *Jia-Zi* marks the sovereignty of the **Lesser Yin**. 
The next *Jia-Zi* marks the sovereignty of the **Absolute Yin**.

Each of these rules for **sixty days**. Six periods of sixty days each makes **three hundred and sixty days**, completing one full cycle <note>Representing a lunar year</note>. This is the essential summary of the timing and duration for the sovereignty of the **Three Yin and Three Yang**.

# The Eighth Difficulty: On the Vital Root of the Pulse

The Eighth Difficulty asks: What does it mean when the pulse at the wrist <term>original: 寸口脉 (cùn kǒu mài); the standard diagnostic pulse located at the radial artery</term> appears balanced and healthy <note>original: 平 (píng); meaning "level" or "even," describing a pulse that feels normal to the touch</note>, yet the patient still dies?

The answer is this: All of the twelve meridians <note>the primary pathways of vital energy that connect the organs</note> are tied to the **Source of Generative Qi**. This "Source of Generative Qi" is the very foundation of the twelve meridians.

It refers specifically to the **Moving Qi between the Kidneys** <note>considered the fundamental "battery" or "pilot light" of human life in traditional Chinese medicine</note>. This is the root of the five solid organs and six hollow organs, the foundation of the twelve meridians, the gate of respiration, and the source of the Triple Burner <note>the system responsible for the movement of fluids and energy through the body's three functional divisions</note>. Another name for it is the **Spirit that Guards against External Pathogens**.

Therefore, this Qi is the fundamental root of a human being. If the root is destroyed, the stems and leaves will naturally wither. When the pulse at the wrist appears balanced yet the person dies, it is because the Generative Qi has been uniquely exhausted within.

<summary>This text explains that the wrist pulse can be misleading; true health depends on the "Moving Qi between the Kidneys," which acts as the body's vital root. If this internal foundation is lost, the patient will die even if their external pulse remains temporarily steady.</summary>

# The Twenty-First Difficulty

The Twenty-First Difficulty states: The *Classic* <note>Referring to the *Nan Jing* or "Classic of Difficulties," a foundational Chinese medical text.</note> says that if a person’s **physical form** is diseased but their **pulse** is not, it is called **life** <note>In this context, "life" refers to a positive prognosis or the likelihood of survival.</note>. The answer is: When we say a person’s **physical form** is diseased while the **pulse** is not, it does not mean the pulse is actually healthy. It means that the **breath-count** <term>breath-count (xī shù): a diagnostic technique where the physician counts the patient's heartbeats against the duration of a single respiratory cycle (one inhalation and one exhalation)</term> does not correspond to the **pulse rate**. This is the **Great Law** <note>original: "大法" (dà fǎ). This indicates a fundamental, unchanging principle of medical diagnosis.</note>.

***

### Explanatory Notes on the Text

In the tradition of ancient medicine, the "Form" (**xíng**) represents the external, observable body—the muscles, skin, and overall vitality. The "Pulse" (**mài**) represents the internal state of the organs and the flow of vital energy (Qi). 

This text presents a paradox:
1. **The Body is Sick, but the Pulse is "Healthy":** If a person looks very ill but their pulse remains steady and follows the correct rhythm relative to their breathing, it suggests they still possess internal "Root Qi." Their body is fighting, and they are likely to recover.
2. **The Body looks Healthy, but the Pulse is "Sick":** If a person appears physically strong but their pulse is erratic or disconnected from their breathing, it is a sign of "False Spirit." The internal foundation has collapsed, and the external appearance is merely a mask. This is considered a fatal sign.

The **Great Law** mentioned here reminds the physician that the pulse must always be measured against the breath. A pulse is not "healthy" just because it feels strong; it is healthy when it is in harmony with the patient’s respiration.

# The Nineteenth Difficulty

The Nineteenth Difficulty asks: The pulses have patterns of "contrary" and "favorable" <note>In Chinese medicine, "favorable" (shun) indicates a pulse that matches the condition, while "contrary" (ni) suggests a dangerous misalignment.</note>. Men and women have their constants, but what does it mean when these are reversed?

The answer is: Men are born at *Yin* <note>original: "寅". The third Earthly Branch, associated with the Tiger, the season of Spring, and the element of Wood.</note>. *Yin* corresponds to Wood and is Yang in nature. Women are born at *Shen* <note>original: "申". The ninth Earthly Branch, associated with the Monkey, the season of Autumn, and the element of Metal.</note>. *Shen* corresponds to Metal and is Yin in nature.

Therefore, the male pulse is [typically stronger] at the positions above the **Gate** <term>Guan (Gate): the middle position of the three pulse points on the wrist, serving as the pivot between the upper and lower body</term>, while the female pulse is [typically stronger] at the positions below the **Gate**.

Because of this, it is the normal state for a man’s **Cubital pulse** <term>Chi (Cubital): the pulse position closest to the elbow, which reflects the state of the Kidneys and the lower body</term> to be constantly weak, and for a woman’s **Cubital pulse** to be constantly flourishing. This is the standard regularity.

A "reversal" occurs when a man has a woman’s pulse, or a woman has a man’s pulse. What kind of sicknesses do these signify?

When a man has a woman’s pulse, it signifies a disease of **Insufficiency** <note>original: "不足" (bu zu). A state of deficiency where the body's vital energy or blood is depleted.</note>. The illness is located in the interior. If the pulse is found [to be abnormal] on the left, the illness is on the left; if found on the right, the illness is on the right. One identifies the location by following the pulse.

When a woman has a man’s pulse, it signifies a disease of **Excess** <note>original: "太過" (tai guo). A state of "surplus" where pathogens are strong or energy is pathologically overactive.</note>. The illness is located in the four limbs. If the pulse is found on the left, the illness is on the left; if found on the right, the illness is on the right. One identifies the location by following the pulse. This is the meaning of the reversal.

# The Twenty-Second Difficulty

**The Twenty-Second Difficulty asks:** The Classics state that within the pulse [and its meridians], there are "movement-disturbances" <term>Shi Dong: 是動; literally "this movement." These are ailments typically affecting the exterior or the channel pathway itself.</term> and "ailments of what is produced" <term>Suo Sheng Bing: 所生病; literally "diseases of that which is produced." These typically refer to internal organ ailments that manifest in the blood.</term>. How is it that one single meridian can transform into these two distinct types of illness?

**The answer is:** The Classics state that "movement-disturbances" relate to the **Qi** <note>Vital Energy or Life Force</note>, while "ailments of what is produced" relate to the **Blood**. When a pathogen <note>An external or internal cause of disease, such as wind, cold, or emotional stress.</note> resides in the Qi, it manifests as a movement-disturbance. When a pathogen resides in the Blood, it manifests as an ailment of what is produced.

# The Nature of Qi and Blood: A Comparison

---

->**Qi**<-
<note>The active, Yang component of the body</note>

- ○ It is the first to be affected by disease; therefore, it first manifests as **Shi Dong** (movement-disturbance).
- ○ Movement is the essential characteristic of Qi.
- ○ Health resides in its harmony.
- ○ Its primary function is to **warm and animate** the body <note>original: "主煦之" (zhu xu zhi). In traditional medicine, Qi is the active, warming force, often compared to the sun's rays or the warmth of breath.</note>.
- ○ When Qi is obstructed and fails to flow, it is designated as a "primary illness" of the Qi.

---

->**Blood**<-
<note>The receptive, Yin component of the body</note>

- ○ It is affected following the Qi; thus, the illness manifests as **Suo Sheng** (ailments of what is produced).
- ○ These are the diseases involving the physical substance produced by the organs.
- ○ Health resides in its smooth presence.
- ○ Its primary function is to **nourish and moisten** the body <note>original: "主濡之" (zhu ru zhi). Blood provides the fluid and nutrients necessary to keep tissues supple.</note>.
- ○ When the Blood becomes stagnant and fails to moisten the tissues, it is called a "secondary illness" of the Blood.

---

**Therefore, the movement-disturbance occurs first, and the ailment of what is produced follows.**
