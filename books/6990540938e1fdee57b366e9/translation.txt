# PREFACE.

The usual point of view in the study of mechanics <note>original: "mechanics"; the branch of applied mathematics dealing with motion and forces</note> is one where attention is mainly directed to the changes that take place over time within a specific system. The principal problem is determining the state of the system—specifically its configuration <note>the positions of all its parts</note> and its velocities—at any required time, provided its state has been defined for at least one point in time. In this approach, the fundamental equations are those that express the continuous changes occurring within that single system. Inquiries of this kind are often simplified by considering alternative states of the system other than those it actually passes through, but our attention is not usually extended beyond conditions that differ only infinitesimally <note>by an immeasurably small amount</note> from those regarded as actual.

For some purposes, however, it is desirable to take a broader view of the subject. We may imagine a great number of systems of the same nature, but differing in the configurations and velocities they possess at a given instant. These differences need not be merely infinitesimal; they may be varied enough to embrace every conceivable combination of configuration and velocity. In this case, we set a different problem: not to follow one particular system through its succession of states, but to determine how the entire group of systems will be distributed among the various conceivable configurations and velocities at any required time, provided the initial distribution was given for some starting time. The fundamental equation for this inquiry is the one that provides the rate of change in the number of systems that fall within any infinitesimal limits of configuration and velocity.

Such inquiries have been called *statistical* by Maxwell. They belong to a branch of mechanics which owes its origin to the desire to explain the laws of thermodynamics through mechanical principles. Clausius, Maxwell, and Boltzmann are to be regarded as the principal founders of this field. The first inquiries in this area were indeed somewhat narrower in scope than those previously mentioned, as they were applied to the individual particles of a single system rather than to a collection of independent systems. 

Statistical inquiries were next directed toward "phases"—or the specific conditions of a system regarding its physical arrangement and velocity—as they succeed one another in a given system over time. The explicit consideration of a great number of systems and their distribution across various phases, as well as the permanence or alteration of this distribution over time, is perhaps first found in Boltzmann’s 1871 paper, "On the Connection between the Theorems regarding the Behavior of Multi-atomic Gas Molecules and Jacobi’s Principle of the Last Multiplier" <note>original: "Zusammenhang zwischen den Sätzen über das Verhalten mehratomiger Gasmoleküle mit Jacobi’s Princip des letzten Multiplicators"</note>.

But although statistical mechanics historically owes its origin to investigations in thermodynamics, it seems eminently worthy of being developed as an independent discipline. This is true both because of the elegance and simplicity of its principles, and because it yields new results and places old truths in a new light in departments quite outside of thermodynamics. Moreover, the separate study of this branch of mechanics seems to provide the best foundation for the study of rational thermodynamics <note>A theoretical approach to thermodynamics based on rigorous mathematical axioms</note> and molecular mechanics.

The laws of thermodynamics, as determined by observation and experiment, express the approximate and probable behavior of systems consisting of a great number of particles. More precisely, they express the laws of mechanics for such systems as they appear to human beings, who lack the fineness of perception necessary to perceive quantities on the scale of single particles, and who cannot repeat their experiments often enough to obtain anything but the most probable results. The laws of statistical mechanics apply to conservative systems <note>Systems where the total mechanical energy is conserved over time</note> of any number of degrees of freedom <note>The number of independent ways a system can move or change</note>,

Even when we consider phenomena that are distinctively thermodynamic, we do not escape difficulties in a matter as simple as the number of degrees of freedom <note>The number of independent ways a molecule can move or store energy.</note> of a diatomic gas. It is well known that while theory would assign six degrees of freedom to each molecule of the gas, in our experiments on specific heat we cannot account for more than five. Certainly, anyone who rests his work on hypotheses concerning the internal constitution of matter is building on an insecure foundation.

Difficulties of this kind have deterred the author from attempting to explain the mysteries of nature. Instead, they have forced him to be contented with the more modest aim of deducing some of the more obvious propositions relating to the statistical branch of mechanics. In this field, there can be no mistake regarding whether the hypotheses agree with the facts of nature, because no assumptions are made in that respect. The only error one can fall into is a lack of agreement between the premises and the conclusions; with care, one may hope to avoid this for the most part.

The content of the present volume consists largely of results obtained by the investigators mentioned previously, although the point of view and the arrangement may be different. Because these results were given to the public one by one in the order of their discovery, their original presentations were not necessarily arranged in the most logical manner.

In the first chapter, we consider the general problem mentioned above and find what may be called the fundamental equation of statistical mechanics. A particular case of this equation will provide the condition of statistical equilibrium—that is <note>original: *i. e.* (id est)</note>, the condition that the distribution of the systems in phase <note>The "phase" of a system refers to its complete state, defined by the positions and velocities of all its parts.</note> must satisfy so that the distribution remains permanent. In the general case, the fundamental equation allows for an integration which yields a principle that can be expressed in various ways depending on the perspective: as the conservation of density-in-phase, the conservation of extension-in-phase, or the conservation of the probability of phase.

In the second chapter, we apply this principle of the conservation of probability of phase to the theory of errors regarding the calculated phases of a system, specifically when the determination of the arbitrary constants of the integral equations is subject to error. In this application, we do not go beyond the usual approximations. In other words, we combine the principle of conservation of probability of phase—which is exact—with those approximate relations that are customarily assumed in the "theory of errors." <note>The "theory of errors" refers to the statistical study of uncertainties in physical measurements.</note>

In the third chapter, we apply the principle of the conservation of extension-in-phase to the integration of the differential equations of motion. As Ludwig Boltzmann has shown, this yields what is known as Jacobi’s "last multiplier." <note>original: "last multiplier"; a mathematical factor used in solving differential equations, named after Carl Jacobi.</note>

In the fourth and following chapters, we return to the consideration of statistical equilibrium and confine our attention to conservative systems. <note>A conservative system is one where the total mechanical energy remains constant over time.</note> We especially consider "ensembles" <term>large collections of independent systems used to represent the various possible states of a single system</term> in which the index (or logarithm) of the probability of phase is a linear function of the energy. Because of its unique importance in the theory of statistical equilibrium, I have ventured to call this distribution **canonical**, and I call the divisor of the energy the **modulus** of distribution. The moduli of these ensembles have properties analogous to temperature; specifically, equality between the moduli is a condition for equilibrium regarding the exchange of energy, whenever such an exchange is made possible.

We find a differential equation relating to the average values within the ensemble that is identical in form to the fundamental differential equation of thermodynamics. In this comparison, the average index of probability of phase (with its sign reversed) corresponds to **entropy**, and the modulus corresponds to **temperature**.

For the average square of the energy anomalies <note>original: "anomalies"; here meaning the deviations or fluctuations from the average value</note>, we find an expression that becomes insignificant compared to the square of the average energy when the number of degrees of freedom is increased indefinitely. An ensemble of systems in which the number of degrees of freedom is of the same order of magnitude as the number of molecules in physical bodies...

We meet with other quantities, in the development of the subject, which—when the number of degrees of freedom <note>The number of independent ways a system can move or store energy</note> is very great—coincide significantly with the modulus <note>In Gibbs's terms, a quantity proportional to temperature</note> and with the average index of probability, taken negatively, in a canonical ensemble. These, therefore, may also be regarded as corresponding to temperature and entropy. The correspondence is, however, imperfect when the number of degrees of freedom is not very great, and there is nothing to recommend these quantities except that their definitions may be regarded as simpler than those previously mentioned. In Chapter XIV, this subject of thermodynamic analogies is discussed at some length.

Finally, in Chapter XV, we consider the modification of the preceding results which is necessary when we consider systems composed of a number of entirely similar particles. This may also apply to systems of several kinds of particles, where all particles of each kind are entirely similar to each other, and when one of the variations to be considered is the change in the number of particles of various kinds contained in a system. This supposition would naturally have been introduced earlier if our object had been simply the expression of the laws of nature. It seemed desirable, however, to separate sharply the purely thermodynamic laws from those special modifications which belong rather to the theory of the properties of matter.

## APPLICATION OF THE PRINCIPLE OF CONSERVATION OF EXTENSION-IN-PHASE TO THE THEORY OF ERRORS.

# CANONICAL DISTRIBUTION

If the probability of a system's state is a linear function of its energy, we shall say that the ensemble <note>original: "ensemble"; a large collection of independent systems considered simultaneously</note> is **canonically distributed**. We shall call the divisor of the energy ($\Theta$) the **modulus of distribution**.

The fractional part of a canonically distributed ensemble which lies within any given limits of phase <note>original: "phase"; the complete state of a system defined by its positions and momenta</note> is therefore represented by the multiple integral:

->$\int \dots \int e^{\frac{\psi - \epsilon}{\Theta}} dp_1 \dots dq_n \quad (93)$<-

calculated within those limits. We may express the same thing by saying that this multiple integral expresses the probability that an unspecified system of the ensemble (that is, one of which we only know that it belongs to the ensemble) falls within the given limits.

Since the value of a multiple integral of the form (23)—which we have called an **extension-in-phase**—bounded by any given phases is independent of the system of coordinates by which it is evaluated, the same must be true of the multiple integral in (92). This is immediately apparent if we divide this integral into parts so small that the exponential factor may be regarded as constant in each part. The value of $\psi$ is therefore independent of the system of coordinates used.

It is evident that $\psi$ might be defined as the energy for which the coefficient of probability of phase has the value of one. However, since this coefficient has the dimensions of the inverse $n$th power of the product of energy and time,<note>See Chapter I, p. 19.</note> the energy represented by $\psi$ is not independent of the units of energy and time. But once these units have been chosen, the definition of $\psi$ will involve the same arbitrary constant as the energy $\epsilon$. Thus, while in any given case the numerical values of $\psi$ or $\epsilon$ will be entirely indefinite until the zero-point of energy has also been fixed for the system, the difference $\psi - \epsilon$ will represent a perfectly definite amount of energy. This difference is entirely independent of the zero-point of energy that we may choose to adopt.

It is evident that the canonical distribution is entirely determined by the modulus <note>The modulus, denoted by the Greek letter Theta ($\Theta$), is a parameter in the distribution that functions like temperature.</note> (considered as a quantity of energy) and the nature of the system being considered. This is because when equation (92) is satisfied, the value of the multiple integral (93) is independent of the units used, the coordinates employed, and the starting point (zero) chosen for the energy of the system.

In treating the canonical distribution, we shall always assume that the multiple integral in equation (92) has a finite value. Otherwise, the coefficient of probability vanishes <note>becomes zero</note>, and the law of distribution becomes meaningless. This assumption will exclude certain cases, but apparently none that will affect the value of our results regarding their application to thermodynamics. 

For instance, it will exclude cases where the system, or parts of it, can be spread out in unlimited space (or in a space that has limits but is still infinite in volume) while the energy remains below a finite limit. It also excludes many cases where the energy can decrease without limit, such as when the system contains material points <note>idealized particles with mass but no size</note> that attract one another inversely as the squares of their distances <note>This refers to Newtonian gravity or electrostatic attraction, where particles could theoretically release infinite energy as they get infinitely close.</note>. # OTHER DISTRIBUTIONS

The properties of statistical equilibrium <note>original: "statistical equilibrium"</note> which have been described are unique to it, or we may ask whether other distributions might have similar properties.

Let η' and η'' <note>η is the Greek letter eta, used here to represent the index of probability</note> be the indices of probability in two independent ensembles <note>An ensemble is a large collection of independent systems used to represent the various possible states of a single system</note> which are each in statistical equilibrium. Then η' + η'' will be the index in the ensemble obtained by combining each system of the first ensemble with each system of the second. This third ensemble will, of course, be in statistical equilibrium, and the function of phase η' + η'' will be a constant of motion <note>A value that does not change as the system evolves over time</note>. 

Now, when infinitesimal <note>extremely small</note> forces are added to the combined systems, if η' + η'' (or a function differing only slightly from it) remains a constant of motion, it must be because of the nature of the forces added, or—if their action is not fully specified—because of the conditions to which they are subject. Thus, in the case already considered, η' + η'' is a function of the energy of the combined system, and the tiny forces added are subject to the law of conservation of energy.

<margin>||</margin> Another natural assumption regarding the added forces is that they should be such that they do not affect the moments of momentum <note>Also known as angular momentum; the measure of an object's rotation</note> of the combined system. To create a case where the moments of momentum of the combined system are constants of motion, we may imagine material particles contained within two concentric spherical shells. These particles are prevented from passing the surfaces of the shells by repulsive forces that always act in lines passing through the common center of the shells. Then, if there are no forces acting between particles in different shells, the mass of particles in each shell will have—besides its energy—the <note>The following phrase is circled in the original:</note> moments of momentum about three axes through the center as constants of motion.

Now let us imagine an ensemble formed by distributing the system of particles in one shell in phase according to the index of probability:

However, the sum of the partial energies for all the original types of vibration that have the same period will be nearly identical (as a function of phase—that is, of the coordinates and momenta) with the sum of the partial energies for the normal types of vibration that have the same, or nearly the same, period after the addition of the new forces. Therefore, if the partial energies in the indices of the first two ensembles (101) and (102) relating to types of vibration with the same periods have the same divisors, the same will be true of the index (103) of the ensemble of compound systems. The distribution represented will differ only infinitely little from one that would be in statistical equilibrium after the addition of the new forces.*

The same would be true if, in the indices of each of the original ensembles, we substituted any function of the total energy related to that period for the term or terms relating to any period which does not occur in the other ensemble, subject only to the general limitation expressed by equation (89). But in order for the ensemble of compound systems (with the added forces) to always be approximately in statistical equilibrium, it is necessary that the indices of the original ensembles should be linear functions of those partial energies relating to vibrations of periods common to the two ensembles, and that the coefficients of such partial energies should be the same in the two indices.†

The properties of canonically distributed ensembles of systems—specifically regarding the equilibrium of new ensembles formed by combining each system of one ensemble with each system of another—are not unique to them. Returning to the case of the canonical distribution, we shall find other analogies with thermodynamic systems if we suppose (as in the preceding chapters\*) that the potential energy ($\epsilon_q$) depends not only upon the coordinates $q_1 \dots q_n$ which determine the configuration of the system, but also upon certain coordinates $a_1, a_2$, etc., of bodies which we call *external*.
