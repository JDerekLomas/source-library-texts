***W*** *ithout a doubt, Medicine, when it was first born, was the knowledge of very few herbs, especially those which served as food for mankind. For in order that mortals might preserve their health as whole as possible, they learned above all else to distinguish the healthful from the unhealthful. And when they perceived that there was a medicinal power even in vegetables and fruits—and that among them some loosened the bowels, others obstructed the same, some cooled, and so forth—from this they began gradually to think about a dietary regimen in diseases, and afterwards also about the use of the remaining plants. For it was not difficult to argue from the lesser to the greater: when they saw that the body was manifestly altered by those things which they chose as the mildest for food, they rightly judged that the same could be done even more so by other things endowed with a more excellent smell and taste. Therefore, driven by necessity, they did not hesitate to try whatever was at hand; and thus, little by little, the multitude of the sick and the healers brought forth an abundance of remedies. For both the Greeks and the Arabs, and Barbarians <note>In this context, "Barbarians" refers to non-Greek or non-Latin speaking peoples, often meaning those from Northern Europe or the East.</note> of every kind, experienced the same fate; hence it is no wonder that the Materia Medica <note>The body of collected knowledge about the therapeutic properties of any substance used for healing.</note> today consists of so many exotic things, so many noble and ignoble, so many praiseworthy and unpraiseworthy, after the learned and the unlearned from the creation of the world until this very hour have heaped up whatever pleased them. # CHAPTER I.
## Concerning Foreign Items, not pertaining to the Medical Workshop.

**§. 1.**
**A**lthough the Catalog of Workshops <note>original: "Catalogus Officinarum"; refers to the official inventory or price list of a pharmacy</note> promises nothing but medicines in its titles <note>original: "in rubro," literally 'in red,' referring to the red-inked headings in legal or formal documents</note>, in the actual text <note>original: "in nigro," literally 'in black'</note> there occur not a few things which by no means deserve that name. Nor would I make mention of these, were it not for the fear that I might seem to have failed to notice them, as they are rashly scattered here and there among the medicines whose censorship I have undertaken. I suppose, of course, as a firm principle, that the Duty of the Pharmacist <note>original: "Pharmacopæus"</note> consists in this: that he prepares for the sick the medicines prescribed by the Physician <note>original: "Medicus"</note>. Whatever, therefore, is not employed by Physicians for the cure of human beings, pertains in no way to the workshop.

**§. 2.**
Now indeed I include, **1.** *those things intended for brute animals:* *Horse Aloes* <note>original: "Aloe caballina"; a coarse, impure grade of aloes used in veterinary medicine</note>, *Horse Sulfur* <note>original: "Sulphur caballinum"; impure sulfur</note>, *Ointment for horse hooves*, and another *against horse mange*, *powder for cattle*, *powder for pigs*. Is it not beneath the dignity of the Pharmacist to prepare, keep, and sell things which by better right would adorn the workshop of a smith or the stable of a groom, or the sheepfold, or the bag of a herdsman or swineherd? And just as it is hardly becoming for a Physician to practice Veterinary medicine <note>original: "artem Veterinariam"</note>: so too he cannot escape the laughter and mockery of those men, who would dare to meddle in such things or hope for any small profit therefrom, for the healing of which any uneducated person has found innumerable remedies everywhere at hand.

**§. 3.**
What shall I say **2.** regarding *poisons*, such as *white*, *yellow*, and *red Arsenic*, *Orpiment* <note>original: "Auripigmentum"; a deep yellow arsenic sulfide mineral</note>, and *Cobalt*? We use them for killing dormice, mice, and flies, but by a prudent and conscientious Physician they are scarcely—if at all—counted among Medicines. ### § 5.

If anyone were to reject the 4. *Cosmetics* <note>original: "Cosmetica"</note> of *Croll* <note>Oswald Croll (c. 1563–1609), a famous practitioner of iatrochemistry or "chemical medicine."</note> or others from the Pharmacopoeia <note>The official list of approved medicinal drugs.</note>, they would find me easily in agreement. I am not unaware that there have been physicians at all times who have taken the care of such things upon themselves. What of it? Even the Chemists <note>Alchemists</note> promise themselves no less riches from the "Oil of Talc" than from the Philosopher's Stone itself, since a lady's rouge is valued in the court as dearer than any gold. Nevertheless, it seems beneath the dignity of Physicians and Pharmacists to insert into the catalog of official medicines those things which are commonly for sale at every perfumer's shop <note>original: "myropolas"</note>. Such items are: *small silk cloths soaked in red dye*, called *Pezette* by the Italians, used for tinting the cheeks and lips; *Talc, prepared however to provide a luster to the skin; Venetian and scented soap with a sea sponge*, which is more useful to bath-house keepers for wiping away filth.

### § 6.

Let the same judgment be held 5. *concerning Perfumes* <note>original: "Odoramentis"</note> in general. Who uses *Ambergris apples <note>Scented pomanders</note>, musk-scented lozenges, "French musk" tablets, benzoin with musk, rose with musk*—whether for incense *with or without musk, incense sticks with or without musk, incense candles with or without musk, or incense powders* (both the common ones and those for the rich and poor)—in the actual care of the sick? Does anyone seek *Cyprian powder* from the Pharmacy? Barbers and those who devote their labor solely to curling and arranging hair for ornament scatter it almost for free and sell it at a tiny price. I will not mention that there are perfumers <note>original: "seplasiarios"</note> who make a profession of preparing and mixing excellent fragrances, from which singular and entirely new scents emerge, with which the old and worn-out compositions can in no way be compared. Therefore, even the balsams, as they are kept in the Pharmacies, are not of great value, if you except the anti-apoplectic balsam <note>Used against strokes or "apoplexy"</note> and perhaps the balsam of rue, which is sometimes desired in cases of necessity.

### §. 9.

Truly, there are still many items occurring in section 8 which suit domestic and mechanical uses more than they do medicinal formulas. *Spanish wax <note>original: "Cera Hispanica"; likely sealing wax</note>, both red and green, is fit for sealing; grafting wax is more useful to gardeners.* **Naval pitch** is for coopers and shipbuilders; **Colophony** <note>a type of resin</note> for lute-players; **Isinglass** <note>original: "Ichthyocolla"; a collagen from fish bladders</note> is convenient for wine-merchants for clarifying wine, and for joiners for gluing. **Tripoli powder, Emery, Pumice, and Schist Alum** are best for polishing metals, glass, and gems, and are extremely necessary for craftsmen; just as **Gypsum and Quicklime** are for cement-makers and stonemasons. But to what end should I mention *yellow and native sulfur, or the ores of antimony and mercury?* For as long as flowers of sulfur are available and not too costly, we can do without yellow and native sulfur. Nor do we use the ores of antimony or mercury in medicines, but rather preparations made from antimony and native cinnabar.

### §. 10.

So that we may contract what has been reviewed so far into a summary, we conclude thus: Whatever things are not employed for the care of sick humans ought to be cast out from the Catalog of Official Medicines as foreign. But remedies intended for beasts, Poisons, Pigments, Cosmetics, Perfumes, Sugared items, and those preserved in salt and vinegar—which are kept for other domestic or mechanical uses—are not employed for the care of sick humans. Therefore, they ought to be cast out from the Catalog of Official Medicines as foreign.

In this way, wretched people are greatly deterred from the use of <term>Medicines</term> <note>original: "Medicamentorum"</note>, nor do they easily feel any relief from those things which nature abhors. Thus, truly, he prostrates the best of Arts and himself, and exposes them to the contempt and mockery of the common people, who does not fear—amidst such an abundance of excellent remedies—to offer any shameful things, troublesome with a cadaverous and dung-like stench, unpleasant with a highly nauseating taste, and abominable in their whole substance to even a less delicate person, or even by their name alone.

->§. 2.<-

For let us suppose that some healing power <note>original: "medendi vim"</note> resides in them; must they therefore be offered to the sick? I should not think so. It certainly proves a physician to be of remarkable simplicity if, by chance, he is unwilling to change those things which were used by the Ancients in the absence of better ones. But he will be judged less prudent or learned if he does not know how to substitute other, far more pleasing things in place of such nauseating ones. ### §. 1.

**S**ince men of every race and nation, as was said above, have contributed their own tokens <note>original: "symbolas," referring to contributions or shares</note> to Medicine, there is no doubt that many additions have also been made of old wives' tales, and of superstitious or magical remedies—that is to say, things that are entirely vain and utterly impious. Indeed, the very beginnings of our Art, which were in Egypt, are hardly able to conceal the vanities of dreams and Oracles. Although that most ancient method of seeking and obtaining help has ceased in these times, there are nonetheless remnants still constantly preserved and passed, as it were, from hand to hand. What more? Even now, many feel no religious scruple in imploring witches <note>original: "lamias," referring to mythical female demons or sorceresses</note> and deceivers; just as they cast the causes of most diseases upon sorcery, so they think they cannot obtain a more successful remedy from anywhere else than from magicians or a "wise woman." Yet I would hardly pay attention to this worst habit of the common people, if only Physicians—shameful to say—did not stain themselves with the same plague, while in imitation of Paracelsus <note>Theophrastus von Hohenheim (1493–1541), a famous Swiss physician and alchemist who challenged traditional medical authority</note> they boast without shame of having learned anything even from the Evil Spirit <note>original: "Cacodæmone," a malevolent spirit or devil</note> himself.

### §. 4.

Furthermore, the powers of precious substances are presumed to be superior based on a rather slippery foundation. For thus far, it is neither established through experience that they are more beneficial in healing than any common remedy, nor does sound reason dictate that those solid things which are almost immutable—such as **Gold** and **Gems**—possess much value in the cure of diseases. If indeed **Pearls**, no less than <term>Bezoar</term> <note>A stony mass found in the stomachs of animals, historically prized as an antidote to poison</note>, are merely morbid stones, what compels me to believe they are therefore more effective against other ailments? 

But perhaps it is rarity that is valued in these things, or their elegance, splendor, and color. What has this to do with **Medicine**? You say: "They serve for decoration, and are reserved especially for magnates and the wealthier sort; for who would not prefer to prescribe oriental Pearls to a Prince rather than cuttlefish bones, even for heartburn or a similar minor ill?" I grant you this: but are they therefore better and <term>cordial</term> <note>Medicines intended to invigorate the heart and spirit</note>? It does not follow. If such things were precious because they were more effective, I would say nothing; but since they are thought to be more effective because they are precious, I cannot help but declare this estimation truly superstitious. 

And yet it is certain that a price was assigned to Gold, Gems, and pearls long before anyone began to think of their internal use. It is told that the son of a wealthy peasant, when his father was ill, bought a Canary bird—which had learned to sing in imitation of a pipe—for twelve <term>Hungarian ducats</term> <note>original: "Ungaris"; gold coins of high value</note>, and served it roasted for him to eat for the sake of his recovery. But the father, tasting the rarest little bird and feeling no restoration of strength from it, said, "Ah, my son, you have been deceived! If there is any virtue in the price, why did you not give me gold coins to swallow? For I could have continued eating those as long as I pleased." But I believe neither Gold, nor things even more precious, are of any help; otherwise, no Prince would ever die. No one would die in the <term>New World</term> <note>original: "Orbe novo"; referring to the Americas</note>, where I hear Gold, Silver, Gems, and Pearls are as common as river pebbles are on our banks. Thus even the gardener spoke very opportunely.

## §. 5.

Let us proceed to other matters and contemplate two specific superstitions; permit me to address them by this name, as they are commonly believed to be appropriate for no other reason than that they possess either some resemblance, or a closer suitability and affinity with certain limbs of the body. The leaves of *Balm* <note>original: "Melissophylli"</note> bear, according to Croll <note>Oswald Croll (c. 1563–1609), a professor of medicine and proponent of alchemy and signatures.</note>, the appearance of a heart (and does not the *Nettle* as well?), and so their qualities are considered excellent against ailments of the Heart. The *Wolf's Tooth* is considered most useful for facilitating the eruption of teeth in children, and for that reason it is kept in the Workshops <note>The "Officinis" or apothecary shops.</note>, to be adorned with silver if one pleases. Nor is it for nothing that one attends to the distinction of sex in applying remedies: for that purpose, the fat of, for example, the *Male Bear and Female Bear* is kept separately in the Shops, so that for a man the male's fat, and for women the female's fat, might be available as undoubtedly more suitable. The Ancients do not seem to have observed this formerly, when they recommended Ivory to the weaker sex: but in these days it would not be beside the point to explore more accurately whether the tooth of a *Sea Cow* <note>likely a Manatee or Dugong</note> might facilitate a difficult birth more than the *Tooth of a male Hippopotamus*.

### §. 6.

To be sure, these matters savor of a "shrewd philosophy," just as those things which are collected superstitiously, whether by reason of the method or by reason of a specific time. It is well known regarding the young shoots of the Elder tree <note>original: "cymis Sambuci"; the tender top-growths of the elderberry bush</note> that when used in salads they move the bowels, and sometimes even induce vomiting, especially if they are plucked in an upward direction. Schroeder notes the same effect when they are reduced to powder in his *Pharmacopoeia Medico-Chymica*, page 677. Van Helmont <note>Jan Baptista van Helmont (1580–1644), a famous Flemish chemist and physician</note> provides the reason for this in his work *On the Magnetic Cure of Wounds*, section 170. # CHAPTER VII.
### Concerning
## Inappropriately Mixed Substances.

->§. 1.<-

**I**f the nature of Simple Medicines <note>Medicines consisting of a single natural ingredient</note> were such that for every disease there existed at least one remedy satisfying all circumstances, we would have no need for any compounding. However, because the same disease frequently varies according to the diversity of the subjects <note>The individual patients</note> and is accompanied by different symptoms, it is therefore necessary to mix Simples more frequently so that the indications of the illness are satisfied in every part. Yet this must be done with judgment, lest anything be added in vain, rashly, or foolishly. For if a composition of Medicines is to be established properly, one must attend to a great many circumstances: the potency, taste, smell, color, consistency, quantity, number, proportion, and order of the ingredients. He who neglects these does not seem to mix Medicines, but rather to confound or even corrupt them. Hence it is no wonder if patients frequently feel greater relief and help from the use of Simples—perhaps recommended by a simple root-gatherer—or from some ointment not artfully devised, than from the entire apparatus of Surgeons, <term>Pharmacists</term> <note>original: "Pharmacopoeorum"</note>, or even Physicians, however impressive and prepared with great labor and expense.

Let us suppose it to be either an antidote to poison <note>original: "alexipharmacam"</note> or a deadly substance <note>original: "deleteriam"</note>; what is to be hoped or feared from it, when not even a single grain of it remains in a dose of a full drachm? But it will be best either to omit what is suspect in a doubtful case, or rather to reject the whole compound entirely, lest by mutilating the formula we are unjust to the Author.

### §. 8.

To what end, I ask? Naturally, so that the <term>Theriac</term> <note>A famous "universal antidote" or polypharmacy containing dozens of ingredients, often used as a status symbol of medical complexity.</note> might come forth more augmented. But let us suppose that all these materials cohere together perfectly in substance; yet, viewed in their form, they display a more than childish simplicity. Does not sound reason dictate that likes should be joined together, and contraries separated? Likewise, that in administering milder medicines we ought to be more liberal, and in potent ones more sparing, and so forth. Why, then, are <term>Anodynes</term> <note>Pain-relievers</note>, <term>Purgatives</term> <note>Laxatives</note>, <term>Aperients</term> <note>Medicines to "open" the pores or vessels</note>, and <term>Astringents</term> <note>Substances that contract tissues</note> not properly categorized?

If indeed that task were to be too laborious, why are the roots, herbs, seeds, juices, etc., not reduced into certain classes, or at the very least, why are the ingredients not listed in alphabetical order, so that they might at least appear to be arranged by some sort of method? What shall I say regarding Proportion? A most generous dose is granted to those things which require a more sparing one, and conversely, the most sparing dose to those which should have been offered more largely. Things of plainly different nature and efficacy are prescribed in equal weight; yet those whose virtue is not dissimilar are prescribed in unequal weight. Let <term>Opium</term> serve as an example: three ounces of it are received, while of <term>Saffron</term> <note>original: "Croci"</note> six drams; <term>Gum Arabic</term> and <term>Cardamom</term> are given in equal weight; of <term>Long Pepper</term> three ounces are taken, but of black and white pepper, six drams. If you demand a reason for this, none can be rendered other than that the Authors wished it so; perhaps they even had others whom they imitated. For even more recent writers think that today they are still permitted to do what was not prohibited in the past.

The *Pills of Hound's-tongue* <note>original: "Pilulæ de Cynoglossa"</note>, as they are read in the *Augsburg Pharmacopoeia* <note>original: "Pharmacopœa Augustana," a highly influential 17th-century pharmaceutical manual</note> corrected by moderns with <term>Castoreum</term> <note>A secretion from beavers used in medicine</note>, take *half an ounce each of Opium, Hound's-tongue root, and Henbane seed, and one and a half drams of Saffron.* Who would not rather approve of one ounce of Saffron and one dram of Opium, while rejecting the <term>Henbane</term> <note>original: "Hyoscyamo," a toxic plant of the nightshade family</note>? Is <term>Colocynth</term> <note>Bitter apple, a harsh purgative</note> rightly mixed in equal weight with <term>Hermodactyls</term> <note>Likely a species of Colchicum or iris root</note>, <term>Myrobalans</term> <note>Astringent fruit</note>, <term>Senna leaves</term>, <term>Agaric</term> <note>A medicinal fungus</note>, and <term>Epithyme</term> <note>Dodder, a parasitic plant</note>? Yet <term>Mesue</term> <note>A famous 9th-century Persian physician</note> orders this in his *Pills of Hermodactyls*, as does <term>Nicolaus</term> <note>Likely Nicolaus Salernitanus, author of the Antidotarium</note> in his *Pills of Hiera, of Myrobalans, and of the Eight Things*, etc. Therefore, one must strictly adhere to the formulas of medicines, even the most absurd ones.

## §. 9.

Indeed, if these mixtures are tolerated or approved, why do we not simply force all the simple and compound medicines available in the apothecary shop into one single mass? There is no doubt that from such an innumerable collection, one or two ingredients would always be found that are directly opposed to any given disease. This, unless I am mistaken, is what **Matthiolus** <note>Pietro Andrea Mattioli (1501–1577), a famous Italian physician and botanist.</note> had in mind when, for his *Antidote*, he not only included the species required for *Mithridatium* and *Theriac* <note>Mithridatium and Theriac were famous "cure-all" compounds containing dozens of ingredients, including opium and viper flesh.</note> and increased them with many additions, but also mixed in the *Theriac* and *Mithridatium* themselves alongside other compounds—as if something might be omitted in one mixture, it would surely be present in the other. However, **Zwelfer’s** <note>Joannes Zwelfer (1618–1668), a chemist known for his critical commentary on the Pharmacopoeia.</note> *Observations* on that matter deserve to be read. I shall add no more: for I already regret the paper and time spent on this examination of compounds and other absurdities. Regarding compounds, whatever they may be, I firmly decree: let them be abolished entirely. For he seems unworthy of the title of Physician who is not able, whenever he wishes, to prescribe better remedies on the spot, more suited to the state of any particular patient. Indeed, we have seen to the point of nausea how little skill or judgment exists in the compositions known until now. I argue, therefore, in this manner: Whatever medicines are neither useful nor necessary, but in many ways inept, those can be dismissed from the Apothecary shops. But most compounds are neither useful nor necessary, but in many ways inept. Therefore, they can be dismissed from the Apothecary shops.

## §. 10.

Truly, this critique of mine regarding official medicines will seem to many to be overly rigorous.
