# General Preface

My friend Zhao Bohou <note>Original: 趙伯厚</note> of Piling, who serves as the head lecturer at the <term>Zhengyi Academy (Zhengyi Shuyuan)</term>, sent me a letter. 

Wang Zhuofu <note>Original: 王倬甫, referring to the scholar Wang Mingcheng</note> of Jiading is profoundly learned in <term>Philology (Xiaoxue)</term> <note>Literally "Small Learning," this refers to the traditional Chinese study of character forms, sounds, and meanings</note>, having inherited the teachings of his fellow countryman, Qian, the Junior Vice Supervisor of Instruction <note>This refers to the master scholar Qian Daxin (1728–1804), a giant of the Qing Dynasty's intellectual history</note>. He is meticulously dedicated; in setting his ambitions <gloss>from the start</gloss>, he sought to align himself with the scholars of antiquity. This spring, Zhuofu arrived in the capital bearing Bohou’s letter to visit me. Upon meeting him, I found his character to be reserved, sincere, and simple. When I questioned him on his studies, I found his grasp of the meanings within the Classics to be vast and exhaustive.

I have read his writings on historical lexicography. His compositions regarding the <term>Explanation of Graphs and Analysis of Characters (Shuowen Jiezi)</term> <note>The earliest comprehensive Chinese dictionary, compiled in the 2nd century CE</note> consist of four types: *The Rectification*, *Original Sounds*, *Comprehensive Understanding*, and *Annotations*. He explores the profound and searches out the hidden <note>Original: "探賾索隱" (tàn zé suǒ yǐn), a classic idiom for deep scholarly investigation</note>, directly uncovering the fundamental reasons for how phonetics and meanings are derived. His work is not merely a collection of superficial views or strained, forced interpretations.

Regarding the <term>Rites of Zhou (Zhouli)</term>, he followed the structural style of the *Illustrative Examples of the Book of Rites* <gloss>Interpretations</gloss>. Using the "Five Rites"—Auspicious, Ominous, Military, Guest, and Festive—as his primary framework, he meticulously divided them into sub-categories. He analyzed and synthesized the classical text, drawing supplementary material from the *Zuo Commentary*, the *Great Commentary of Ancient Books*, and the *Records of the Elder and Younger Dai* <note>These are all foundational texts of the Confucian canon and historical records</note>. He organized these by category into a work titled <term>Inquiry into the Five Rites of Zhou (Zhou Wuli Kao)</term>.

While residing in the capital for several months, responding to the inquiries of **Vice Minister Lü Hetian** <note>Lü Peifen (1822–1893), a prominent scholar-official</note>, Zhuofu analyzed the merits and errors of various scholarly houses, including those of **Zheng Xuan, Jia Gongyan, Dai Zhen, Cheng Yaotian, and Ruan Yuan** <note>These are the most significant names in the "Evidential Scholarship" movement, known for their rigorous philological analysis of the Classics</note>. Lü Hetian was so deeply impressed <gloss>clapping his hands in rhythm with the insights</gloss> that he wrote a preface for the work and requested that all of Zhuofu’s writings be immediately handed over to the carvers <term>Commit to the woodblock (Dujue): A term for the process of carving characters into wood for printing a book</term>. 

Zhuofu, however, felt that the specialized language of technical families and craftsmen <note>Original: *Chouren* (疇人), historically referring to families who specialized in astronomy or mathematics</note> was not something he was traditionally trained in. He felt hesitant and cautious <gloss>lonely and uncertain</gloss>, fearing his calculations were not yet sufficiently precise. Furthermore, his drafts had not yet undergone a final, rigorous review. He argued his position firmly, and even the influential Lü Hetian could not change his mind. 

At that time, the study of **Evidential Research** <note>Original: *Zhizheng* (致證), the practice of using concrete proof to verify ancient texts</note> was flourishing. However, many petty, unrefined scholars <note>Original: *Zhanbi* (佔畢), a derogatory term for those who merely recite texts without understanding them</note> were undermining the Great Way. They acted like "peddlers" <gloss>trading in scraps</gloss>, plagiarizing and stitching together the work of others, which resulted in a disastrous state for scholarship...

**Master Zheng** <note>Zheng Xuan (127–200 CE), the definitive commentator on the *Rites of Zhou* whose notes are essential to understanding the text</note>, in his commentary on the "Wheelwright" section, states: "The carriage box is 6 *chi* and 6 *cun* wide, and the two **hubs** <term>Hub (*gu*): The central part of the wheel through which the axle passes</term> together measure 6 *chi* and 4 *cun*. Subtracting from within the track... <unclear>one</unclear> *cun*, the total width between the two **hub-ends** <term>Hub-end (*zhi*): The outer protruding ends of the hub/axle area</term> is 1 *zhang*, 1 *chi*, and 6 *cun*." 

**Scholar Dai** <note>Dai Zhen (1724–1777), a brilliant Qing dynasty polymath and expert in ancient engineering</note> expanded upon this, stating that the space between the poles is 6 *chi* and 6 *cun*, and the portion of the hub that enters beneath the carriage box is <unclear>one</unclear> *cun*. Regarding these two theories, the Late Grand Master often remarked that they were difficult to reconcile. 

For instance, if one doubles the 6-*chi* **bow-measurement** <note>Original: *gong* (弓), referring to the curved structural components of the carriage</note> and adds the width of the sections, the total is 1 *zhang* and 2 *chi* 6 *cun*. # Author’s Postscript

I, Zongsu, was born and raised in a land of rivers and lakes. My travels were limited to a few hundred miles, where I relied entirely on boats and oars to move with the wind under the moonlight. By the time I was over forty years old, my understanding of the systems of **wheels, carriage-bodies, shafts, and crossbars** <note>Original: *lun, yu, zhou, heng* (輪輿輈衡), the four primary components of an ancient vehicle</note> was based solely on what I could imagine by following the illustrated explanations of the three masters: **Dai**, **Cheng**, and **Ruan**. <note>Referring to the scholars Dai Zhen, Cheng Yaotian, and Ruan Yuan, whose works were the standard references for ancient technology in the 18th and 19th centuries.</note>

In the *Guimao* year <note>1843</note>, during the second lunar month, I traveled by sea to the &nbsp; Capital. Upon landing at Tianjin, I hired two horse-drawn carriages. One had two shafts and was pulled by a single horse in harness, much like the "Great Carriage" of antiquity. However, its wheels had only sixteen spokes; its hubs were not covered in leather but were instead reinforced with iron at both ends; the horse was hitched without the use of a crossbar; the axle-ends were not carved with hooks; and the **felloes** <term>the wooden rim of the wheel</term> were not bound with rope. These features were utterly different from what is recorded in the *Record of Trades*. <note>The *Record of Trades* (Kao Gong Ji) is the oldest technical encyclopedia in China, dating back over 2,000 years. The author is noting the evolution—or divergence—of technology since that time.</note>

Upon arriving at the &nbsp; Capital, I sought out Censor Chen Songnan and Vice Minister Lü Hetian <note>Lü Xianji, the author of the preceding preface</note> to validate my studies. The Vice Minister used the commentaries of **Zheng**, **Jia**, **Dai**, **Cheng**, and **Ruan** <note>Adding the earlier commentators Zheng Xuan and Jia Gongyan to the list of authorities</note> regarding the four crafts of the wheelwright and carriage-maker to enlighten me significantly. He then commanded me to analyze the merits and flaws of these various interpretations. At that time, my travel trunk contained only the illustrated works of Scholar Dai, Master Cheng, and Master Ruan.

The explanations provided here are all based on excerpts from the *Supplemented Commentary on the Rites of Zhou*, through which I have verified facts and clarified points of doubt. From the summer through the autumn, the general outline of the work was roughly established. Later, I served as an advisor in Dangtu, but I was forced to return to my hometown to flee from the encroaching armies. <note>This refers to the Taiping Rebellion, which devastated the Anhui and Jiangsu regions during the mid-19th century.</note> War pressed in from all sides; we lived in such proximity to death that we were like neighbors to the ditches and ravines. Recently, while attending to my mother and teaching students, I have found a few moments of leisure. The space for installing the spokes and the larger space intended to accommodate the axle are both created by chiseling the wood. If one were to hollow out the center of the hub and *then* shave the exterior of the hub into its final shape, the wood would inevitably be damaged or split. Therefore, the process of shaving the hub’s exterior must be performed before defining the **inner hub-end** <note>original: 賢 (xian) — the side of the hub facing the carriage body</note> and the **outer hub-end** <note>original: 軹 (zhi) — the side facing away from the carriage</note>.

The term for the "hollow" <note>original: 藪 (sou) — literally a thicket or marsh, used here as a technical term</note> should be written as *gao* <note>original: 橰</note>. The *Shuowen Jiezi* <note>the first dictionary of Chinese characters, 2nd century CE</note> states: "The *gao* is the hollow interior of a chariot hub." It is pronounced similarly to *sou*, which is why the two terms were used interchangeably as phonetic substitutes. In a small chariot, this hollow space is two feet from the outer hub-end and one foot from the inner hub-end. In a large cart, the hollow is six inches from both the outer and inner ends, situated exactly in the center of the hub.

**Xu Shen** <note>the author of the *Shuowen*</note> explained that there are thirty hollow spaces <note>the mortises for the thirty spokes</note> gathered into a single hub, which is why it is called *gao*. The character uses a phonetic component that means "a flock of birds crying out together" <note>original: 皋 (gao)</note>.

The term for **remainder** <note>original: 拹 (xie)</note> was anciently interchangeable with the word for "collaboration" or "harmony." It refers to the surplus of a complete number. In the *Book of Changes*, it mentions a "second remainder," and the commentator **Yu Fan** <note>164–233 CE</note> notes that this is the leftover amount from a calculation; this serves as our evidence for the term's meaning.

The "remainder of the enclosure" refers to the surplus of two inches remaining from the three-foot measurement <note>the previous page established a hub circumference of 3 feet 2 inches</note>. To "shave the hollow using the remainder" means to use these two inches as the standard depth for chiseling the mortises. The width of the chisel hole and the **spoke-tenon** <note>original: 菑 (zi) — the end of the spoke that is inserted into the hub</note> must correspond to one another; therefore, the width is also two inches. A later passage says, "measure the depth of the hole to determine the width of the spoke," which is definitive proof of this calculation. If the fit is wide, the mouth of the hole is calculated at one-third of an inch.

Two parts of the measurement correspond to the thickness of the **spoke** <term>original: 輻 (fu)</term>. The base of the **mortise** <term>original: 鑿 (zao) — the hole where the spoke is inserted</term> is reduced by half, resulting in one-third of an inch, which matches the thickness of the spoke’s tapered end. 

The total width of the thirty mortise openings is two feet, while the total width at the bottom of these holes is one foot. Within a total **hub** <term>original: 毂 (gu)</term> circumference of three feet two inches, if we subtract the two feet occupied by the openings, we calculate that the distance between each mortise opening is four *fen* <note>a traditional unit of measurement roughly equivalent to a tenth of an inch</note>. 

Given a hub diameter of one foot, one *fen*, eight *li*, five *hao*, nine *miao*, and one *hu* <note>these are incredibly precise sub-divisions of an inch, used here to demonstrate rigorous mathematical calculation</note>, we subtract four inches <gloss>representing the combined depth of two mortises</gloss> to find the inner circumference. This inner circumference measures one foot, eight inches, nine *fen*, eight *hao*, three *miao*, and five *hu*. After subtracting the one foot occupied by the bases of the mortises, the distance between each mortise base is approximately two *fen*, nine *li*, six *hao*, nine *miao*, and four *hu*.

**Zheng** <note>referring to the commentator Zheng Xuan, 127–200 CE</note> states: The "rib" is one-third of the measurement.

It refers to the hollow, vase-like space inside the hub. Currently, the diameter of this center-hole is measured at three and five-ninths inches. This "vase" area is the section of the hub where the spokes are gathered. The term "beehive" implies a point of convergence; the center-hole is where all the spokes converge.

**Dai** <note>referring to the Qing dynasty scholar Dai Zhen, 1724–1777</note> states: The hollow within the **hub** <term>original: 轂 (gu)</term> is shaped like a vase; this is the part that receives the axle. **Analysis:** In antiquity, there was never a definition of the **fang** <term>original: 防 (fang) — a technical limit or fractional measure</term> as being "one-third." **Zheng’s** <note>the commentator Zheng Xuan</note> explanation is utterly baseless. If the *sou* were indeed the vase-like hollow inside the hub, then it would be located within the "large and small piercings" <note>the openings at either end of the hub for the axle</note>. Anyone wishing to measure the capacity of the *sou* would have to measure both the large and small piercings together. 

However, the text below simply says to "measure the *sou* with **millet**" <note>millet seeds were a standard ancient unit for measuring volume</note>. This implies measuring the *sou* as a single, distinct unit. Therefore, it is quite clear that the *sou* is not the vase-like hollow inside the hub. **Dai** refutes the theory that the *sou* is the place where the thirty spokes are fixed, but in doing so, he "mistakes the straight for the crooked" <note>original: 以不枉為枉 (yi bu wang wei wang) — a metaphor for misidentifying a correct point as an error</note>.

**Cheng** <note>likely the scholar Cheng Yaotian</note> states: The *Royal Regulations* <note>a chapter of the Classic of Rites</note> mentions that for sacrifices, one uses the *fang* of the number, and for mourning, one uses the *fang* of three years. The commentary interprets this as "one-tenth." If the *fang* is one-tenth of the circumference, its measurement is three inches, two *fen*, five *li*, and four *hao*.

# [A remainder of] one *miao*, six *hu*, and six [further parts] <note>extremely small units of measure used in ancient Chinese mathematics for precision</note>; taking the whole number of three inches to be the depth of the spoke-mortise, this is called:
->**Patting the *sou***<-

**Analysis:** It is stated that **Cheng** <note>Cheng Yaotian, 1725–1814, a renowned scholar of the "Search for Truth" school</note> is correct in identifying the **sou** <term>original: 藪 (sou) — here interpreted as the spoke-mortise</term> as the **spoke-mortise** <term>original: 輻鑿 (fuzao) — the hole where the spoke is inserted into the hub</term>. His insight that the depth of the spoke-mortise should equal the **hub-crescent** <term>original: 圍朏 (weifei) — a fractional part of the hub's circumference</term> is particularly outstanding. However, his calculation of the *fei* as being one-tenth of the hub’s circumference—thereby arriving at the whole number of three inches—is not yet precisely accurate. Why is this? The depth of the mortise is determined by the width of the **spoke** <term>original: 輻 (fu)</term>. For war chariots, passenger chariots, and hunting chariots, the established width of the spoke is two inches; therefore, it is very clear that the *sou* must also refer to a depth of two inches. Thus, I argue that the *fei* represents the remainder of the three-foot [circumference].

**Ruan** <note>Ruan Yuan, 1764–1849, a preeminent scholar and official</note> states: The hollow interior of the **hub** <term>original: 轂 (gu)</term> is called the *sou*. Because the *sou* is a hollow object, its name is also used as a unit of measure. The *Records of the Ritual of Visiting* <note>the *Pingli ji*</note> states: "Sixteen *dou* <note>a dry measure</note> is called a *sou*." This confirms it. Looking at the *Records* <note>the *Kaogongji*</note>, it says to "measure the *sou* with **millet**" <note>millet seeds were used to measure the volume of a container</note>; although the volume of the hub's *sou* is not necessarily as large as sixteen *dou*, we can know that it is a general name for a hollow space that receives contents. The word **fei** <term>original: 朏 (fei) — meaning the crescent moon</term> should, according to the *Analytical Dictionary of Characters* <note>the *Shuowen Jiezi*</note>, be written as **le** <term>original: 𠂈/阞 (le) — meaning one-third</term>. It is currently written with the "mound" radical as **le** <term>original: 阞</term>, which is a borrowed character.
