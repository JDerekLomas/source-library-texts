In YARROW, several stalks occasionally rise up above the height of a cubit <note>about 18 inches</note>, rounded and somewhat hard: around which are leaves that are indeed oblong, variously dissected at the sides, and as if joined together from many parts, each of which appears to approach the thin leaves of Coriander: at the top stand out rounded, broad umbels, whose small flowers are white or purple. The roots send down many fibers. The herb has a scent that is not unpleasant, but medicinal.

Achillea grows near the edges of plowed fields, meadows, and gardens, and not infrequently in the fields themselves in fertile and fallow soil: and indeed that which has a white flower is more common.

It produces flowers and umbels from May into a great age <note>late in the season</note>; meanwhile, the seeds are perfected.

By more recent authors it is called *Millefolium* <note>literally "thousand-leaf"</note>. According to Galen <note>A prominent Greek physician in the Roman Empire</note>, the **Achillea** <note>Yarrow</note> is not unlike the Ironworts in its properties; it is clearly cleansing and moderately cooling, but it excels in its astringency. Many say that the leaves, especially when fresh, soothe toothaches when chewed.

## On the Lesser Milfoil, or Thousand-leaf Soldier. CHAPTER XVI.

The LESSER Milfoil is a small plant, scarcely nine inches high, whose stalks are clothed with oblong but tiny leaflets, very finely divided at the sides like the feathers from the wings of small birds: the rounded umbels at the tips produce little flowers that are yellow in color (as we have happened to see in Belgium). The roots are thin fibers.

This kind of Milfoil is found more rarely: it grows in rich and fertile soil, and occasionally in meadows.

Its flowers are especially conspicuous in the month of August.

The description in Dioscorides <note>Ancient Greek botanist and author of De Materia Medica</note> sufficiently shows that the <term>Stratiotes Chiliophyllos</term> <note>original: "στρατιώτης χιλιόφυλλος", meaning "Thousand-leaf Soldier"</note> is the Milfoil: the height of the plant corresponds; the shape of the leaves agrees; though a certain ambiguity occurs regarding the color of the flower, which Dioscorides describes as white: he says <term>small white flowers</term> <note>original: "ἄνθη μικρὰ λευκὰ"</note>, as the common copies have it. However, Andreas Lacuna <note>A 16th-century Spanish physician and commentator on Dioscorides</note> adds from an ancient manuscript <term>ochra</term> <note>original: "ὠχρά"</note>, that is, of a yellow color. Modern times call it Lesser Milfoil and Yellow Milfoil: it is unknown to the apothecaries and the common people.

There is indeed another Soldier with the surname <term>potamios</term> <note>original: "ποτάμιος", meaning "of the river"</note>, which we discuss among the aquatic plants in the fourth Pemptad.

In terms of its properties, the Thousand-leaf Soldier, besides a moderate coolness, also possesses some astringency.

It is very useful for stopping all flows of blood, and for consolidating both fresh and old ulcers: it also glues wounds. Indeed, Galen says there are those who use it for fistulas.

## On Osyris. CHAPTER XVII.

Osyris is a tall and very greatly branched herb: its height is two cubits <note>about 3 feet</note> or higher: the color is a pale greenish; the stalks or twigs are thin and round: the leaflets are oblong, narrow, and thin like those of Flax: the flowers are tiny, clinging in clusters along the leaves, of a herbaceous color: from there the seed is produced, covered in a thin down, small and blackish. The root is fibrous.

It grows spontaneously in many places in Greece: among the Italians, and elsewhere, it is sown in gardens and pleasure-grounds.

**D**EAR NOBLE AND MAGNIFICENT MEN, we offer and dedicate to you our Commentaries on the History of Plants, which we now release to the public. I greatly desire for you to be persuaded that this was done by me not out of some sudden whim or by chance, but from a long-standing feeling of singular respect. Indeed, it is not just recently, but for a long time now, that I have desired to be of service in some way to you and to this city over which you preside. But since I have been unable to declare this through any official duty until now, I have decided to show it in some measure through the dedication of this work of ours. 

Now, regarding what has been achieved in writing these Commentaries on plants, although many might perhaps expect us to give a long-winded speech, we have preferred to leave that to the judgment of others and the fair Reader, rather than to boast much about our own late-night labors. Nevertheless, we dare to affirm and profess (and indeed, as we hope, without any mark of arrogance) that we have labored and striven with all zeal so that the histories or descriptions of individual plants might be presented clearly, plainly, and as briefly as possible (cutting away the superfluous); and then, that they might be arranged into certain classes in the most convenient and efficient order possible. 

With what labor, or with what kind of care and industry these things were performed by us, will be easily recognized by anyone who wishes to observe the multitude of plants described in these Commentaries, and their variety, and who wishes to weigh more attentively why these have been arranged by us into six <term>Pemptades</term> <note>From the Greek 'pempas', meaning a group of five; Dodoens organized his work into six main sections, each containing five books, totaling thirty books.</note> or thirty Books.

For whether the antiquity of the city or its borders, its location, its great convenience, its elegance, or many other such qualities are weighed—or even if the Princes who have ruled here are set forth—there is everywhere offered the most ample and rich material for speaking and writing. It is certainly greater and more abundant than can be treated and polished by us in so narrow a time or according to its true dignity. In antiquity, this city yields to none, if its borders are considered. For this is the land of those once called the Ambivariti <note>A Celtic or Belgic tribe mentioned by Caesar; the author is attempting to link them etymologically to Antwerp</note>, whom Julius Caesar mentions in his *Commentaries on the Gallic War* <note>original: "Commentariis suis de bello Gallico"</note>. Indeed, if his words are diligently weighed, the Ambivariti can be none other than those who inhabit and dwell within the borders of Antwerp.

Julius Caesar reports that the Usipetes and Tenchteri (Germanic tribes), having occupied the settlements of the Menapii, sent a large part of their cavalry across the Meuse <note>original: "Mosa"</note> to the Ambivariti for the sake of plundering and gathering grain. He notes they were away for several days when ambassadors were sent to him; these men, having received an answer, said they would return after three days once the matter was deliberated. They desired such a delay of time so that, in the meantime, the cavalry they were expecting could (as is likely) be present. From this, it is quite apparent that the Ambivariti occupied remote places even beyond the Meuse, and were not confined by narrow borders. Otherwise, there would have been no need to desire a delay of longer time for the return of the horsemen; for from nearby and neighboring places, a return would have been quickly accomplished. Furthermore, a large part of the cavalry could only be sent to widely spreading lands.

The Usipetes and Tenchteri had settled at that time not far from the confluence of the Meuse and the Rhine—that is, in that tract of what is called in our age Guelders <note>original: "Geldriae"</note>, which is situated below the city of Nijmegen <note>original: "Neomagum"</note>, between the Meuse and the Waal <note>original: "Vahalim"</note> (the part of the Rhine received by the Meuse). From this territory of Guelders, the widely spreading and more remote places beyond the Meuse belong to the Ambivariti: namely, from the city called 's-Hertogenbosch <note>original: "Buscoducis"</note> all the way to the bank of the Scheldt <note>original: "Scaldis"</note>. Indeed, that entire region shows clear signs and traces of having once been under the sea—specifically before the shores of the sea and the banks of the rivers were confined by the erection of dikes. For this reason, it was called **Antwarpten** (which in Latin you might call an "alluvion" or "addition" <note>The author suggests the name comes from land "thrown against" the shore by water</note>). Caesar, attempting to express the word as closely as he could, called the inhabitants the Ambivariti.

That this was the name of a people, and not a town, is sufficiently clear not only from Julius Caesar’s *Commentaries*, but is also testified by Audoenus <note>St. Ouen</note> in the *Life of St. Eligius*, whom he writes labored among the Flemings and fought with constant urgency in "Andoverpis" <note>Antwerp</note> around the year 640 AD. The <term>Ambivariti</term><note>A Celtic or Germanic tribe mentioned by Julius Caesar, located near the Meuse river.</note> are categorized among those whom Ptolemy, in Book II of his *Geography*, calls the <term>Tongri</term><note>The Tungri, a Germanic people who replaced the Eburones in the region of modern-day Tongeren, Belgium.</note>, whose city he records as <term>Aduacutum</term><note>Likely referring to Atuatuca Tungrorum, modern Tongeren.</note>. From this city, it appears that Julius Caesar named these people the <term>Atuatuci</term> instead of the Tongri, whom he also numbers among the Belgians, establishing the Rhine as the boundary of Belgic Gaul. However, according to Pliny in Book IV of his *Natural History*, the Tongri or Atuatuci are Germans. He states: "Along this entire sea (namely the German Sea/North Sea) as far as the river Scheldt, Germanic tribes dwell in an immeasurable expanse." And indeed, if one considers their language and customs, those formerly called Tongri or Atuatuci, along with the Ambivariti—the greatest part of whom are contained within the territory of <term>Brabant</term>—should be numbered among the Germans rather than the Gauls.

In this widely extending territory of the Ambivariti, there stands a town built long ago which took its name from the jurisdiction itself and still retains it, called **Antwerp** <note>original: "Antwarpten"</note>. The rest, or at least the largest part of the field, takes its name from a certain <term>Campanus</term>, borrowed (as it is believed) from the name of <term>Campania</term><note>Referring to the Campine or Kempen region of the Low Countries.</note>. Although histories do not openly record at what time this town was built, it is sufficiently clear that it is very ancient. For around the year of our Lord 837, it was occupied by the **Northmen** <note>Vikings</note>, plundered, and devastated by fire; it was undoubtedly constructed and inhabited long before that year.

This town began to be built and inhabited under most happy auspices, which is clearly shown by the city having been frequently increased and enlarged, and now being most vast and famous. That it was a small town in the beginning is fully demonstrated not only by the gates but also by several remaining old walls. Afterward, it received no small increases, and not just once, but it grew significantly and was enlarged by new additions many times. Witnesses to the earlier growth are the streets and public districts retaining their names from the walls, as well as certain gates destroyed not so long ago. 

And although it was no small city when it was contained within those boundaries and walls, yet as the multitude of people grew and the number of citizens multiplied, it quickly grew into an even greater city, attaining that breadth and magnitude which it was seen to have in the year 1542, when it was besieged for several days by **Martin van Rossum** and other conspirators. Since that time, it has not only been adorned with many districts and magnificent buildings, but it also received a significant addition on the northern side with the walls being widely extended. Likewise, a few years ago, a very large addition was made to the south, namely on the occasion of the **Citadel** <note>original: "castri"</note> erected by the **Duke of Alba**, which, now being partly demolished, has provided a most opportune occasion for the enlargement of the city and the extension of the walls.

It is situated about a few hours' journey from the Ocean, if one were to measure it by foot; a location clearly less exposed to dangers and storms, and sufficiently safe from the wandering waves and floods of the sea. <note>The text describes the transition of power during the 5th and 6th centuries.</note> While Clovis <note>original: "Clodoueo"</note>, King of the Franks—who later obtained the name of Louis the Great—was waging war with Alaric, King of the Visigoths, who held the seat of his kingdom at Toulouse and was most severely persecuting the children of Albero and all the descendants and kinsmen of Clodio the Long-Haired, Vaubert sent his children to the Emperor Zeno, by whom they were granted senatorial honor. However, Ansbert, even as an elder, subsequently obtained from the Emperor Justinian the perpetual and hereditary Marquisate of the Holy Roman Empire at the Scheldt <note>original: "Scaldim"</note>. To him fell as wife Blitildis, daughter of Chlothar, King of the Franks; he was the son of Louis the Great, and King first of Soissons, then, upon the death of his brothers, of all France. He granted to his son-in-law Ansbert the Moselle province, from the city of Metz all the way to Cologne <note>original: "Coloniam Agrippinam"</note>, under the title of a Duchy. From this Ansbert, that first Marquis of the Holy Roman Empire, the most noble lineage of the Carolingians, celebrated in the histories of all, has its origin.

For Ansbert had a son, Arnoald; and he had Arnulf, whose son Ansegisel had as his wife Begga, the daughter of Pippin of Landen. From her was born Pippin, surnamed the Fat <note>original: "cognomento Crassus"</note>, the father of that famous Charles Martel, who, celebrated for his very bravely conducted affairs and many victories, won for himself the greatest authority and power among the Franks. From Charles Martel was sired Pippin, called the Short: after the son of King Childeric was removed, he received the government and diadem of the Kingdom of France with the approval of Zachary, the Roman Pontiff. ->DEDICATORY EPISTLE<-

Having taken possession of the City, the King was crowned there, which he then fortified and left to be possessed by his followers for several years. Indeed, not a few other Dukes, Kings, and Emperors owe the origins of their lineage to the Margraves <note>A Margrave was a military governor of a medieval border province or "march."</note> of the Holy Empire and of this City, namely by acknowledging their maternal descent from Godfrey the Bearded, Duke of Brabant. Such was John, Duke of Burgundy, born of the daughter of Louis, Count of Flanders, who had as his grandfather John, Duke of Brabant, descended from that same Godfrey (though with several generations between them). John’s son was Philip the Good; his grandson through Philip was Charles the Bold, whose only daughter was married to Maximilian I, Emperor of the Romans. From her, many most powerful Dukes, Kings, and Emperors have since come forth, whom our own age has seen and recognized.

Since, therefore, such great Princes, Dukes, Kings, and Emperors credit the beginnings and origins of their lives to the Margraves of the Holy Empire and of this City, this Margravate and this City cannot but be held superior to others in both nobility and dignity—which in other respects already surpasses and far excels other cities in magnificence, trade, wealth, and splendor. These brief remarks of ours concerning the excellence of this City, of whatever sort they may be, should not be thought written in the hope that any glory will be added to so great a City from this source, but rather lest we should seem to have failed in our zeal or duty, and so that the expectations of certain people might be satisfied, if not fully, then at least to some extent.

It will now be your part, **MOST DISTINGUISHED MEN**, to receive with a grateful and kind spirit not only these remarks, but also our *Commentaries on Plants* <note>original: "Commentarios nostros de stirpibus"</note>, inscribed to you. We hope these may be able to confer as much utility to students as this City provides convenience to the whole world through its abundance and variety of marketable goods. We pray to God, the Best and Greatest, that He may preserve this City happily flourishing and safe for as long as possible, and at the same time, that He may direct your counsels to the glory and honor of His name.

At Antwerp, **In the year 1582.**
