The excellence of painting **orchids** <note>original: 蘭 (lán)</note> begins with **Spirit Resonance** <note>original: 氣韻 (qìyùn); the vitality or "breath" of a painting, considered the most important of the Six Principles of Chinese painting</note>. The **ink** <note>original: 墨 (mò)</note> must be of the finest quality, and the water must come from a fresh spring. The inkstone should be scrubbed of old residue, and the **brush** <note>original: 筆 (bǐ)</note> must be pure.

With a firm mind, first distinguish the four **leaves** <note>original: 葉 (yè)</note>; their varying lengths hold the mystery. One leaf should overlap another to capture charm and beauty. Beside the point where leaves meet,

yet another leaf is added. Among clusters of three or four, two leaves add a sense of fullness. The ink should maintain a consistent tone, yet swirl with "old" and "tender" textures <note>Referring to the contrast between mature, bold strokes and delicate, new growth</note>. When applying the brush,

use light ink, but use scorched ink for a fresh brilliance. The brush should move like a bolt of lightning; avoid any hesitation. Everything depends on the **writing momentum** <note>original: 寫勢 (xiěshì); the gestural flow and energy of the brushstroke, emphasizing that painting is akin to calligraphy</note>, whether the leaves are seen from the front, back, or at an angle.

Strive for a natural distribution of all parts. Buds clinging to their calyxes should all return to a single point of origin. Whether facing the wind or reflecting the sun, the flowers and calyxes should appear graceful.

Delicate and lovely, they endure frost and defy snow. With leaves half-drooping and low branches, the movement of the foliage is like a phoenix dancing. The blossoms and calyxes are elegant,

drifting like fluttering butterflies. The sheath-husk wraps the base, while small leaves are scattered about. For the heart of the flower, one must use **flying white** <note>original: 飛白 (fēibái); a brush technique where the hairs of the brush separate, leaving white streaks of paper visible within the stroke, suggesting speed and lightness</note>. Nearby, you may place plantain grass

on a grassy slope. Or, you might add one or two stalks of old bamboo. **Thorns** <note>original: 荆棘 (jīngjí)</note> and brambles here and there can further enhance this wonderful sight.

### The Feathered Tribe

The feathered tribe possesses ten thousand forms, making it difficult to capture every shape in a painting. There are brocade *Luan* <note>original: 錦鸞, a mythological bird similar to a phoenix</note> and colorful phoenixes; white cranes and golden orioles; tiny sparrows perching on branches and the giant Roc <note>original: 巨鵬, a legendary bird of immense size from Daoist mythology</note> soaring through the heavens. Their flowing songs are like flutes, and their turning wings are like gathered clouds. Their feathers shimmer with the "five colors" <note>referring to a full palette of colors</note>, and their voices shift through a thousand melodies. They bathe at will in the Western Pond and sing freely within the Jade Forest; they circle over islands in the sea and rest in the shade of flowers. Whether flying up or down, crouching or kicking, stretching or bending, they are full of spirit. In flight, song, sleep, or feeding, each must be depicted with lifelike vitality.

Feathers, wings, beaks, and claws are all brought to light through the brush. One begins by drawing the beak and the eyes, aligning them with the upper "lip." From the eyes, sketch the head and forehead, then trace the cheeks and the back feathers. Use semi-circular strokes, varying in size and brush pressure. These should resemble the "broken mirror" <note>original: 破鏡, a technical term for the overlapping, curved shape of feathers</note> pattern—whether short, long, pointed, or flat. 

The tail feathers should be illustrated finely and with even spacing. Gradually add the smaller tail feathers with a few additional strokes. The feathers along the spine should follow one another in sequence. The thighs, breast, and belly must be rendered with careful deliberation. Once the entire body is finished, add the soles of the feet. Whether the bird is grasping a branch or standing on a trunk, its posture should feel dynamic and sweeping. The secret to "unfurling the fist" <note>original: 展拳, referring to the way a bird opens its claws to grasp a branch</note> is to avoid a scattered or detached appearance. 

Huang Quan’s <note>original: 黃筌 (c. 903–965), a master of bird-and-flower painting renowned for his realistic "meticulous color" style</note> flying geese and Emperor Huizong’s <note>original: 徽宗 (r. 1100–1126), the Song emperor famous for his refined and accurate bird paintings</note> standing hawks have been praised throughout the ages. Everyone aspires to achieve such <term>Divine Works (神品): the highest grade of artistic achievement in Chinese art, where the work transcends human effort to seem as if created by the gods</term>. Tang dynasty scholars and Song dynasty lords composed these short verses, now preserved within the *Illustrated Compendium of the Three Realms* <note>original: 三才圖會 (Sancai Tuhui), a famous Ming dynasty encyclopedia from which this text likely originates</note>.

# General Rules for Eliminating Red
<note>original: "Chu Hong" (除紅). A traditional Chinese game played with four dice. In Chinese culture, the numbers one and four on dice are traditionally colored red, while others are black or green. In this game, the number four holds the highest value.</note>

*   To begin rolling the dice, players first compete with a single die to determine the turn order. The player who rolls a "four-red" <note>rolling the number four, which is painted red</note> goes first, followed by the others in descending order of their results. For example, if three people are playing and one person rolls a red first, the remaining two must compete again as described above to determine who goes next. Those who do not roll a red at all are placed last. This same logic applies to groups of four, five, or more players.

*   In the game of Eliminating Red, four dice are used. Rolling four "fours" is considered the "King" <note>the highest possible hand</note>. If a player does not roll four reds, their score is calculated based on the other five numbers. Scores of eight points or fewer are all considered "reward patterns" <term>shangse (賞色): hands that grant an immediate win or prize without needing to be compared against other players' rolls</term>; these do not require further competition. Scores from nine to twelve points are considered "competitive patterns" <term>saise (賽色): hands that must be compared against the rolls of opponents to determine a winner</term>, with the exception of the specific patterns known as "Willow Leaves" and "Twelve Hours."

*   Any roll that produces "double reds" <note>two dice showing the number four</note> is categorized as "Strong Red" and is generally not used for scoring, except for the specific patterns "Red Leaves" and "Steadily Rising." If none of the four dice show a red (a four), the hand is classified as "Scattered" and is disregarded, with the exception of the "Blended Flower" and "Plain Leaf" patterns.

# Regulations for the Game of Eliminating Red

*   Whenever a player rolls a **Reward Pattern** <term>shangse (賞色): high-value hands that grant an immediate win or an extra turn</term>, they retain the dice and immediately roll again. The next player is only permitted to begin their competitive turn once the current player rolls a **Competitive Pattern** <term>saise (賽色): hands that set a point value that must be challenged by opponents</term>. If a player rolls a "Strong Red" or a "Miscellaneous Pattern," the turn passes to the next person. If Reward Patterns appear repeatedly, the player may continue rolling indefinitely.

*   When engaged in a **Competitive Pattern**, the point total of the first player to roll must be recorded. The play must continue until a Reward or Penalty pattern is rolled; results such as "Miscellaneous Patterns" or "Strong Red" are not counted in this specific phase of competition.

*   If a player rolls exactly one point less than the score they are competing against, it is known as **"Stepping on the Foot"** <term>tajiao (蹋脚): a specific narrow loss</term>. This incurs a penalty of one "chit" <note>a token or counter used to track debts and scores during the game</note>. If the competition results in any standard Penalty Pattern worth three chits or fewer, the player must pay the penalty required by that hand plus one additional penalty chit. If the Penalty Pattern is worth four chits or more, the player simply pays the amount dictated by the pattern without any further addition.

*   If a player rolls the same number of points as the opponent, it is called **"Catching Up"** <term>ganshang (趕上): matching the score</term>, which earns the player a reward of one chit. # Regulations for the Game of Eliminating Red

*   Whenever a player rolls the **"Willow Leaves"** <term>liuyeer (柳葉兒): a specific dice combination, usually considered unlucky or low-value</term>, they are penalized four chits. If this roll matches the opponent's score, the opponent is still penalized one chit for being **"Caught Up"** <note>referencing the "Catching Up" rule from the previous page where matching a score triggers a transfer of chits</note>.

*   Whenever a player in a **Competitive Pattern** <term>saise (賽色): a roll intended to beat an opponent's established score</term> exceeds the opponent's score by exactly one point, it is called **"Overwhelming"** <term>yadao (壓倒)</term> and is rewarded with two chits. However, if the player rolls any variety of **Reward Pattern** <term>shangse</term> during this challenge:
    *   If the pattern is valued at three chits or less, the player is penalized the value of the pattern plus one additional chit.
    *   If the pattern is valued at four chits or more, they are penalized only the value of the pattern itself, without an additional penalty. <note>In this specific competitive phase, rolling a high-value fixed pattern instead of a point value is treated as a failure to compete properly, resulting in a penalty rather than the usual reward.</note>

*   Whenever a player in a competitive roll exceeds the opponent’s score by two or three points, they are rewarded with only one chit. If they roll two or three points less than the opponent, they are penalized only one chit.

Whenever rolling dice, whether there are three or five players, the sequence of the cycle—whether moving counter-clockwise to the left or clockwise to the right—must be agreed upon beforehand so there is no confusion during play. If a player whose turn it is fails to roll, they are penalized three chits, to be given to the next person in the sequence. Anyone who rolls out of turn before it is their time is penalized five chits, to be given to the person who was supposed to roll.
