# The Science of Culture

...different grades of civilization. The details of the inquiry will, I think, prove that stages of culture may be compared without taking into account how far tribes who use the same tool, follow the same custom, or believe the same myth, may differ in their physical build or the color of their skin and hair.

A first step in the study of civilization is to dissect it into details and classify these into their proper groups. For instance, when examining weapons, they are to be classified as spears, clubs, slings, bows and arrows, and so forth. Among the textile arts, we categorize matting, netting, and various levels of making and weaving threads. Myths are divided under headings such as myths of sunrise and sunset, eclipse myths, and earthquake myths. There are also local myths which explain the names of places with a fanciful tale, and <term>eponymic myths</term> <note>myths that explain the origin of a tribe or nation by claiming they descended from an imaginary ancestor whose name is derived from the tribe's name</note> which account for the parentage of a tribe by turning its name into the name of an imaginary ancestor. 

Under rites and ceremonies, we find such practices as various kinds of sacrifice to the ghosts of the dead and to other spiritual beings, the custom of turning to the east during worship, and the purification of ritual or moral "uncleanness" using water or fire. These are just a few miscellaneous examples from a list of hundreds. An <term>ethnographer’s</term> <note>a researcher who studies and describes the unique cultures and customs of different groups of people</note> business is to classify such details to map out their distribution in geography and history, and the relations that exist among them. 

What this task is like can be almost perfectly illustrated by comparing these details of culture with the species of plants and animals studied by a naturalist. To the ethnographer, the bow and arrow is a species, the habit of flattening children’s skulls is a species, and the practice of counting by tens is a species. The geographical distribution of these things, and their transmission from region to region, must be studied just as a naturalist studies the geography of botanical and zoological species. Just as certain plants and animals are unique to certain districts, the same is true for instruments such as the Australian boomerang, the Polynesian "stick-and-groove" method for making fire, and the tiny bow and arrow used as a <term>fleam</term> <note>original: "phleme"; a sharp surgical instrument used for bloodletting</note> by tribes near the Isthmus of Panama. # THE SCIENCE OF CULTURE

Just as a catalog of all the plant and animal species in a region represents its Flora and Fauna, the list of all the aspects of a people's general life represents the whole of what we call their culture. And just as distant regions often produce plants and animals that are similar—though by no means identical—the same is true for the details of civilization among their inhabitants. The strength of this comparison between the spread of biology and the spread of civilization becomes clear when we see how often the same causes have produced both simultaneously. In region after region, the same factors that introduced cultivated plants and domesticated animals also brought with them corresponding skills and knowledge. The same course of events that brought horses and wheat to America also brought the use of guns and iron hatchets. In return, the Old World <note>a term for Europe, Africa, and Asia</note> received not only maize <note>corn</note>, potatoes, and turkeys, but also the habit of smoking and the sailor’s hammock.

It is worth considering that accounts of similar cultural phenomena appearing in different parts of the world actually provide incidental proof of their own authenticity. Some years ago, a prominent historian asked me a question that highlights this point: "How can a report regarding the customs, myths, or beliefs of a primitive <note>original: "savage"</note> tribe be treated as evidence when it relies on the testimony of a traveler or missionary who may be a superficial observer, more or less ignorant of the native language, a careless recycler of gossip, or even biased and intentionally deceitful?" 

This is indeed a question that every <term>ethnographer</term> <note>a researcher who studies and describes the unique cultures and customs of different groups of people</note> should keep clearly and constantly in mind. Naturally, a researcher is required to use their best judgment regarding the trustworthiness of the authors they quote and, if possible, obtain several different accounts to confirm each point in every location. But beyond these precautions, the "test of recurrence" <note>the principle that if independent observers report the same specific custom in different places, the custom is likely real rather than a mistake or invention</note> comes into play.
