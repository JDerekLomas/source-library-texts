Indeed, if anyone wishes to judge this matter based on the foundations of the Christian faith, he will find it to be otherwise. A Christian Prince ought to love his neighbor just as much as the lowliest of his subjects, if he wishes to be a Christian; if he neglects this, he sins against his neighbor, to whom he owes charity. A prince, as a human being, is bound by natural law <term>Jus naturale; the universal system of rules and ethics inherent in human nature and discoverable by reason</term>, which I explained in *Book 1 of the Politics, chapter 2, number 38 and verse 1*; this establishes a certain kinship among us, and consequently, it is a crime for one man to harm another. *Law 3, On Justice and Law.* If anyone should do this, surely the mutual debt of humanity is violated, and that law of nature is struck against.

Nor is this a valid conclusion: "A prince cannot be punished by his subjects. Therefore, when he unjustly offends them, he does not sin against them." For to suffer an injury and to be able to avenge it are two different things; it is not necessary that if the latter is removed <note>meaning, if the right to take revenge is taken away</note>, the former is also taken away. For in this way, a son cannot punish his father, and yet it cannot be wrongly said that a father has transgressed against his son. Thus Pharaoh said to Moses and Aaron: "I have sinned against the Lord your God, and against you." *Exodus chapter 10, verse 16.* And yet they were the subjects of Pharaoh, quite absolutely. 

But far be it from us to proclaim Pharaoh more humane than the most pious David was. He, therefore, said: "Against you alone," he says, "have I sinned" <note>quoting Psalm 51, David's prayer of repentance</note>; that is, "You knew the gravity of the sin when it was not yet public." See *Emanuel Tremellius’s notes on the said Psalm 51; Mr. Johann Arndt in the Exposition of the Psalter, Psalm 51, sermon 2, number 2.* To God alone was the "hidden crime of the house," as *Virgil* speaks in *Book 1 of the Aeneid*, revealed. 

The Chaldean Paraphrast <note>A reference to the Targum, an ancient Aramaic translation and exposition of the Hebrew Bible</note> understood this passage in the same way, which has: "Before you alone I have sinned, and evil before you I have done." <note>original: "קָדָמָךְ לְבַלְחוֹדָךְ"</note> Nor is Aretius Felinus <note>A pseudonym for the Protestant reformer Martin Bucer</note> irrelevant in his explanation of the Psalms. David had also sinned against his most faithful soldier Uriah, and the whole army, which he had ordered to be brought into danger for the sake of killing him; but while he was weighing how much he had despised and offended God by this crime of his, he was so tormented and torn apart in his soul that he felt only that one thing, complained of only that one thing, and said to the Lord: "*Against you, against you alone have I sinned.*" And that, "because I so despised your word, because I provoked you so greatly; this," I say, "kills me, tears me apart, and flays me." etc.
