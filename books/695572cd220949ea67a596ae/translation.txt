# INTRODUCTION.

WHEN it is considered that Pythagoras was the father of philosophy, authentic memoirs of his life cannot fail to be uncommonly interesting to every lover of wisdom, and particularly to those who reverence the doctrines of Plato, the most genuine and the best of all his disciples. And that the following memoirs of Pythagoras by Iamblichus are authentic is acknowledged by all the critics, as they are for the most part obviously derived from sources of very high antiquity; and where the sources are unknown, there is every reason to believe, from the great worth and respectability of the biographer, that the information is perfectly accurate and true.

Of the biographer, indeed, Iamblichus, it is well known to every beginner <note>original: "tyro"</note> in Platonism that he was honored by all the Platonists who succeeded him with the title of *divine*. After the praise passed on him by the sharp-witted Emperor Julian—"that he was later indeed in time, but not in genius, to Plato" <note>original: "Οἶδα μὲν οὖν καί Πλάτωνα τὸν μέγαν, καί μετά τουτόν ἄνδρα τοις χρόνοις μέν, οὐ τῇ μήν φύσει, καταδεεστέρον, τὸν Χαλκιδέα φημι τὸν Ἰαμβλιχον, κ. λ." — "I am indeed aware of the great Plato, and after him a man who was later in time, but truly not inferior in nature: I mean Iamblichus of Chalcis, etc." Julian, Oration 4.</note>—all further praise of him would be as unnecessary as the defamation of him by certain modern critics is contemptible and useless. For these little men <note>original: "homonculi"</note>, looking solely to his deficiency in style and not to the greatness of his intellect, perceive only his small blemishes, but have not even a glimpse of his surpassing excellence. Of this very extraordinary man there is a life extant by Eunapius <note>A 4th-century Greek sophist and historian</note>, the substance of which I have given in my *History of the Restoration of the Platonic Theology*, and to which I refer the English reader. At present I shall only select from that work the following biographical particulars respecting our Iamblichus: He was descended of a family equally illustrious, fortunate, and rich. His country was Chalcis, a city of Syria, which was called *Coele* <note>Hollow Syria</note>. He associated with Anatolius, who was the second in rank to Porphyry, but he far excelled him in his attainments and ascended to the very summit of philosophy. 

But after he had been for some time connected with Anatolius, and most probably found him insufficient to satisfy the vast desires of his soul, he applied himself to Porphyry, to whom (says Eunapius) he was in nothing inferior, except in the structure and power of composition. For his writings were not so elegant and graceful as those of Porphyry: they were neither agreeable, nor clear <note>original: "perspicuous"</note>; nor free from impurity of diction. And though they were not entirely involved in obscurity, and perfectly faulty; yet as Plato formerly said of Xenocrates, he did not sacrifice to the *Mercurial Graces* <note>A metaphor for eloquent and persuasive speech, referencing Hermes/Mercury, the god of rhetoric</note>. Hence he is far from detaining the reader with delight, if that reader merely regards his style; but he will rather turn away and dull the reader's attention, and frustrate his expectation. However, though the surface of his concepts is not covered with the flowers of eloquence <note>original: "elocution"</note>, yet the depth of them is admirable, and his genius is truly sublime. Iamblichus shared in an eminent degree the favor of divinity, on account of his cultivation of justice; and obtained a numerous multitude of associates and disciples, who came from all parts of the world, for the purpose of participating in the streams of wisdom, which so plentifully flowed from the sacred fountain of his wonderful mind. Among these was Sopater the Syrian,² who was most skillful both in speaking and writing; Eustathius the Cappadocian; and of the Greeks, Theodorus and Euphrasius. All these were excellent for their virtues and attainments, as well as many other of his disciples, who were not much inferior to the former in eloquence; so that it seems wonderful how Iamblichus could attend to all of them, with such gentleness of manners and kindness of disposition as he continually displayed.

He performed some few rituals relative to the worship of divinity by himself, without his associates and disciples; but was inseparable from his close friends in most of his daily activities. He imitated in his diet the frugal simplicity of the most ancient times; and during his meals, cheered those who were present by his behavior, and filled them as if with nectar by the sweetness of his conversation.

A celebrated philosopher named Alypius, who was deeply skilled in dialectic <note>The art of investigating or discussing the truth of opinions</note>, was a contemporary of Iamblichus, but was of such a diminutive stature that he had the appearance of a pygmy. However, his great abilities amply compensated for this trifling physical defect.

But Iamblichus, when he was alone, admired the acuteness of the question, and often privately resorted to Alypius, whom he very much applauded for his acumen and sagacity; so that after his decease, he wrote his life. Iamblichus,¹ leaving behind him many roots and fountains of philosophy; which, through the cultivation of succeeding Platonists, produced a fair variety of vigorous branches and copious streams.

For an account of the theological writings of Iamblichus, I refer the reader to my above-mentioned *History of the Restoration of the Platonic Theology*; and for accurate critical information concerning all his works, to the *Greek Library* <note>original: "Bibliotheca Græca"</note> of Fabricius.

Regarding the following work, the *Life of Pythagoras*, it is necessary to observe that the original has been transmitted to us in a very imperfect state. This is due partly to the numerous verbal errors in the text, partly to the lack of connection between the narrated events, and partly because many details are repeated in different places using the exact same words. Therefore, the conjecture of Kuster, one of the German editors of this work, is highly probable: that it had not received the final revisions of Iamblichus. Instead, it is likely that others compiled this treatise from the disorganized materials found among his manuscripts after his death. Notwithstanding all its defects, however, it is—as I have previously observed—a most interesting work; the benefits that its distribution is calculated to produce are inestimable. Since two of the most celebrated German critics, Kuster and Kiessling, have produced two splendid editions of this work, it is evident they must have been deeply impressed with a conviction of its value and importance.

With respect to the collection of Pythagorean <note>original: "Pythagoric"</note> Sentences in this volume, it is almost needless to observe that they are incomparably excellent. It is deeply to be regretted that the Greek original of the *Sentences of Sextus* <gloss>1</gloss> is lost, leaving only the fraudulent Latin version by the Priest <note>original: "Presbyter"</note> Ruffinus. I call it a fraudulent version because Ruffinus, wishing to persuade the reader that these sentences were written by a bishop named Sixtus, has in many places perverted and contaminated the meaning of the original. 

In the selection I have made from these sentences, however, I have endeavored—and I trust not in vain—to give the genuine sense of Sextus, unmingled with the crude and corrupted <note>original: "barbarous and polluted"</note> additions made by Ruffinus. If the English reader possesses my translation of the *Sentences of Demophilus*, along with Mr. Bridgman’s translation of the *Golden Sentences of Democrates* and the *Similitudes of Demophilus*, <gloss>2</gloss> he will then possess all the extant Pythagorean sentences, except for those of Sextus which I have not translated due to the very impure and false state in which they currently exist.
