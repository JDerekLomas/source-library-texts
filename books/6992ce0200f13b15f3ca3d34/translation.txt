Regarding the inspection and counting of arrows: The officials in charge of arrow counting shall call out the shots and record the tallies. The drummers shall wait for the names to be called. Archery must not be treated as a commonplace or simple matter. 

Those who shoot over a short distance with a flat trajectory, using a stiff bow to launch **heavy arrows**, and are able to hit the target, are ranked as **Second Class**. 

Those who shoot over a long distance with a high trajectory, using a light arrow and a soft bow, and are able to hit the target frequently, are ranked as **Third Class**. 

<note>This ranking system prioritizes the physical power and armor-piercing capability of heavy bows over the mere distance achieved by lighter equipment.</note>

The <term>Wokou: Literally "dwarf pirates," referring to the Japanese raiders and soldiers active along the Chinese coast during the Ming Dynasty</term> use arrows that do not fly far, specifically because their arrows are very heavy. Because the arrows are heavy, they penetrate deep into the human body. The <term>Iron Tang: Tiexin; the metal shank extending from the arrowhead into the wooden shaft</term> of these heavy Japanese arrows is remarkably long—measuring seven or eight inches, or even up to one foot. This is the reason they sink so deeply into their targets. 

For testing long-distance shooting, eighty paces shall be the limit. For the infantry bow, a standard of five feet shall be used <gloss>referring to the Central Plains foot, a traditional unit of measure</gloss>. 

The materials and cloth coverings for the targets used with infantry bows are to be manufactured by each <term>Squadron Commander: Bazong; a military officer in charge of approximately 100 men</term>. Each squadron is to have four targets of various sizes. All targets must have the figure of a foreign enemy painted on cloth. These are to be supported by two wooden poles, with ropes at the four corners to secure them, allowing them to be easily carried on the march. 

The targets should stand seven feet high, resembling a man on horseback, and measure two feet wide, resembling a man’s torso from the front. In all cases, the targets should be made slightly "fatter" than an actual person <note>This was likely a psychological tactic to build the confidence of soldiers during training by providing a slightly larger striking surface</note>.

Once the lower ranks have finished a round of firing, and the first gun has been reloaded, they fire again to maintain speed. The **bird-gun** <term>Niao chong; the standard Chinese matchlock musket, named for its precision, which was said to be sufficient to hit a bird in flight</term> is fundamentally a superior weapon and the primary tool upon which we rely to strike the enemy. Yet why is it that the various soldiers do not reflect upon the importance of this reliance?

Whether during drills or on the battlefield, when the masses fire all at once, the smoke and fire create a blinding fog. It is not something that can be viewed clearly with one eye or pointed out with one hand. Yet, almost all the soldiers fail to hold the gun barrel level; they do not press the stock against their cheeks, and they do not align the **sights** <term>Zhao xing; literally "shining star," the metal bead or notch used for aiming</term>. Instead, they let their hands hang low, holding the gun with one hand and using the **match-cord** to ignite the powder with the other. 

Why is it that they do not use the **serpentine** <term>Longtou; literally "Dragon Head," the mechanical arm that holds the burning match and lowers it into the priming pan when the trigger is pulled</term>? It is simply because they are inexperienced, fearful, and panicked. In their haste, they do not take the time to fix the match-cord into the serpentine, seeking only convenience and speed. Firing in such a manner, they will certainly miss the mark. What then is the value of the bird-gun? 

Furthermore, the weapon is named "bird-gun" because it is said to be capable of hitting a bird in flight, owing to its precision and high hit rate. If one fires it in this haphazard way, the direction of the shot is not controlled by the man, and its destination is unknown. How could one <note>The text likely continues to ask how one could hope to defeat the enemy with such poor technique</note>...

Each squad has ten guns. If they are divided into two ranks, there are five guns per rank; if divided into five ranks, there are two guns per rank. If they are not divided into ranks, the ten guns form a single line.

### Fixed Schedule for Training
According to the national military administration system, the schedule is **three days of drill**<gloss> and </gloss>**five days of rest**<gloss>.</gloss> Each month, this provides **ten**<gloss> </gloss>**days** of training. <note>Li Jing (571–649 AD) was a brilliant general of the Tang Dynasty. His strategies were so influential they were codified into the "Seven Military Classics" of China.</note>

The spacing and density of the various units, as well as the placement of the troops, should follow the previously mentioned methods. ## The Method of Pei Xu
<note>Pei Xu was a military figure, likely from the Tang Dynasty, whose theories on practical encampment were highly regarded for their realism over superstition.</note>

In general, the encampment of a military force should be modeled after the design of city walls and palaces. It must be sturdy and secure so that it cannot be easily breached or thrown into chaos. 

Ancient methods often relied on the systems of the **Nine Palaces**, the **Six Jia**, the **Supreme One**, the **Heavenly Gate**, and the **Earthly Door**. These methods are all confusing and impractical for real-world affairs. 
<note>The original terms—*Jiugong*, *Liujia*, *Taiyi*, *Tianmen*, and *Dihu*—refer to complex systems of Taoist divination and cosmology often used in "magical" military strategy (Qimen Dunjia). The author here argues for a shift toward rational, terrain-based planning.</note>

Today, we simply take advantage of the mountains, rivers, and the terrain. We seek the convenience of water and grazing grass, and we establish defenses according to the difficulty or ease of the landscape. 

If the ground is flat, arrange the troops in a square; if the camp surrounds water, make it circular; if opening a path through mountains, make it winding; if following the flow of a river, let the camp curve with it. The goal is simply to be adaptable to the situation and convenient for use.

The rule states: Whenever making camp outside of enemy territory where the land is broad and flat, one should deploy the **Great Square Formation**. 
<note>original: *Dafang Zhen*</note> 

Inside this camp, there are seventeen small sub-camps. The distance between these sub-camps should be enough to accommodate the space of one additional camp. However, if one is within enemy territory, the layout must be narrow and tight, and one must not leave enough space between sub-camps to accommodate another. 

The four corners of the camp should be rounded <gloss>Rounded corners make it easier to inspect and secure the perimeter</gloss>. This is the first rule.

# Nine Methods of Encampment from the Various Schools

Whenever establishing a camp, one must define the boundaries and erect defensive barriers to ward off external enemies. The ancient methods consist of nine types. Generally, if the army is not staying for a long duration, they should employ "standing spears," "barred spears," "wagon camps," or <term>chevaux de frise</term> <note>original: *juma* (拒馬), literally "horse-blockers"; portable wooden frames with protruding spikes used to stop cavalry</note>.

Whenever employing the **Method for Wagon Encampments**, place one wagon every fifty paces. Every hundred paces, select one to serve as a <term>combat wagon</term> <note>original: *zhanche* (戰車); specifically outfitted for active engagement rather than just transport or barrier use</note>. Combat squads emerge from within the wagons. The sub-encampments and the outer perimeter of wagons are arranged in horizontal rows, with the oxen kept in the center for protection.

## Method for Wooden Palisades

Whenever constructing a **Wooden Palisade**, it is usually because the enemy is pressing too closely to allow for the building of permanent city walls or earthworks, or because the terrain—such as mountains or rivers—is too rocky and lacks enough soil for <term>rammed-earth construction</term> <note>original: *banzhu* (板築); a traditional method of building walls by tamping earth between wooden frames</note>. In such cases, timber is erected to form a palisade.

The shape (square or round) and the height are determined by the specific circumstances. The base of the timber must be buried deeply. The logs should be layered and staggered to fill any gaps.

## Method for Rope Encampments

Whenever employing the **Method for Rope Encampments**, it is for the purpose of protecting the horses. If the army enters enemy territory and fodder or grain supplies are insufficient, one must immediately select a location with water and grass to graze the livestock. Each man is issued one **horse-blocking rope**. At nightfall, these are used to form a "rope camp" to protect the animals.

The construction involves planting spears to serve as support posts. These are arranged in two tiers, with the horse-blocking ropes tied to them in a continuous, interlocking chain. The horses are kept in the center of the camp, with officers and herdsmen distributed among them; on all four sides, the horses are kept bridled so they cannot break loose and escape. 

Outside the camp, <term>watch posts</term> <note>original: *gengpu* (更鋪); small stations for sentries assigned to the night watches</note> are established. At night, the watch drums are struck in a circuit around the camp to maintain a state of readiness. Soldiers inside and outside the camp must each remain within their own designated boundaries and are not permitted to cross over or mingle. By doing so, infiltrators will find no opportunity to enter the camp.
