# Felt Fern

<note>original: "Shiwei"; scientific name: *Pyrrosia lingua*, literally "Flame-colored Tongue"</note>

The **Felt Fern** is a perennial medicinal herb featured prominently in the <term>Materia Medica <note>original: "Bencao," the traditional Chinese pharmacological record</note></term>. As illustrated in the accompanying woodcut, the plant is characterized by its broad, tongue-shaped fronds that grow from creeping rhizomes, often found clinging to the surface of damp rocks or tree trunks.

The name "Stone Leather" <note>a literal translation of the characters "Shi Wei"</note> refers to the plant's habitat and the tough, leathery texture of its leaves. In traditional practice, the Felt Fern was harvested for its "cooling" properties. It was primarily used to treat ailments of the bladder and urinary tract, as well as to clear heat from the lungs.

The illustration highlights the plant's morphology:
*   The **broad fronds** are shown with a distinct midrib.
*   The plant is depicted growing adjacent to a **rock formation**, emphasizing its nature as a lithophyte <note>a plant that grows on stone</note>.
*   The small dots often seen on the underside of the leaves in such woodcuts represent the sori <note>clusters of spore-producing receptacles</note>, which were a key identifying feature for ancient herbalists.

# Felt Fern

The **Felt Fern** grows upon the rocks within the mountain valleys of Huayin <note>a mountain in modern-day Shaanxi province, one of the Five Sacred Mountains of China</note>. Today, it can be found throughout the regions of Jin, Jiang, Chu, Hai, Fuzhou, and Jiangning Prefecture.

It grows in dense clusters upon stone surfaces. Its leaves resemble those of the willow tree, though the undersides are covered in fine hairs and spotted patterns that look like leather; it is for this reason that the plant is so named <note>The name "Shiwei" literally translates to "Stone Leather"</note>. It is said that those plants which grow where they cannot hear the sound of running water are of the finest medicinal quality.

The leaves should be harvested during the second and seventh lunar months and then dried in the shade for later use. This plant is also known by the names **Stone Swallow** and **Stone Skin**. Its flavor is a combination of bitter and sweet, and it is entirely non-toxic.

The Felt Fern is used to treat exhaustion as well as the <term>five types of urinary blockage <note>original: "Wulong," a category of ailments involving painful or obstructed urination</note></term>, abdominal masses, and internal obstructions caused by the accumulation of "heat." It is also effective for clearing heat from the bladder. Before medicinal application, the leaves should be lightly toasted.

# Seven-lobed Yam (*Bixie*)

The **Seven-lobed Yam** <note>original: *Bixie*; likely *Dioscorea septemloba*</note> grows in the valleys of Zhending <note>a historical region in modern-day Hebei Province</note>. Today, it is found throughout the various prefectures of Heshan, Jingdong, Jing, and Shu. The roots are yellowish-white with many nodes, roughly the thickness of a finger. Both the shoots and leaves are green, growing as a climbing vine. The leaves are trifurcate <note>split into three parts</note>, resembling those of the **Mountain Yam** <note>original: *Shanyu*</note> or the **Wild Bean** <note>original: *Mengdou*</note>. Its flowers come in several varieties—yellow, red, or white—though there is also a variety that bears no flowers but produces white seeds.

The roots are harvested in spring and autumn and then dried in the sun. According to older accounts, there is a variety of this medicine with thorny stems and solid white roots. The variety without thorns has roots that are hollow and soft; the soft variety is considered superior for medicinal use.

Currently, the specimens recently produced in Chengdejun <note>a Song-dynasty administrative region</note> have roots that also resemble the **Mountain Yam** but are hard in texture. Its shoots form trailing vines with leaves resembling **Buckwheat** <note>original: *Qiaomai*</note> and triangular seeds. The roots of this variety may be harvested at any time; one should use a sharp knife to slice them into pieces and dry them in the sun for use.

Another name for this plant is **Red Node** <note>original: *Chijie*</note>. Its flavor is bitter and balanced, and it is non-toxic. It is used to treat paralysis, weakness caused by "wind" disorders <note>referring to neurological or rheumatic conditions</note>, dizziness, and epilepsy. It tonifies the **Water Organ** <note>original: *Shuizang*; referring to the Kidneys in traditional Chinese medicine</note>, strengthens the tendons and bones, boosts the "essence" <note>vital life force</note>, and improves eyesight. It is also used to treat loss of speech following a stroke.

Medicinally, it is prized for strengthening the kidneys and bones and treating neurological conditions like paralysis and stroke-induced speech loss.</summary>

# Swallow-wort (*Baiwei*)

**Swallow-wort** grows in plains and river valleys. It is currently found in various prefectures of Shaanxi, as well as in Chu, Shu, Run, and Liao. Both its stems and leaves are green, and it closely resembles the leaves of a willow. It produces red flowers during the sixth and seventh months <note>Roughly July and August in the Gregorian calendar.</note> and bears fruit in the eighth month.

The roots are yellowish-white, similar to <term>Achyranthes <note>original: *Niuxi* (Ox-knee); a common medicinal root used to strengthen joints and promote blood flow.</note></term> but are shorter and smaller. The roots should be harvested on the third day of the third month and <note>To "dry in the shade" (*yingan*) is a specific pharmaceutical preparation method intended to preserve the plant's potency by avoiding direct sunlight.</note> dried in the shade for medicinal use.

It is also known as "White Curtain" <note>original: *Baimu*</note>, "Wei Grass" <note>original: *Weicao*</note>, "Spring Grass" <note>original: *Chunchao*</note>, and "Bone Beauty" <note>original: *Gumei*</note>. Its flavor is bitter, its nature is very cold, and it is non-toxic. It is used to treat sudden confusion and loss of consciousness, all manner of malevolent influences or ghostly possessions <note>In historical medical texts, these terms often refer to acute psychological distress, seizures, or unexplained sudden illnesses.</note>, and is primarily indicated for sudden <term>wind-strike <note>original: *Zhongfeng*; in traditional medicine, this refers to a sudden collapse, stroke, or acute neurological episode attributed to internal or external "wind."</note></term>.

# China Root <note>original: *Ba Qia*; the species *Smilax china*. This plant is a relative of the sarsaparilla used in Western herbalism.</note>

**China Root** grows in the mountains and wilds. Today, it is found in abundance throughout the prefectures and counties near the capital <note>referring to the Ming capital, Beijing</note> as well as in the Jiangsu and Zhejiang regions. The stems grow as climbing vines, reaching two or three feet in length, and are covered with thorns. Its leaves resemble those of the holly or the <term>Lindera <note>original: *Wu yao*; *Lindera aggregata*, a shrub used in traditional medicine</note></term>; they are also similar to the leaves of the water caltrop, though somewhat larger. In autumn, it produces yellow flowers and bears black seeds <note>berries</note> about the size of a cherry.

Its root grows in chunky masses and is reddish-yellow in color. The roots are harvested in the second and eighth months <note>of the lunar calendar</note> and dried in the sun for medicinal use. In the regions of Jiangsu and Zhejiang, it is known as <term>Vajra Root <note>original: *Jin gang gen*; "Vajra" implies the root is as hard and indestructible as a diamond</note></term>. When soaked, it produces a red juice. It can be boiled into a starch for food; it is said that eating it will <term>ward off miasma <note>original: *pi zhang*; "miasma" referred to the pestilential vapors thought to cause diseases like malaria in humid, southern climates</note></term>.

The leaves can be pounded with salt and applied to <term>wind-swellings <note>swelling associated with the 'wind' pathogen, often characterized by sudden onset</note></term> and malignant sores; this common folk remedy is effective. Poor rural households also use the root to brew wine to treat <term>wind-toxins <note>pathogenic factors causing skin eruptions or joint pain</note></term> and <term>leg weakness and numbness <note>original: *jiao ruo bi*; symptoms often associated with Beriberi or rheumatic conditions</note></term>. Preparing it as a decoction is particularly excellent for <term>ascending qi <note>original: *shang qi*; a condition where energy or breath moves upward abnormally, leading to coughing or wheezing</note></term>.

**Flavor:** Sweet. **Nature:** Warm. **Toxicity:** Non-toxic.

**Primary indications:** Cold pain in the lower back and spine, and <term>wind-numbness <note>original: *feng bi*; pain or loss of sensation in the joints and muscles caused by environmental factors</note></term>. It supplements vital energy <note>original: *qi*</note> and stops <term>frequent urination <note>original: *xiao bian li*; literally "convenience of urine," here referring to excessive frequency</note></term>.

# Great Blue

The origins of **Great Blue** <note>original: *Daqing*; typically refers to *Isatis tinctoria* or *Clerodendrum cyrtophyllum*.</note> were not recorded in older texts. However, it is now found throughout the prefectures of **Jiangdong** <note>The region east of the Yangtze River.</note>, as well as in the various prefectures of **Jingnan**, **Mei**, **Shu**, **Hao**, and **Zi** <note>These areas span modern-day Hubei, Sichuan, Anhui, and Shandong provinces, indicating the plant was widely distributed by the time of this writing.</note>.

In the spring, it sprouts bluish-purple stems that resemble the shoots of the **Chinese pink** <note>original: *Shizhu*; *Dianthus chinensis*.</note>. The leaves and the reddish-purple flowers are similar to those of **water pepper** <note>original: *Maliao*; *Polygonum hydropiper*.</note> and also resemble **lilac daphne** <note>original: *Yuanhua*; *Daphne genkwa*.</note>. The root is yellow.

During the third and fourth months of the lunar calendar, the stems and leaves are harvested and dried in the shade for medicinal use. Its flavor is bitter, and its nature is "greatly cold" <note>In traditional herbology, "cold" plants are used to counteract "hot" diseases like fevers and infections.</note> and non-toxic.

It is used to treat:
*   **Seasonal epidemics** <term>Seasonal epidemics (shiqi): infectious diseases attributed to unseasonable weather or "pathogenic winds."</term>
*   **Head-wind** <term>Head-wind (toufeng): a traditional category of headache, often chronic or recurring, believed to be caused by "wind" entering the body.</term>
*   **Intense fevers**
*   **Mouth sores**

# **Mugwort**

Mugwort leaves were not traditionally recorded as originating from specific prefectures or regions; older texts simply stated they grow in fields and wilderness. Today, they are found <gloss>everywhere</gloss>. Those that come from the Fudao region <note>Historical Fudao was located in present-day Tangyin County, Henan Province, famous for producing high-quality mugwort known as "Qi-Ai."</note> are considered the finest. 

It is said that this particular variety is exceptionally superior for curing the "hundred diseases" through **moxibustion** <note>The practice of burning dried mugwort on or near the skin at specific points to facilitate healing.</note>. In early spring, it spreads across the ground and produces sprouts. The stems resemble <term>wormwood</term> <note>original: *hao* (蒿); plants of the *Artemisia* genus</note>, but the undersides of the leaves are white. Plants with short sprouts are considered best. 

The leaves should be harvested on the third day of the third month and the fifth day of the fifth month <note>These dates on the lunar calendar are considered times of peak potency, particularly the fifth day of the fifth month, which is the Dragon Boat Festival.</note>. They should be dried in the sun and must be aged for a long time before they are fit for use <note>Aged mugwort, or "Chen Ai," is preferred in traditional medicine because it burns more mildly and is less irritating than fresh leaves.</note>. 

Among the common folk, the fresh leaves are also pounded to extract a juice. Drinking this juice stops "evil qi" <note>Noxious influences or stagnant energy causing pain</note> in the heart and abdomen. 

One name for this plant is *Bingtai* <note>original: 永臺 (*Yongtai*); likely a transcription error for 冰台 (*Bingtai*), meaning "Ice Terrace," a name derived from the ancient practice of using a crystal or ice lens to catch the sun's rays and ignite the mugwort.</note>; another name is *Yicao* <note>literally "Doctor's Herb."</note>.

**Properties:** 
The taste is bitter, its nature is slightly warm, and it is non-toxic.

**Primary Uses:** 
*   Mainly used in moxibustion to treat the "hundred diseases."
*   Stops diarrhea and the vomiting of blood.
*   Treats <term>erosion sores</term> <note>original: *nichuang* (䘌瘡); chronic, gnawing sores or ulcers, often in the lower body.</note> of the lower regions.
*   Addresses uterine bleeding in women.
*   Benefits the *yin* energy and promotes the growth of flesh and muscle.
*   Wards off wind and cold.
*   Helps a person to conceive children.

Mugwort leaves were not traditionally recorded as originating from specific prefectures or regions; older texts simply stated they grow in fields and wilderness. Today, they are found <gloss>everywhere</gloss>. Those that come from the Fudao region <note>Historical Fudao was located in present-day Tangyin County, Henan Province, a region famous for producing high-quality mugwort known as "Qi-Ai."</note> are considered the finest. 

It is said that this particular variety is exceptionally superior for curing the "hundred diseases" through **moxibustion** <note>The practice of burning dried mugwort on or near the skin at specific acupuncture points to stimulate healing through heat.</note>. In early spring, it spreads across the ground and produces sprouts. The stems resemble <term>wormwood</term> <note>original: *hao* (蒿); a general term for various plants in the *Artemisia* genus</note>, but the undersides of the leaves are white. Plants with short sprouts are considered best. 

The leaves should be harvested on the third day of the third month and the fifth day of the fifth month <note>These dates on the lunar calendar are considered times of peak medicinal potency. The fifth day of the fifth month (Dragon Boat Festival) is especially significant for gathering herbs to ward off summer pestilence.</note>. They should be dried in the sun and must be aged for a long time before they are fit for use <note>Aged mugwort, or "Chen Ai," is preferred in traditional medicine because it burns with a gentler, more penetrating heat and is less irritating to the skin than fresh leaves.</note>. 

Among the common folk, the fresh leaves are also pounded to extract a juice. Drinking this juice stops "evil qi" <note>In this context, "evil qi" refers to noxious influences, sudden illness, or stagnant energy causing acute pain</note> in the heart and abdomen. 

One name for this plant is *Bingtai* <note>original: 永臺 (*Yongtai*); likely a transcription error for 冰臺 (*Bingtai*), meaning "Ice Terrace." Ancient texts claim that one could carve a lens out of ice and use it to focus sunlight onto mugwort to start a fire.</note>.

# **Duckweed**

**Duckweed** <note>original: *Shuiping* (水萍).</note> grows in the marshes of Leize, though today it can be found everywhere in streams and ponds. This is the "large duckweed" of the water; its leaves are rounded and smooth, measuring about an inch across. Beneath each leaf is a small dot resembling a bubble. One name for this plant is **Yellow Floating Heart** <note>original: *Xingcai* (荇菜); *Nymphoides peltata*.</note>. The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary and botanical guide.</note> refers to it as *Ping*, noting that the larger variety is called *Pin*. 

It begins to grow in late spring and can be mixed and steamed to serve as a **cooked vegetable** <term>ru</term>. It can also be pickled in **vinegar** <note>original: *kujiu* (苦酒), literally "bitter wine," a common term for vinegar in ancient texts.</note> to serve as a side dish for wine. It should be harvested in the third month and dried in the sun.

The scholar Su Gong <note>A prominent Tang Dynasty physician and naturalist.</note> states that there are three distinct types:
*   The large one is called **Water Clover** <note>original: *Pin* (蘋); *Marsilea quadrifolia*.</note>.
*   The medium one is **Yellow Floating Heart** <note>original: *Xingcai* (荇菜).</note>, which is the same as the **Water Mallow** <note>original: *Fukui* (鳧葵)</note> described below.
*   The small one is the **Common Duckweed** <note>original: *Fuping* (浮萍); *Lemna minor*.</note> that grows in ditches and furrows.

Large duckweed is rarely utilized in medical prescriptions today. However, common physicians use the "floating duckweed" to treat seasonal fevers; it is highly effective at inducing sweat. It is also known by the names **Water Flower**, **Water White**, and **Water Fresh**.

Its flavor is pungent and sour, its nature is cold, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat sudden intense heat, itchy skin, and **edema** <note>original: *shuiqi* (水氣), literally "water-qi" or fluid retention.</note>. It is also used to counteract the effects of alcohol, promote the growth of beards and hair, and quench **wasting-thirst** <term>xiaoke</term> <note>A traditional medical term for symptoms of excessive thirst and urination, often associated with diabetes.</note>. Furthermore, it helps direct *qi* downward and treats burns, scalds, and wind-rashes.

# King Melon

The **King Melon** grows in the marshes and fields of the Lu region <note>Modern-day Shandong province.</note>, as well as among the walls and fences of residential homes; nowadays, it can be found everywhere. In the *Monthly Ordinances* <note>original: *Yueling* (月令), a classic ritual calendar found in the *Book of Rites*.</note>, the passage stating "in the fourth month, the King Melon grows" refers to this very plant. Its leaves resemble those of the **Snake Gourd** <note>original: *Gualou* (栝樓); *Trichosanthes kirilowii*.</note>; they are round without lobes or indentations and are covered in fine, hair-like prickles. 

In the fifth month, it blossoms with yellow flowers, beneath which develop fruits shaped like small pellets. These are green when young and turn bright red when ripe. The root resembles that of the **Kudzu** vine but is thinner and contains a great deal of starch; it is called **Earth Melon Root** <note>original: *Tugua gen* (土瓜根).</note>. 

In the northern regions, the fruits <gloss>hang in clusters</gloss> linked together, each about the size of a jujube date, with yellow skin and white flesh. The sprouts and leaves are entirely similar to the southern variety, though the shape of the root differs. 

The roots are harvested in the third month and dried in the shade. People in the Jun and Fang prefectures call it the **Old Crow Melon** <note>original: *Laoyagua* (老鴉瓜).</note> or the **Lou Melon** <note>original: *Lougua* (蔞瓜).</note>, though the Lou Melon is actually a distinct species. In terms of medicinal properties, its flavor is bitter, its nature is cold, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat **Gu-poison** <term>Gu-poison (蠱毒): a legendary type of medicinal or magical toxin associated with southern folklore, often involving the concentrated venom of several insects or reptiles.</term>, and it is also used to treat frequent urination or urinary incontinence.

## Great Burnet

The **Great Burnet** <term>Diyu (地榆); *Sanguisorba officinalis*</term> grows in the valleys of Tongbai and Yuanju <note>Tongbai is located in modern-day Henan province; Yuanju is in modern-day Shandong.</note>; nowadays, it can be found everywhere. Its perennial root <gloss>a root that survives underground for multiple years</gloss> produces new sprouts within the third month of the year.

When the plant first emerges, it spreads across the ground, but the stem eventually grows straight to a height of three or four feet, with leaves branching out in opposite pairs. These leaves resemble those of the **Elm** <note>The "yu" (榆) in the plant's name refers to the Elm tree, as the foliage is similar.</note>, though they are slightly narrower and more elongated, with serrated edges and a green color. In the seventh month, it blossoms with purple flowers that shaped like mulberries. The root is black on the outside and red on the inside, resembling the root of a **Willow**.

The roots are harvested in the second and eighth months and dried in the sun; the leaves are not used for medicinal purposes. Its flavor is bitter, its nature is slightly cold, and it is non-toxic. It is used to drain pus and to stop the vomiting of blood, profuse nosebleeds <note>original: *bihong* (鼻洪), literally a "nasal flood."</note>, excessive menstrual flow, heavy uterine bleeding, and various blood-related ailments occurring both before and after childbirth. It also treats "red and white" dysentery <note>In traditional medicine, "red" refers to bloody stools, while "white" refers to the presence of mucus.</note> as well as watery diarrhea. When boiled into a thick decoction, it stops **Intestinal Wind** <term>Changfeng (腸風); a traditional term for rectal bleeding often attributed to "wind" and "heat" pathogens.</term>.

# Small Thistle

Regarding the **Small Thistle** <term>Xiaoji (小薊); *Cirsium setosum*</term>, the *Classic of Materia Medica* <note>Refers to the *Shennong Benjing*, the earliest known Chinese pharmacopeia.</note> does not record the specific provinces or lands where it originates. Today, it can be found everywhere. Its common name is the **Green Prickly Thistle** <note>original: *Qingciji* (青刺薊)</note>.

The plant grows over a foot tall, and its leaves are covered in many prickles. From the center of the plant, a flower head emerges that resembles the **Safflower** <note>Carthamus tinctorius</note> but is blue-purple in color. People in the north call it **Thousand Needle Grass** <note>original: *Qianzhencao* (千針草)</note>. 

During the second month of the year, when the sprouts have just grown two or three inches high, they can be harvested along with the roots to be eaten as a cooked vegetable; they are quite delicious. 

The sprouts are harvested for medicinal use in the fourth month, and the roots are harvested in the ninth month. Both should be dried in the shade before being added to prescriptions. One may also pound the fresh root to extract the juice; drinking this juice is a proven remedy for stopping the vomiting of blood, nosebleeds <note>original: *nüxie* (衄血)</note>, and blood in the stool. 

The roots and sprouts of the **Great Thistle** <term>Daji (大薊); *Cirsium japonicum*</term> are similar to this plant, though they are simply larger and fleshier; however, their medicinal potencies differ. Aside from breaking up blood stasis <note>The "breaking" of blood refers to resolving internal bruising or stagnant circulation.</note>, the Great Thistle also treats carbuncles and swellings. The Small Thistle, however, specializes primarily in blood-related ailments. Its flavor is sweet and its nature is warm.

# Seaweed

Seaweed grows in the marshes of the Eastern Sea; it is currently produced in various prefectures such as Deng and Lai. <note>These refer to coastal regions in modern-day Shandong Province.</note> There are two varieties. One variety has leaves resembling **Chinese mint** <note>original: *jisu* (雞蘇), an aromatic herb</note> and stems like sinew, reaching four to five feet in length. Another variety has stems shaped like **hairpins** and leaves like **mugwort**; this is called **"Clustered Algae."** <note>original: *juzao* (聚藻)</note>

What is commonly used as Seaweed today is that which grows within the sea, with roots attached to the stones on the seafloor. It is black in color, resembling a mass of tangled hair but coarser. Its leaves are similar to freshwater algae but larger; it is known as **"Large-leaf Algae."** <note>original: *dayezao* (大葉藻)</note> The *Classic of Materia Medica* <note>Referring to the *Shennong Ben Cao Jing*, the foundation of Chinese herbology.</note> states that its primary use is for governing **goiters and tumors**.

According to the *Classic*, Seaweed is also named *Zao*. However, the *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary and encyclopedia, dating to the late first millennium BCE.</note> refers to *Zao* as **"Stone Clothing."** It also states that *Zao* is alternatively called **"Water-like"** or **"Stone Hair."** This **"Stone Hair"** is in fact **"Zhili."** <note>A term historically used for certain aquatic mosses or lichens.</note>

Its flavor is bitter, its nature is cold, and it is non-toxic. It is used to treat undigested food, the **five types of esophageal blockages** <note>original: *wuge* (五膈), referring to various obstructions in the throat or chest</note>, phlegm congestion, edema caused by fluid retention, **beriberi**, and **"running piglet" qi**. <note>original: *bentunqi* (賁豚氣); a traditional medical syndrome characterized by a sensation of gas or energy rushing upward from the lower abdomen to the throat, often linked to palpitations or anxiety.</note>

# Shiny Bugleweed (Zelan)

Shiny Bugleweed grows near the great marshes of Ru’nan <note>Ru’nan is a historical prefecture in modern-day Henan Province.</note>. Nowadays, it is also found in the prefectures of Jing, Xu, Sui, Shou, Shu, Wuzhou, and Hezhong. The root is a purplish-black color, resembling the root of a chestnut tree. In the second or third month of the year, the sprouts emerge, reaching a height of one, two, or even three feet. The stems are a bluish-purple color and are four-sided <note>The "four-angled" stem is a classic identifying feature of the Lamiaceae or mint family</note>. The leaves grow in opposite pairs, appearing much like peppermint and possessing a faint fragrance. In the seventh month, it produces purplish-white flowers; the calyx <note>The protective outer layer of a flower</note> is entirely purple, also resembling the flowers of peppermint. The sprouts should be harvested in the third month and dried in the shade. The variety produced in Shouzhou <note>Modern-day Anhui Province</note> produces no flowers or seeds. It is a most essential ingredient in medicinal formulas for women <note>Historically used to treat postpartum issues and menstrual irregularities</note>.

There is also a variety called **Horse Orchid** <note>original: *Malan*; likely *Aster indicus*</note> which grows near marshes and looks very similar to Shiny Bugleweed, but it has a foul odor and a pungent taste. It is also used primarily to break up blood stasis, heal wounds from metal blades, and stop internal bleeding.

Additionally, there is a variety called **Mountain Orchid** <note>original: *Shanlan*</note> that grows on the sides of hills. It resembles the *Liujinu* plant <note>Sweet Wormwood or *Artemisia anomala*</note>; its leaves have no lobes and do not grow in opposite pairs. The centers of the flowers are a faint yellowish-red. It also has the power to break up blood stasis, and all these varieties may be used for such purposes.

It is also known by the names **Tiger Orchid** (*Hulan*), **Dragon Jujube** (*Longzao*), and **Tiger Sedge** (*Hupu*). Its flavor is bitter and pungent; its nature is slightly warming and non-toxic.

# Fangji

<term>Fangji: The dried root of *Stephania tetrandra* (known as Han Fangji) or *Sinomenium acutum* (known as Wood Fangji). It is a staple of traditional medicine used to "expel wind" and drain dampness from the body.</term>

->[Image: A woodcut botanical illustration of the Fangji plant, showing its climbing vine-like stem, heart-shaped leaves, and the thick, knotted root system that is the primary source of its medicinal properties.]<-

Fangji originates in the valleys of Hanzhong <note>A region in modern-day Shaanxi Province known historically for its rich herbal diversity.</note>. Today, it is also widely found throughout the Jiangxi and Zhejiang regions.

The plant grows as a climbing vine, often reaching lengths of over ten feet. Its stem is slender, roughly the thickness of a chopstick. The leaves resemble those of the *Bixie* plant <note>The Seven-lobed Yam, or *Dioscorea septemloba*</note>. The root itself is substantial, often as thick as a finger, and possesses a yellowish-white color.

There are two primary types distinguished in classical medicine:
*   **Han Fangji** <note>original: *Hanfangji*</note>: The root is light and porous. It is considered superior for promoting the flow of fluids and treating edema <note>The accumulation of excess water in the body’s tissues.</note>.
*   **Wood Fangji** <note>original: *Mufangji*</note>: The root is solid and dense. It is primarily used to treat "wind" related ailments and joint pain, though it is considered to have a more "blocking" or astringent effect than the Han variety.

# Fangji

Fangji grows in the river valleys of Hanzhong <note>A region in Shaanxi province famous for high-quality medicinal herbs.</note>; today it is also found in the Qianzhong region <note>A historical administrative area covering parts of modern-day Guizhou and western Hunan.</note>. However, those produced in Hanzhong are the most distinct: when the root is broken open, the internal markings resemble the spokes of a wheel <note>original: *chefu jie* (車輻解). This refers to the radial pattern of the vascular bundles, a key diagnostic feature for identifying the authentic root.</note>. These specimens are yellow, solid, and fragrant.

The stems and stalks are very tender, while the sprouts and leaves are small and resemble those of the Morning Glory. If you snap the stem and blow into one end, the air passes directly through to the other side, similar to the structure of the *Mutong* vine <note>The Akebia plant, literally "the wood that communicates," named for its air-conducting vessels.</note>.

In contrast, those found in other regions are greenish-white, hollow and soft, and possess a fishy odor. The skin is wrinkled and covered in small, nail-like protrusions <note>original: *dingzuzi* (丁足子), likely referring to the leaf scars or small rootlet attachments on the rhizome.</note>; these are known as <term>Wood Fangji (*Mufangji*)</term>. 

The roots are harvested in the second and eighth months of the lunar calendar and dried in the shade for use. Although Wood Fangji is not commonly included in medicinal prescriptions today, it was frequently used in ancient formulas.

Another name for this herb is **Jieli** <note>original: *Jieli* (解離), meaning "The Dissolver of Stagnation" or "Release from Separation."</note>. Its flavor is pungent and bitter; its nature is balanced to warm and is non-toxic.

It is highly effective in treating "damp-wind" <note>A traditional pathological category involving joint pain, heaviness, and stiffness caused by environmental moisture and wind.</note>, as well as facial paralysis <note>specifically the deviation of the mouth and eyes</note> and pain in the hands and feet. It also disperses lingering phlegm and is primarily used to treat Lung Qi conditions such as coughing and wheezing.

# Galangal

Historically, records did not list the specific prefectures and regions where Galangal <note>original: *Gāoliángjiāng* (高良薑)</note> was produced. # Tianma

Tianma <note>original: 天麻 (tiānmá); *Gastrodia elata*, an orchid that lives underground for most of its life cycle</note> grows in the regions of Yunzhou and Lizhou, as well as on Mount Tai, Mount Yi, and various other mountains. In the spring, the shoots emerge; when they first appear, they resemble those of a peony. A single stalk sprouts directly upward, reaching a height of two to three feet. Shaped like the shaft of an arrow and possessing a greenish-red color, it is also known as the **Red Arrow** <note>original: 赤箭 (chìjiàn)</note>. The stalk is hollow; from its midpoint upward, small, pointed leaves are attached sparsely to the stem. At the tip, spikes form which flower and bear seeds about the size of a bean. These seeds do not fall from the plant until the summer.

The root is shaped like a cucumber, and ten to twenty of them typically grow joined together. Large specimens weigh half a *jin* <note>approximately 250 grams</note> or five to six *liang* <note>approximately 150-180 grams</note>. The skin is yellowish-white and is called **Dragon Skin** <note>original: 龍皮 (lóngpí)</note>, while the flesh inside is known as **Tianma**. 

The roots are harvested during the second, third, fifth, or eighth lunar months. When first gathered, while they are still moist, the skin is scraped off, then they are briefly boiled in water and dried in the sun for storage. Alternatively, the fresh roots may be preserved in honey and eaten as a fruit, which is considered a great delicacy. 

The flavor is **acrid** and its nature is **balanced**; it is **non-toxic**. It is used to treat all varieties of <term>wind-dampness numbness</term> <note>original: 諸風濕痺 (zhū fēng shī bì); a category of illness including rheumatism and joint pain thought to be caused by environmental "wind" and "dampness"</note>, muscle spasms in the four limbs, pediatric epilepsy, and disorders caused by sudden fright. It is also beneficial for the waist and knees and strengthens the power of the sinews and bones.

**Glossary of Terms:**
*   **Tianma:** Heavenly Hemp
*   **Red Arrow:** The descriptive name for the plant's stalk
*   **Dragon Skin:** The outer peel of the medicinal tuber
*   **Wind-Dampness Numbness:** A condition similar to modern rheumatism or neuralgia
*   **Pediatric Epilepsy:** Seizure-like conditions in children
*   **Illustrated Materia Medica:** The title of the source text (*Tu Jing Ben Cao*)
*   **Yunzhou / Mount Yi:** Specific geographic locations in historical China where the herb was sourced

# Asafoetida
<note>original: 阿魏 (*āwèi*); the resin of *Ferula assa-foetida*, known for its pungent odor and potent medicinal properties</note>

Asafoetida originates from the Western Barbarian regions <note>original: 西蕃 (*Xīfān*); a historical term for the regions of Central and Western Asia</note> and the Kunlun Mountains. Today, it is found only in Guangzhou <note>referring to the city's role as the primary maritime trade hub for imported exotic goods during the Ming Dynasty</note>. 

Older accounts claim that its sprouts, leaves, and roots closely resemble those of the Dahurian Angelica <note>original: 白芷 (*báizhǐ*); a common Chinese medicinal herb</note>. The highest quality of the medicine is made by pounding the juice from the roots and simmering it in the sun to form cakes. The second best quality consists of roots that have been sliced, threaded together, and sun-dried. 

The variety currently coming out of Guangzhou is said to be a resinous sap that drips from a tree and congeals into a solid form. These two accounts of the plant's nature differ. The *Miscellaneous Morsels from Youyang* <note>original: 酉陽雜俎 (*Yǒuyáng Zázǔ*); a Tang Dynasty miscellany of exotic lore and natural history compiled by Duan Chengshi</note> states: 

"The Asafoetida tree grows in the Kingdom of Persia <note>original: 波斯國 (*Bōsī Guó*)</note>, where it is called the *Ayu* tree <note>a transliteration likely derived from a Middle Persian name for the plant</note>. It grows to a height of eight or nine feet. Its bark is greenish-yellow in color. In the third month, it produces leaves that resemble mouse ears. It has neither flowers nor fruit. When a branch is broken, a sap flows out like maltose syrup. Over time, it hardens and congeals; this substance is called Asafoetida."

Others say that the sap is collected and mixed with rice or bean flour to create a blended product. This description is closer to the variety currently presented in Guangzhou.

Its flavor is acrid, its nature is balanced <note>meaning it is neither heating nor cooling in the body</note>, and it is non-toxic. It is used to treat <term>consumptive wasting disease</term> <note>original: 傳屍 (*chuánshī*); literally "corpse-transmission," a historical term for infectious, chronic illnesses like tuberculosis</note>, to break up <term>abdominal masses</term> <note>original: 癥積 (*zhèngjī*); refers to hard tumors or stagnations in the digestive tract</note>, and to expel "cold qi." It is used to ward off pestilence and treat malaria. Additionally, it is primarily used for <term>sudden turmoil disorder</term> <note>original: 霍亂 (*huòluàn*); a historical term for acute vomiting and diarrhea, often translated as cholera</note>, pain in the heart and abdomen, and disorders of the "kidney qi." It treats <term>warm miasma</term> <note>original: 溫瘴 (*wēnzhàng*); a type of epidemic illness associated with hot, humid climates</note> and serves as an antidote against toxins found in all types of herbs and vegetables.

# Stemona Root
<note>original: 百部 (*bǎibù*); literally "hundred parts," so named because the plant produces a large cluster of many tuberous roots. In traditional medicine, it is primarily used to treat chronic coughs and to eliminate parasites.</note>

Stemona root is found throughout the regions of the Yangtze and Huai rivers, as well as in the provinces of Shaanxi, Henan, and Shandong <note>original: 江湖淮陜齊魯 (*jiānghú huái shǎn qí lǔ*); these terms refer to historical geographic regions and provinces of China</note>. # Li-fragrant Seeds

# Fennel Seeds
<note>original: 蘹香子 (*huáixiāngzǐ*). This is an archaic name for Fennel.</note>

Fennel seeds are also known as *Huixiang* <note>original: 茴香</note>. The *Classic of Materia Medica* <note>The *Shennong Bencao Jing*, the oldest known Chinese pharmaceutical text.</note> does not record its place of origin. Today, it is found in the southern regions of Jiaozhi and Guangdong, among various foreign peoples, and in nearby commanderies.

For medicinal use, those brought by foreign trade ships are most commonly used, though some say these are not as potent as the varieties found closer at hand. In the third month, it sprouts leaves that resemble "old coriander" <note>Cilantro that has grown large and tough.</note> but are extremely sparse and fine, growing in thickets. By the fifth month, the plant reaches a height of three to four feet. In the seventh month, it produces flower heads shaped like a parasol or umbrella, yellow in color. It bears fruit like grains of wheat, though smaller and green. 

People of the north call it "Native Fennel" <note>original: 土茴香 (*tǔ huíxiāng*)</note>. It is said that the names *Hui* and *Huai* are used because their sounds are similar.

In the eighth and ninth months, the seeds are harvested and dried in the shade. Today, it is very commonly planted in the gardens and orchards of households living near the main roads. 

**Properties:** Its flavor is acrid, its nature is balanced, and it is non-toxic. 

**Medical Uses:** It treats sudden "Kidney Qi" <term>Kidney Qi (shènqì): In traditional Chinese medicine, this refers to the vital energy of the Kidney system. It is also very effective for regulating "Small Intestine Qi" <note>original: 小腸氣 (*xiǎocháng qì*). This historical term typically refers to lower abdominal pain or conditions like inguinal hernias.</note>.

# Coltsfoot Flowers

Coltsfoot flowers <note>original: 款冬花, *kuǎndōnghuā*</note> grow in the mountain valleys of Changshan <note>An ancient commandery in modern-day Hebei Province</note> and near the water in Shangdang <note>A region in modern-day Shanxi Province</note>. Today, they are also found in Guanzhong <note>the "land within the passes," the central Shaanxi plain</note>. The roots are purple, the stems are bluish-purple, and the leaves resemble those of the *Bixie* <term>Seven-lobed Yam: *Dioscorea septemloba*</term>.

In the twelfth lunar month, it opens yellow flowers with bluish-purple calyxes. When they first emerge one or two inches above the soil, they look like chrysanthemum buds; the stalks are straight and fleshy, and they produce no seeds.

There is also a variety with red flowers; its leaves are like those of a lotus but are upright and funnel-shaped. The larger leaves can hold a *sheng* <note>a traditional unit of volume, approximately one liter</note>, and the smaller ones hold several *ge* <note>deciliters</note>. They are colloquially called "Bee-Funnel Leaves" <note>original: 蜂斗葉, *fēngdǒuyè*</note> or "Water-Funnel Leaves" <note>original: 水斗葉, *shuǐdǒuyè*</note>.

The flowers are harvested in the eleventh lunar month and dried in the shade. Some say the flowers grow beneath the ice and should be harvested on the morning of the New Year <note>the first day of the first lunar month</note>.

Its alternative names include *Tuowu*, *Kedong* <note>literally "frozen pearl," referring to its ability to bloom in ice</note>, *Huxu* <note>literally "Tiger's Whiskers"</note>, *Wanxi*, and *Shidong*. Its flavor is acrid and sweet; its nature is warm and non-toxic.

It moistens the Heart and Lungs, benefits the Five Viscera <term>Wu Zang: the Heart, Liver, Spleen, Lungs, and Kidneys</term>, eliminates irritability, and supplements the body against exhaustion. It is used to dissolve phlegm and stop coughing, and to treat the coughing of blood, palpitations caused by a deficiency in the heart, and to clear the vision by "washing the liver." It is also used for wind-stroke <note>a traditional diagnostic category for conditions like stroke or sudden paralysis</note> and other such ailments.

## Safflower

The Safflower <note>original: 紅藍花, *hónglán huā*</note> is indeed the same as the "Red Flower" <note>original: 紅花, *hónghuā*</note>. It originally grew in the Liang and Han regions <note>referring to parts of modern-day Shaanxi and Sichuan provinces</note> and the Western Regions <note>the lands of Central Asia along the Silk Road</note>. Today, it can be found everywhere, planted by people in their gardens and fields.

The seeds are sown in winter, the sprouts emerge in spring, and the flowers bloom in summer. Beneath the flower, it forms a globular, nest-like head <note>the botanical involucre</note> covered in many spines. The petals and leaves emerge from the top of this head. Gardeners harvest the flowers while they are still wet with dew; once picked, new flowers bloom again until the cycle is exhausted and the harvest ends. Within the flower head, seeds form that are white and granulated, about the size of a small bean.

The flowers are dried in the sun to be used for dyeing a "True Red" color and for making rouge <note>original: 臙脂, *yānzhī*; a traditional cosmetic for lips and cheeks</note>. It is primarily used to treat postpartum blood disorders <note>in traditional medicine, safflower is a "blood invigorator" used to clear stasis</note>, and its seeds possess the same efficacy. Because its leaves somewhat resemble those of the Indigo plant <note>original: 藍, *lán*</note>, it was given the name "Red Indigo" <note>Honglan</note>; it is also called "Yellow Indigo."

The *Records of Diverse Matters* <note>original: 博物志, *Bówùzhì*, a 3rd-century work on geography and myth</note> states that this plant was obtained by Zhang Qian <note>the famous diplomat and explorer who opened the Silk Road during the Han Dynasty</note>. Its flavor is acrid, its nature is warm, and it is non-toxic. It is used to treat "wind-strike" in women, characterized by <term>blood heat</term> <note>a condition where heat in the blood causes restlessness or skin issues</note> and excessive thirst. It also treats <term>throat blockage</term> <note>original: 喉痺, *hóubì*</note> where the throat is swollen, congested, and impassable.

# Capital Three-Edged Sedge

The <term>Capital Three-Edged Sedge <gloss>Jing San Leng</gloss></term> <note>original: 京三稜, *jīng sān léng*; identified by modern botanists as *Sparganium stoloniferum*, commonly known as the Bur-reed</note> is found throughout the regions of <note>modern-day Henan, Shaanxi, and the Yangtze and Huai River basins</note> He, Shaan, Jiang, and Huai. In the spring, the sprouts grow to a height of three to four feet, resembling <term>Wild Rice <gloss>Jiaopu</gloss></term> <note>original: 茭蒲, *jiāo pú*; likely referring to *Zizania latifolia* or cattails</note>. The leaves are all characterized by having three distinct edges. During the fifth and sixth months, it produces flowers similar to those of <term>Nut Grass <gloss>Shacao</gloss></term> <note>original: 沙草, *shā cǎo*; likely *Cyperus rotundus*</note>, colored yellow and purple. 

The roots are harvested after the frost. The skin and fibrous whiskers are shaved away to reveal a yellow interior. The flavor is slightly bitter. The root is shaped like a small <term>crucian carp</term>, and those that are physically heavy are considered to be of the highest quality. This plant mostly grows near shallow water or within ponds and marshes. 

The roots initially grow into clumps as large as <term>Aconite <gloss>Fuzi</gloss></term>. Sometimes there are flat tubers that produce a lateral root, which in turn forms another clump and sends up new sprouts. Those that do not produce sprouts but only grow thin, spindly roots are called <term>Chicken-Claw Three-Edge <gloss>Jizhua San Leng</gloss></term>. Furthermore, those that do not produce even thin roots are called <term>Black Three-Edge <gloss>Hei San Leng</gloss></term>. Their size is inconsistent, and while they appear black on the outside, they are white once the skin is removed.

In <note>modern-day Shanxi province</note> Hezhong Prefecture, there is also a variety known as the <term>Stone Three-Edge <gloss>Shi San Leng</gloss></term>. Its roots are yellowish-white and shaped like the <term>prongs of a hairpin</term>. The leaves are green, resembling cattail sprouts, and grow to about a foot in height; these leaves also possess three edges. In the fourth month, it produces white flowers similar to those of <term>Water Pepper <gloss>Hongliao</gloss></term>. The roots are harvested in the fifth month and are also used to disperse <term>accumulated Qi <gloss>jiqi</gloss></term> <note>A traditional medical term referring to internal blockages, masses, or severe digestive stagnation</note>.

The <term>nature and flavor <gloss>qiwei</gloss></term> is bitter and balanced, and it is non-toxic. It is used to treat <term>malignant blood <gloss>exue</gloss></term> <note>A term for stagnant or pathological blood flow, often following childbirth or injury</note> in women and to promote menstrual flow. It also treats abdominal distension due to gas, as well as resolving bruising and internal blood stasis resulting from falls or physical trauma.

# Turmeric

<note>original: 薑黃, *jiānghuáng*; *Curcuma longa*. In traditional Chinese medicine, turmeric is distinct from common ginger, valued more for its ability to "break blood" and treat pain than for culinary heat.</note>

Turmeric was not recorded in ancient texts regarding the specific provinces or prefectures from which it originated. Today, it is found in abundance throughout the <term>Jiangguang <gloss>the southern regions of Jiangnan, Guangdong, and Guangxi</gloss></term> and <term>Shuchuan <gloss>Sichuan province</gloss></term> regions. 

The leaves are a vibrant green, roughly one to two feet long and three to four inches wide. They feature slanted veins similar to those of a red banana leaf, though they are smaller in size. The flowers are a mixture of red and white; they begin to wither by mid-autumn and return in the spring. Notably, the flowers of this plant emerge first, followed by the leaves. 

It does not produce fruit. The root is coiled and yellow, resembling fresh ginger but more rounded and segmented. Some claim that the "true" turmeric is actually old ginger that has been planted for more than three years, which then becomes capable of flowering. These flowers grow from the base of the root, much like those of the <term>Suohe <gloss>Zingiber mioga, or Japanese ginger</gloss></term>. 

The root segments are hard, with a pungent and spicy aroma and flavor. It is often found in places where ginger is cultivated. The roots are harvested in the eighth month, sliced into pieces, and dried in the sun. In terms of medicinal property, its taste is pungent and bitter; it is considered "very cold" in nature and is non-toxic. 

Its primary medicinal uses are for treating accumulations and masses in the chest and abdomen, as well as <term>zhuwu <gloss>a category of sudden, acute illnesses often attributed to "malevolent strikes" or parasitic infections</gloss></term>. It is used to descend <term>qi <gloss>vital energy</gloss></term>, break up blood stasis, dispel wind-heat, and dissolve abscesses or swellings. Its medicinal potency is said to be even more intense than that of <term>Yujin <gloss>Curcuma aromatica, or Wild Turmeric</gloss></term>.

## Long Pepper

<note>original: 蓽撥, *Bìbō*; *Piper longum*. This plant is a close relative of black pepper but with a more intense, earthy heat. It was a significant commodity in the maritime spice trade.</note>

Long Pepper originates from the kingdom of <term>Persia <gloss>modern-day Iran; though the plant is native to South Asia, it reached China via Persian trade routes</gloss></term>, though it is also found in <term>Lingnan <gloss>the region south of the Five Ridges, including modern Guangdong and Guangxi</gloss></term>. It grows most abundantly within bamboo groves. In the first lunar month, it sends out sprouts that grow into clusters reaching three to four feet in height. Its stems are as slender as <term>chopsticks <gloss>original: 筯, zhù</gloss></term>. The leaves are a vibrant green, round, and two to three inches wide; they resemble mulberry leaves but are glossy and thick.

In the third month, the plant produces white flowers on its exterior surface. By the seventh month, it bears fruit about the size of a little finger, measuring one to two inches long, and colored a bluish-black. These fruits resemble <term>mulberries <gloss>original: 椹子, shènzi</gloss></term>. In the ninth month, the crop is harvested. The leaves are treated with ash to cure them and then dried. People of the south prize the plant for its pungent fragrance; some take the fresh leaves and eat them raw.

The flavor is <term>acrid <gloss>spicy</gloss></term>, its nature is <term>greatly warming</term>, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat "cold *qi*" <note>In traditional medicine, "cold qi" refers to ailments caused by poor circulation or exposure to cold environments</note>, nausea and vomiting, abdominal bloating and fullness, and indigestion. It is also used for <term>cold hernias <gloss>hánshàn; pain in the lower abdomen or scrotum</gloss></term>, swollen nodes, and infertility in women caused by internal cold. Furthermore, it treats coldness in the waist and kidneys and dispels stagnant blood and *qi*.

## Betel Pepper

<term>Betel Pepper</term> <note>original: 蒟醬, *jǔjiàng*; *Piper betle*</note> grows in the regions of Ba and Shu <note>modern-day Sichuan and Chongqing</note>. ## Turmeric Tuber

Turmeric Tuber <note>original: 鬱金, *yùjīn*; likely referring to *Curcuma aromatica* or Aromatic Turmeric</note> grows in the lands of Shu <note>modern-day Sichuan province</note> and among the Western Rong <note>a historical term for ethnic groups living to the west of China</note>. Foreigners <note>literally "Hu" people</note> call it *Mashu* <note>original: 馬述</note>. It is also found in the southern prefectures of Guangnan <note>the Guangdong and Guangxi regions</note> and Jiangxi, though these varieties are not as high in quality as those from the Sichuan region.

In the fourth month of the lunar calendar, the sprouts emerge, resembling those of Common Turmeric <term>Jianghuang <note>original: 薑黄, *Curcuma longa*</note></term>. The flowers are white with a reddish tint at the tips. In late autumn, the stems emerge from the center of the plant, but they produce no fruit. The roots are yellow-red in color. To prepare it for use, one should harvest the lateral "child" roots from the four sides, peel off the skin, and dry them over a fire.

While ancient medical recipes rarely employed this herb, it is now frequently found in pediatric formulas and is widely used by horse doctors <note>veterinary practitioners</note>. Its flavor is pungent and bitter, and its essence is cold; it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat blood accumulations, to direct the <term>Qi <note>the body's vital energy</note></term> downward, to promote the regeneration of flesh <note>the healing of skin and muscle</note>, and to stop bleeding. It is effective at "breaking" evil blood <note>dissolving stagnant or congealed blood</note>, and treating bloody urine, as well as wounds caused by metal weapons <note>original: 金瘡, *jīnchuāng*</note>.

# Aloe

Aloe originates from the country of Persia <note>original: 波斯國, *Bōsī guó*</note>. Nowadays, it only arrives via Guangzhou. The plant grows in the mountains and wilds; its resin drips like tears and hardens to form the substance used for medicine. It is harvested regardless of the season or month. It is colloquially known as “Elephant Gall” <note>original: 象膽, *xiàngdǎn*</note>, a name given simply because of its extremely bitter taste.

Aloe is also known by the names *Nahui* or *Nuhui* <note>original: 訥會 / 奴會; these are phonetic transliterations likely derived from the Arabic *alwa* or Persian name for the plant</note>. Its flavor is bitter, its nature is cold, and it is non-toxic.

It is primarily used to treat irritability and agitation caused by “heat-wind” <term>heat-wind: a traditional Chinese medical concept where external heat factors lead to internal distress</term>, as well as heat trapped in the chest and diaphragm. It brightens the eyes and calms the heart. It is used to treat childhood epilepsy and convulsions <note>original: 驚風, *jīngfēng*; a category of acute childhood illnesses involving fever and tremors</note>. It also treats the “five types of malnutrition” <term>five types of malnutrition: a group of childhood digestive and wasting diseases known as *wǔgān*</term>, kills the “three worms” <term>three worms: a traditional term for various types of intestinal parasites</term>, and heals hemorrhoids, sores, and fistulas. Additionally, it serves as an antidote to neutralize poisoning from Croton seeds <note>original: 巴豆, *bādòu*; a potent and potentially toxic purgative plant</note>.

## Lingling Fragrance
<note>original: 零陵香, *línglíng xiāng*. Scientifically identified as *Lysimachia foenum-graecum*, a fragrant herb used in traditional medicine and perfumes.</note>

Lingling Fragrance grows in the mountain valleys of Lingling <note>A region in modern-day Hunan province, famous for these aromatic plants.</note>. # Villous Amomum (Suoshami)

<term>Villous Amomum; *Amomum villosum*</term> <note>original: 縮砂蜜, *suōshāmì*. Known today primarily as *sharen* (砂仁), this is a ginger-family plant used to treat digestive disorders.</note>

## Zedoary
### (Penge)

**Zedoary** <note>original: 蓬莪, *péng'é*; commonly known today as *Curcuma zedoaria*.</note> grows in the western regions and various prefectures of the south <note>original: 廣南, *Guangnan*; referring to the regions of modern-day Guangdong and Guangxi.</note>. The sprouts emerge in the third month of the lunar calendar in open fields. Its stem is as thick as a copper coin and reaches a height of two to three feet. The leaves are a bluish-white color, one to two feet long and about five inches wide, appearing quite similar to those of **Japanese Ginger** <note>original: 蘘荷, *rànghé* (Zingiber mioga).</note>.

In the fifth month, it produces flowers in the form of a spike; they are yellow with tips that are slightly purple. The root resembles ginger but grows more luxuriantly. Beneath the main root are tubers that look like chicken or duck eggs, though their size is inconsistent.

In the ninth month, the roots are harvested. The <term>coarse outer skin<gloss>original: 麤皮, *cūpí*</gloss></term> is shaved off, and the remaining root is steamed until cooked, then dried in the sun for use. This substance is extremely hard and difficult to grind or process. When it is time to use it, one must roast it in hot ashes until it is thoroughly heated through. While it is still hot, place it into a mortar and pound it; it will then break apart into a powder.

Its flavor is bitter and pungent; its nature is warm and non-toxic. It is used to treat "blood and qi" disorders in women and pain in the heart/chest area. It is used to "break up" <term>abdominal masses<gloss>original: 痃癖, *xuánpǐ*; a traditional term for chronic lumps or stagnations in the abdomen.</gloss></term> and "cold qi" <note>Referring to ailments caused by the stagnation of "cold" energy.</note>. It is effective when rubbed into or mixed with wine or vinegar for consumption.

# Asiatic Pennywort
<note>original: 積雪草, *jīxuěcǎo*; commonly known today as Gotu Kola, a staple in both culinary and medicinal traditions across Asia.</note>

Asiatic Pennywort grows in the river valleys of Jingzhou <note>a historical province covering parts of modern Hubei and Hunan</note>, though it is now found everywhere. Its leaves are round and about the size of a coin, with thin and strong stems that spread along the sides of mountain streams. Because the leaves resemble coins, the people of the Jing-Chu region call it <term>Ground Coin Herb<gloss>original: 地錢草, *dìqiáncǎo*</gloss></term>. *Xu Yi’s Medicinal Illustrations* refers to it as <term>Stringed-Coin Herb<gloss>original: 連錢草, *liánqiáncǎo*</gloss></term>. The seedlings and leaves should be harvested in the eighth and ninth months and dried in the shade for use.

Another account states that Stringed-Coin Herb is bitter in taste, cold in nature, and non-toxic. It grows in the low-lying wetlands of Xianyang and also in the ponds and marshes of Linzi and Jiyang commanderies; in these locations, it is very fragrant. It is commonly said that the round leaves resemble <term>Peppermint<gloss>original: 薄荷, *bòhé*</gloss></term>. In the regions of Jiangdong, Wu-Yue, and Danyang commandery <note>coastal and lower Yangtze regions</note>, it is extremely abundant, and the locals frequently eat it as a raw vegetable. 

In Liucheng commandery, north of the Yellow River, it is universally called <term>Sea Perilla<gloss>original: 海蘇, *hǎisū*</gloss></term>. It thrives near water and does not die during the winter. It is also found in the two capitals, Xianyang and Luoyang, where it is sometimes called <term>Foreign Peppermint<gloss>original: 胡薄荷, *húbòhé*</gloss></term>. It is found in many locations and is primarily used to treat intense heat, malignant sores, <term>carbuncles and ulcers<gloss>original: 癰疽, *yōngjū*</gloss></term>, spreading skin infections, <term>erysipelas<gloss>original: 赤熛, *chìbiāo*; literally "red fire," referring to acute skin inflammation</gloss></term>, skin redness, and general bodily heat.

## Willowleaf Swallowwort
<note>original: 白前, *báiqián*; scientifically known as *Cynanchum stauntonii*. The name literally translates to "White-Front."</note>

**Willowleaf Swallowwort** grows in Suizhou <note>a region in modern-day Hubei Province</note> and along the banks of river islands. In the second month of the year, the seedlings sprout to a height of about one foot. They resemble the slender willow in appearance, with leaves that are long and pointed. The roots are white and resemble those of <term>Achyranthes<gloss>original: 牛膝, *niúxī*, literally "Ox-knee"</gloss></term>, though they are shorter and smaller. The roots should be harvested in the third and eighth months and dried in the shade for medicinal use.

The scholar-official Su Song <note>a famous Song Dynasty scientist and pharmacologist</note> states: "Nowadays, it is found throughout the regions near the capital, as well as in the Huai and Zhe administrative circuits. The seedlings grow one to two feet high. The leaves resemble those of the willow, or sometimes the peach tree. In the sixth month, it produces red flowers. The roots resemble Achyranthes and are white in color."

# Willowleaf Swallowwort
<note>original: 白前, *báiqián*; scientifically identified as *Cynanchum stauntonii*</note>

In older records, the specific prefectures and territories where **Willowleaf Swallowwort** originated were not documented. The scholar-hermit **Tao Hongjing** <note>original: 陶隱居, *Táo Yǐnjū*, a famous 5th-century alchemist and physician</note> stated that it was produced in regions near the capital. Nowadays, it is found throughout the prefectures of **Central Shu** <note>modern-day Sichuan</note> as well as the **Huai and Zhe** regions <note>the areas surrounding the Huai River and the provinces of Zhejiang and Jiangsu</note>.

The seedlings resemble <term>Asarum<gloss>original: 細辛, *xìxīn*, often called Wild Ginger</gloss></term> but are larger in size. They are white in color and brittle, breaking easily. Some varieties have leaves resembling those of a willow, while others look like the sprouts of the <term>Genkwa Flower<gloss>original: 芫花, *yuánhuā*, *Daphne genkwa*</gloss></term>. Both types grow to about a foot in height and are typically found on river islands or sandy shoals.

The roots are white and longer than those of Asarum; they also bear a resemblance to <term>Achyranthes<gloss>original: 牛膝, *niúxī*, literally "Ox-knee"</gloss></term>. They are white and slightly <unclear>firm</unclear> <note>original: 蘄, *qí*; this character may refer to a specific texture or a regional variety from the Qi region</note>. The climbing, vine-like varieties used by some today which possess a bitter taste are not the genuine herb.

The roots should be harvested in the second and eighth months of the year and sun-dried for use. Its flavor is sweet, its nature is slightly warm, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat <term>Qi Reversal<gloss>original: 逆氣, *nìqì*</gloss></term> in the chest and flanks, as well as coughing and <term>Ascending Qi<gloss>original: 上氣, *shàngqì*</gloss></term> <note>a condition in traditional medicine where the breath is forced upward, causing panting or respiratory distress</note>.

# Ladybell
<note>original: 薺尼, *jìní*; scientifically identified as *Adenophora triphylla*, a species of flowering plant in the bellflower family</note>

In older records, the specific regions where the Ladybell <note>original: 薺尼, *jìní*</note> grows were not documented. Today, it is known to be found throughout the regions of Sichuan, Shu, Jiangsu, and Zhejiang. In the spring, its sprouts and stems grow to look very much like Ginseng <note>original: 人參, *rénshēn*</note>, though its leaves are slightly different. The root resembles that of the Balloon Flower <note>original: 桔梗, *jiégěng*; *Platycodon grandiflorus*</note>, but it is distinguished by the fact that it has no central core.

The roots should be gathered during the second and eighth lunar months and dried in the sun for medicinal use. Its flavor is sweet, and its nature is "cold" <note>in Traditional Chinese Medicine, "cold" plants are used to clear heat and toxins</note>. It is primarily used to detoxify all manner of medicinal poisonings. It is also used to treat snake and insect bites, manic fevers, and acute epidemic diseases.

# White Medicine
<note>original: 白藥, *báiyào*; historically associated with *Stephania cepharantha*</note>

**White Medicine**, also known as **White Medicine Resin** <note>original: 白藥脂, *báiyàozhī*</note>, grows in Bianzhou <note>modern-day Kaifeng, Henan province</note>. Its root resembles a smaller version of the Rehmannia root <note>original: 地黃, *dìhuáng*</note>. The plant grows as a creeping vine, and its leaves are similar to those of the Wolfberry <note>original: 枸杞, *gǒuqǐ*</note>.

# White Medicine

**White Medicine** <note>original: 白藥, *báiyào*; likely referring to the root of *Stephania cepharantha*</note> originates in Yuanzhou <note>modern-day Guyuan, Ningxia</note>. Today, it is also found in Kui <note>modern-day Chongqing</note>, Shi <note>modern-day Enshi, Hubei</note>, Jiangshan <note>modern-day Zhejiang</note>, and throughout Lingnan <note>the region covering Guangdong and Guangxi</note>.

In the third month, the seedlings sprout; they resemble bitter lettuce <note>original: 苦苣, *kǔjù*; a type of chicory or sow thistle</note>. In the fourth month, the stems turn red and grow long, resembling the vines of a calabash <note>original: 葫蘆蔓, *húlúwàn*</note>. It produces white flowers in the sixth month and sets seeds in the eighth month. It is also known as **Baqia** <note>original: 菝葜, *báqiā*; typically refers to China Root or *Smilax*, suggesting a similarity in form or local nomenclature</note>. In the ninth month, the roots are harvested, washed with water, chopped into small pieces, and dried in the sun; these are called **White Medicine Seeds** <note>original: 白藥子, *báiyàozǐ*</note>.

The variety produced in Jiangxi has leaves that resemble the Tallow tree <note>original: 烏柏, *wūbiù*; *Triadica sebifera*</note> and seeds like mung beans. By the eighth month, these seeds turn a bright red color.

**Properties and Medical Applications:**
Its flavor is pungent, its nature is warm, and it is non-toxic. Its primary uses include:
*   **Golden Sores:** Used to heal wounds caused by metal weapons <note>such as blades or arrows</note> and to promote the growth of new flesh <note>original: 生肌, *shēngjī*; tissue regeneration</note>.
*   **Skin Diseases:** It is effective in treating malignant sores, scabies <note>original: 疥癬, *jièxuǎn*</note>, and "wind-itch" <note>original: 風瘙, *fēngsào*; a traditional term for various pruritic or itchy skin conditions associated with 'wind' pathogens</note>.

# Prince’s-feather
<note>original: 葒草, *hòngcǎo*; the species *Persicaria orientalis* (formerly *Polygonum orientale*)</note>

Prince’s-feather is precisely what is known as "Water Red" <note>original: 水紅, *shuǐhóng*</note>. Older records did not specify the prefectures or counties of its origin, simply stating that it grows beside water. Today, it can be found everywhere in low-lying, damp areas. It resembles water pepper <note>original: 蓼, *liǎo*, referring to plants in the knotweed family</note> but features larger leaves and a reddish-white color; it grows to a height of over ten feet <note>original: 丈餘, *zhàng yú*; a *zhang* is roughly 3.3 meters</note>.

The "Odes of Zheng" <note>a section of the *Classic of Poetry*, the oldest existing collection of Chinese poetry</note> state: "In the marshes grows the Wandering Dragon" <note>original: 游龍, *yóulóng*</note>; this refers to this very plant. Lu Ji <note>a 3rd-century scholar and poet</note> noted that another name for it is "Horse Knotweed" <note>original: 馬蓼, *mǎliǎo*</note>. The *Classic of Materia Medica* <note>original: 本經, *Běnjīng*; referring to the *Shennong Bencao Jing*</note> states that it resembles Horse Knotweed but is larger. If this is so, then Horse Knotweed is likely its own distinct variety <note>original: 穜, *zhǒng*, meaning a species or seed-type</note>.

The seeds are harvested in the fifth lunar month, though they are rarely used in medicine today. Instead, the roots and stems are primarily taken to make a medicinal broth to treat "foot-qi" <note>original: 脚氣, *jiǎoqì*; a historical term often associated with beriberi or swelling and weakness of the legs</note>. Its flavor is salty, its nature is slightly cold, and it is non-toxic. It also resolves "wasting-thirst" <note>original: 消渴, *xiāokě*; a traditional medical term for symptoms involving excessive thirst, often linked to diabetes</note>, clears internal heat, brightens the eyesight, and augments one's vital energy <note>original: 益氣, *yì qì*</note>.

# Nut Grass
<note>original: 莎草, *suōcǎo*; also known as *Cyperus rotundus* or purple nutsedge</note>

The root of Nut Grass is also known as <term>Xiangfu zi</term> <note>Literally "Fragrant Aconite-like seed," referring to the aromatic medicinal tubers</note>. It grows in fields and wild areas everywhere. Some claim that the variety from Jiaozhou <note>An ancient region encompassing parts of modern-day northern Vietnam and southern China</note> is superior; these are as large as jujube dates. Those found in more accessible, nearby regions are smaller, resembling apricot kernels. The shoots, stalks, and leaves all look like the <term>Three-edged Sedge</term> <note>Sanleng; typically referring to species of *Scirpus* or *Sparganium*</note>. The roots resemble <term>Aconite</term> (Fuzi) and are covered all over with fine hairs. The variety growing in nearby regions has leaves and shoots like chives but is thinner, with roots about the size of a chopstick head. They are harvested in the second and eighth months of the lunar calendar.

# Wild Duck Mallow
<note>original: 鳧葵, *fúkuí*. This aquatic plant is commonly known today as the Yellow Floating Heart (*Nymphoides peltata*).</note>

The Wild Duck Mallow is simply the **Water Fringe** <note>original: 荇菜, *xìngcài*</note>. It is found everywhere in ponds and marshes. Its leaves resemble those of the **Water Shield** <note>original: 蓴, *chún*; *Brasenia schreberi*</note>, and its stems and roots are very long. The flowers are yellow, and it grows in great abundance within the water. 

According to the *Erya* <note>The oldest extant Chinese dictionary, dating to roughly the 3rd century BCE</note>, the *xing* is called the *jieyu* <note>original: 接余</note>, and its leaves are called *fu* <note>original: 符</note>. The commentator **Guo Pu** <note>A famous 4th-century scholar and poet</note> states that they grow in clusters in the water; the leaves are round and sit at the tip of the stems, the length of which fluctuates according to the depth of the water. 

People of the **Jiangdong** region <note>The region south of the lower reaches of the Yangtze River</note> eat it. This is the same plant referred to as the "uneven water-fringe" in the "Zhounan" section of the *Classic of Poetry* <note>original: 詩經, *Shījīng*. This refers to the famous opening poem, "The Osprey," where the plant symbolizes a maiden being sought by a prince.</note>. 

**Lu Ji** <note>A 3rd-century scholar known for his works on natural history</note> says: "The stems are white while the leaves are a purplish-red. They are perfectly round, over an inch in diameter, and float upon the surface. The roots are at the bottom of the water, as thick as the prongs of a hairpin, green on the upper part and white below." 

If one boils the white stems and soaks them in **bitter wine** <note>original: 苦酒, *kǔjiǔ*; in ancient culinary contexts, this usually refers to vinegar</note>, they become crisp and delicious, making an excellent accompaniment for wine. 

Nowadays, people rarely eat it, and it is seldom used in medical prescriptions. Its flavor is sweet, its nature is cold, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat <term>wasting-thirst disorder</term> <note>original: 消渴, *xiāokě*; a traditional diagnosis for conditions characterized by extreme thirst and urination, similar to diabetes</note>, to relieve <term>heat strangury</term> <note>original: 熱淋, *rèlín*; painful or difficult urination attributed to internal 'heat'</note>, and to promote urination.

**Lǐcháng** <note>original: 鱧腸, *lǐcháng*; commonly known as False Daisy or *Eclipta prostrata*.</note> is indeed the **Liánzǐcǎo** <note>original: 蓮子草, "Lotus-seed Grass."</note>. One variety has leaves that resemble those of a willow but are glossy. Its stems are similar to **Purslane** <note>original: 馬齒莧, *mǎchǐxiàn*; *Portulaca oleracea*.</note> and stand one to two feet high. The flowers are small and white, and its fruit is like small, thin pods. **Sū Gōng** <note>A famous Tang Dynasty official and pharmacologist who edited the *Newly Revised Materia Medica*.</note> states that those with seedlings resembling the **Inula** <note>original: 旋復, *xuánfù*; a yellow flowering herb used in respiratory medicine.</note> belong to this kind.

Another variety has stems and seedlings that are thin and withered, appearing somewhat like a lotus flower but yellow in color. The fruit also forms pods, but they are round. People in the South call this variety **Liánqiào** <note>original: 蓮翹; while this name usually refers to Forsythia today, in this historical context it refers to a local variant of the False Daisy.</note>. 

If you pluck the seedlings of either of these two varieties, a juice will flow out that turns black in a very short moment. For this reason, it is frequently used in medicines for **blackening the whiskers and hair** <note>original: 烏髭髮, *wūzīfà*.</note>. It is commonly called **Hànliánzǐ** <note>original: 旱蓮子, "Dry Land Lotus Seed."</note>. It should be harvested in the third and eighth lunar months and dried in the shade. It is also known as **Jīnlíngcǎo** <note>original: 金稜草, "Golden Ridge Grass."</note>.

The flavor is sweet and sour, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat **bloody dysentery** <note>original: 血痢, *xuèlì*.</note>. For cases where bleeding from acupuncture or cautery wounds becomes profuse and cannot be stopped, applying this herb topically will cease the bleeding immediately. Smearing the juice on the hair and eyebrows causes them to grow rapidly and thickly. It is also made into an ointment to be applied inside the nose to **replenish the brain** <note>original: 添腦, *tiānnǎo*; a traditional concept referring to nourishing the marrow and clearing the sensory orifices.</note>.

<summary>This text describes the two varieties of the Lǐcháng plant (False Daisy), detailing its physical characteristics and its notable ability to turn black, which made it a popular hair dye. It also outlines its medicinal applications for stopping bleeding and treating dysentery.</summary>

# Fragrant Grass (*Máo xiāng*)

**Fragrant Grass** grows throughout the various prefectures of the <term>Jiannan Circuit<gloss>An administrative region in southwest China, primarily modern-day Sichuan</gloss></term>. Today, it is also found in the prefectures and commanderies of **Shaanxi**, **Hedong**, and **Jingdong**. 

In the third lunar month, the seedlings sprout, looking much like **barley**. By the fifth month, the plant produces **white flowers**, though some varieties bear **yellow flowers**. In some cases, the plant produces fruit, while in others it does not.

The harvesting schedule is as follows:
*   **Roots:** Gathered in the first and second lunar months.
*   **Flowers:** Gathered in the fifth lunar month.
*   **Seedlings:** Gathered in the eighth lunar month.

The specific variety characterized by **dark brown** stems and leaves with **white flowers** is known as **Fragrant Grass** <note>original: 茅香, *máo xiāng*; likely *Hierochloe odorata*, known for its vanilla-like scent</note>.

In terms of medicinal properties, its flavor is **bitter**, its nature is **warm**, and it is **non-toxic**. It is primarily used to treat <term>Evil Affliction<gloss>zhòng è; a traditional term for sudden illnesses thought to be caused by "evil winds" or miasmas</gloss></term>, to warm the stomach, and to stop vomiting. It is also used to treat **cold pains** in the heart and abdomen <note>The "heart and abdomen" refers generally to the chest and epigastric region in traditional anatomy</note>.

# **Rangoon Creeper** (*Shǐ jūn zǐ*)

The **Rangoon Creeper** <note>original: 使君子, *shǐ jūn zǐ*; scientific name: *Combretum indicum*</note> grows in **Panzhou** <note>A historical administrative region located in the borderlands of modern-day Guangdong and Guangxi provinces</note>. Its wood grows as a **climbing vine**, and its fruit resembles a **green nut** or a small fruit pit.

In traditional medicine, it is primarily used to treat the <term>Five Childhood Malnutrition Disorders<gloss>wǔ gān; a traditional category of pediatric ailments characterized by digestive issues, abdominal swelling, and wasting, often caused by intestinal parasites</gloss></term>. It is also highly effective for **killing intestinal parasites** and treating **diarrhea and dysentery**.

The physician **Guo Shijun** <note>A famous doctor from the Northern Song dynasty known for his expertise in pediatrics</note> frequently utilized this herb when treating children, achieving remarkable results. Because of his success with the remedy, the plant was named in his honor <note>The name "Shǐ jūn zǐ" literally translates to "The Gentleman [Master] Shijun"</note>.

# **Rangoon Creeper** (*Shǐ jūn zǐ*)

The **Rangoon Creeper** <note>original: 使君子, *shǐ jūn zǐ*; scientific name: *Combretum indicum*</note> grows in **Jiao**, **Guang**, and various other prefectures; it is found throughout the entire **Lingnan** region <note>A historical geographical area south of the Nanling Mountains, comprising modern-day Guangdong, Guangxi, and northern Vietnam</note>. It grows wild in the mountains and fields as well as along riverbanks.

Its **leaves** are green, about the width of two fingers and two inches long. Its **stem** grows as a climbing vine, appearing like dried resin. In the **third month**, it produces **pale red flowers**; after some time, they turn a **deep red**, and each flower has **five petals**. In the **seventh or eighth month**, it bears **seeds** about the size of a thumb and roughly one inch long. These are very similar to the fruit of the **Cape Jasmine** <note>original: 梔子, *zhī zi*; Gardenia</note> but are characterized by having **five ridges**. The outer shell is a greenish-black color, and the inner kernel is white.

The fruit is harvested in the **seventh month**. Its flavor is **sweet**, its nature is **warm**, and it is **non-toxic**. It is primarily used to treat the <term>Five Childhood Malnutrition Disorders<gloss>wǔ gān; a traditional category of pediatric ailments often associated with digestive issues, abdominal swelling, and parasitic infections</gloss></term> and **cloudy white urine**. It is also used to **kill intestinal parasites** and to treat **diarrhea and dysentery**.
