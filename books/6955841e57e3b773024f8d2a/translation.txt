The collection of Irish Triads, which is edited and translated here for the first time, has been preserved in the following nine manuscripts, dating from the fourteenth to the nineteenth century:

**L**, that is, the Yellow Book of Lecan, a vellum <term>manuscript made from calfskin</term> from the end of the fourteenth century, pages 414b–418a; this is a complete copy.

**B**, that is, the Book of Ballymote, a vellum from the end of the fourteenth century, pages 65b–66b (this copy is incomplete at the end).

**M**, that is, the Book of Húi Maine, a vellum from the fourteenth century, folio 190a¹ to folio 191a². This is a complete copy beginning with "Armagh is the head of Ireland" <note>original: ‘Ceand Erenn Ardmacha’</note> and ending with "three prohibitions regarding food: eating it in haste <note>original: 'díescaidheadh'</note>, eating it after its proper time, and eating it without giving thanks" <note>original: ‘tri hurgairt bidh a caitheam díescaidheadh a chaitheam iarna coir a caitheam gan altughudh’</note>. In our collection, we can see that the Triads are organized into certain groups based on their subject matter. For example, the first sixty-one entries are all topographical <term>topographical: relating to the naming and description of specific places</term>—though the first thirty-one of these are not actually triads at all. Among the rest, those dealing with legal matters stand out clearly (§§ 149-172).

We have no way of knowing exactly when this collection was compiled, except by looking at internal evidence. This includes the age of the language and a few references to historical events that we can date approximately.

The language used in the Triads can be described as late Old Irish. Their system of verbs matches the style found in the "Continental glosses" <note>original: *glosses*; these are Old Irish notes and explanations found in manuscripts stored in mainland Europe</note>,² which suggests they were not written later than the year 900. On the other hand, certain patterns in how nouns are declined <term>declined: changing the form of a word to show its grammatical case, such as possessive or plural</term>—which appear in all the surviving manuscripts—make it impossible to date them much earlier than the second half of the ninth century (roughly 850–900 AD).

For instance, the possessive <note>genitive</note> singular form of certain noun groups <note>i- and u-stems</note> no longer ends in *-o*; this has been replaced everywhere by the ending *-a*.³ We know this is a significant change because of the *Annals of Ulster* <note>a chronological record of medieval Irish history</note>, which serves as a reliable guide for tracking how the language developed century by century. This possessive ending in *-o* is found for the last time in the year 816 in words like *rátho* <note>of a fort</note> and *Ailello* <note>of Ailill</note>. From that point forward, the ending *-a* is always used.

From that point onward, it always ends in *-i*, as seen in our text: "in Cooley" <note>original: *hi Cúailgni*</note> (§ 43) and "of water" <note>original: *d'uisce*</note> (§ 64).

However, in section 32, we find "transgressions" or "leaps over" <note>original: *tair-leme*</note> instead. Likewise, "accidental use" <note>original: *foimrimm*</note> forms its plural as *foimrimme* in section 163.

The word for "door" <note>original: *dorus*</note> is a neuter noun in Old Irish, making its plural either *dorus* or *doirsea*. In our text (§§ 173, 174), the word is treated as masculine and uses the plural form *doruis*.

The word for "ridge" <note>original: *Druimm*</note> is an "*i*-stem" in Old Irish, but in the later language, it changes into an "*n*-stem." In section 51, we find the plural form "ridges" <note>original: *drommanna*</note>.

The neuter word for "rank" or "grade" <note>original: *grád*</note> in section 166 uses the plural form *gráda*, rather than the Old Irish form *grád*.¹

Based on these linguistic grounds, I would conclude that our collection was compiled sometime during the second half of the ninth century (roughly 850–900 AD). That it cannot be dated any earlier is also clear from another piece of evidence. Professor Zimmer <note>Heinrich Zimmer (1851–1910), a prominent German Celtic scholar</note> taught us to search every ancient Irish text for clues as to whether it was composed before or after the Viking period. I find no words from the Norse language in the Triads—or if there are any, I have missed them—but there are two distinct references to the Viking age. In section 232, a Viking in his "hauberk" <note>original: *Gall ina lúirig*; a 'Gall' or foreigner (Viking) wearing a coat of chainmail</note> is mentioned as one of three things that are hardest to talk to. Additionally, in section 44, Bangor in County Down is called unlucky or unfortunate. This is undoubtedly because, as the interlinear note <note>original: gloss</note> says, the monastery was repeatedly plundered and destroyed by the Norse during the early ninth century (specifically in 823 and 824 AD).

# PREFACE

In attempting to trace the origin of the Triad <term>Triad: a group of three related things, often expressed as a proverb or list</term> as a literary form among the Irish, one must remember that it is just one of several similar list-like sayings common in Irish literature. For instance, the collection printed here contains three duads <term>duad: a pair or group of two</term> (items 124, 133, and 134), seven tetrads <term>tetrad: a group of four</term> (items 223, 230, 234, 244, 248, 251, and 252), and one heptad <term>heptad: a group of seven</term> (item 235). An entire Irish law book is written in the form of heptads;<gloss>¹</gloss> while triads, tetrads, and other groupings appear throughout every part of the Laws.<gloss>²</gloss> These organized arrangements were, of course, a great help for memorization.

If the Triad were the only such form, one might reasonably consider the idea that it originated from the influence of the doctrine of the Trinity <note>the Christian belief in one God in three persons</note> on the Celtic imagination. It has often been noted that this doctrine led to many unique features in Irish folklore, literature, and art. I would also not deny that the sacred nature of the number three, combined with the fact that these are easier to write, may have contributed to the popularity of the Triad. It is certainly the most common of the various numerical sayings and the only one that has survived to the present day.

Regardless, I believe the model for Irish triads, tetrads, pentads <term>pentad: a group of five</term>, and others should be sought in the list-like sayings—*numerical proverbs* <note>original German: *Zahlensprüche*</note>—found in Hebrew poetry across several books of the Old Testament. I am grateful to my friend, Reverend Carl Grüneisen, for the following list of these sayings, which I quote from the Vulgate <term>Vulgate: the late-4th-century Latin translation of the Bible</term> version.

> Three are the vigorous ones <note>original: *bacheriosi (?)*—a difficult Latin term, likely meaning youthful or spirited</note>: a terrifying armed warrior ready for battle, a lion from a cave when it devours its prey, and a wild boar from the forest when it rushes at someone in a rage. <note>original Latin: "Tres bacheriosi (?) sunt: terribilis bellator armatus promptusque ad praelium, leo de spelunca quando praedam devorat, aper ferus de silva quando furore in aliquem irruit."</note>

Triads occur sporadically in the literature of most other nations and have occasionally been collected. But I am not aware that this kind of composition has ever attained the same popularity elsewhere as in Wales and Ireland, where the creation of triads seems at times almost to have become a sport.

The wittiest triads are undoubtedly those in which the third item contains an anticlimax. Two perfect examples of this kind were composed by the poet Heinrich Heine when he tells the foreigner visiting Germany that he need only know three words of the language: *Bread, Kiss, Honor* <note>original German: *Brot, Kuss, Ehre*</note>; and in his often quoted witticism: *The Frenchman loves freedom like his bride, the Englishman like his wife, and the German like his old grandmother.* <note>original German: *Der Franzose liebt die Freiheit wie seine Braut, der Engländer wie seine Frau, der Deutsche wie seine alte Grossmutter.*</note>

# GLOSSES AND NOTES

**235. Three things that are difficult for them** <note>original: trí huais doibh</note>: that is, it is nearly troublesome for them. Namely, to act as a **surety** <note>original: urrdhas; a person who takes legal responsibility for another's debt or behavior</note> for a king or a bishop, because of the half **honor-price** <note>original: eneaclann; the legal value of a person's status in Irish law</note> of the king, or for one who is under rebuke there. **To go to battle** <note>original: dul fri cath</note>: that is, to enter into suretyship for the waging of war. **For a captive** <note>original: fri cimidh</note>: that is, to enter into suretyship for a farmer or for the keeping of peace. These are seven things forbidden to anyone who would act as a guarantor for them. **To go for an exile** <note>original: dol ar dheoruighe</note>: that is, to act as surety for a wanderer <note>original: diaraigh</note>, meaning one who has no home or residence. **For a fool** <note>original: ar druith</note>: that is, a person without sense. **For a vagabond** <note>original: ar dhiaraigh</note>: one who cannot be constrained or bound by law. **For an undutiful son** <note>original: ar angar</note>: a son who does not support his parents <note>original: mac iongar</note>. **For a dotard** <note>original: ar esccong</note>: an old man after his senses have left him. **Every surety is truly anxious** <note>original: imnedhach dona gacha ráth</note>: every such suretyship is truly troublesome. This involves enduring violent death, or intense preparation, or a proclamation regarding every promise one makes, one after another; that is, taking oaths against the oaths of the other man, claiming he did not enter into suretyship or for the sake of payment.

**236. Drying** <note>original: ag tioradh</note>: that is, parching or drying grain.

**238. Brightness** <note>original: luchra</note>: that is, a laugh or a smile.

**239.** What are the three happinesses that a fortunate person receives? **Not difficult** <note>original: ní handsa; a standard formula in Irish texts meaning "the answer is simple"</note>: I am not ignorant of that. **Carrying** <note>original: iomarchor</note>: that is, transporting. **Ale without a vat** <note>original: cuirm gan ára</note>: a drink for one who has no house; that is, finding a drink by chance while on the road.

**240. Laughter** <note>original: gaire</note>: that is, good laughter.

**241.** One who provides entertainment or company in a gathering. **A fool** <note>original: druith</note>: that is, an idiot. **An entertainer** <note>original: foirsire</note>: a buffoon or a juggler. **A lapdog** <note>original: oirce</note>: a pet or a small dog.

**243. Affliction** <note>original: léan</note>: that is, sorrow. **Betrayal** <note>original: brath</note>: that is, of one's neighbor.

**244. Giving a judgment in falsehood** <note>original: a breith a ngó</note>: a false or lying legal decision. **Without security** <note>original: gan disle</note>: being under fear without any legal remedy. **Without stones** <note>original: gan ailic</note>: without stone boundary markers around it; namely, the eye and the wilderness.

**246. A reward** <note>original: duas</note>: [glossed here as] bad knowledge.

**247. Well-famed** <note>original: socla</note>: that is, having a good reputation. **Wooing** <note>original: suirge</note>: that is, with women.

**248.** Four things for which a **lord** <note>original: flaith</note> feels hatred or dislike. **A fool** <note>original: baoth</note>: that is, a silly or lewd person. **Unsteady** <note>original: uttmhall</note>: that is, too quick or restless. **A talkative man without a story** <note>original: fer labhar disceoil</note>: a wordy, chatty person who has no actual news or tales. **A man of historical knowledge** <note>original: fer coimhghne cuimhnech</note>: one with fair wisdom and a memory for ancient history <note>original: senchus</note>.

**251. Easily awed** <note>original: somna</note>: that is, easily frightened or fearful. **Sober** <note>original: sobraicch</note>: that is, strong or vigorous.

**252. Affection** <note>original: condailbhe</note>: that is, being supportive or biased toward one's own side. **Ill-tongued** <note>original: doingthe</note>: that is, foul-mouthed.

**253. Three forbidden things for a prince** <note>original: trí sirrechta flatha</note>: things that are prohibited for a noble. Feasts without art <note>original: ealadha; such as poetry or music</note> to describe them. A company without the drone of a piper at its head.
