The name literally means "attached child," referring to how these roots grow attached to the side of the main "mother" root (*Wu Tou*). *Aconite Root* <note>Original: *Fu Zi* (附子)</note> is a primary medicinal herb <note>Original: *Yao Cao* (藥草)</note> featured within the Materia Medica <note>Original: *Ben Cao* (本草), a traditional category of literature documenting medicinal substances.</note> of Traditional Chinese Medicine <note>Original: *Zhong Yi* (中醫).</note>

In medical practice, this plant is used to "restore devastated Yang" and drive out deep-seated cold from the body. Because the raw plant is highly toxic, it is rarely used without extensive processing—such as soaking and boiling for many hours—to transform it into a safe and effective remedy.

## Aconite Root <note>Original: *Fu Zi* (附子), the lateral root of *Aconitum carmichaelii*.</note>

*Crow’s Head* <note>Original: *Wu Tou* (烏頭), the parent root of the aconite plant.</note>, *Heavenly Hero* <note>Original: *Tian Xiong* (天雄), a long, slender root that has failed to produce "children" or lateral roots.</note>, *Aconite Root*, and *Side-Growth* <note>Original: *Ce Zi* (側子), small lateral roots that grow off the main lateral roots.</note> all originate from the soil of Shu <note>Original: *Shu Tu* (蜀土), referring to modern-day Sichuan Province, famous for producing high-quality aconite.</note>. However, these four varieties are actually all derived from a single species of plant. 

The sprouts of this plant grow to a height of about three to four feet. The stem is four-angled, and the leaves resemble those of mugwort. The flowers are a purplish-blue color and grow in spikes. The fruit is small and black, resembling a mulberry. 

According to the *Classic of Materia Medica* <note>Original: *Ben Jing* (本經), referring to the *Shennong Ben Cao Jing*, the foundation of Chinese herbalism.</note>, those roots harvested in the winter are called *Fuzi* (Aconite Root), while those harvested in the spring are called *Wutou* (Crow’s Head). However, the *Guangya* <note>An ancient Chinese dictionary compiled by Zhang Yi during the Cao Wei period.</note> states that *Xidu* <note>Original: *奚毒*, an archaic name for Aconite.</note> is another name for *Fuzi*. It further clarifies the stages of growth: 
* At one year old, it is called *Cezi*. 
* At two years old, it is called *Wuhui* <note>Original: *烏喙*, meaning "Crow’s Beak," due to the root's hooked shape.</note>. 
* At three years old, it is called *Fuzi*. 
* At four years old, it is called *Wutou*. 
* At five years old, it is called *Tianxiong*. 

When planted for a single year, all five of these forms may be present. Its flavor is pungent, its nature is extremely hot, and it is highly toxic. It is used to treat "Yin poisoning" <note>Original: *Yin Du* (陰毒), a severe condition where internal cold has collapsed the body's vitality.</note> and "Cold Damage" <note>Original: *Shang Han* (傷寒), a category of external febrile diseases caused by cold pathogens.</note>, as well as numbness and pain caused by cold, wind, and dampness. It also treats "Great Wind" <note>Original: *Da Feng* (大風), a term often referring to leprosy or severe neurological conditions.</note> and acute spasms or contractions of the muscles and bones.

## Crowdipper <note>Original: *Ban Xia* (半夏). The tuber of *Pinellia ternata*. The name literally translates to "Mid-summer," referring to the timing of its harvest.</note>

Crowdipper grows in the river valleys of Huaili <note>An ancient district in what is now Shaanxi province.</note>, though it is found in many locations. Those sourced from Qizhou <note>Modern-day Jinan, Shandong province.</note> are considered of the highest quality.

The sprouts emerge in the second lunar month. Each stalk typically bears three leaves at its tip; these are light green and closely resemble bamboo leaves, though they are notably glossy. The variety found in the regions south of the Yangtze River <note>Original: *Jiangnan* (江南).</note> has leaves that resemble those of a peony. The roots consist of layered parts, larger at the top and smaller at the bottom, with yellow skin and white flesh.

The roots are harvested during the fifth and eighth lunar months. They are processed by boiling them in ash <term>Ash-boiling: *Hui zha* (灰煠), a traditional pharmaceutical technique used to neutralize the acrid, throat-irritating toxins of the raw plant.</term> for two days, then washed in a hot water decoction and dried in the sun. Those that are round, white, and have been aged for a long time are considered the best.

A variety that grows in level marshes is very small and is known as *Sheep’s Eye Crowdipper* <note>Original: *Yangyan Ban Xia* (羊眼半夏).</note>. There is also another plant whose sprouts and roots are extremely similar to Crowdipper, but its sprouts grow one to two feet high and its root is as large as a chicken egg. This variety mostly grows in the shade of forests; some suggest it is actually a small version of the *Tiger’s Paw* <note>Original: *Hu Zhang* (虎掌), or *Pinellia pedatisecta*.</note>, and it is easily confused with the true Crowdipper.

Crowdipper has a pungent taste and is toxic. It is primarily used to treat "Stomach Cold" <note>A Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) pattern characterized by cold sensations in the abdomen and the inability to retain food.</note>, vomiting, and retching. It is a most essential ingredient in medicinal prescriptions.

It is also known as *Field Guard* <note>Original: *Shou Tian* (守田).</note>, *Earth Pattern* <note>Original: *Di Wen* (地文).</note>, *Water Jade* <note>Original: *Shui Yu* (水玉).</note>, and *Maiden* <note>Original: *Shi Gu* (示姑).</note>.

# Tiger’s Paw

Tiger’s Paw <note>Original: *Huzhang* (虎掌). In modern botanical terms, this is often identified as *Pinellia pedatisecta*.</note> grows in the mountain valleys of Hanzhong <note>A region in modern Shaanxi province.</note> and in Yuanju <note>An ancient county in modern Shandong province.</note>; it is also found in the prefectures and commanderies north of the Yellow River. In the third or fourth month of the lunar calendar, the sprouts emerge to a height of over one foot. It has a single stem topped with leaves shaped like claws, with five or six lobes distributed in a pattern that is both sharp and rounded. One plant cluster produces seven or eight stems. Occasionally, a single stem emerges to form a flower spike <note>An inflorescence.</note> that stands straight up like a rat’s tail. In the middle, a leaf shaped like a spoon <note>A spathe, a large leaf-like bract enclosing the flower cluster.</note> wraps around the stem to form a chamber, with an opening on the side that is sharp at the top and wide at the bottom. Inside are tiny greenish-brown flowers. It produces fruit the size of hemp seeds; when ripe, they turn white and fall to the ground on their own. Each seed produces one plant.

In the ninth month, when the sprouts wither, the roots <note>The tubers.</note> are harvested. They are placed in a vessel with hot water and soaked for five to seven days. Once the water cools, it is changed; this is done three or four times a day to wash away the slime. It is then sun-dried for use. The people of Jizhou <note>An ancient province covering parts of modern Hebei and Shanxi.</note> plant this in their vegetable gardens; they also call it *Tiannanxing* <note>Original: 天南星, "Star of the South Heaven." In modern contexts, this name usually refers to the *Arisaema* genus, showing how historical names often overlapped between similar-looking toxic tubers.</note>. Its flavor is bitter, its nature is warm yet slightly cold, and it is highly toxic. It is primarily used to treat heart pain, chills and fever, bound energy, accumulations, *Fuliang* <note>A "Hidden Beam," referring to a hard, painful mass in the upper abdomen.</note>, injured sinews, and conditions involving atrophy or contraction. It eases the water passages <note>Acts as a diuretic.</note>, and treats dampness under the <gloss>scrotum</gloss> as well as <gloss>dizziness caused by wind</gloss>.

# Rhubarb

Rhubarb <note>Original: *Dahuang* (大黃). In medicinal contexts, this usually refers to *Rheum palmatum* or related species.</note> grows in mountain valleys everywhere. The variety from Shuchuan <note>Modern Sichuan province</note> characterized by "brocade patterns" <note>Original: *jinwen* (錦紋). This refers to the marbled, veined appearance of the cross-section of high-quality rhubarb root, which was a primary marker of its potency.</note> is considered the best. The next best varieties come from the Qin and Long regions <note>Modern Shaanxi and Gansu provinces</note> and are known as Tibetan Rhubarb.

In the first month of the lunar calendar, the plant produces green leaves that resemble those of the castor bean <note>*Ricinus communis*</note>. The roots are similar to taro, reaching one to two feet in length, with side roots resembling those of burdock; even the smaller roots look like taro. In the fourth month, it opens yellow flowers, though some varieties have bluish-red flowers that resemble buckwheat blossoms. The stems are bluish-purple and shaped like bamboo.

The variety produced in the Jianghuai region <note>The area between the Yangtze and Huai rivers</note> is called Earth Rhubarb <note>Original: *Tu-dahuang* (土大黃). Often refers to wild or local dock species (Rumex) used as a substitute for true rhubarb.</note>. It flowers in the second month and produces small fruit.

Additionally, Dingzhou <note>In modern Hebei province</note> produces a type called Sheep’s Hoof Rhubarb <note>Original: *Yangti dahuang* (羊蹄大黃); likely *Rumex japonicus*.</note>, which is extremely effective for treating scabies and itchy rashes. When the sprouts first emerge, the leaves look like sheep hooves. As it grows over several years, the leaves become similar to those of the pokeberry <note>*Shanglu* (商陸)</note>, though they are narrower and more pointed. In the fourth month, spikes emerge from the stalks, with five to seven stems clustering together. The flowers and leaves are the same color. It bears fruit similar to buckwheat, but lighter and smaller. It ripens in the fifth month, at which time it turns yellow. It is also called Golden Buckwheat. When the roots are harvested and cut open, they also display brocade patterns. They are sun-dried for use and are also referred to as Earth Rhubarb.

Its flavor is <term>bitter</term> and its nature is <term>greatly cold</term>; it is non-toxic. It resolves <term>wind-heat</term>, disperses accumulated heat and <term>wind-congestion</term>, promotes digestion, transforms <term>qi</term>, and guides the blood. It greatly relieves blockages and stagnation, "pushing out the old to bring in the new" <note>Original: *tuichen zhixin* (推陳致新). This is a classic medical idiom describing rhubarb's powerful ability to purge the bowels and clear the system of toxins to allow for healing.</note>, and is used to treat all types of sores and boils.

# Rhubarb
<note>Original: *Dahuang* (大黃), literally "The Great Yellow."</note>

Rhubarb grows in mountain valleys everywhere. The variety from Shuchuan <note>The historical name for the Sichuan region</note> characterized by "brocade patterns" <note>Original: *jinwen* (錦紋). This describes the beautiful, marble-like veining seen in the cross-section of the dried root, which was considered proof of the highest medicinal quality.</note> is considered the best. The next best varieties come from the Qin and Long regions <note>Modern-day Shaanxi and Gansu provinces</note> and are known as Tibetan Rhubarb.

In the first month of the lunar calendar, the plant produces green leaves that resemble those of the castor bean <note>Original: *Bima* (蓖麻), *Ricinus communis*</note>. The roots are similar to taro, reaching one to two feet in length, with side roots resembling those of burdock; even the smaller roots look like taro. In the fourth month, it opens yellow flowers, though some varieties have bluish-red flowers that resemble buckwheat blossoms. The stems are bluish-purple and shaped like bamboo.

The variety produced in the Jianghuai region <note>The fertile plains between the Yangtze and Huai rivers</note> is called Earth Rhubarb <note>Original: *Tu-dahuang* (土大黃). This usually refers to local wild dock species (*Rumex*) which were often used as a cheaper or more accessible substitute for true medicinal rhubarb.</note>. It flowers in the second month and produces small fruit.

Additionally, Dingzhou <note>In modern Hebei province</note> produces a type called Sheep’s Hoof Rhubarb <note>Original: *Yangti-dahuang* (羊蹄大黃). This plant, likely *Rumex japonicus*, is named for its leaves which are shaped like the hoof of a sheep.</note>

# Tingli
<note>Original: *Tingli* (葶藶). In traditional medicine, this refers to the seeds of plants like *Descurainia sophia* or *Draba nemorosa*. It is a classic "fluid-draining" herb used primarily to relieve severe swelling and respiratory distress.</note>

<term>Tingli</term>

Tingli grows in the flat marshes and fields of Gaocheng <note>A region in modern-day Hebei province</note>. It is found throughout the prefectures and commanderies of Jingdong, Shaanxi, and Hebei, though the variety produced in Caozhou <note>Modern-day Heze, Shandong province</note> is considered particularly superior.

In early spring, the plant sprouts seedlings with leaves six to seven inches high, which bear a resemblance to <term>Shepherd's Purse</term> <note>Original: *Ji* (薺), *Capsella bursa-pastoris*</note>. The roots are white, while both the branches and the stems are green. In the third lunar month, it blooms with tiny yellow flowers. It produces seed pods that are flat and small, like grains of broomcorn millet <note>Original: *Shu* (黍)</note>, though slightly elongated and yellow in color.

The seeds are harvested after the <term>Start of Summer</term> <note>Original: *Lixia* (立夏), a solar term occurring around May 5-6</note> and dried in the sun. The *Monthly Ordinances* <note>Original: *Yueling* (月令), a classic ritual text detailing seasonal activities</note> states: "In the first month of summer, the delicate grasses die." The Han dynasty scholars Xu Shen and Zheng Kangcheng both noted in their commentaries that "delicate grasses" <note>Original: *Micao* (靡草)</note> refer to plants of the same class as Shepherd's Purse and Tingli. Because these plants wither and die by the height of summer, they must be gathered at this specific time.

The herb is also known by the names Dingli, Caohao, Dashishi, and Dashi. Its flavor is acrid and bitter, and its medicinal nature is "extremely cold" <note>Original: *Dahan* (大寒). In Chinese medicine, this describes a powerful cooling effect used to treat "heat" or inflammatory conditions</note> and non-toxic. It is used to benefit the small intestine and to circulate "water vapor" <note>Original: *Shuiqi* (水氣), referring to accumulated fluids or edema</note> to treat swelling and bloating.

# Balloon Flower

<term>Balloon Flower</term> <note>original: *Jiegeng* (桔梗); botanical name: *Platycodon grandiflorus*.</note>

The Balloon Flower grows in the valleys of **Mount Songgao** <note>The Central Sacred Mountain, Mount Song in Henan Province</note> and in **Yuanju** <note>an ancient area in modern-day Shandong</note>, though today it is found everywhere. The root is about the size of a pinky finger and is yellowish-white in color. In spring, it sprouts seedlings with stems over a foot high. The leaves resemble those of an **Apricot** tree but are longer; four leaves grow together in an opposite, whorled pattern. 

When the leaves are young and tender, they may be boiled and eaten. In summer, the plant blooms with purple-blue flowers that closely resemble those of the **Morning Glory** <note>original: *Qianniuzi* (牽牛子)</note>. After autumn, it produces seeds. In the eighth lunar month, the roots are harvested, finely sliced, and dried in the sun for use. The leaves are known by the name **Yinren** <note>meaning "hidden endurance"</note>. 

There is a distinction to be made regarding the root: those varieties that have a core and those that do not are actually the **Ladybell** <note>original: *Qini* (薺苨); *Adenophora* species</note>. While the Ladybell can also be used to neutralize toxins, the two plants are often confused. The primary difference is that the underside of the Ladybell leaf is glossy and hairless. 

The Balloon Flower from **Langzhong** <note>a city in Sichuan Province</note> has a yellow root that looks much like a **Hollyhock** root. Its stems are slender and green, and its leaves are small and green, resembling **Chrysanthemum** leaves. In ancient prescriptions, this variety was also used on its own. 

The plant is known by several other names: 
*   **Liru** <note>meaning "beneficial to the standard"</note>
*   **Fangtu** <note>meaning "chamber map"</note>
*   **Baiyao** <note>meaning "white medicine"</note>
*   **Gengcao** <note>meaning "stiff herb"</note>

Its flavor is pungent and bitter, its nature is "slightly warm," and it possesses a small amount of toxicity. 

It is used to treat:
*   Heat in the **Upper Burner** <note>The upper functional division of the body, including the heart and lungs</note>
*   Fright epilepsy and convulsions
*   **Throat Obstruction** <note>original: *Houbi* (喉痺); severe sore throat or loss of voice</note>
*   Sudden distress during pregnancy <note>original: *Renshen zhong'e* (妊娠中惡); sudden illness or "evil wind" striking during pregnancy</note>
*   Aching pain in the heart and abdomen
*   Nosebleeds
*   **Bone-Socket Wind** <note>original: *Gucao feng* (骨槽風); a traditional term for ailments like mandibular osteomyelitis or severe jaw infections</note>
*   Tooth swelling and pain

# Henbane Seed

<term>Henbane Seed</term> <note>original: *Langdangzi* (莨菪子); *Hyoscyamus niger*.</note> grows in coastal river valleys and within the Yongzhou region <note>an ancient province covering parts of modern-day Shaanxi and Gansu</note>; today, it can be found almost everywhere. The seedling stalks reach a height of two to three feet.

The leaves resemble those of <term>Rehmannia</term> <note>original: *Dihuang*</note>, <term>Cowherb</term> <note>original: *Wangbuliuxing*</note>, and <term>Safflower</term> <note>original: *Honglan*</note>, and are about three fingers wide. In the fourth month, it produces purple flowers. The seedlings, pods, and stems are all covered in fine white hairs. In the fifth month, it bears fruit enclosed in a shell resembling a poppy capsule <note>original: *Yingzi*, referring to *Papaver somniferum*</note> and shaped like a small pomegranate. Inside the chambers are very fine, greenish-white seeds that look like grains of rice. Another name for this plant is the <term>Heavenly Fairy Seed</term> <note>original: *Tianxianzi*</note>. The seeds should be harvested in the fifth month and dried in the shade.

I carefully note that the <term>Divine Farmer’s Classic</term> <note>original: *Benjing*</note> states that Henbane is "cold" in nature. However, later scholars frequently claim it is "extremely hot." Yet, the "Biography of Chunyu Yi" in the <term>Records of the Grand Historian</term> <note>original: *Shiji*</note> records that when the Consort of the King of Zichuan was pregnant but could not give birth, Yi administered a pinch of Henbane medicine mixed with wine. She delivered the child immediately. If the condition was one of obstructed labor, how could it be treated by a "hot" medicine? <note>In traditional Chinese medicine, obstructed or difficult labor was often attributed to "heat" or stagnation that required "cooling" or "descending" medicines to resolve; the author uses this famous historical cure to prove the plant's "cold" nature.</note>

It is also called <term>Hengtang</term> or <term>Xingtang</term>. It has a bitter and sweet taste, is cold in nature, and is toxic. It is used as a smudge <note>burned as incense</note> to treat <term>tooth-worms</term> <note>original: *Yachong*; a historical term for dental caries/cavities based on the belief that small worms ate the teeth</note> and is used as a wash to treat <term>genital sweating</term> <note>original: *Yinhan*; likely referring to scrotal eczema or hyperhidrosis in the groin area</note>.

# Grass Wormwood
<note>original: *Caohao* (草蒿). This refers to a specific variety of wormwood, identified here as being synonymous with *Qinghao* (*Artemisia annua*), famously known in modern medicine as the source of the anti-malarial compound artemisinin.</note>

**Grass Wormwood** is the same as **Sweet Wormwood** <note>original: *Qinghao* (青蒿)</note>. It grows in the valleys and marshes of **Huayin** <note>A region in modern-day Shaanxi Province, near the sacred Mount Hua</note>, though today it can be found everywhere. In the spring, it produces sprouts with extremely fine leaves. When the plant is young and tender, people often harvest it to mix with various aromatic vegetables for food. By summer, it reaches a height of four to five feet. After autumn begins, it opens small, pale yellow flowers. Below the flowers, seeds form that are about the size of millet grains. Between the eighth and ninth lunar months, the seeds are harvested and dried in the shade.

The roots, stems, seeds, and leaves are all used for medicinal purposes. When the plant is dried and roasted to make a beverage, its fragrance is exceptionally fine. Sweet Wormwood is also known by the name **Fangkui** <note>original: *Fangkui* (方潰)</note>.

In the preparation of this medicine, one must follow strict rules:
* If using the **seeds**, do not use the **buds** <note>original: *jiong* (莔); likely referring to the immature flower heads or specific involucres</note>.
* If using the **leaves**, do not use the **roots**.
* If using the **roots**, do not use the **stems**.

If these four parts are used together, they will conflict and cause illness rather than cure it. It is considered of superior quality if it has been soaked in the **urine of a young boy** <note>A traditional processing method (*paozhi*) in Chinese medicine believed to enhance the herb's ability to "clear heat" and direct the medicine to specific parts of the body</note>.

It is called **Sweet Wormwood** <note>*Qinghao*</note> and also **Fangkui**. Its flavor is **bitter**, its nature is **cold**, and it is **non-toxic**.

It primarily treats **scabies and itchy scabs**, as well as **malignant sores**. It **kills lice** and clears **lingering heat trapped within the joints** <note>This refers to "bone-steaming" fevers or chronic low-grade fevers associated with internal deficiency</note>. It also **brightens the eyes**.

## Inula Flower
<note>original: *Xuanfuhua* (旋復花). Also known as *Inula japonica*, a yellow flower often used in traditional medicine to resolve phlegm and stop vomiting.</note>

**Inula** grows in marshy plains and river valleys; today it can be found in almost any location. After the second lunar month, the plant begins to sprout, appearing most frequently near the water’s edge. It greatly resembles **Safflower** <note>original: *Honglan* (紅藍)</note> but lacks its characteristic thorns. The plant grows to a height of about one to two feet, featuring leaves similar to those of a willow and slender stems.

In the sixth lunar month, it blooms with flowers resembling small chrysanthemums, about the size of a small copper coin and deep yellow in color. People in the rural fields of **Shangdang** <note>A historic commandery located in modern-day Shanxi Province</note> refer to it as the **Gold Coin Flower** <note>original: *Jinqianhua* (金錢花)</note>. Between the seventh and eighth months, the flowers are harvested and dried in the sun.

It is also known by the names **Daishen** <note>"Wearing a Mulberry"</note>, **Golden Boiling Herb** <note>original: *Jinfeicao* (金沸草)</note>, and **Shengshen** <note>"Abundant Mulberry"</note>. Its taste is sweet, and it is considered slightly toxic. It is primarily used to treat *qi* <note>vital energy</note> imbalances in the ribs and intestines, to dispel alternating chills and fever, and to treat edema. Furthermore, it is used to treat stagnant water in the bladder, to stimulate the appetite, and to stop vomiting or the inability to swallow food.

## Inula Flower
<note>original: *Xuanfuhua* (旋復花). Also known as *Inula japonica*, a yellow flower often used in traditional medicine to resolve phlegm and stop vomiting.</note>

**Inula** grows in marshy plains and river valleys; today it can be found in almost any location. After the second lunar month, the plant begins to sprout, appearing most frequently near the water’s edge. It greatly resembles **Safflower** <note>original: *Honglan* (紅藍), referring to Carthamus tinctorius</note> but lacks its characteristic thorns. The plant grows to a height of about one to two feet, featuring leaves similar to those of a willow and slender stems.

In the sixth lunar month, it blooms with flowers resembling small chrysanthemums, about the size of a small copper coin and deep yellow in color. People in the rural fields of **Shangdang** <note>A historic commandery in modern-day Shanxi Province known for its medicinal herbs</note> refer to it as the **Gold Coin Flower** <note>original: *Jinqianhua* (金錢花)</note>. Between the seventh and eighth months, the flowers are harvested and dried in the sun.

It is also known by the names **Daishen** <note>meaning "Wearing a Mulberry"</note>, **Golden Boiling Herb** <note>original: *Jinfeicao* (金沸草)</note>, and **Shengshen** <note>meaning "Abundant Mulberry"</note>. Its taste is sweet, and it is considered slightly toxic. It is primarily used to treat *qi* <note>the body's vital energy</note> imbalances in the ribs and intestines, to dispel alternating chills and fever, and to treat **edema** <term>shuizhong: the accumulation of fluid in the tissues</term> by promoting healthy fluid circulation.

## Blackberry Lily
<note>original: *Shè gān* (射干). Known scientifically as *Iris domestica* (formerly *Belamcanda chinensis*), it is also commonly called the Leopard Lily.</note>

**Blackberry Lily** is found growing throughout the mountain valleys. The plant is easily identified by its leaves, which grow in two distinct rows and overlap at the base, spreading out at the top in a shape that resembles a folding fan. Due to this unique appearance, many people refer to it as the **Flat Bamboo** <note>original: *Bianzhu* (扁竹)</note>.

The stems are firm and upright. During the summer months, it produces striking flowers that are orange or yellow, dotted with deep purple spots like the coat of a leopard. When the flowering stage is complete, it produces black, lustrous seeds that resemble blackberries.

The medicinal part of the plant is its rhizome <note>the thick, horizontal underground stem</note>. It is harvested in the spring and autumn. In medical practice, it is considered bitter in taste and cold in nature. It is most famously used as a powerful remedy for **throat-blockage** <term>houbi: a traditional term for severe sore throat or tonsillitis</term> and to clear heat and toxins from the lungs.

<margin>The roots should be cleaned of fibrous hairs and sliced before being dried for use in decoctions.</margin>

# Blackberry Lily
<note>original: *Shègān* (射干). Known scientifically as *Iris domestica* or *Belamcanda chinensis*, also called the Leopard Lily due to its spotted petals.</note>

The **Blackberry Lily** grows in the mountain valleys and fields of **Nanyang** <note>A region in modern-day Henan Province famous for its long history of herbal medicine</note>; today it can be found growing in almost any location. In the spring, the plant produces sprouts that reach a height of two to three feet. Its leaves resemble those of **Wild Ginger** <note>original: *Manjiang* (蠻薑), likely referring to a species of Alpinia</note> but are narrower and longer, spreading out horizontally like the feathers of a wing. From the center of the leaves, a stalk emerges that looks like a **Daylily** <note>original: *Xuancao* (萱草)</note> but is much stiffer and tougher.

In the sixth lunar month, the plant blooms with yellow-red flowers. The petals are marked with fine, delicate patterns. In autumn, it bears fruit within seed pods; the seeds inside are black. The roots have many fibrous whiskers; the outer skin is yellowish-black, while the inner flesh is a yellowish-red color. The roots should be harvested on the third day of the third lunar month and dried in the shade for medicinal use.

It is also known by the names **Black Fan** <note>original: *Wushan* (烏扇)</note>, **Black Rush** <note>original: *Wupu* (烏蒲)</note>, **Black Scepter** <note>original: *Wusha* (烏翣)</note>, **Black Whistle** <note>original: *Wuchui* (烏吹)</note>, and **Grass Ginger** <note>original: *Caojiang* (草薑)</note>. 

The flavor of the root is bitter, and it is considered to have a small amount of toxicity. It is highly effective in treating **throat obstruction** <term>original: *houbi* (喉痹); a condition involving severe swelling or blockage of the throat</term> where the patient cannot even swallow liquids. It also has the power to clear **menstrual blockages** in women, treat **lingering pathogenic energy** <term>original: *zhuqi* (疰氣); a traditional term for chronic infectious ailments or exhaustion syndromes</term>, and dissipate **blood stasis** <note>original: *yuxue* (瘀血), referring to localized poor circulation or bruising</note>.

# Wisteria and Goosefoot
<note>original: *Luó* (蘿) and *Lí* (藜). In traditional Chinese botanical and literary contexts, these two plants represent the "wild" or "simple" herbs. Wisteria (*Luo*) often refers to various climbing vines or epiphytes, while Goosefoot (*Li*), also known as Lamb's Quarters, is a common wild edible.</note>

<note>The accompanying illustration depicts the **Wisteria** vine climbing gracefully near a **Goosefoot** plant, which stands beside a scholar’s rock—a decorative stone used in Ming Dynasty gardens to represent the ruggedness of nature within a refined setting.</note>

**Wisteria** <note>original: *Luó* (蘿)</note>
This plant is characterized by its long, trailing vines that cling to trees or rocks. It is frequently mentioned in poetry to describe the secluded dwellings of hermits, where the vines cover the walls and pathways. 

**Goosefoot** <note>original: *Lí* (藜)</note>
The goosefoot plant is a hardy herb with leaves that are often covered in a fine, powdery substance. It is well-known in history because its sturdy stalks can be dried and fashioned into walking sticks for the elderly, known as "Goosefoot Staffs." Traditionally, a soup made from its tender leaves was considered the humble fare of virtuous scholars who lived in poverty.

### False Hellebore
<note>original: *Lílú* (藜蘆); scientifically known as *Veratrum nigrum*. This plant is a highly potent and toxic member of the Melanthiaceae family.</note>

**False Hellebore** grows in the valleys of **Mount Tai** <note>Taishan, one of the Five Sacred Mountains of China</note>. It is also found throughout the various prefectures of **Shaanxi** and **Shannan** <note>the region south of the Qinling Mountains</note>. In the second month of the lunar calendar, the plant begins to sprout.

The leaves are green and resemble the emerging "heart" of a weaver’s shuttle; they also bear a resemblance to the **Plantain** herb <note>original: *Cheqian* (車前)</note>. The stems are similar to the white part of a scallion, but have a blue-purple hue and reach a height of five or six inches.

The stems are encased in a black skin that looks like the outer casing of a shuttle. Its flowers are a fleshy red color. The roots resemble horse intestines and are approximately four to five inches in length, with a yellowish-white color. The roots are harvested in the second or third months and dried in the shade.

There are two varieties of this plant. One is the **Water False Hellebore** <note>original: *Shui Lilu* (水藜蘆)</note>. While its stems and leaves are largely the same as the medicinal variety, it grows on rocks within large mountain streams. Its root system is very dense, having over a hundred fibers, but it is not suitable for medicinal use.

The variety currently used in medicine is called the **Scallion-white False Hellebore** <note>original: *Congbai Lilu* (葱白藜蘆)</note>. Its root fibers are much fewer, numbering only about thirty. Those specimens that grow on high mountains are considered to be of the best quality.

In the local customs of **Junzhou** <note>an ancient prefecture in modern-day Hubei</note>, it is colloquially called **Deer Scallion** <note>original: *Lucong* (鹿葱)</note>. Other names include **Luxuriant Scallion** <note>original: *Congran* (葱苒)</note>, **Caltrop Scallion** <note>original: *Congling* (葱菱)</note>, and **Mountain Scallion** <note>original: *Shancong* (山葱)</note>.

The flavor is pungent, its nature is slightly cold, and it is toxic. It is primarily used to treat "upward-rushing Qi" <note>a term for respiratory distress or coughing</note>, to clear away long-standing accumulations of pus and blood, and to treat diarrhea and dysentery. It also treats "evil wind" <note>a traditional category of ailments often involving sudden onset or neurological symptoms</note>.

<gloss>It is used to heal scabies and ringworm, treat headaches, and kill intestinal parasites.</gloss>

## Snake-head Cinquefoil

**Snake-head Cinquefoil** <note>original: *Shéhán* (蛇含); generally identified as *Potentilla kleiniana*. The name refers to its use as a remedy for snakebites</note> grows in the mountain valleys of **Yizhou** <note>a historical province covering parts of modern Sichuan, Chongqing, and Guizhou</note>. Today, it is also found in many nearby areas. It grows atop soil and stones, or in low-lying damp ground. In the region of **Shu** <note>an ancient name for the Sichuan basin</note>, local households also cultivate it in their gardens.

A single stem bears either five or seven leaves <note>referring to the palmate or "hand-shaped" arrangement of the foliage</note>. There are two varieties of this plant; the one with fine leaves and yellow flowers is considered the superior choice for medicinal preparation. The roots should be harvested in the eighth month of the lunar calendar and dried in the shade for later use.

Another name for this herb is **Snake-clasp** <note>original: *Shéxián* (蛇銜)</note>. Its flavor is bitter, its nature is "slightly cold" <note>in Traditional Chinese Medicine, "cold" herbs are typically used to reduce inflammation or "heat"</note>, and it is non-toxic.

It is used to treat injuries caused by snakes, insects, bees, and **venomous serpents** <term>original: *huǐ* (虺); an ancient term for a small, dangerous pit viper</term>. It is also effective in treating **erysipelas** <term>original: *dān* (丹); a red, painful skin infection</term>, **scrofula** <term>original: *luǒ* (瘰); a swelling of the lymph nodes</term>, and alternating chills and fever in children.

## Shuqi (Evergreen Hydrangea)

**Shuqi** grows in the mountain valleys of Jianglin, as well as the Shu-Han region <note>the historic heartland of Sichuan and southern Shaanxi</note>. It is the sprout of the **Changshan** plant. **Changshan** <note>the root of the *Dichroa febrifuga* plant</note> itself grows in the mountain valleys of Yizhou and Hanzhong, though the species is also found throughout the prefectures of Jingxi, Huaizhe, and Hunan.

The leaves resemble those of the tea plant but are narrower and longer, growing in symmetrical, opposing pairs. The stems are round and marked by distinct joints. In the third lunar month, the plant produces red flowers with green calyxes. By the fifth month, it bears round fruit containing three seeds per pod.

The taller sprouts reach a height of no more than three or four feet. The roots are yellow and resemble those of the Vitex shrub <note>original: *jing* (荆)</note>. Notably, the variety produced in Haizhou <note>a coastal region in modern Jiangsu</note> has leaves that resemble those of the Catalpa tree <note>original: *qiu* (楸)</note>.

The roots are harvested in the eighth lunar month and should be dried in the shade before use. Its flavor is pungent and its nature is slightly warm; it is considered toxic. It is used to treat **chronic intermittent fevers** <term>original: *jiǔ nüè* (久瘧); long-standing malaria-like illnesses</term>. It is primarily indicated for treating **hard internal masses** <term>original: *jiān zhēng* (堅癥); stubborn physical lumps or tumors</term> and for dispersing **accumulations** <term>original: *jī jù* (積聚); the buildup of stagnant energy, fluid, or food in the abdomen</term>.

# Gansui (Kansui Root)

**Gansui** grows in the mountain valleys of Zhongshan <note>Zhongshan refers to the central mountain regions of ancient China</note>. It is also found in Shaanxi and the regions east of the Yangtze River <note>original: *Jiangdong* (江東)</note>. Some say that the specimens produced in Jingxi are of the highest quality.

The sprouts resemble **Sun Spurge** <term>original: *Zeqi* (澤漆); *Euphorbia helioscopia*, a plant often used to treat swelling</term>; the stems are short and small, and the leaves contain a milky sap. The root has a red skin and white flesh, forming a shape like a **string of pearls** <note>original: *lianzhu* (連珠); referring to the beaded or knotted appearance of the tuberous roots</note>. It also bears a resemblance to licorice root with its bark still intact. 

The roots are harvested in the second lunar month; they should be sliced at the joints and dried in the shade. Those that are solid and heavy are considered superior. 

There is also a variety known as **Grass Gansui**. Its sprouts consist of a single stem. In the sixth lunar month, its leaves resemble those of the **Castor Oil plant** <note>original: *Bima* (蓖麻); *Ricinus communis*</note>. An ancient commentary <note>The OCR reads "Gui" (鬼), but in medicinal texts, this is often a transcription error for the physician Zhen Quan (甄權)</note> notes that using the leaves is quite harmful; even consuming a large quantity <note>original: "one sheng," a volume roughly equal to a liter</note> raw will not induce the intended medicinal purging.

The **Tang Dynasty Commentary** <note>refers to the *Tang Bencao*, the world's first state-sanctioned pharmacopeia, compiled in 659 AD</note> states that Grass Gansui is the same as the **Zaoti** plant. It is also known by several other names:
* **Gangao** <gloss>Sweet Straw</gloss>
* **Linggao** <gloss>Mound Straw</gloss>
* **Lingze** <gloss>Mound Marsh</gloss>
* **Zhongze** <gloss>Double Marsh</gloss>
* **Shengtian** <gloss>Raw Field</gloss>

In terms of its medicinal nature, its flavor is bitter and sweet. It is characterized as "greatly cold" <note>indicating a powerful cooling and downward-moving energy</note> and is toxic. It is capable of purging twelve types of fluid-related diseases <term>original: *shuiji* (水疾); ailments involving the retention of excess water or edema</term> and is used to treat hardness and a feeling of fullness in the heart and abdomen.

**Bailian** <note>original: *白斂* (*Bailian*); scientific name: *Ampelopsis japonica*</note> grows in the valleys of Mount Heng, as well as throughout the prefectures and commanderies of the Yangtze and Huai rivers, and the regions of Jing, Xiang, Huai, Meng, Shang, and Qi.

## Qingxiangzi <note>Feather Cockscomb Seed; *Celosia argentea*</note>

### Characteristics and Habitat of the Hyacinth Orchid

**Baiji** <note>Hyacinth Orchid; *Bletilla striata*</note> grows in the mountain valleys of **Beishan** <note>North Mountain</note>, as well as in **Yuanju** and **Yueshan**. It is also found throughout the various prefectures of the **Yangtze and Huai Rivers**, the **Yellow River** region, **Shan**, **Han**, and **Qian** <note>modern-day Guizhou</note>.

It grows on rocky mountains. In the spring, the plant produces sprouts that reach a height of about one foot. These sprouts resemble the **windmill palm** <term>binglü 栟櫚; *Trachycarpus fortunei*</term> and **black hellebore** <term>lilu 黎蘆; *Veratrum nigrum*</term>. At the tip of the stem, a platform-like structure emerges; the leaves are two fingers wide and green in color.

In the summer, it produces purple flowers. In the seventh month, it bears fruit, which turns yellowish-black when ripe. By winter, the leaves wither. The root resembles a **water caltrop** <note>original: *lingmi* 菱米</note> and is white with three corners. New buds sprout from the tips of these corners. The roots are harvested in the second and seventh months of the lunar calendar.

### Medicinal Properties and Names

This herb is also known as **Gangen** <note>Sweet Root</note> or **Lianjicao** <note>Connected Orchid Herb</note>. Its flavor is **pungent**, its nature is **slightly cold**, and it is **non-toxic**.

It is effective in treating **knotted heat** <note>refring to localized inflammation, swellings, or hard lumps</note> that will not dissipate. It is primarily used to treat **impotence and atrophy** <term>yin xia wei 陰下痿; weakness or dysfunction in the lower regions</term>, and it is used to treat **dark spots and acne** <note>original: *youpao* 黝疱</note> on the face.

# Great Euphorbia

The Great Euphorbia <note>original: *Daji* 大戟; *Euphorbia pekinensis*</note> is the root of the Sun Spurge. <note>original: *Zeqi* 澤漆; *Euphorbia helioscopia*</note> It grows in Changshan, <note>A mountain range in modern-day Hebei Province.</note> though it is now commonly found in most nearby regions. In the spring, it sprouts red buds that gradually grow into clumps about one foot in height. Its leaves resemble those of a young willow, being small and rounded. During the third and fourth months, it blooms with yellow and purple flowers that are as round as apricot blossoms. 

Its root resembles a turnip <note>original: *Wujing* 蕪菁</note> or a slender ginseng; the outer skin is yellowish-black while the inner flesh is yellowish-white. The roots should be harvested in autumn or winter and dried in the shade. 

Specimens produced in the Huai River region <note>original: *Huaidian* 淮甸</note> have round stems reaching three to four feet in height, with yellow flowers and leaves that emerge from the center much like lily sprouts. Those found in the Jiangnan region <note>the lands south of the Yangtze River</note> have leaves resembling the peony. <note>original: *Shaoyao* 芍藥</note> 

It is also known as the *Qiongju*. <note>original: *Qiongju* 邛鉅</note> Its flavor is both bitter and sweet, its nature is "greatly cold," and it possesses a degree of toxicity. It is used to break up both new and old <term>stagnations</term> <note>accumulations of fluid or energy that cause illness</note>, discharge "evil blood" <note>pathological blood stasis</note> and abdominal masses, and treat "thunderous rumbling" <note>borborygmus or loud intestinal noises</note> within the belly. It can stimulate the menstrual flow and is capable of inducing miscarriage in pregnant women.

# Guanzhong

**Guanzhong** <note>original: *Guanzhong* 貫眾; identified as the Holly Fern or Wood Fern (*Dryopteris*). It is a staple of traditional pharmacy for its cooling and anti-parasitic properties.</note> grows in the valleys of Mount Xuan, as well as in Yuanju, Shaoshi Mountain, and the various prefectures of Shaanxi and Hedong. It is also found frequently in the **Jingxiang** <note>original: *Jingxiang* 荆襄; a historical region covering parts of modern-day Hubei and Hunan provinces.</note> area, though it rarely produces flowers.

In spring, the shoots emerge with a reddish hue. The leaves are as large as those of the bracken fern, while the stems are three-sided. The green leaves resemble the feathers of a small chick; hence, it is also known as **Phoenix Tail Grass** <note>original: *Fengweicao* 鳳尾草.</note>. The root is purplish-black and shaped like a large melon, with black, fibrous "hairs" underneath, giving it the appearance of an old chicken.

The roots are harvested in the third lunar month and dried in the sun. People of the Jingnan region grind the root into a powder; taking one spoonful <note>The text specifies a "one-qian" spoon, a traditional measurement roughly equal to 3–4 grams.</note> mixed with water is particularly effective at stopping nosebleeds.

It is also known by the names **Joint-Passer** <note>original: *Guanjie* 貫節</note>, **Canal-Passer** <note>original: *Guanqu* 貫渠</note>, **Hundred-Headed** <note>original: *Baitou* 百頭</note>, **Tiger-Curl** <note>original: *Hujuan* 虎卷</note>, **Flat-Reed** <note>original: *Bianfu* 扁苻</note>, **Earl-Duckweed** <note>original: *Boping* 伯萍</note>, and **Medicinal-Algae** <note>original: *Yaozao* 藥藻</note>.

Its flavor is bitter, its nature is slightly cold, and it is considered toxic. It is primarily used to treat "evil heat" <note>A traditional concept referring to inflammatory or febrile conditions caused by external pathogens.</note> and various toxins within the abdomen. It also eliminates **head-wind** <note>original: *toufeng* 頭風; a term for chronic, episodic headaches or dizziness.</note>, heals **metal wounds** <note>original: *jinchuang* 金瘡; injuries caused by blades or metallic weapons.</note>, and kills **tapeworms** <note>original: *cunbaichong* 寸白蟲; literally "inch-white worms," a reference to the segments of tapeworms.</note>.

# Ya Zi (Tooth-Seed)

**Ya Zi** <note>original: *Yazi* 牙子; literally "Tooth-Seed," a synonym for the plant *Agrimonia pilosa* or similar varieties used in traditional medicine</note> is the same as the **Wolf’s Tooth** <note>original: *Langyazi* 狼牙子</note>. It grows in the river valleys of **Huainan** and is widely found in **Yuanju**, **Jiangdong**, **Dongjing**, and various eastern prefectures. The seedlings resemble the **Snake Strawberry** <note>original: *Shemei* 蛇莓; *Duchesnea indica*</note>, but they are thicker, larger, and a deep, dark green. The root is black and shaped like the teeth of a wild animal; it is for this reason that the plant was given its name.

The roots are harvested in the third and eighth months of the lunar calendar and are then dried in the sun.

This plant is also known by the following names:
*   **Wolf’s Tooth** <note>original: *Langya* 狼牙</note>
*   **Wolf’s Canine** <note>original: *Langchi* 狼齒</note>
*   **Wolf’s Seed** <note>original: *Langzi* 狼子</note>
*   **Great Tooth** <note>original: *Daya* 大牙</note>

**Properties and Usage:**
Its flavor is bitter and sour; its nature is cold and toxic. ## He Shou Wu

It stops heart pain and benefits the blood and <term>qi</term> <note>vital energy</note>. ### Chinese Clematis

<note>original: *Wei Ling Xian* 威靈仙; *Clematis chinensis*</note>

Chinese Clematis originates from the Shangluo Mountains in Shangzhou and Mount Hua <note>two significant mountain ranges in modern-day Shaanxi Province</note>, as well as the plains and marshes of Shaanxi. It can also be found in Hedong, Hebei, Jingdong, and the various prefectures of the Jianghu region. 

Among all herbs, this plant is the very first to sprout in the spring. Its stems are shaped like the legs of a hairpin and have four distinct edges. The leaves resemble those of a willow and grow in tiers; each tier consists of six or seven leaves arranged in a circle like the spokes of a wagon wheel. Some plants may have six or seven such tiers.

In the seventh month of the lunar calendar, the flowers are harvested. They are light purple or sometimes a greenish-white, forming spikes that resemble those of the cattail <note>original: *putaizi* 莆臺子</note>, though some varieties resemble the heads of chrysanthemums. The fruit is green. The roots are dense and grow with many fibers, looking much like a clump of fine grain roots <note>likely referring to the fibrous roots of millet or similar grasses</note>. Every year, the foliage completely withers away.

The roots should be harvested in the ninth month and dried in the shade. It is considered best to harvest them on *Bing*, *Ding*, *Wu*, or *Ji* days <note>these are specific days in the traditional Chinese sexagenary cycle associated with the elements of Fire and Earth</note>. Furthermore, roots gathered from locations where the sound of running water cannot be heard are of the highest quality <note>a traditional belief that noise or the presence of water might "wash away" or diminish the plant's potent 'dry' and 'heating' medicinal properties</note>.

This herb is also known by the name **The Dissolver** <term>original: *Neng Xiao* 能消; literally "Capable of Consuming," referring to its ability to break down internal masses or blockages</term>. Its flavor is bitter, its nature is warm, and it is non-toxic. 

It is used to treat chronic accumulations <term>original: *jiju* 積聚; internal lumps or stagnant energy</term> in the waist, kidneys, legs, and knees, as well as various "cold" diseases within the intestines. For those suffering from ailments that have persisted for many years without improvement, taking this medicine never fails to produce immediate results.

## Pharbitis Seed (Morning Glory)

Historically, the specific regions where <term>Pharbitis Seed</term> <note>original: *Qianniu zi* 牽牛子; seeds of *Ipomoea nil*</note> originated were not recorded, but today it can be found everywhere. The seeds are planted in the second month of the lunar calendar, and sprouts appear in the third month. It grows as a climbing vine that spreads across fences and walls, with some reaching heights of twenty to thirty feet. Its green leaves are characterized by three pointed lobes.

In the seventh month, the plant produces flowers that are a faint red tinged with a bluish-green hue. They resemble the flowers of the <term>bindweed</term> <note>original: *Guzi hua* 鼓子花; *Calystegia sepium*</note> but are larger. In the eighth month, the plant bears fruit; the exterior is wrapped in a thin white skin that forms a small sphere. Within each sphere are four or five seeds, roughly the size of buckwheat, which have three distinct ridges. There are two varieties of these seeds: black and white.

The seeds are harvested after the ninth month. This plant is also known by the name <term>Golden Bell</term> <note>original: *Jinling* 金鈴</note>. He described how, if harvested after the Autumn Festival, the plant's shape resembles a basin or a steamer; its seeds look like small turtles, and its vines resemble those of the mountain yam. This description refers to this very plant.

In terms of medicinal properties, the seed's flavor is bitter, its nature is cold, and it is considered toxic. It is primarily used to "descend the Qi" <note>a traditional medical concept referring to relieving internal pressure, congestion, or difficulty breathing</note>, treat <term>swelling of the legs</term> <note>original: *jiaoman* 腳滿; refers to lower-extremity edema</note> and water-bloating, eliminate "wind-toxins," and promote urination.

## Jack-in-the-Pulpit
<note>original: *Tiannanxing* 天南星. This name literally translates to "Star of the Southern Sky." In modern botany, it refers to species of *Arisaema*.</note>

The <term>Jack-in-the-Pulpit</term> grows in level marshlands and is now found everywhere. In the second month of the lunar year, the sprouts emerge, appearing like lotus stalks. The stems reach a height of about one foot or more. The leaves resemble those of the **Konjac** <note>original: *jurou* 蒟蒻 (*Amorphophallus konjac*)</note>, with two branches clasping one another. In the fifth month, it blooms; the flower resembles a snake's head and is yellow in color. In the seventh month, it bears seeds in a spike, which look like pomegranate seeds and are red in color. The root resembles a taro bulb but is round. 

The roots are harvested in the second and eighth months. This plant is of a similar class to the Konjac; it is also known as "Tiger’s Paw" <note>original: *huzhang* 虎掌; historically used for *Pinellia pedatisecta*, which shares similar properties and appearance</note>. A smaller variety is called *Youba* <note>likely *Arisaema thunbergii*</note>; its sprouts grow one to two feet high, and while the stems resemble Konjac, they lack the mottled spots. Its roots are shaped like chicken eggs. 

There is also the **White Konjac**, also known as "Ghost Taro" <note>original: *guiyu* 鬼芋</note>. Its roots all resemble the Jack-in-the-Pulpit, but the Jack-in-the-Pulpit is smaller, with a soft, oily, and fine texture. When roasted <note>original: *pao* 炮; a traditional processing method involving heat to reduce toxicity</note>, the Jack-in-the-Pulpit cracks easily; this is the primary way to distinguish between them.

Its flavor is bitter and acrid, and it is poisonous. It is primarily used to treat <term>stroke</term> <note>original: *zhongfeng* 中風; in a traditional context, this refers to symptoms like paralysis or spasms attributed to internal "wind"</note>, to remove phlegm, and to treat numbness. It also functions to descend *qi*, break up hard internal accumulations, and dissipate abscesses and swellings. Furthermore, it is used to benefit the chest and diaphragm, dissipate blood stasis, and induce abortion.

## Sheep’s Hoof (Dock)
<note>original: *Yangti tu* 羊蹄禿. This refers to species of Dock or Sorrel, most likely *Rumex japonicus*. The name "Sheep's Hoof" refers to the shape of the leaves, while "tu" (bald) likely refers to its medicinal use in treating hair loss.</note>

**Sheep’s Hoof** is a wild vegetable. It originally grew in the rivers and marshes of Chenliu <note>An ancient prefecture in what is now Henan Province</note>, but it can now be found everywhere. It typically grows in low-lying, damp ground. 

In the spring, the sprouts emerge, reaching a height of three to four feet. The leaves are narrow and long, appearing quite similar to **lettuce** but with a deeper, darker green color. The nodes along the stem are a purplish-red. The flowers are a greenish-white and form in spikes. The seeds have three edges (triangular), resembling those of the <term>Siberian Motherwort</term> <note>original: *chongwei* 茺蔚 (*Leonurus japonicus*)</note>. 

The plant withers by mid-summer. Its root resembles that of **burdock** but is more firm and solid. People today harvest the roots fresh and grind them with vinegar to apply to <term>scabies and ringworm</term>; this treatment is known to be quickly effective.

It is also known by the following names:
*   **Eastern Dweller** <note>original: *Dongfang su* 東方宿</note>
*   **Connecting Worm-Land** <note>original: *Lianchong lu* 連蟲陸</note>
*   **Ghost’s Eye** <note>original: *Guimu* 鬼目</note>
*   **Xu** <note>original: *Xu* 蓄</note>

The flavor is bitter, its nature is cold, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat <term>baldness</term> and the itching of scabies. It is used to clear heat and treats conditions such as female genital erosion, spreading skin sores, carbuncles, and hemorrhoids. It is also used to kill internal parasites.

# Wild Rice Root

Wild rice root is currently found everywhere amidst the rivers, lakes, ponds, and marshes. People of the Jiangnan region <note>The "South of the River" region, known for its lush waterways.</note> refer to it as **water-oat grass** (*jiaocao*). It grows within the water, and its leaves resemble those of cattails or reeds. In the spring, it produces shoots that are sweet, delicious, and fit to be eaten; these are known as **wild rice vegetable** (*gucai*), also called **water bamboo** (*jiaobai*).

In plants that have grown for many years, the center produces a white stalk the size of a child's arm, which is called a **wild rice hand** (*gushou*). If there is a black substance found within the stalk, it is called **water-oat smut** (*jiaoyu*). <note>This refers to the presence of the fungus *Ustilago esculenta*, which causes the stem to swell into the edible "water bamboo" but eventually produces black spores.</note> Its root is similar to that of a reed, though its "cooling" and diuretic properties are even more potent.

In the marshy lowlands of the two Zhejiang provinces, wild rice grass is most abundant. Their roots intertwine as they grow; over time, they bind with the soil and float upon the water's surface. The local people call these floating masses **wild rice peat-islands** (*gufeng*). Those seedlings that possess stems are called **wild rice ginger-grass** (*gujiangcao*).

When the plant bears fruit in the autumn, the seeds are known as **carved barbarian rice** (*diaohu mi*). <note>This refers to the dark, slender grains of wild rice, which were once considered one of the "Six Grains" of ancient China before the plant became primarily a vegetable crop.</note> The ancients considered this a fine delicacy. In terms of medicinal nature, it is **greatly cold**. It is primarily used to treat <term>chronic heat in the stomach and intestines</term>, as well as <term>wasting and thirsting disorder</term> <note>A traditional term for symptoms similar to diabetes.</note>. It also stops excessive thirst and aids in the ease of urination.

# Common Knotgrass <note>original: 萹蓄 (biǎnxù); *Polygonum aviculare*</note>

**Common Knotgrass** is also known as **Flat Bamboo**. It originally came from the mountain valleys of **Donglai** <note>A historical region in what is now Shandong Province.</note>, though today it can be found growing almost anywhere. In the middle of spring, it emerges from the ground along roadsides. The young plants resemble the **Fringed Pink** <note>original: 瞿麥 (qūmài); *Dianthus superbus*</note>, with slender green leaves like those of bamboo and red stems shaped like the tines of a hairpin. Between the nodes, very tiny flowers bloom in a greenish-yellow hue. Its roots are similar to those of **mugwort**.

In the fourth and fifth months of the lunar calendar, the seedlings are harvested and dried in the shade. It has the power to kill **stinging caterpillars** <note>original: 蛓 (zhì); likely the larvae of the slug moth, known for causing skin irritation.</note>.

In the *Odes of Wei* <note>A section of the *Classic of Poetry*, the oldest existing collection of Chinese songs and poems.</note>, the phrase *"How lush and green is the bamboo"* appears. Commentators explain that "green" refers to **king-grass** <note>original: 王芻 (wángchú); *Arthraxon hispidus*</note> and "bamboo" refers to **Flat Bamboo**—which is to say, this very Common Knotgrass described here.

Its flavor is bitter and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat **spreading sores**, **scabies**, **itching**, **carbuncles**, and **hemorrhoids**. It is also used to kill the <term>Three Worms<note>A traditional Chinese medical term for various internal parasites believed to inhabit the body and cause decay.</note></term> and to treat **genital erosion** in women.

# Wolf’s Poison <note>original: 狠毒 (hěndú); a variant name for 狼毒 (lángdú), the *Stellera chamaejasme* or "Stellera root."</note>

**Wolf’s Poison** grows in the mountain valleys of **Qinting** <note>A historical region in present-day Gansu Province.</note> and **Fenggao** <note>A historical county near Mount Tai in Shandong Province.</note>. Today, it can also be found in the prefectures and commanderies of **Shaanxi**, as well as in **Liaozhou** and **Shizhou** <note>original: 遼石州 (Liáoshízhōu); likely referring to regions in modern-day Shanxi Province.</note>.

The seedlings and leaves resemble those of **Indian Poke** <note>original: 商陸 (shānglù); *Phytolacca acinosa*</note> and **Rhubarb** <note>original: 大黃 (dàhuáng)</note>. There are fine hairs on the stems and leaves. The plant flowers in the fourth lunar month and bears fruit in the eighth month. Its root has yellow bark and white flesh.

The roots should be harvested in the second and eighth months of the lunar calendar and dried in the shade. Those that have been aged and are dense enough to sink in water are considered of superior quality. Another name for this plant is **Wolf’s Weed** <note>original: 稂毒 (lángdú)</note>.

Its flavor is acrid, and it is highly toxic. It is used to treat **phlegm-retention** <note>original: 痰飲 (tányǐn); a traditional medical category for fluids that stagnate in the chest or digestive system.</note> and **abdominal masses** <note>original: 癥瘕 (zhèngjiǎ); a term for chronic obstructions or tumors in the abdomen.</note>. It is also used to kill rats, as well as birds and wild beasts.
