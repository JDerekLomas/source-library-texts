# TO THE READER.

<image-desc>An ornamental woodcut initial 'N' at the start of the text. It features a figure (possibly a putto or classical figure) seated within decorative foliage and strapwork, holding a branch or staff.</image-desc>

I DO NOT DOUBT that the title of our book, *On Occult Philosophy* <note>original: "de occulta Philosophia"</note>, or *On Magic*, will by its rarity entice a great many to read it. Among these, some of distorted opinion and weak mind—and many even malicious and ungrateful toward our talent—will approach. These men, in their rash ignorance, taking the name of Magic in the worst sense, will cry out almost as soon as they see the title that we are teaching forbidden arts, sowing the seeds of heresy, providing a stumbling block to pious ears and a scandal to excellent minds. They will claim I am a sorcerer <term>maleficum: a practitioner of harmful or "black" magic</term>, that I am superstitious, that I am possessed by demons, because I am a Magician. 

If I were to answer them that among learned men, the word "Magician" <note>original: "Magus"</note> does not signify a sorcerer, nor one who is superstitious or possessed, but rather a wise man, a priest, and a prophet; that the Sibyls <note>Prophetic women of Greek and Roman antiquity who were believed to possess divine knowledge</note> were Magicians, and therefore prophesied most clearly concerning Christ; and furthermore, that the Magi, through the wonderful secrets of the world, recognized that Christ, the author of the world, was born, and were the very first of all to come and worship him; and that the very name of Magic is accepted by philosophers, praised by theologians, and not even unwelcome to the Gospel itself—I believe those censors of such stubborn arrogance would sooner forbid themselves the Sibyls, the holy Magi, and even the Gospel itself, before they would receive the name of Magic into their favor. So much do they consult their own consciences...

->TO THE READER<-

...so that neither Apollo <note>The Greek and Roman god of music, healing, and prophecy.</note>, nor all the Muses, nor even an angel from heaven could vindicate me from their curses. To these people, I now give counsel that they should neither read our writings, nor understand them, nor remember them: for they are harmful, they are poisonous; the very mouth of Acheron <note>original: "Acherontis ostium"; Acheron was the river of woe in the Greek underworld.</note> is in this book; it speaks stones—let them beware lest it knock out their brains. 

But you, who come to read with an open mind, if you apply as much discretion of wisdom as bees do in gathering honey, then read securely: for I think you will receive no small amount of utility, and a great deal of pleasure. If you find anything that does not please you, pass it by and do not use it: for I do not approve of those things for you, but merely relate them. Yet do not reject the rest on that account; for it happens to those inspecting the volumes of physicians that they read of poisons at the same time as antidotes and medicines. 

I confess, furthermore, that Magic <note>original: "Magia"</note> itself teaches many superfluous things, and curious wonders intended for outward show; leave these aside as vain, yet do not remain ignorant of their causes. But those things which can be done for the utility of men—to avert evil events, to destroy sorceries <note>original: "maleficia"; specifically harmful magic or curses.</note>, to cure diseases, to drive out apparitions <note>original: "phantasmata"</note>, and to preserve the soundness of life, honor, and fortune, without offense to God or injury to religion—who would not judge those things to be as useful as they are necessary? 

But because I have warned you that I have written many things by way of relating them rather than affirming them (for it seemed necessary to do so so that we might omit as little as possible), many things moreover from the Platonists <note>original: "Platonicorum"</note>...

->TO THE READER<-

We have followed the opinions of the <term>Gentile philosophers</term><note>Non-Christian thinkers, primarily from Ancient Greece and Rome</note> and others where they suggested arguments suited to our writing style. Therefore, if I have erred anywhere, or if anything has been said too freely, forgive my youth; for I composed this work when I was less than a young man. Thus I may excuse myself and say: *When I was a child, I spoke as a child, I understood as a child; but when I became a man, I put away childish things* <note>original: "dum eram paruulus, loquebar ut paruulus... euacuaui quæ erant paruuli" — a reference to 1 Corinthians 13:11</note>. Indeed, in my book *On the Vanity and Uncertainty of the Sciences*, I have largely retracted this present book.

But here, perhaps, you will rebuke me again, saying: "Behold, you wrote this as a youth and retracted it as an elder; why then have you published it?" I confess, I began to write these books when I was very young, yet with the hope that I would one day release them more corrected and enriched. For that reason, I first offered them for correction to Johannes Trithemius, the Abbot of <term>Würzburg</term><note>original: "Peapolitano" refers to St. James's Abbey in Würzburg; Trithemius was a famous occult scholar and cryptographer</note>, formerly of Sponheim, a man most industrious in secret matters. 

It happened afterwards, however, that the work was intercepted before I could put the finishing touches to it. Because of corrupted copies, it began to circulate in a mutilated and unpolished state, flying through the hands of many in Italy, France, and Germany. Struck by this single misfortune, I decided to publish it myself, thinking there would be less danger if the books went forth from my own hand somewhat more refined, rather than being made public by the hands of others as torn and disordered fragments. # AGRIPPA

From my youth, I have always been a curious and fearless explorer of the effects of wonders and operations full of mysteries. I have long deliberated within myself whether I might restore <term>Magic</term> <note>original: "Magiam"; here referring to the ancient "Magia" or wisdom of the Magi</note>—that ancient discipline of all wise men—by redeeming and purging it from the errors of impiety, adorning it with its own proper reasoning, and vindicating it from the injuries of slanderers. Until now, I never dared to descend into this arena. However, after the conversation we shared at <term>Würzburg</term> <note>original: "Herbipoli"; the Latin name for the city of Würzburg</note> regarding these matters, your excellent skill and learning, along with your ardent encouragement, gave me the audacity and spirit to proceed.

Therefore, having selected the opinions of philosophers of proven faith—and having purged the introductions of those malicious men who, lying with simulated knowledge, taught that the traditions of the <term>Magi</term> <note>The "Magi" were ancient Persian priest-scientists; Agrippa seeks to separate their true wisdom from later "dark" superstitions</note> and the rejected books of darkness were published by them—I have dispersed the shadows. In these recent days, I have composed three books on Magic, gathered in a brief compendium, and inscribed them with the less offensive title *Of Occult Philosophy*. 

I now submit these to your examination—you who possess the fullest expertise in these matters—to be corrected, judged, and evaluated. If anything has been written by me that might tend toward an insult to nature, an offense to the heavenly beings, or an injury to religion, I ask that you condemn the error. But if, with the scandal of impiety dissolved and purged, you find it maintains the tradition of truth, then I ask that you protect it. I desire you to act in such a way with these books of ours, and with Magic itself, so that nothing remains hidden...

<summary>In this dedicatory letter, Agrippa explains his lifelong fascination with the mysterious and his mission to "purge" the study of magic from superstitious and impious associations. He credits a conversation in Würzburg for giving him the courage to compile his findings into three books titled "Of Occult Philosophy."</summary>
