### Editor’s Preface.

Galileo’s dialogue on the two principal systems of the world may be described as one of the most remarkable books ever written: on the one hand, because of the tragic fate it brought upon its author, and on the other—and primarily—because of its content, presented in the most engaging form. The main, though by no means the only, interest of the book lies in the fact that it reveals to the reader, with tangible clarity, the contact between modern science and scholastic natural philosophy <note>Scholasticism was a medieval school of thought that relied heavily on Aristotelian logic and Church dogma.</note> and the resulting reactions. Just as the contact points of different types of rock and the transformations occurring there allow a geologist to understand the history of the Earth, Galileo’s book is a key for the cultural historian to grasp the revolution in the world-view. From it, one can measure what it means to make a new idea, such as the Copernican theory, comprehensible and palatable to a wide audience. However, the book by no means addresses only the question of the two world systems; it is even more concerned with the entire method of scientific research. From this point forward, this method was to be seemingly more modest, but in truth more laborious and fruitful; it no longer believes it can know everything *from the outset* <note>original: "a priori" — knowledge gained through deduction rather than observation.</note> or that it already knows everything. Instead, it takes on the difficult task of finding the traces of momentous laws in seemingly insignificant clues and in everyday yet unnoticed phenomena. Galileo’s book taught his contemporaries—and this lesson may not yet be superfluous for wide and influential circles today—that the essence of science and scientific education is not exhausted by logically trained thinking and a number of ready-made formulas, but rather that the infinitely more difficult art of accounting for facts through observation and experiment is the primary means of knowledge.

Given the great interest that the book commands, the previous lack of a German edition is fundamentally surprising.

# Editor’s Preface

To be sure, the biography of Galileo written by Viviani <note>Vincenzo Viviani (1622–1703), Galileo’s last pupil and first biographer.</note> in the year 1654 mentions a German translation of the *Dialogue*; however, if this claim should be correct against all probability, that translation is today entirely lost. The attempt to organize a German edition therefore requires no further justification. The difficulties of the undertaking are, admittedly, not small: on the one hand, it is almost impossible to do justice to that beauty of form in the original—which marks the *Dialogue* as a classic work of Italian literature—without taking liberties in the translation; on the other hand, an edition intended for wider circles (and to produce such a one was the goal I set for myself) should rightfully contain those references that facilitate the understanding and appreciation of the book. These notes should make the work comprehensible within its own time, according to its merits and weaknesses, and offer the reader the possibility of understanding the progress of knowledge <term>Erkenntnisfortschritt: the evolutionary advancement of human understanding and scientific discovery</term> that takes place within it. In both respects, as I am well aware, the present edition leaves much to be desired. This stems partly and predominantly from the fact that my own powers were insufficient for a flawless fulfillment of the task, and partly from the desire to avoid an unwanted disproportion between the scope of the necessary explanations and the text of the *Dialogue* itself. Whether I have succeeded in approaching the goal I envisioned, at least to some extent, I leave to the judgment of the reader.

I could not bring myself to undertake a shortened adaptation, as tempting as that thought was; for although certain parts of the *Dialogue* may, to our modern sensibilities, deal perhaps too thoroughly with the refutation of obsolete views, it did not seem permissible to suppress such material. The *Dialogue* is more than just a book; within it, a piece of cultural history and a thought-process of humanity unfolds.
