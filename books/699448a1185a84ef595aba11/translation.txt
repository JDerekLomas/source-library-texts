# PREFACE

**W**e hasten to bring to light the first volume of these <term>relics<note>original: "nshkharats'". Literally 'relics' or 'remnants,' this term is often used in Armenian to describe precious fragments or surviving works of ancient literature.</note></term> of Armenian literature, which recounts the origins, character, and wars of the <term>Tatars<note>original: "T'at'arats'". In the 13th century, Armenians used this term to refer to the Mongols. This work is a primary source for the Mongol invasions of the Near East.</note></term>. It was written by Vardan the Historian, one of the pupils of <term>Vanakan Vardapet<note>Hovhannes Vanakan (1181–1251) was a celebrated scholar and head of the school at the monastery of Khoranashat; he was a teacher to many of the era's greatest historians.</note></term>, in the 13th century—specifically in the year 1247—and was copied in 1272. The specific copy we have in our hands is a manuscript by the same Grigor, a priest of the <term>Akants Hermitage<note>Also known as the Monastery of Akner in Cilician Armenia, a major center for manuscript illumination and historical writing.</note></term> in <term>Cilicia<note>The Armenian Kingdom of Cilicia (1198–1375), located on the Mediterranean coast, was a vital ally to the Crusaders and a center of Armenian learning during the Mongol period.</note></term>. This copy does not contain a separate <term>colophon<note>original: "yishatakaran". A formal inscription by a scribe at the end of a book, providing historical context about the writing of the manuscript.</note></term> regarding the scribe; however, in the margins of the copy, here and there, the following is seen written in the same hand:

->“Lord, have mercy on Vardan, your servant.”<-

Furthermore, this copy is bound together with the chronology of <term>Michael the Syrian<note>Michael I (1126–1199), the Patriarch of the Syriac Orthodox Church. His "Universal Chronicle" was a massive historical undertaking that was translated into Armenian by Vardan Areveltsi.</note></term>, the Patriarch, which is clearly the translation of the same Vardan, as announced in its own colophon. The language and the narrative style of this history itself testify that it is his own original composition. Within this history of the Tatars, there are also accounts concerning the Armenian Kingdom, and for this reason, it is considered a part of the History of the Armenians. Having divided the work into chapters according to the various events narrated within, we now present it for publication.
