# Introduction.

If history becomes more of a burden than a pleasure to an aspiring youth—because they would gladly begin a new, or perhaps even a primordial epoch <note>original: "Urwelt-Epoche"; referring to a desire to start entirely fresh without the weight of the past</note> from their own self—then those advancing in education and age must very often acknowledge with lively gratitude how many diverse, useful, and helpful things have been left to them by their ancestors.

Nothing stands still. Despite all apparent setbacks, humanity and science <note>The German "Wissenschaft" encompasses all systematic branches of knowledge, including the arts and humanities</note> must always move forward, even if both should ultimately close back upon themselves again. Exceptional spirits have always been found who wished to share their thoughts. Much that is worthy of esteem has come down to us from them, from which we can convince ourselves that our ancestors never lacked striking insights into nature.

The circle which humanity is destined to run is determined enough, and despite the great standstill caused by barbarism, it has already completed its course more than once. Even if one wishes to attribute a spiral motion to it, it nevertheless returns again and again to that region through which it has already passed. On this path, all true insights and all errors repeat themselves.

In order to inform oneself about the <term>Theory of Colors</term> <note>German: "Farbenlehre"; Goethe's holistic approach to how we perceive color, contrasting with purely mathematical optics.</note>, one had to at least traverse the entire history of <term>Natural Science</term> <note>German: "Naturlehre"; at this time, the term encompassed physics and the general study of the laws of nature.</note>, and not leave the history of philosophy out of consideration. A concise presentation would have been desirable; but under the given circumstances, it was not achievable. We therefore had to resolve to provide only materials for the history of the theory of colors, and to sift through, to some extent, that which had accumulated with us for this purpose.

What we understand by that expression will not be difficult to interpret. Whoever provides materials for a building always brings more and less than is required. For, from that which has been brought together, much must often be taken away,

Prompted by such considerations and pressed by such necessities, we mostly let the authors speak for themselves; indeed, we would have preferred to provide the originals rather than the translation, had not a certain uniformity and more general usefulness persuaded us to do the opposite. The insightful reader will converse with each author individually; we have sought to facilitate his judgment, not to forestall it. The supporting evidence <note>original: "Belege"</note> is at hand, and a capable mind will easily synthesize them. The repetition at the conclusion will be helpful in this regard.

If we were permitted one more lighthearted remark here, we would say: that by this method of allowing every author to freely express his error as well as his truth, the friends of the untrue and the false are also provided for; they are hereby given the best opportunity to bestow their applause upon the strangest and least sustainable ideas.

After this first part, which actually constitutes the basis of our endeavor, we have added characteristic sketches and individual biographical traits concerning many a significant man, presented aphoristically <note>Aphoristically: expressed in short, pithy, or concise statements rather than a continuous narrative.</note>

# On the Nature of Vision and Color

Whatever is brought forth from those parts in such a way that it strikes the sight <note>original: "Gesicht"; here referring to the visual faculty or the sense of sight itself</note> is either smaller or larger than the parts of the sight, or completely equal to them. That which is equal is not perceived, which is why we call it transparent. By the smaller, however, the sight is contracted <note>original: "gesammelt"; literally gathered or collected</note>, while by the larger it is dilated <note>original: "entbunden"; literally released or unbound</note>. Both are related to the warm and cold that act upon the skin, to the sour that acts upon the tongue, and to the pungent <note>original: "Hizigen"; literally 'heaty' or sharp</note> which we also call bitter.

Through **Black** and **White**, the same effects are produced, but as appearances for a different sense, yet arising from the same causes. Therefore, it can be asserted: by **White** the sight is dilated, while by **Black** it is contracted.

A lively impulse, however, and a kind of different fire, presses from within toward the eyes and likewise dilates the sight; and as it forcefully penetrates and melts the passages of the eyeballs, a fiery water is frequently shed, which we call a tear. That impulse, however, is a fire that encounters the external fire.

Now, when the internal fire rushes out like a flash of lightning, while the external fire penetrates and is extinguished in the moisture, we are blinded by the colors arising from such mutual interaction; and that from which the effect originates, we call **luminous** or **shining**.

# Pliny

Since this author is available in everyone's hands, both in the original <note>Latin</note> and in translations, it would be superfluous and useless to have his text reprinted here; all the more so because anyone who seeks to understand and interpret him in detail finds many difficulties which we do not hope to overcome. We prefer, therefore, to insert an essay in which a friend summarizes what Pliny said of colors and coloring <term>colorit: the technical application and harmony of colors in painting</term>, and expresses his opinion on how, according to the natural progress of the art of painting, the individual details might be understood and arranged.

May this attempt serve as an example of how one can develop and represent a significant world event out of its own nature, and use the transmitted reports only insofar as they stand in harmony with necessity. The main points, upon which everything depends, then emerge more brilliantly; gaps are discovered and, where not filled, at least identified; and in this way, something instructive and stimulating is achieved for the present, while also preparing the way for the future.
