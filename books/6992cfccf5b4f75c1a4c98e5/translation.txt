# Illustrated Gazetteer of the Maritime Kingdoms
<note>original: "海國圖志" (Hǎiguó Túzhì)</note>

<note>This title slip belongs to a landmark publication in modern Chinese history. Compiled by the scholar-official <term>Wei Yuan (1794–1857)</term> and first published in 1842, this work was a direct response to China's defeat in the First Opium War. Wei Yuan's goal was to provide a detailed account of the geography, history, and military technology of Western nations, famously advocating that China should "learn the superior techniques of the barbarians to control them."</note>

The **Appalachian Mountains** <note>original: "俺山" (Ǎnshān), likely referring to the southern reaches of the Appalachian or Alleghany range</note> reach a height of approximately one hundred *zhang* <term>zhang: a traditional Chinese measurement; 100 zhang is roughly 330 meters or 1,100 feet</term>. There are several rivers here, the largest being the **Alabama River** <note>original: "亞喇羅麻河" (Yàlǎluómǎ Hé)</note>; these all flow into **Mobile Bay** <note>original: "磨庇理灣" (Móbìlǐ Wān)</note> to reach the sea. The territory spans five degrees of latitude from south to north. Consequently, the southern region is sweltering in the summer, while the north experiences some frost and snow, though it is never severely cold. For this reason, the birds and beasts of the region have no need for dens or burrows.

The local soil produces rice, grains, and fruits, all of which blossom in the third month and ripen by the fourth or fifth months. The interior of the country is intensely hot, and the inhabitants often retreat into the mountains during the summer to escape the heat. The pine trees produced here are stunted and weak, and the grain crops are often hollow, containing more husks than solid grain.

Elsewhere, the lands to the south near the **Gulf of Mexico** <note>original: "美是哥海" (Měishìgē Hǎi)</note> and those to the north near the mountains are entirely fertile. The territory is vast and the population is sparse; the tracks of wild beasts and birds crisscross the wilderness. Those engaged in farming and animal husbandry must constantly remain on guard against predatory animals. **Cotton** is the primary crop, followed by **sugarcane**, rice, **tobacco**, and various fruits. All these commodities are transported to **Mobile Bay** for export. The region is divided into thirty-six districts <note>original: "部落" (bùluò), literally "tribes" or "clans," used here to describe administrative counties</note>, with a total population of 308,997 people.

The **French** <note>original: 法蘭西 (Fǎlánxī)</note> were the first to arrive and settle in this land. However, the **Spaniards** <note>original: 西班牙 (Xībānyá)</note> also desired the territory, leading to a conflict between the two nations. Consequently, the area was initially treated as common ground. Later, **Great Britain** <note>original: 英吉利 (Yīngjílì)</note> contended with **France** for control. In 1762 <gloss>the 27th year of the Qianlong reign</gloss>, the British gained possession of it. It was not until 1797 <gloss>the 2nd year of the Jiaqing reign</gloss> that the land first came under the control of **America** <note>original: 美理哥 (Měilǐgē)</note>. In 1817 <gloss>the 22nd year of the Jiaqing reign</gloss>, it was formally organized as a state.

To the east, it borders **Alabama** <note>original: 亞喇罷麻 (Yàlǎbàmá)</note>; to the north, it borders **Tennessee** <note>original: 典尼西 (Diǎnníxī)</note>; and to the west, it is bounded by the **Mississippi River** <note>original: 美士細比河 (Měishìxìbǐ Hé)</note>. To the south, it reaches the **Gulf of Mexico** <note>original: 墨西哥 (Mòxīgē)</note>. In terms of size, it is slightly smaller than China's **Zhili Province** <note>Zhili was the northern imperial province surrounding Beijing, known today as Hebei; this comparison helps the reader visualize the scale of the American state</note>.

In the north lie the **Appalachian Mountains** <note>original: 押罷拉旣俺山 (Yābàlājì'ǎn Shān)</note>, which reach their terminus here. The terrain in the northern part of the territory is quite steep and rugged. For this reason, some of the northern river waters flow diagonally into the great **Mississippi River**, while others flow eastward into **Alabama**. Only the rivers originating within the territory’s own borders flow directly south into the **Gulf of Mexico**. 

The climate and seasonal temperatures are no different from those in **Alabama**. However, the western border lacks protective dikes or barriers; as a result, the land frequently suffers from the seasonal flooding and overflowing of the **Mississippi River**.

When the Europeans <note>original: 歐羅巴人 (Ōu luó bā rén)</note> first arrived, these indigenous peoples <note>original: 土蠻 (tǔ mán); literally "local barbarians," a term common in 19th-century Chinese texts to describe native populations</note> numbered roughly one hundred thousand; today, there are some three or four hundred thousand. Across their lands, there are dozens of ancestral tribes. Their skin is reddish, and their eyes and hair are a lustrous black; their hair is long and coarse, while their bodies are tall and powerful.

Their minds are clear and they are trustworthy. When discussing communal affairs, they show great respect for the elderly, and in battle, they advance without ever retreating. However, if they harbor a grudge, it is as if they cannot coexist under the same sky. If they are bullied or mistreated, they will sit in indignant silence, brooding for a long time. Then, they will suddenly rise and face any danger—even "treading through boiling water or fire" <note>original: 赴湯蹈火 (fù tāng dǎo huǒ); a classical idiom for risking one's life for a cause</note>—to ensure their revenge is settled. Should they be captured by an enemy, they will fold their hands and accept execution; they would never kneel to beg for mercy. Such is the temperament of these indigenous people.

However, they possess no books and do not understand writing. Nor do they know how to farm, weave, cook, or draw water. They "eat hair and drink blood" <note>original: 茹毛飲血 (rú máo yǐn xuè); a traditional Chinese phrase used to describe a primitive lifestyle</note> and consume only wild plants, vegetables, and melons.
