## History and Evolution of the Russian State in the Northern Ocean <gloss>Originally missing, now supplemented</gloss>

According to the *Examination of the Four Tributaries of the Imperial Qing Dynasty* <note>original: "Huangqing Siyi Kao," a historical record of foreign nations known to the Qing court</note>, the Russian state reaches the [Arctic] Sea to its northeast. To the south, it borders the territories of the Khalkhas, the Dzungars, the Kazakhs, and the Torghuts <note>These refer to various Mongol and Central Asian nomadic groups that formed the frontiers of the Qing Empire</note>. To the west, it borders various Western countries <note>The text uses "Xiyang," a term that generally referred to Europe during this period</note>. 

During the Qin and Han dynasties [approx. 221 BCE – 220 CE], this region belonged to the Hunyu, Qushe, and Dingling nations; the Xiongnu <note>A powerful confederation of nomadic peoples who were the primary rivals of early Chinese dynasties</note> eventually annexed all these lands. 

During the Tang Dynasty [618–907 CE], it was the Guligan state, situated north of the Hanhai <note>A term historically used for the Gobi Desert or, more likely here, the region around Lake Baikal</note>; its territory extended north all the way to the sea. 

During the Yuan Dynasty [1271–1368 CE, under Mongol rule], these were the lands of the *Alousi* <note>original: "阿羅思," a phonetic transliteration of "Russia" or "Rus" used during the Mongol era</note>, the *Jilijisi* [Kyrgyz], and the new territories of the *Angke* <note>Likely referring to the Angara River region in Siberia</note>.

The *History of Yuan* <note>The official history of the Mongol-led Yuan Dynasty</note> states that the Kyrgyz lands were over 10,000 *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters or 0.3 miles</note> south of the capital, Dadu <note>modern-day Beijing</note>. Their territory was 1,400 *li* long and half as wide. The Qian River <note>the Yenisei River</note> flows through it to the northwest and empties into the Angke'la River <note>the Angara River</note>, which then flows north into the sea. Today, these lands are possessed by Russia.

Depending on the number and severity of crimes committed by a thief, some are punished by the cutting off of the nose <term>劓刑, *yixing*</term>, while others are branded with fire and then exiled. Those who privately mint counterfeit currency have molten copper poured down their throats as a means of execution. Those who privately sell tobacco or alcohol are heavily punished, their family property is confiscated, and they are sent into exile. 

In cases where a wife kills her husband due to an illicit affair, the wife is buried alive in the earth with only her head exposed until she dies; the lover is hung from a tree until dead. In cases of simple adultery, the wife is heavily punished but is not divorced from her husband; the lover is heavily punished and fined silver, which is paid to the government. If a young boy and a girl commit an act of "adultery" <note>likely referring to premarital relations</note>, they are both heavily punished and then forced to marry one another.

The local customs follow the "Religion of the Lord of Heaven" <note>original: *Tianzhujiao*. While this term usually means Catholicism, the author uses it here to refer to Eastern Orthodoxy</note>. They do not recognize the traditional lunar markers of the new moon or full moon <note>original: *shuowang*</note>; instead, a month consists of twenty-nine, thirty, or thirty-one days. Twelve months constitute one year. Each year, following the four seasons, there are four "Great Fasts" <gloss>Lent or liturgical fasting seasons</gloss>, lasting either forty days or thirty-some days. The day the winter fast ends is considered the beginning of the new year. 

In ordinary times, they observe a fast every seven days <note>referring to the Sabbath or Sunday worship</note>. They are known to be thrifty and have a distaste for military conflict, yet their character is also described as boastful and greedy. They are fond of flattery and humor, and they have a great love for engaging in legal disputes and litigation.

On every auspicious day, the men gather to drink together. When they are intoxicated, they recite poetry and dance. The women do not avoid guests; they compete with one another to display their fine ornaments and play games in groups, singing as they walk along the roads. 

Those of lowly status, upon seeing a person of honor, remove their hats and stand upon the ground to perform a bow <term>叩, *kou*</term>. Those of high noble rank do not remove their hats. When equals meet, both remove their hats and bow while standing. When a man and a woman meet, the man removes his hat, and the woman stands and bows. They consider the removal of felt boots to be a sign of refinement <note>original: *qu zhan xie*. This likely refers to the etiquette of removing outdoor footwear or a specific style of Russian boot</note>, and curly hair is regarded as beautiful.

For marriages, they also utilize matchmakers. On the day of the wedding, the couple goes to pay their respects at the "Church of the Lord of Heaven" <note>As noted previously, this refers to the Eastern Orthodox Church</note>. Only after the recitation of scriptures is complete do they perform the ceremony of the nuptial cup <term>合巹, *hejin*</term>. For funerals, coffins are used, and the deceased are all taken inside the church for burial. 

They do not drink tea. They clothe themselves in felt, coarse wool, and ramie cloth. They use wheat and leaven to make cakes and bread; they do not prepare rice. For every meal, they use spoons and small forks; they have no chopsticks <term>筯, *zhu*</term>. 

Their customs place a high value on trade, and those who labor in agriculture are few. They understand how to sow seeds, but they do not understand the practice of weeding or cultivating the soil. They do not use oxen for plowing. Those who live along the riverbanks are skilled at swimming. They plant melons. Their silver coins, both large and small, have values of three, ten, fifty, and one hundred *wen* <note>The author is equating Russian currency—likely kopecks—to the *wen*, the standard Chinese copper cash, to explain their relative value to the reader</note>.

Li Ling <note>A famous Han Dynasty general who surrendered to the Xiongnu in 99 BCE after being outnumbered</note> served as the Right Worthy King, while Wei Lu <note>Another Han defector</note> served as the King of the Dingling. Later, the Zhizhi Chanyu <note>A Xiongnu leader</note> defeated the **Gekun** <note>The ancient name for the Kirghiz/Kyrgyz people</note>. At that time, their land lay 7,000 *li* east of the Chanyu’s court and 5,000 *li* north of Cheshi <note>An ancient kingdom in the Turpan Basin</note>. After the Zhizhi Chanyu established his capital there, the name of the land was corrupted in later generations to *Jiegu*, and gradually it was called *Hegu*, also known as *Hegusi*.

It is situated 3,000 *li* directly northwest of the **Huige** <note>The Uighurs</note>. To the south, it rests against the Tanman Mountains. The land is marshy in the summer and covered in deep snow during the winter. The people are all tall and large, with red hair, pale faces, and green eyes. They consider black hair to be an ill omen; those with black hair and eyes are certainly said to be the descendants of Li Ling. Both men and women are mostly fair-skinned. They are a vigorous and brave people, accustomed to the frequent cold. Even in the freezing weather, they cultivate crops: there are varieties of millet, grain, pulse, wheat, and barley. Their horses are exceptionally strong and large. 

Their ruler is called the *A-re* <term>A-re: A title of the Kyrgyz rulers, later adopted as a surname</term>, and thus his family took *A-re* as their surname. He resides at the Qingshan <note>literally "Green Mountain"</note>. From there to the Uighur capital, it is a forty-day journey by camel. Six hundred *li* north of the Uighur capital, one finds the Xian’e River <note>Likely the Selenga River</note>. The water to the east of the river comes from the Snow Mountains; the land there is rich with springs. To the east of the Qingshan lies the water called Jianchi <note>literally "Sword Pool"</note>. People cross it using small boats. All these waters flow northeast through their kingdom, eventually merging and flowing north into the sea. 

The Gekun is a powerful nation. Its territory is equal in size to that of the **Turks**. To the east, it reaches the Guligan; to the south, it borders Tibet; and to the southwest, it reaches the Karluks. In the 22nd year of the Zhenguan era <note>648 CE</note>, they came to the imperial court to offer tribute. The Emperor <note>Tang Taizong</note> placed their land under the jurisdiction of the Jiankun Prefecture, which was subordinate to the Yanran Protectorate. During the Qianyuan era <note>758–760 CE</note>, they were defeated by the Uighurs. Later, the name was corrupted to **Xiajiasi** <note>The Tang-era name for the Kyrgyz</note>; it is said the Uighurs called them this, meaning "yellow and red faces."

It is also said that the **Guligan** <note>An ancient Siberian people, likely the ancestors of the Yakuts or Buryats</note> reside north of the Hanhai <note>Here referring to the vast northern desert or Lake Baikal region</note>. Their land abounds in lilies and produces excellent horses. This territory reaches the sea to the north and is the farthest from the Chinese capital. If one crosses the sea further north, the days are long and the nights are short. When the sun sets, by the time a sheep's spleen has finished cooking, the east is already bright again. This is because it is close to where the sun sets <note>An early Chinese description of the "Midnight Sun" or the Arctic Circle</note>. 

During the reign of Emperor Taizong, they came to court, and their land was designated as the Yuanque Prefecture. Their great chieftain offered horses as tribute; the Emperor named the ten most extraordinary ones the "Ten Steeds."
