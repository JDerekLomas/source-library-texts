# TO ALESSANDRO FARNESE
## MOST ILLUSTRIOUS CARDINAL

->GIROLAMO FRACASTORO<-
->OF VERONA SENDS GREETINGS<-

<note>Girolamo Fracastoro (c. 1476–1553) was a renowned Veronese physician, poet, and scholar, famous for naming the disease "syphilis" and for his early theories on contagion.</note>

Since man was born for the sake of his fellow man, most holy Prince <note>The term "Prince" here refers to Farnese's high status as a Prince of the Church (Cardinal).</note>, I see that it has often been a matter of doubt by which benefit we are able to be more helpful: by that which is acquired through leisure <term>otium: scholarly or contemplative leisure, distinct from mere idleness</term> in contemplating and writing, or by that which comes through the transaction of business <term>negotia: active public life or political affairs</term> and by performing many excellent deeds. For many have indeed greatly benefited not only their kinsmen and friends, but also Republics and often many nations by their actions; yet many others have done so by writing. Between these two, there seems to me to be this difference: those who help through business provide a more immediate and more visible utility—wealth, patronage, honors, liberties, and many other things of that kind. Those, however, who help through leisure, if they do not bring a utility that is quite so immediate, they certainly bring one that is more universal and more perpetual, destined to overcome the injuries of time and fortune. In this way, disciplines, laws, histories, and arts—once discovered, increased, and handed down through the generations—have been able to reach all ages and all nations. However, to be able to be useful in both ways has always been granted to very few indeed, whom God has especially loved; for leisure and business are usually not a little opposed to one another. For this reason, Kings and leading men, because they are detained and distracted by actions and can less devote themselves to leisure, have themselves been accustomed to be useful more through the one (that is, through actions), but less through the other (that is, through those benefits which proceed from leisure). But truly, whoever are truly Kings and Princes certainly take care and provide that what they cannot perform through themselves, they nevertheless provide through others; lest, while they excel in one kind, they should seem to fail the human race in the other—for whose benefit they were born and appointed by God. In which matter, no less...

From this, indeed, it happens that Kings take no small account and care of talents <note>original: "ingeniorum," referring to intellectual or creative genius</note>; they nourish and cherish them, and in a way, they are the cause of their being brought forth. <meta>This page introduces the author's motivation for writing a systematic treatise on contagion, noting the gaps left by ancient Greek and Roman medical authorities.</meta>

The records handed down by our ancestors, both those that survive and those we have seen ourselves, have been committed to writing. Regarding the **sympathy** <note>original: "sympathia"; the natural affinity or "attraction" between certain things</note> and **antipathy** <note>original: "antipathia"; the natural repulsion between certain things</note> of things, Galen <note>Claudius Galenus (129–c. 216 AD), the most influential physician of the Roman Empire</note> reports that he had already written some things, but those writings have either perished or, as far as we know, have not yet come to light in our age. Regarding **contagion**, Hippocrates <note>The "Father of Medicine" (c. 460–c. 370 BC)</note> indeed seems to have touched upon some points concerning diseases that spread among the people <note>original: "populariter uagantes morbos"; referring to epidemics</note>. However, he was one who observed them rather than one who explained their underlying natures.

Galen, coming after him, demonstrated more things, but even he—as well as his follower Paul of Aegina, Aetius of Amida, and other ancients—left behind, it seems to me, many things that are greatly to be desired. More recent authors, however, seem to have said nothing more about contagion than that it arises from some **hidden property** <note>original: "occulta quadam proprietate"; a common medieval explanation for phenomena that couldn't be explained by the four humors</note>. 

But as to what the nature of contagions is in general, by what principle they affect us, how they are created, and why some leave behind a **tinder** <note>original: "fomitem"; the author's famous term for "seeds" or carriers of infection that linger on clothes or surfaces</note> while others propagate themselves even at a distance; and why some diseases are contagious despite being mild and gentle, while others are not at all contagious even though they are much more bitter and burning; and how contagion differs from poisons—these and many other such things no one has attempted to explain. They thought they had done enough if they reduced the causes of all these things to those "hidden properties" (as they call them), in which they believed not only they should rest, but they instructed others to do the same. Content with these, they have philosophized on a great many matters, which I have always considered unworthy of a philosopher. 

For while some causes are most universal and most remote from things, others are closer and more particular, and finally, some are most immediate and specific. To have reached the specific and most immediate causes in hidden and difficult matters is surely the work of God or a divine being. To stand still at the most universal causes is the mark of a lazy and unrefined mind. But to inquire into the middle causes and to strive toward the specific ones (as much as is granted to man) is surely the mark of a philosopher. 

You will perhaps marvel that I have taken up these two subjects to write about, one of which our ancestors left almost untouched, and the other which everyone admits cannot be known by man. But truly, because both the sympathy of things and the nature...

The nature of contagions <note>original: "contagionum"</note> is placed in a difficult and arduous position, and we indeed concede this. Furthermore, we say that he would be foolish or rather arrogant who presumes to inquire into their most immediate and proper causes. Nevertheless, if that same man investigates the intermediate causes, I do not doubt that he will find many things which can both delight and be of great benefit. This is the purpose I have set for myself in this investigation of ours. Truly, I would wish no one to wonder that the subject of contagions was passed over by our ancestors, since it happens in all disciplines that not everything can be completed by those who found them, because they pursue universal principles and the beginnings of things more. Thus, in that <term>philosophy</term> <note>Natural philosophy, the precursor to modern science</note> which concerns nature, countless things have been left partly untouched and partly not fully discussed. For I see that neither how understanding <note>original: "intellectio"</note> occurs within us has been handed down thus far, nor has the nature of those qualities which they call spiritual been sufficiently demonstrated, along with many other things. This happens similarly in <term>medical matters</term> <note>original: "re medica"; the field of medicine</note> as well. In this field, very many things still remain which our fathers left to be investigated by their descendants and grandchildren, and we furthermore shall leave them to our own grandchildren. Moreover, many of those things which were already written perish; many indeed live on, but not among all people; such is the condition and vicissitude of mortal affairs. Whether or not the inquiry into <term>contagions</term> <note>The transmission of disease by touch or proximity</note> which we are presently undertaking is necessary, everyone who reads what we shall write will be able to see, if the Gods grant it. You, however, will receive with a willing mind these things, whatever they may be, which according to ancient custom we dedicate to a leading man—to you, moreover, because they are now yours and were brought forth for your sake. Then, with the great authority you possess, you will protect them, so that no one may dare to pursue with slanders those things which have been accepted by you and sent out under your auspices.

<image-desc>A large historiated drop cap 'P' depicting a bearded man, possibly a scholar or shepherd, seated in a natural landscape with trees and a small animal at his feet.</image-desc>

First, let us treat of that hidden agreement and disagreement of things, which they call <term>Sympathy and Antipathy</term> <note>From the Greek 'sympatheia' (feeling together) and 'antipatheia' (feeling against). Fracastoro is exploring why certain things in nature are naturally drawn together or repelled.</note>, than which nothing is more wonderful in all of nature, and nothing more desired to be known. A young chick, scarcely born, fears neither horse nor elephant, yet is so terrified of a kite <note>A type of bird of prey.</note> seen even from afar that it immediately flees to its mother. The same enmity exists between the lamb and the wolf, and many others. The <term>Marsi and Psylli</term> <note>Ancient tribes renowned in antiquity for their supposed immunity to snake venom and ability to charm serpents.</note> handle serpents unharmed. We read that the Thessalians and certain families in Crete were accustomed to cast a spell by praising, but by their very gaze would cause infants to waste away. Lightning melts gold while leaving the chest it is in untouched, and consumes wine while the vessel is not harmed at all. They say that the <term>Adamant</term> <note>original: "Adamantem"; historically referring to the diamond or the hardest known gemstone.</note> softens only with the blood of a goat, and that Citron fruits wither when placed on a heap of bread. When a string on a lute is struck, we see another string tuned to the same pitch move, even if it is not nearby. Indeed, it is said that if a drum made of wolf skin is struck, those drums constructed from lamb skin are ruined. The <term>Herculean stone</term> <note>original: "Herculeus lapis"; the lodestone or natural magnet.</note> draws iron to itself; Diamond and Amber draw hairs and twigs. But what is as much a marvel as that miracle unknown to the ancients, the <term>navigator's compass</term> <note>original: "navigatoriæ pyxidis"; literally 'the navigator's box'.</note>, whose iron needle, tempered by a magnet, turns itself at an incredible distance always toward those parts which look to the Poles? What of the fact that the Lion, the terror of all animals, is nevertheless terrified by a poultry cock, especially when it lets out its song? They affirm that a Bull, however raging, becomes tame immediately if tied to a fig tree. There is such enmity between Cabbage and Rue that they refuse to be planted near each other. When water touches the point **f**, the air is indeed held by force in **b. f.** The proof of this is that if the pore were to be broken open, the air that was inside would rush toward the surface of the whole (**a. b. c.**) and would expand along with it. It would unite in such a way that it would be distributed equally through all its parts and make the whole uniform everywhere. This indicates that the parts of air enclosed in pores are not contained there according to their natural inclination. 

Nevertheless, they remain in those pores and do not leave as long as the porous object is within the region and sphere of air. Why then should this new air enter while the old air leaves, since there is no more reason for one to be there than the other? Especially since, if this new air entered, it too would be detained by force in the pores for the same reason. If that air then departed, another would have to succeed it, and so on to infinity. Therefore, it is necessary that the original air, which chance first enclosed there, remains there as long as the porous object is kept within the sphere of air. 

However, when it happens that the porous object is no longer surrounded by the sphere of air—but is either placed entirely in water, or at least touches water at some extremity—then the air inside the openings immediately rushes out and moves toward the location of the whole body of air. Water then succeeds it. This water also suffers a certain "violence" <note>In natural philosophy, 'violence' refers to a movement against an element's natural place or tendency.</note> for the same reason, because it too is separated from its own whole. However, water is held in that porous space with less "violence" than air was, because of a compatibility of nature; both the water and the porous body possess weight <note>Latin: *grauis*, heavy or having mass</note> and share a certain analogy. 

There is also another reason why water should succeed the air and be drawn upward: because that which pulls and moves is the whole body of air seeking the retreat of the air trapped in the pores. That which is moved, however, is a very small part of the water which is drawn into the tiny pores; it offers little resistance because of its smallness. This is similar to what happens with pulleys made with spiral screws <note>Latin: *trocleis per helices*, likely referring to a worm gear or Archimedean screw mechanism</note>, where we lift even the heaviest weight because the lifting happens gradually and laterally. 

A sign that water follows easily when attracted in this way is this: if the pore is very wide, such as a pipe <note>Latin: *fistula*</note>, the water does not move up into it, nor does the air in that pipe depart. But in small and slanted pores, the air does depart. Therefore, either this is the cause of water entering porous things, or other factors concur, such as "propulsion" (which happens through various forms) and the "attraction of moisture" which occurs naturally in porous materials. Let this be sufficient regarding this matter.

### On the analogy of things in action. Chapter 11.

There is, furthermore, another agreement and disagreement of things in action, either simply or for a certain end. For not all things act upon all things, but only certain things upon certain others. The reason for these must be sought, but in a universal sense. Therefore, in every action, three things are especially required, particularly if it is to occur for the sake of a certain goal. These are the **power of the agent** <note>the ability of the thing doing the acting</note>, the **fitness of the matter**, and the **suitable application**. By application, I mean contact, position, duration, and in some cases, instruments. When all these agree, then the hoped-for action occurs and the goal is obtained; things of this kind are called **Analogous** <term>Analogous: here meaning "proportionate" or "harmoniously aligned" for a specific result</term>. If, however, all, or many, or even one of these is lacking, the action is frustrated, either entirely or in its goal, and these are called non-analogous.

It happens very often that one or more of these are lacking: for sometimes the power of the agent is less than it ought to be, and sometimes greater. For this reason, before it can introduce the intended form, it often consumes the subject, or melts it, or turns it into vapor. For this reason, a large flame cannot be nourished on a small candle. For it immediately melts the entire fuel, which ought to have been turned gradually into smoke and from there into flame. 

Sometimes the matter itself is unfit. For a flame will never rise from earth or water, but rather vapor, which will vanish. Nor will it rise from mud or stone, unless these have perhaps first been converted into many other things. If a flame is to exist, it must be made from a mixture—and not just any mixture, but from something dry or oily, close to the nature of fire. 

In the same way, a human being will not be nourished by just anything, even if it is tender. For he is not nourished by grass like a horse, because it is foreign to the nature of man. Things are foreign in a universal sense: some because of contrary qualities which are far apart; others because of spiritual qualities <note>referring to the "vital spirits" or subtle essences in nature</note> which belong to opposites and cause the soul to flee, or even repel certain things. Others are foreign because of the mode of their substance, such as hardness, softness, thickness, or stickiness and the like. Often, both the agent and the matter will be suitable, but the mode of application will not agree. Much greater and more admirable are those agreements and disagreements which occur when the appearance of good or evil is brought to the **imagination** <note>original: "phantasia"; the faculty that processes mental images and sensory data</note>, through which it is no longer just a single sense that judges, but the living being itself. ### On Fear. Chapter 19.

It is manifest that fear is a certain kind of sadness, for we all feel fear because of some evil. If, therefore, the evil is already present and currently causing harm, it becomes simple sadness. If, however, it is not yet present but looms as something about to happen, it is then called fear and dread. For fear is nothing other than sadness arising from an imminent evil. Now, the first thing that happens in fear is the flight of heat inward: that heat which is outside the heart moves toward the heart, while the heat already in the heart retreats into its innermost depths. Because of this, those who are afraid grow pale and tremble as the heat deserts their limbs. In fear, there is a great gathering and flight of heat to the heart because the heart itself is laboring most intensely.

### Why some diseases are contagious and others are not, and why some contagious diseases are mild. Chapter 10.

First, therefore, we must ask why some diseases are contagious and others are not, and how it happens that some, though they are more burning and severe, bring about no contagion, while others, being calmer and milder, are highly contagious—a matter that certainly holds some doubt. *For if contagion follows power and action, those diseases which are more acute would seem more contagious; if, however, contagion follows parching heat <note>original: "adustionem"; the medical concept of "burning" or scorching of the humors</note> (as many physicians believe), it would similarly seem that those which are more burning are more contagious. ### How contagion agrees with poisons, and how it differs. Chapter II.

Some contagions agree and have a certain kinship with poisons, since just as poisons—hiding deceitfully through a certain enmity—destroy the animal and seek the heart, so too certain contagions are accustomed to do. For this reason, we are accustomed to call certain fevers "poisonous." However, they differ from one another significantly, because poisons can neither properly cause putrefaction, nor can they produce in a second subject the same kind of principle and "seed-bed" <note>original: "seminarium"; Fracastoro's famous term for the seeds of disease.</note> that existed in the first. The proof of this is that those who are poisoned are not contagious to others. 

The reason for this is that there are two kinds of poisons. For some kill by a spiritual quality <note>A non-material or "breath-like" influence.</note>, such as the majority of serpents and the gaze of the Catoblepas <note>A legendary Ethiopian beast said to kill with its eyes.</note>. Others, however, work through a material quality. Those which act through spiritual forms can indeed corrupt by putting the innate heat to flight and inflicting intolerable sorrow; yet they can generate nothing similar to themselves, since all generation arises from the primary qualities <note>Heat, cold, moisture, and dryness.</note>. Because of this, we have never seen anything produced in a poisoned person like that which is emitted by a Viper or a Basilisk. 

Of those poisons which work through a material quality, some are hot, such as those called caustic and burning; others are cold, such as opium and henbane <note>original: "hyoscyamus"</note> and that sort. Truly, those which are hot and burning are all consistent in dryness; therefore, they are more apt to burn away than to putrefy and induce contagion. If certain things are called "putrefactive" by physicians, this is said improperly, for they are simply caustics. They call these "putrefactive" and those "caustic" because caustics first create a crust and what is called an eschar <note>A dry scab or slough formed by burning or corrosive applications.</note>, while the so-called putrefactives first induce a blister, as if the natural heat were evaporating in them just as in things that rot. 

But in truth, those things called putrefactive—such as Arsenic, Orpiment <note>Arsenic trisulfide, a yellow mineral pigment.</note>, the Pine Caterpillar <note>original: "Pythiocampe"; the larvae were used in ancient medicine but were known to be highly irritating.</note>, and Spanish Fly <note>original: "Cantarides"</note>—are also properly caustics. Furthermore, they do not putrefy, but burn. That which evaporates from them cannot be a seed-bed of contagion, insofar as it is dry. Therefore, those poisons which are hot and burning are not contagious for this reason; and those which are cold and numbing are also not accustomed to putrefy, and thus these do not bring about contagion either. This, then, is how contagious things differ from poisons.

### On other differences of contagion. Chapter 12.

It remains now for us to follow up on other differences and their causes; for not all contagions behave in one single way. Some first arise within us and are generated in someone, and soon pass from that person into another; others first come from the outside, and once established, are propagated from one person to another. Some wander over the surface and scarcely pluck at the skin; others occupy more solid parts; others the internal parts; and others all parts, both internal and external. Furthermore, some cling and affect us most promptly, some more slowly; and some manifest themselves immediately, others not until long after. Some, in addition to this, are lethal, while others carry no danger to life. However, all those born in a living body can disseminate infection, but in a dead body, they cannot. And of bodies, some receive them most easily, others either never or with difficulty. From this, it may also be asked whether we can become accustomed to pestilences, just as we do to poisons.

It is manifest, therefore, that the <term>seeds of contagion</term> <note>original: "seminaria"; Fracastoro's famous concept of "seeds" or "germs" of disease</note> first arise in us, not only in the case of <term>scabies</term> <note>a contagious skin disease caused by mites</note>, <term>achores</term> <note>scabby eruptions on the head</note>, and <term>phthisis</term> <note>tuberculosis or wasting of the lungs</note>, but even in the fevers which are called pestilential. The cause is that nothing prevents putrefactions from occurring in us and in our <term>humors</term> <note>the four bodily fluids—blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile—believed to govern health</note> which are both foul and enclosed, from which those seeds are made, being of a mixture that is sometimes sluggish and sometimes strong. It has been said above that these bring about contagions, and no one doubts that it can happen this way in a first person. 

That which might perhaps be doubtful is whether in a second person who is affected, a similarly foul and enclosed putrefaction occurs, or not. For if it does not occur, how are new seeds made in that person which can infect a third? But if it does occur, by what is it made, since the same causes are not present in the second person as were in the first? In the first person, the causes were obstructions, a fullness and badness of the humors, and similar things. In the second person, however, it is not necessary for any of these to be present, since we see someone who is temperate and perfectly healthy nevertheless conceive a contagion from another, the seeds alone being sufficient to bring it in. 

It must be said, therefore, that in the second person a foul and enclosed putrefaction also occurs—foul indeed, because that which receives the putrefaction from the seeds is deeply corrupted on account of their great adherence, action, and <term>analogy</term> <note>the principle that "like produces like" in the spread of infection</note>. Such a thing is also "enclosed" because of the great evaporation that takes place; and even if it were not such by itself, it matters nothing as long as it happens deeply. For in the second person, the principle and seed were entirely such as they were in the first, since it has been said that there is a power in the seeds to propagate and beget their own likeness, just as <term>spirits</term> <note>the "vital spirits" or subtle substances believed to animate the body</note> do. In the first person, therefore, the causes were those which commonly tend to produce putrefactions in us.

There are other fevers which are in a way midway between truly pestilential and non-pestilential, since many indeed perish from them, but many also escape. ### On the Causes. Chapter 7.

These matters were indeed out in the open and before our very eyes. However, regarding the causes and how these things came about, there was great disagreement and much debate among physicians, with different men holding different opinions. Some placed the cause in "adustion" <note>original: "adustionem"; a medical term for the "scorching" or parching of bodily humors due to excessive heat</note> and sharpness of the fluids; others in "fullness" <note>original: "plenitudinem" or plethora; an excess of blood or humors</note>; others in the lack of perspiration from the body; and others still in some hidden property. For this reason, each man asserted remedies according to his own opinion, not without bringing mockery upon the medical art, as such great dissension could not be hidden from the common people.

Since these fevers were wandering and common to many at one time, it must be judged that they also had a common cause, contracted chiefly from the air, which appears reasonable enough. This was especially true in the year 1528, following a rainy winter with southern winds, when in the spring many rivers flooded—among us, specifically the Adige <note>original: "Athesis"</note> and the Po <note>original: "Padus"</note>, and among others, different rivers. There were likewise certain mists, by which a great many buds of trees, especially olives, withered away. It is believed that the beginning of these fevers was given by these causes.

Those cases which now appear scattered have a particular cause within us. In those whom they first affect, they acquire a similar putrefaction to that which first drew its common origin for many from the air. Since this contagion appears mild enough in comparison to truly pestilential ones, and is not usually contracted quickly, nor leaves behind "tinder" <note>original: "fomitem"; refers to infectious material or "fomites" that linger on clothes or surfaces</note>, nor extends to a distance, but spreads slowly and through touch alone, it must be judged that its "seeds" <note>original: "seminaria"; the author's term for the seeds of contagion</note> are not particularly sharp. In short, they are not far removed from the nature of vapors, yet are sharp enough to bring about a contagion of this kind.

Therefore, it must be judged that a similar infection was first in the air, which, once taken into us, brought this type of fever. Although these are not true plagues, they seem to be on the very threshold of them.
