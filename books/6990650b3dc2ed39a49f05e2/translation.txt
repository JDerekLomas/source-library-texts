*...in the Arabic language <note>original: "lingua Arabica"</note>, and in those very buildings to which it owes the fact that it is no longer heard as something utterly foreign and barbaric, even at Oxford <note>original: "Oxonii"</note>. Behold, then, prostrate at your knees, a philosopher who is indeed a Pagan <note>original: "Ethnicum"; often used to mean a Gentile or non-Christian</note>, but of good character; and regarding his religion, there is perhaps nothing for which anyone might find fault, except for that unfortunate sin of misfortune, that he did not happen to be born among Christians.*

# BY EDWARD POCOCKE, S.T.D. <note>Doctor of Sacred Theology</note>

## *Preface to the READER by the Professor of the Hebrew and Arabic Languages in the Academy of Oxford.*

Since this Book has been translated from Arabic into the Latin language <note>original: "in linguam Latinam ex Arabica"</note> at my persuasion and encouragement, and is now published in both languages together, it seemed appropriate to preface a few words regarding both the Author and the work, so that the reason for this plan might be made clear. The name of the Author which our Arabic manuscript <term>Codex; a handwritten book, particularly an ancient or medieval one</term> displays is Abu Jaafar Ibn Tufail <note>original: "Abu Jaafar Ebn Tophail"</note>. He is, however, designated as Abu Bakr <note>original: "Abu Becr"</note> both in the Hebrew version and in the Commentary of Moses of Narbonne <note>original: "Mosis Narbonensis"</note>, as well as by Mordecai Comtino <note>original: "Mardochæum Comtino"</note>, a Jew who praises him more than once.

But this is not at all surprising, since those who have taken their surnames from their sons <note>This refers to the Arabic naming convention called the *kunya*, where a man is called "Father of [his first son]"</note> not infrequently enjoy two or even more names for this reason. Thus Muhammad <note>original: "Mohanimedes"</note> himself, who is commonly called Abu’l-Qasim <note>original: "Abulkaſem"</note>—according to the custom of the Arabs who generally add the name of the firstborn to the father's titles—that is, Father of Qasim, was also addressed at other times as Abu Ibrahim <note>original: "Abu Abrahim"</note>, that is, Father of Abraham, from the name of another son, as witnessed by Al-Jannabi where he discusses his names. So too the fifth Caliph <term>Khalifa; the civil and religious leader of a Muslim state</term> of the Abbasids, namely Harun al-Rashid <note>original: "AlRaſhid Harun"</note>, was called both Abu Jaafar and Abu Muhammad; the sixth, Al-Amin, was called Abu Abdallah and Abu Musa, and even Abu’l-Abbas, as reported by...

# PREFACE.

It seems beyond doubt that our Abu Ja’far or Abu Bakr <note>The author, Ibn Tufayl, was known by these kunyas or nicknames</note> lived at the same time as Averroes <note>original: "Averroe"; the Latinized name of Ibn Rushd, a famous Andalusian philosopher</note>, who is said to have died at a very advanced age in the 595th year of the Hijra <note>The Islamic calendar; 595 AH corresponds to 1198 AD</note> (which falls in the year 1198 AD), and thus he flourished more or less five hundred years ago. From the same evidence, one may gather that he also wrote other works; however, since these have not come into our hands, our discussion must be confined only to the present book. This work bears the title, *The Epistle* (for the Arabs and Jews love to call even the shortest treatises by this name) *of Hayy ibn Yaqdhan* <note>The name means "Alive, son of Awake"</note>, *and of Asal* (for so it is consistently read elsewhere, although at the end of the Preface in the manuscript copy it is read as 
<margin>* And so it seems Ibn Khallikan reads</margin> *Absal) by the error, I believe, of the scribe, since in the Hebrew version and the commentary of Moses of Narbonne he is always called Asal, as he is by Mordecai Comtino, and in our own copy, Asal), and *of Salaman*. These names, he says, were bestowed by Abu Ali Avicenna <note>original: "Abu Ali Avicennam"; the Latinized name of Ibn Sina</note> [or Ibn Sina]. Where exactly he did so, he does not indicate. <margin>* see also Ibn Khallikan</margin> since among his writings in the same *History* a book is listed with the title *Hayy ibn Yaqdhan*.
