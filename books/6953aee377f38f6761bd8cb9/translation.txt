# INTRODUCTION.

---

It appears to me that there are two descriptions of persons by whom the present work must be considered to be of inestimable worth: the lovers of antiquity and the lovers of ancient philosophy and religion. To the former of these it must be invaluable, because it is replete with information derived from the wise men of the Chaldeans <note>Ancient inhabitants of Mesopotamia known for their astronomy and astrology</note>, the prophets of the Egyptians, the dogmas of the Assyrians, and the ancient pillars of Hermes <note>Refers to the legendary "Emerald Tablet" or pillars upon which Hermes Trismegistus was said to have inscribed all knowledge</note>. To the latter, it is invaluable because of the doctrines contained in it. Some of these originated from the Hermaic pillars and were known by Pythagoras and Plato, serving as the sources of their philosophy. Others are profoundly theological and unfold the mysteries of ancient religion with an admirable conciseness of diction, and an inimitable vigor and elegance of conception. To

To this may also be added, as the finishing touch <note>original: "colophon of excellence"</note> of its quality, that it is the most comprehensive, the clearest, and the most satisfactory defense in existence of genuine ancient theology.

This theology—the sacred operations of which, called *theurgy* <term>theurgy: a ritual practice intended to invite the presence or action of divine beings</term>, are developed here—has for the most part been viewed only in its corrupted forms since its destruction. It has been seen among uncivilized nations or during the decline and fall of the Roman Empire, with which it gradually fell while overwhelmed with pollution. At length, it totally vanished from what is called the civilized part of the world. This will be evident to the intelligent reader from the following remarks. These remarks are a summary of what I have discussed more extensively elsewhere on this subject; they also demonstrate that the religion of the Chaldeans, Egyptians, and Greeks is no less scientific than it is sublime.

In the first place, this theology celebrates the immense principle of all things as something superior even to existence <note>original: "being"</note> itself.
