# PREFATORY MEMOIR.

---

The materials available for describing the life of Jacob Boehme <note>original: "Jacob Behmen"</note> are quite limited. They consist for the most part of a few surprising incidents, some of which would be considered legendary or unbelievable if they were not so well-documented and narrated by Boehme himself. These incidents, more or less rephrased, have served as the basis for biographies by successive editors of his works.

Jacob Boehme, though a shoemaker by trade, was a very remarkable man and became the greatest theosopher <note>A philosopher who seeks direct knowledge of God and the world through spiritual ecstasy or divine illumination.</note> who has ever lived. His writings cannot be understood without considerable effort and a spiritual sympathy with his ideas; consequently, they have seemed like foolishness to many, and only a few have discovered what has been called the "Pearl" hidden within them.

Regarding Jacob Boehme’s birth and parentage, it is known that he was born in the year 1575 at Alt-Seidenberg (Old Seidenberg), a small market town in Northern Germany. It is now situated on the border of the Prussian frontier and within two miles of the principal town of Görlitz, which at that time was a place of much importance and remains a significant business center today. He was named after his father, and his mother’s name was Ursula. He was not their only child, although neither the names of the other children nor any mention of them appears to be recorded, aside from a reference to a "dead brother." As a boy, he was employed with other lads to tend cattle and perform similar chores that could be found for him near home. Even at that age, he appears to have been of a pensive and dreamy disposition, much inclined to reverie. He did not have a robust constitution, a trait that continued even into his manhood.

One of the earliest recorded incidents of Jacob’s life occurred while he was herding cattle. It is told that one day at noon, having left his companions to look after the herd, he went to a nearby hill called the Land’s Crown. There, a rock formation bore some resemblance to a cave. Finding an opening through the brushwood, he entered and saw a large wooden vessel filled with money. The sight so alarmed him that he ran back to his companions and told them what happened; but although they returned with him immediately, both the entrance and the fairy-like money had disappeared. As he himself narrates, a "foreign artist" found and appropriated the hidden treasure; and, in consequence, came to an untimely end.

The place of this strange occurrence is still known, and is one of the excursions from *Goerlitz*. It is called the "Landskrone," and is in reality a basaltic hill, on the summit of which an ancient robbers' stronghold formerly stood—which may to some extent account for the hidden treasure—and is now occupied by a castle-like building used as an inn and a "Belvedere" <note>A building designed to provide a beautiful view.</note>, from which an expansive view of the surrounding country is obtained.

As *Jacob* grew up and completed his elementary schooling, his father, not finding him sufficiently strong for agricultural work, apprenticed him to a shoemaker at *Goerlitz*. After he had worked there for some time, another singular incident occurred to him. We are told that while his master had temporarily left him in charge of the shop, a man, who was a stranger to *Jacob*, entered and desired to purchase a pair of shoes. *Jacob*, not venturing to sell anything in his master’s absence, declined to serve him; but the stranger becoming persistent about it, *Jacob* offered them to him at such a high price as would certainly protect him from his master’s blame and would probably discourage the stranger from purchasing. The stranger, however, paid the price demanded and took the shoes. Having left the shop, he called to the lad, "*Jacob*, come outside," or, as some of his biographers more dramatically render it, "*Jacob, Jacob*, come forth!" 

The lad did so as soon as he had recovered from his astonishment, and the stranger addressed him: "*Jacob*, you are as yet but small, but the time will come when you shall be great and become another man, and the world shall marvel at you. Therefore, be pious, fear God, and reverence His Word; especially read the Bible diligently where you have comfort and instruction, for you must endure much misery and poverty, and suffer persecution. But be courageous and persevere, for God loves and is gracious to you." The stranger then pressed his hand, gave him a kind look, and departed—with the shoes.

It is questionable whether these incidents were visionary experiences rather than actual occurrences to JACOB BOEHME; but the result of them was that his thoughts assumed a deeply religious form—far deeper than that of the mere "Lutheran Faith" in which he had been brought up. This operated upon him so strongly in the years to come that his spirit was able to penetrate into the innermost mysteries of nature: to foresee and foretell what is now known as electricity, with its immense possibilities; and also to divine the three great principles or first laws of nature—attraction, equal resistance producing orbicular <note>original: "orbicular" — meaning circular or spherical motion.</note>...

By reducing these ideas to a mathematical form, Newton established that grand theory of the universe—the Solar System—which Thomas Carlyle described as the most perfect approach to a theory that has ever been proposed.

Meanwhile, however, *Jacob* continued his occupation as a shoemaker, although we are told that at times he worked almost unconsciously for days at a time when his thoughts were in devout contemplation. At other times, he did not hesitate to rebuke any coarse conversation in which the other workmen might indulge. This so irritated his master that he discharged him from his service, and *Jacob* was obliged to set out as a traveling journeyman <note>A "journeyman" was a trained worker who practiced his trade by traveling and working for different masters rather than owning his own shop.</note>. In the year 1594, he returned to *Goerlitz*, where he became a master shoemaker and married *Catherine*, daughter of John Hunshman, a butcher of *Goerlitz*.

About six years after his marriage, *Jacob's* first "Illumination" <note>A spiritual awakening or mystical vision.</note> occurred. This was the result, as he says, of strenuously clinging by faith to the promise of the Holy Spirit given to those who seek it. It happened in the following manner:

We are told that while sitting in his room one day, he noticed a burnished pewter dish which reflected the sunlight with such brilliance that he was overcome with an inward ecstasy. It seemed to him as if he could now look into the principles and deepest foundations of things. He initially believed it was only his imagination, and in order to banish it from his mind, he went out into the fields near the town. But there he noticed that he gazed into the very heart of things, even the grass and herbs. He felt that actual Nature harmonized with what he had seen in his vision, and that he could distinguish the essences, uses, and properties of plants in a manner that was, to him, a most wonderful insight or revelation. He is said to have kept his knowledge of this occurrence mostly to himself; returning home, he continued to take care of his family and worked on in silence until the year 1610. At that time, a further Illumination occurred to him, of which he wrote a memorial.
