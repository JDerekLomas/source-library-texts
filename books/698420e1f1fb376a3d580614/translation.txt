For a Physician, as **Macrobius** writes, foreknows either the benefits or the ailments that will arise in the body: thus, as **Hippocrates** says, it behooves the Physician to speak concerning the sick man of *the present, the past, and the things that are yet to come* <note>original Greek: τὰ τε παρεόντα, καὶ τὰ προγεγονότα, καὶ μέλλοντα ἔσεσθαι</note>, that is, those things which are, those which were, and those which will soon follow: which corresponds to **Divinations**—or as we might interpret it, skillful conjecture. Therefore, he who excels in these two gifts (allowing others to rest for now) shall be heard of through the herald **Homer** as being *worth many other men* <note>original Greek: πολλῶν ἀντάξιος ἄλλων</note>; and by the promise of **Sirach**, *he shall receive glory from the king* <note>original Greek: παρὰ βασιλέως λήψεται δόξαν</note>, or as others read, *a gift* <note>original Greek: δόμα</note>. Truly, histories testify that the more civilized Nations held the guardians of **Health** in distinguished honor. Thus, as **Suetonius** records, **Gaius Julius Caesar** granted **Citizenship** to all who practiced Medicine in Rome; and **Augustus**, having been delivered from a life-threatening crisis by the skill and aid of **Antonius Musa**, besides a more generous reward, granted the right to wear gold rings <note>The "jus annulorum aureorum" was a mark of high social status, effectively elevating the recipient to the equestrian rank.</note> both to him and to his present fellow-practitioners <note>original Greek: ὁμοφύλοις (*homophylois*)</note>, as well as to all future ones, according to the account of **Caelius Rhodiginus**. # PREFACE TO THE BENEVO-
## LENT READER.

That saying of *Solon* is well known: "I grow old always learning many things" <note>original Greek: *γηράσκω δ' αἰεὶ πολλὰ διδασκόμενος*; original Latin: *senesco semper multa addiscens*. Solon was one of the Seven Sages of Greece.</note>. This is what *Plato* repeats in his *Dialogue on Philosophy* and applies to the study of Wisdom: what else, he asks, is *Philosophy* than doing what *Solon* said? For he said that "for the one who learns constantly, old age arrives with many things." We say the same thing, by right and merit, concerning true Physicians, whose Art *Hippocrates* solemnly pronounced to be long <note>A reference to the famous aphorism *Ars longa, vita brevis* (Art is long, life is short), suggesting that the medical craft is too vast to master in one lifetime.</note>, so that they never fail to have something they can learn, even up to extreme old age. For although we do not deny that other disciplines possess their own difficulties—since our mind, for the purpose of knowing things, is (according to <term>the Philosopher</term><note>Referring to Aristotle.</note>) like a <term>blank slate</term><note>original: *tabula rasa*; the philosophical idea that the mind is born without innate ideas.</note> upon which nothing is actually written, yet all things can be inscribed upon it, though not without labor and continuous practice—this is nonetheless most especially verified in the Medical Art. Granted indeed, Medicine is not called a <term>Science</term> in the strict sense, but an <term>Art</term> <note>In the 17th century, a "science" referred to theoretical, certain knowledge, while an "art" referred to a practical skill or application involving judgment.</note>, because it has a Subject of Operation rather than a purely scientific one; yet it is not called the Most Noble of all Arts without reason. Hence its knowledge extends most widely and requires the most perfect investigation of many things. Whether you consider the *Subject of Operation*—namely Man, whose present health must be defended and whose absent health must be restored—or the *means through which* this must be accomplished, a wide-open field of contemplation presents itself everywhere. The *Subject of Operation* is that matter in which health is to be defended or restored, specifically Man himself; if one lacks the most perfect knowledge of him, surely he must frequently deceive others and be deceived himself. The *means through which* health is preserved and defended designates all those things which, by whatever reasoning, are designed to defend and preserve our body in its state according to nature, as well as to alter, correct, and reduce it back to suitable health when it is in a state contrary to nature.

And indeed, by Hercules, how great those things are! For one must track down the most hidden powers of herbs, examine the qualities of plants, investigate the nature of four-footed animals, birds, and fish, and unlock the faculties of metals and minerals—
