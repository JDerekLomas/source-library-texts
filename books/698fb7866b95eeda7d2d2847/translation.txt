How much of what is recognized through sensory perception remains foreign to the development of mathematical thought! Volcanic activity does not merely act in a transformative and destructive manner; it is also formative in that it produces solid rock. We have attempted to describe these formative processes in the fourth volume of *Cosmos* and have depicted the mostly crystalline rock types—produced by the solidification of liquid earths—categorized into specific mineral groups according to their composition (according to the association of their constituent parts). These volcanic formations of solid matter, breaking forth sometimes on the slopes of high conical mountains in narrow lava streams, or in earlier times without any permanent structures as widespread rock layers from the network of fissures in the plains, are occasionally interrupted by outpourings of water. Such water outpourings deserve special attention all the more because the diversity of their causes has long been misunderstood, and they partly belong, as I have previously mentioned, to purely meteorological phenomena.

Remarkable mud eruptions, which are claimed to be mixed with actual fragments of iron pyrites <note>original: "Schwefelkies"; a common mineral also known as fool's gold</note>, are also produced by the small volcanoes of the islands of Ramri and Cheduba (the latter at latitude 18° 52') on the coast of Arracan <note>modern Rakhine State, Myanmar</note>, in the eastern part of the Bay of Bengal. 39 The mud, which the Geological Society of Calcutta sent to Ehrenberg <note>Christian Gottfried Ehrenberg (1795–1876), a famous naturalist and pioneer in microscopy</note> in 1846 for examination, had the consistency of a silver-gray, greasy, plastic clay; it contained polythalamia <note>term for multi-chambered microscopic organisms</note>, phytolitharia <note>microscopic plant silica particles</note>, and predominantly (as in the Patagonian coastal mountain layers) lime-shelled seawater organisms: thus again suggesting a connection between volcanic activity and the once-living forms of the foraminifera <note>single-celled organisms with shells</note>. 40

Most granite deposits, says Carl Friedrich Naumann in his classic textbook of *Geognosy* <note>An early term for geology, specifically the study of the Earth's structure and the materials that compose it.</note> 46, are evidently of more recent origin than the Silurian and Devonian formations.
