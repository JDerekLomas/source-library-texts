All these perfections compose and beautify this panegyric; therefore, if this Author compares the wisdom of Raymond to the image of Minerva <note>The Roman goddess of wisdom and strategic warfare</note> painted by the great artist Amulius <note>A Roman painter mentioned by Pliny the Elder, known for his serious and demanding style; he famously only painted for a few hours a day and always wore a toga while working</note>, I must also compare this learned oration to that same image. The rarity of that painting (as this Author notes, following Pliny) consisted in the fact that anyone, from any side, even the opposite, who looked at it, seemed to be the only one receiving the Goddess’s attention. She looked in all directions at once, so that however many people looked at her attentively, there were just as many who seemed to be the only ones favored by her eyes: *looking at the one who looks, from wherever she was looked at.* <note>original: "Spectantem aspectans, quacumque aspiceretur"</note>

I do not intend, nor would it be appropriate, to make the comparison as extensive as the Author himself; but speaking of the sciences that are compatible with his status, I must say briefly that if a Philosopher, a Rhetorician, or a Theologian attends to this oration, it will seem to him not only that he is the only one addressed by it, but also that it speaks to him alone.

# On the Movement of the Heart and Blood

->Observations on the Living Pulse<-

The heart is the foundation of life, the **beginning of all things** <note>original: "principium omnium"</note>. It is situated within the chest, protected by the ribs as if by a cage. We observe through dissection and careful study that when the heart contracts, it sends the blood throughout the body in a constant circuit. This movement is not merely for the distribution of heat, but for the very maintenance of the spirit.

The **vital spirit** <note>original: "pneuma"</note> is carried by the arteries to every limb. It is through this <term>Refining Process</term> <note>A historical medical concept where the heart was believed to "cook" or refine blood into a higher form of energy called spirits.</note> that the body remains warm and capable of motion.

As it is written in the ancient scrolls, **Life is in the blood** <note>original: "Sanguis est vita"</note>. <gloss>The blood serves as the oil that feeds the lamp of the body</gloss> Therefore, any significant loss of this precious fluid leads to a rapid weakening of the whole man, as the "lamp" begins to flicker and fade. 

We must also consider the role of the lungs, which the Greeks called *the breathers* <note>original: "pneumones"</note>, for they provide the air necessary for the heart to stay cool. Without this cooling, the heart would surely consume itself in its own heat.
