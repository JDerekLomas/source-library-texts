# PREFACE.

<note>original: "ἀΰλους καὶ νοερὰς, ἢ τὰς ψύχας καὶ αἰσθητὰς φύσεις"</note> immaterial and intellectual beings, or souls and sensible natures. But perhaps these matters are too philosophical: and such <term>sympathies</term> <note>The author refers to the concept of "sympathia," a natural or spiritual connection between things.</note> <note>original: "συμπάθειαι"</note> are to be attributed to God rather than to nature.

I certainly, who never saw Valerius Cordus in person, nor had any connection to him by natural or civil occasion, have been moved by a certain hidden <note>original: "Αὐτόματοι δ' ἀγαθοὶ ἀγαθῶν πρὸς ἔρωτας ἴενται"</note> (Good men go of their own accord to the feasts of the good) <note>A Greek proverb suggesting that kindred spirits find one another.</note> of souls and a wonderful agreement of studies. Having been stirred by love for his departed spirit, I proposed to myself to seek out by all means whatever he committed to writing, to adorn them—both in other ways and by adding illustrations of plants—and finally to publish and defend them through myself and other excellent and most learned patrons, among whom you hold the chief place. I hope that I have achieved this in great part: and for that reason, I give thanks to the immortal God, and I pray that your Excellencies will forgive me if I have acted too boldly. Indeed, for this boldness, Hieronymus Herold of Leipzig, a distinguished physician of Nuremberg in our age, was my advisor. By his counsel, just as my love for Cordus was inclined, so my respect toward you easily subscribed. Moreover, I do not at all doubt that you will undertake the patronage of Cordus and his books with a cheerful and ready mind, so much so that I think it superfluous to exhort you with many words. I would not indeed say that all his opinions must be defended, as we are but human; but rather those which seem consistent with the truth, as most of them are: and I defend the erudition, industry, and efforts of this most excellent youth in general, and finally his fame and glory, which he most amply deserved.

Certainly, some of the more recent writers have profited from the discoveries and writings of Cordus—partly published, partly unpublished—and have adorned their own writings with them, having confessed this with no honesty. This pained me not a little, as I believe praise should be attributed to each person for their own work; and this was no small reason for me to think about the publication of his works. Recently, a most learned friend wrote to me regarding these books of Cordus in these words:

> "I would wish you to excuse Valerius Cordus himself in the preface of his works, because the final hand was not applied to them. For having set out for Italy, he was taken by a burning fever at Rome; although he had observed very many plants throughout Italy, and had corrected many things in his *Annotations on the Books of Dioscorides* <note>Dioscorides was the primary ancient authority on medicinal plants.</note>: all of which were stolen by certain students of medicine. Indeed, Cornelius Sittard, a physician of pious memory and his associate and companion on his journey and even in his illness (for he had escaped from the illness through a quartan fever), affirmed to me that Cordus had changed his opinion on many matters."

So he said: who also indicated that in the previous year, Valerius's brother, a physician at Hildesheim and most skilled in the chemical art and the remedies prepared by that art for medicine, had met his end. Certainly, the writings of Cordus, however imperfect, seemed to me worthy to see the light of public day and to pass on to posterity, just as Virgil's *Aeneid* once did to Augustus. For even the unfinished works of skilled craftsmen are deservedly preferred to the works of the unskilled upon which they have placed the final hand. And although innumerable things concerning <term>materia medica</term> <note>The study of the medicinal properties of natural substances.</note> have been discovered from the death of Cordus until this day—which had been hidden from him, as many learned men everywhere have applied themselves to this most pleasant study with great rivalry—nevertheless, his highest praise is this: that among the first investigators of these things in our century, he discovered very many things, entirely following his father's...

# PREFACE
<sig>a iij</sig>

...he is the heir to both his father’s learning and his glory. For, if I am any judge, his father Euricius <note>Euricius Cordus (1486–1535), a famous German humanist, physician, and botanist.</note>, a man most cultivated in every branch of learning, a most skilled physician, and an excellent poet, had no equal in the scholarly knowledge of plants <note>original: "Stirpium"</note> while he lived. Yet the son, emulating his father’s zeal and diligence in the study of medicinal substances <term>materia medica: the study of the therapeutic properties of natural substances</term>, even surpassed him. He was spurred on, aided, and advanced toward this highest praise by his father’s example, by his own youthful fervor, by the dexterity of his genius, by a desire for honest glory, by many long-distance travels, and by the greatest labor and industry. Finally, with the singular grace of God leading the way and sustaining all other causes, he has earned the right to be counted and celebrated by all posterity among the first, the leaders, and the most distinguished authors and champions of the knowledge of plants and the entire field of medicinal substances—a knowledge recalled from ancient times, adorned, and expanded. No good or learned man ought to, or could, envy him this glory. Indeed, if it had pleased God to leave him longer in this life, no one among all those who excel today in the knowledge of botany (if I may say so without offense to them) would snatch this palm of victory from him, any more than one could wring the club from the hand of Hercules.

Therefore, most distinguished and learned men, receive with a serene brow and a favorable countenance these books of so great a student of your Academy—prepared and edited by me so that they might not perish or be suppressed by ambitious men lying in wait for another’s glory. Defend them, as far as is possible and right, now that they have been taken into your protection.

-> And love me in return, who am most devoted and respectful toward you. <-
-> Farewell. Zurich <note>original: "Tiguri"</note>. May 30th, <-
-> in the year 1561. <-
