# Preface to Elegant Musings from the Five Hundred Dan Cave-Heaven

# Preface

*Elegant Musings from the Five Hundred Dan Cave-Heaven* is a work recording the conversations of the <term>Xiaolian</term> <note>original: 孝廉 (xiàolián). A title for a candidate who passed the provincial level civil service examinations; literally "Filial and Incorruptible."</note> Shuyuan. During his years of <unclear>living in reclusion</unclear> and <unclear>resting in the valleys</unclear>, the Scholar has used his blossoming talent to speak. Having just completed his official evaluation in the capital, he now dwells in his cottage with much leisure. He is in a state of gentle composure with no immediate official task; thus, he has written books "as tall as his own body" to present to the world. Even before the first pages are <term>carved into pear wood</term> <note>original: 鐫梨 (juānlí). Refers to the traditional woodblock printing process where pear wood was the preferred material for carving text.</note>, the next draft is already finished. His vigor in composition is rarely matched in this age. Those who read his books might assume they are the works of a seasoned elder, not realizing he is actually a dashing and elegant young man. His talent and learning are truly difficult to reach, but his youth makes his achievements even more unattainable.

The Scholar possesses a collection of five hundred stones, reminiscent of the style of <term>Old Mi</term> <note>original: 米老 (Mǐ Lǎo). Refers to Mi Fu, a famous Song dynasty painter and calligrapher known for his obsession with collecting unusual rocks.</note>. As for the term "Cave-Heaven," the concept originates from Taoist scriptures. It borrows the idea of a <term>dwelling of the spirits</term> <note>original: 洞天 (dòngtiān). A "Grotto-Heaven" or sacred terrestrial paradise in Taoism.</note> to serve as a title for a literary collection. Such usage has a long history, appearing in the *Biographies of the Immortals*; the Song dynasty work *Record of the Cave-Heaven for Clearing Summer Heat* is a prime example of this tradition. The Scholar’s previous work, *Superfluous Talk from Shuyuan*, has already spread in popularity both at home and abroad. To call a work "Superfluous Talk" is to use plain language; to call it "Waving the Whisk" <note>original: 揮塵 (huīzhǔ). "Waving the deer-tail whisk" is a classical idiom for "Pure Conversation" (Qingtan), referring to the refined, philosophical debates of scholars in the 3rd and 4th centuries.</note> is to use refined language, yet both are works that record his conversations. This is because, in ancient times, those who engaged in such sophisticated talk would invariably wave a whisk.

His book takes the discussion of poetry as its primary focus. However, beyond expressing his inner spirit and character, his attention to social customs and moral transformation is something that all mindful people in the world will surely recognize. Alas! Does Shuyuan merely use his writings to express his youthful ambition? How could he be indifferent to the matters of <term>statecraft</term> <note>original: 經世 (jīngshì). Practical governing and the ordering of society.</note>? Nevertheless, regarding the "whisk" [this world of dust], I privately have some thoughts on the changes of the age...

# Preface (Continued)

I have reflected on why those engaged in refined conversation feel they must brandish an <term>elk-tail whisk</term> <note>original: 麈 (zhǔ). The tail of a large deer or elk, used to make whisks that became symbols of authority and intellectual elegance.</note>. Before the Han dynasty, this practice was rarely heard of. Once the moral character of the Western Capital <note>referring to Chang'an, the capital of the Western Han</note> declined, the fashion for <term>Pure Conversation</term> <note>original: 清談 (qīngtán). A philosophical, witty, and highly stylized form of discourse popular among the elite in the 3rd–6th centuries.</note> in the Eastern Capital <note>referring to Luoyang</note> began to flourish, and thus the elk-tail whisk appeared.

Along with the <term>armrest</term> <note>original: 隱囊 (yǐnnáng). A soft pillow or bag used to lean on while sitting on the floor.</note> and the square cushion, the world regarded these as the hallmark possessions of the <term>Wang and Xie families</term> <note>original: 王謝 (Wáng-Xiè). The two most prestigious aristocratic clans of the Eastern Jin period, epitomizing cultural refinement.</note>. At that time, the use of the whisk was highly prominent. Those serving as high officials and scholars grasped jade handles and styled themselves as "Masters of Conversation." Even the fashionable youths in their fine robes followed this trend like the wind. 

Conversely, the actions of men like <term>Tao Kan</term> moving his bricks or <term>Zu Ti</term> beating his oar <note>original: 陶侃之甓祖逖之楫. Tao Kan moved 100 bricks every morning to keep fit for service; Zu Ti struck the water with an oar while swearing to reclaim northern China from invaders. Both were men of action rather than talk.</note>, though born from the resolve of noble patriots, were viewed by the people of that time as "vulgar" or "crude." 

Ever since the scions of the Sima family became emperors, the <term>Five Barbarians</term> <note>original: 五胡 (Wǔhú). Non-Han nomadic tribes who established kingdoms in northern China.</note> rose in succession and split the Central Plains. This period of turmoil lasted for over a hundred years, passing through the Jin, Song, Qi, Liang, and Chen dynasties until ending with the Sui. This entire era was the time when the men of the Southern Dynasties brandished their whisks.

The people of the Tang dynasty rose up, and tribes from the north and south came to pay court. Their martial achievements nearly surpassed those of the Han. When their great work was finished, they composed music—how magnificent and refined! Most of the meritorious officials of the <term>Lingyan Pavilion</term> <note>original: 凌烟 (Língyān). A gallery commissioned by Emperor Taizong to commemorate 24 great contributors to the Tang Empire.</note> were brave warriors and fierce generals; they were better suited to long spears and great halberds. That they could not use a whisk is no cause for surprise. 

As for the <term>Scholars of Yingzhou</term> <note>original: 瀛洲學士 (Yíngzhōu xuéshì). A group of eighteen elite scholars recruited by the early Tang court.</note>, they were all of the upright and elegant sort, yet when we search the records today and examine the paintings of the period, we also find few instances of them using whisks. Perhaps because the Tang was at its peak of strength, the elk-tail whisk fell into decline. The tradition of the <term>fly-whisk</term> <note>original: 拂子 (fúzi). A similar-looking object, but associated with religious rituals rather than secular intellectual debate.</note> only evolved into separate branches within the Chan Buddhist and Taoist sects. 

Yet, diligent scholars and those enamored with antiquity still spoke of the elk-tail whisk constantly, their praise never fading. By the time of the Song dynasty, people valued not the physical object, but rather the "name" and reputation of the whisk. Why was this so? It was when the virtue of the Song...

Referring to the intellectual and political rivalry between the followers of Su Shi (Shu) and the Cheng brothers (Luo).</note> clashed recklessly; it was then that the *Records of the Whisk* <note>original: 麈史 (Zhǔ Shǐ). By the time the Jin invaders entered the capital at Bian and the "mud horse crossed the river" <note>original: 泥馬渡江. It was at this moment that the *Waving the Whisk: Front and Back Records* <note>original: 揮麈前後錄 (Huīzhǔ Qiánhòu Lù). Thereupon, the traitor [Qin] Hui controlled the state. Today they discussed peace; tomorrow they ceded land. The barbarian threat in the Central Plains lasted for more than a hundred and seventy years before it finally ceased.

Alas! How strange it is! As for the affairs of the world, I am but a foolish man and dare not claim to know them. Yet now, Shuyuan’s book is specifically titled *Whisk Waving*. For this reason, I cannot help but be deeply moved.

Shuyuan’s years are still in their prime, yet he has already set his heart upon <term>statecraft</term> <note>original: 經世 (jīngshì). The practical application of Confucian learning to solve the administrative and social problems of the day.</note>. In the future, he will surely bring forth his grand talent and vast learning. Within the ranks of the imperial entourage <note>original: 屬車豹尾 (shǔchē bàowěi). Literally "attendant carriages and leopard tails," symbols of the Emperor's procession.</note>, he will carry his brush and await consultation, polishing the great imperial works—a task that belongs to no one else but him. As for holding the military banner to govern a region, or presiding over the sacrificial vessels to forge alliances, his strategies for navigating crises will surely be of a caliber rarely seen in any age. I see the evidence of this potential in the courage he displays in writing this book.

I previously wrote a preface for his *Redundant Talks*. Now that this book is complete, Shuyuan has again requested my contribution. Therefore, I have taken my feelings of deep admiration for Shuyuan and expressed them here; my own subtle purpose in observing the world and being moved by the times is also recorded in this piece.

# Preface

The mountains, rivers, and wilderness of the Central Lands do not inherently possess poetry; the stony wastes and marshes do not inherently possess poetry. Rather, it is the vast, surging vital energy <note>original: 氣 (qì). The life force or breath that animates the world in Chinese philosophy.</note> that stretches over ten thousand miles across the Central Plains—coiling like a dragon, overflowing with wild power—that must eventually find an outlet. This is what Heaven has designed and Earth has completed, and it is the pent-up energy entrusted to extraordinary men of talent to be released as songs and poems. This is what is meant by "surpassing the eight horizons and being as vigorous as a sudden wind."

Though the axis of the Central Plains is vast, ever since the Jin Dynasty produced the two Xies <note>original: 二謝 (Èr Xiè). Referring to the famous nature poets Xie Lingyun and Xie Xuanhui, who defined the landscape poetry of their eras.</note> and the Tang Dynasty produced Li [Bai], Liu [Zongyuan], Meng [Jiao], and Han [Yu], the essence of beauty and splendor has been fully expressed and released. Thus, the inspiration of the mountains and rivers may be exhausted, and the power of brush and ink may also be spent. Could the authors of the Song and Yuan dynasties truly stand as tall as those previous worthies?

Beyond the Southern Seas, there is a large island called Selat <note>original: 息力 (Xīlì). A phonetic transcription for "Selat" (the Straits), a common historical Chinese name for Singapore.</note>, which was the ancient Kingdom of Johor. Its customs were once uncultivated <note>original: 狉獉 (pīzhēn). A term suggesting a primitive or pre-literate state.</note>, and its people wore their hair in mallets <note>original: 椎髻 (chuí jì). A hairstyle associated by Chinese scholars with "unrefined" or non-Han peoples.</note>. Westerners developed the land, but they were solely focused on commerce and trade. Amidst these remote corners and unrefined hinterlands, the scholar <note>original: 孝廉 (xiàolián). A title for those who passed the provincial-level civil service examinations.</note> Qiu Shuyuan <note>original: 邱淑源. Also known as Qiu Shuyuan (邱菽園, 1874–1941), a famous poet, activist, and leader in the Singaporean Chinese community.</note> traveled as a guest. He said to others, "To clear the wilderness and reform the customs—is this not the responsibility of us Confucians?" 

For this reason, he established the Lize Society <note>original: 麗澤社. A literary society founded in Singapore to promote Chinese culture and classical education.</note> to instruct the descendants of merchants who were capable of study. He also wrote dozens of books to clarify the profound mysteries of poetry. In the tradition of the "light carriage collecting folk songs" <note>original: 輶軒采風 (yóuxuān cǎifēng). A reference to the ancient practice where imperial officials collected local poems to gauge the social and moral health of a region.</note>, nothing takes precedence over poetry. For a scholar specializing in the Classics, nothing takes precedence over poetry. By using poetry to find joy in the correctness of one's nature and temperament, one can then proceed to the study of the Book of History, the Rites, and general literature. Through these writings, one realizes that while the poetry of the Central Plains <note>original: 中原 (Zhōngyuán). The cradle of Chinese civilization, referring to the Yellow River Valley.</note> has nearly exhausted its vastness, the poetry of the outer oceans is just now flourishing in its unique creativity.

The <term>Xiaolian</term> <note>original: 孝廉 (Xiàolián). Literally "Filial and Incorruptible," a title for those who passed the provincial-level civil service examinations.</note> and I once discussed poetry. He described it using the qualities of being "winding" and "clear." I reflected on the words of the *Classic of Changes*, the *Book of Documents*, and the *Spring and Autumn Annals*, finding that none are not winding in their depth. Similarly, the words of <term>Quzi</term> <note>Qu Yuan, the famous poet of the Warring States period known for "The Lament."</note>, the Master of <term>Longmen</term> <note>Sima Qian, the "Grand Historian" who wrote the *Records of the Grand Historian*.</note>, and <term>Changqing</term> <note>Sima Xiangru, a master of the *fu* (rhapsody) poetic form during the Han Dynasty.</note> are none if not clear. <term>Zhongni</term> <note>The courtesy name for Confucius.</note> once said: "Language should simply express the meaning." Those whose words truly express their meaning naturally possess a "winding" quality within them.

<term>Liu Xie</term> <note>A 5th-century literary critic, author of *The Literary Mind and the Carving of Dragons*.</note> remarked that the "winding" and the "clear" are qualities to be savored, and that clarity and elegance are the foundations of all literature. Is this not how we should examine the source of an author's inspiration?

Regarding the "Grotto of Five Hundred Stones": the *Xiaolian* crossed the sea and obtained five hundred extraordinary stones. He used this number to name both his estate and this book. This volume does not merely discuss poetry; it holds poetry as its very soul. He has painstakingly polished every chapter and carved every line, pouring his heart into the work. Daily, he greets the spirits of the great masters—the two Xies, Li Bai, Liu Zongyuan, Meng Jiao, and Han Yu—amidst the grotto and the vast, dark sea. There, he fully expresses a spirit that "surpasses the eight horizons and is as vigorous as a sudden wind."

The man who wrote this is surely a magnificent and extraordinary scholar. <term>Selat</term> <note>original: 息力 (Xīlì). A phonetic rendering of "Selat" (Straits), referring to Singapore.</note> is a place I once traveled to. The heavenly winds there are brisk, and the seafaring raft is ready at hand. Floating along, I intend to follow the *Xiaolian*, waving the <term>whisk</term> <note>original: 揮塵 (huīchén). A "chowry" or fly-whisk held by scholars during "pure conversation" (intellectual debate).</note> as we each testify to the spiritual heights we have reached.

# Preface to Wielding the Whisk in the Five Hundred Stones Grotto

<margin>Five Hundred Stones Grotto, Wielding the Whisk, page 7</margin>

When one is exhausted in the countryside, one goes to the market towns; when exhausted in the market towns, one goes to the capital; when exhausted in the capital, one goes overseas. I discarded <term>exam-preparation scripts</term> <note>original: 帖括 (tièguā). # On the Evolution of Poetry

Poetry reached its absolute peak during the **Tang Dynasty**. Every possible path was opened by the Tang poets, and every school of style was established by them. They went so far as to exhaust every form—from poems with lines of one word to those with nine, and even experimental structures consisting entirely of "level" or "oblique" tones. There was nothing they did not overturn or explore. This era was much like the **Hundred Schools of Thought** during the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods, where various academic sects emerged all at once. Having reached such a magnificent height, it has been exceedingly difficult for later generations to continue the tradition.

# Only the Pillow-Silk Knows the Traces of Tears

During the Daoguang era [1821–1850], there was a provincial graduate from Houguan <note>A historic county in Fuzhou, Fujian province.</note> named Wang Tingjia (styled Weifu, also known as the "Qiaoyin Hermit"). Regarding these two, the Venus clam is shaped like a tongue, which is how it earned its name. It is fresh, succulent, and delightful. When cooked and placed in the mouth, if one chews it slowly and carefully, the flavor is exquisite and long-lasting; it is truly a superior accompaniment for wine. 

As for the scallop, it has long enjoyed a refined reputation throughout the land. Previously, I had only encountered the dried variety, which led me to suspect that its fame was merely a <term>recluse's hollow rumor</term> <note>original: "chushi xusheng" (處士虚聲); a literary idiom suggesting a reputation that far exceeds the actual reality</note>. However, upon arriving in Fuzhou and eating them fresh, I found the color and fragrance to be uniquely peerless. I could not stop marveling at my own culinary good fortune. Assessing its quality, one might say it stands as a "younger brother" to the common mussel and a peer to the Venus clam.
