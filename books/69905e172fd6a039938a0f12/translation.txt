# ON WITCHES

**I** CANNOT offer sufficiently just thanks to God, the Best and Greatest, that He has provided my pen with such a subject for writing, the publication of which has resulted in the abandonment, in many places, of the cruel practice of venting rage upon innocent blood. Because of this work, the fierce atrocity and tyranny of the devil is being driven away from that human butchery—a sacrifice than which no other smells more sweet to him. For I see that by the publication of my books *On the Deceptions of Demons* <note>original: "de Præstigiis dæmonum," Weyer’s most famous work published in 1563</note>, I have gained this benefit: that certain more distinguished magistrates now not only treat those most wretched old women (who are branded with the hateful name of "witches" <term>witches</term> <note>original: "lamiae." In Roman myth, Lamia was a child-eating demon; here Weyer uses it to describe the women accused of witchcraft.</note> in the trials of the common people) more mildly, but even acquit them from capital punishment. This is done despite a custom introduced by long-standing tradition and the biased influence of authority. Furthermore, the frequent congratulations of the most learned men—of every profession, status, and religion—testify at length that my late-night labors have not been fruitless, as they seem to join in my opinion with their own approval. However, I understand that because of the weariness caused by a long treatise (though the difficulty and magnitude of the subject demands it), some people only look at those books fitfully or read them superficially, without sufficiently weighing the force of the arguments. Therefore, I wished to set forth in brief the matters concerning witches that must be discussed, lest anything hinder a "delicate reader" from a deeper knowledge of our judgment.

Otherwise, it almost always happens that when a dispute is raised concerning the actions of witches, testimonies of Holy Scripture are immediately brought forward as objections. In these passages, one reads the name of a "magician," or an "evildoer," or an "enchanter," or even a "juggler" <term>juggler</term> <note>original: "praestigiator," one who uses illusions or sleight of hand to deceive the eyes.</note> (as others interpret it), or a "poisoner" <term>poisoner</term> <note>original: "veneficus." In Latin, the word for a sorcerer and a poisoner is the same, which Weyer uses to argue that biblical "witches" were actually physical criminals, not supernatural ones.</note>. It is then immediately asserted, without distinction, that the people commonly called witches or "wise-women" <note>original: "sagas"</note> are denoted by these names. I find, however, that these monstrous people—along with their arts, deceptions, or illicit divinations—are explained in various ways by the Rabbis and Hebrew interpreters. They are also rendered with different names by our Latin translators, and the Greek version does not exactly harmonize with the Hebrew idiom or the Latin translation. This will be more manifest if you compare the Hebrew context with any version whatever (and not lazily), and if you observe more attentively the judgments of the Rabbis and interpreters in all those places where any mention is made of these portents: such as in Exodus chapters 7, 8, 9, and 22; Leviticus 19 and 20; Deuteronomy 18; Jeremiah 27; Daniel 2; 2 Kings 21 <note>original: "4 Reg. 21." In the Vulgate Bible, the books of Samuel and Kings are counted as 1-4 Kings.</note>; 2 Chronicles 33 <note>original: "2 Paralip. 33."</note>; and the rest.

Therefore, I have judged it most fitting—especially in this most ulcerated century, which is still so unlearned and ignorant in the discernment and censure of demonic impostures of this kind—to first separate "Witches" from the others with whom they are usually confused without distinction. I do this so that the mixing of terms and names does not pour darkness upon that variety of opinions. From such confusion, matters are often entangled in such inextricable folds that hardly any Theseus <note>In Greek mythology, Theseus used a ball of thread to find his way out of the Minotaur's labyrinth.</note> offers himself to show a way out. Truly, such has been the infamous labyrinth of magic, incantations, and poisonings until now. By this method, the things of which I shall speak here will be more clearly distinguished; and it will also be openly established where I depart from the poorly-rooted opinion of others, based on reason and Holy Scripture...

Soon obeying the command, <note>Referring to Aaron’s rod/serpent from the previous context in Exodus 7:12.</note> it attacked the Egyptian rods—which were crawling in the likeness of serpents—one after another, and devoured every last one of them. Augustine confirms that corporeal matter is not subject to the power of a demon regarding its transformation into a form, <margin>Augustine, book 3, *On the Trinity*</margin> saying: "It is not to be thought that this matter of visible things serves these transgressing angels <note>original: "transgressoribus angelis," referring to fallen angels or demons.</note> at their whim, but God alone." I would not think that one should always trust words or writings that go beyond the limits of reason; I wish for the mystery of our Christian faith to be excepted here. <note>Weyer is making a crucial distinction: he accepts biblical miracles as matters of faith, but insists that modern claims of "miracle girls" who don't eat must be held to the standards of medical reason.</note>

<margin>Vital heat.</margin>
<margin>Substantial moisture.</margin>
Life, beyond all controversy, consists of a unique **heat**, which is nourished and sustained by a tenacious, fatty, oily, and airy **moisture** <note>This refers to the Galenic theory of "innate heat" and "radical moisture," which were thought to be the fuels of life.</note>. This moisture, diffused into the substance of the "similar parts" <note>A term in ancient anatomy for tissues that are uniform in substance, like bone, nerve, or membrane.</note>, escapes all sharpness of the eyes. In the consensus of these two—namely, the hot and the moist—life endures. The former is considered the craftsman of all actions; the latter is spread beneath it so that it might remain more durable. Therefore, as long as that pleasant, native moisture can foster the vital heat, the animal remains among the living. If this moisture is abundant, thicker, and more resistant to dissipation, it is more slowly absorbed by the vital heat which, wrapped within it, gradually loses and consumes small portions. Furthermore, since the mass of living creatures always flows away and diminishes for that reason, unless another similar substance is substituted for that which has perished, the whole will surely exhale and dissipate by degrees. But in place of this consumed native moisture—which arises from the seed and the very first beginnings of our generation—you will find nothing to replace it. Therefore, when the "substantial fuel" (as they call it) of the vital heat fails, it must necessarily be extinguished, and headlong death follows. Furthermore, so that this might be countered, the assistance of foods is necessarily summoned, so that by the addition of a certain grateful moisture, that which is native may be bedewed; thus, it can more steadily resist the voracity of its own heat. Although the heat always feeds on some of the original moisture,

it feeds less where a supply of accessory nourishment is available. When this is entirely removed, the quality of the native heat is rendered sharper, consuming the native moisture more quickly—especially when the body is moved and exercised. For this reason, without the addition of nourishment, the human body cannot persist for a long time. Here, certainly, there will be one consideration for a **rational man** born for any sort of actions and not stuffed with superfluous food; and another inspection for some bloodless, torpid, and **irrational little beast**, inspired by a spark of heat and drenched in much cold and gluey moisture. Of this sort is the **chameleon**, which, when variedly provoked by me at another time—even with a burning candle moved toward its eyes and snout—hardly moved at all while alive. I grant, indeed, that in a cold body such an abundance of thick and tenacious moisture can sometimes be accumulated, which the innate heat devours only over a longer time; however, no one will easily show me the bodies of mortals with such a constitution who have sustained fasts of many years—such as some that are preached by authors. I do not doubt that those authors handed down those stories based on the reports of others rather than through the eyewitness observation of the truth.
