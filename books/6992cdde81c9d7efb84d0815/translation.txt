# Diagram of the Seven External and Eight Internal Pulses <note>original: "Qi Biao Ba Li Zhi Tu" (七表八裏之圖). This diagram categorizes pulse qualities into two main groups: those felt near the surface (External/Yang) and those felt deeper in the tissue (Internal/Yin).</note>
## The Seven External Pulses | The Eight Internal Pulses

**Floating** <term>Floating pulse (Fu Mai): A pulse felt with very light pressure on the skin, associated with the exterior of the body.</term> belongs to Metal
**Hollow** <term>Hollow pulse (Kou Mai): A pulse that is floating and large but feels empty in the center, like the stalk of a scallion.</term> belongs to Fire
**Slippery** <term>Slippery pulse (Hua Mai): A smooth, rounded pulse that feels like pearls rolling in a basin, often associated with dampness or pregnancy.</term> belongs to Water
**Full** <term>Full pulse (Shi Mai): A forceful and long pulse felt at all levels of pressure.</term> belongs to Fire

---

**Wiry** <term>Wiry pulse (Xian Mai): A taut, straight, and long pulse that feels like a guitar string.</term> belongs to Wood
**Tight** <term>Tight pulse (Jin Mai): A forceful, vibrating pulse that feels like a twisted rope.</term> belongs to Wood
**Surging** <term>Surging pulse (Hong Mai): A pulse that comes on strong like a wave but fades away gently.</term> belongs to Fire
->The seven external pulses belong to Yang; the eight internal pulses belong to Yin.<-

---

**Faint** <term>Faint pulse (Wei Mai): An extremely thin and soft pulse that is difficult to feel and may seem to disappear.</term> belongs to Earth
**Deep** <term>Deep pulse (Chen Mai): A pulse only felt with heavy pressure near the bone, associated with the interior of the body.</term> belongs to Water
**Relaxed** <term>Relaxed pulse (Huan Mai): A moderate pulse that is slightly slow but steady.</term> belongs to Earth
**Choppy** <term>Choppy pulse (Se Mai): A thin, uneven pulse that feels like a knife scraping bamboo.</term> belongs to Metal

---

**Slow** <term>Slow pulse (Chi Mai): A pulse with fewer than four beats per breath cycle.</term> belongs to Earth
**Hidden** <term>Hidden pulse (Fu Mai): A pulse deeper than the "Deep" pulse, requiring the practitioner to push the muscle aside to reach the bone.</term> belongs to Wood
**Soggy** <term>Soggy pulse (Ru Mai): A floating, thin, and soft pulse that disappears under pressure, like a thread on water.</term> belongs to Water
**Weak** <term>Weak pulse (Ruo Mai): A deep, thin, and soft pulse that lacks force.</term> belongs to Metal

<margin>Fundamental Nature</margin> <note>original: "Zong" (宗). Here it refers to the primary or "ancestral" classification of these pulses as the root of external diagnosis.</note>

**<term>Floating (Fu): felt with a light touch like wood floating on water</term>, <term>Hollow (Kou): felt like a scallion stalk, firm on the outside but empty inside</term>, <term>Slippery (Hua): smooth and flowing like pearls rolling in a basin</term>, <term>Full (Shi): forceful, long, and hard against the fingers</term>, <term>Wiry (Xian): taut and long like a musical string</term>, <term>Tight (Jin): forceful and vibrating like a thick, twisted rope</term>, and <term>Surging (Hong): wide and powerful like a rising wave</term>—these Seven External Pulses are indeed the fundamental nature.**

<gloss>These seven types of pulses all belong to Yang <note>the active, external, and warm principle</note> and are entirely related to the "Exterior" <note>the skin, hair, and muscles</note> of the body; therefore, they are called the "Original Form."</gloss>

---

<margin>The Same Category</margin> <note>original: "Tong" (同). This indicates that the following pulses share the same functional classification.</note>

**<term>Faint (Wei): extremely thin and soft, barely perceptible</term>, <term>Deep (Chen): felt only with heavy pressure near the bone</term>, <term>Slow-loose (Huan): relaxed and slightly slow but regular</term>, <term>Choppy (Se): rough and jagged, like a knife scraping bamboo</term>, <term>Slow (Chi): a slow rate of fewer than four beats per breath</term>, combined with <term>Hidden (Fu): deeper than the "deep" pulse, requiring pressure against the bone to feel</term>, <term>Soggy (Ru): thin and soft, felt only on the surface like a silk thread in water</term>, and <term>Weak (Ruo): deep, thin, and lacking force</term>—these Eight Internal Pulses are alike.**

<gloss>These eight types of pulses all belong to Yin <note>the passive, internal, and cool principle</note> and are entirely related to the "Interior" <note>the organs and blood</note> of the body; therefore, they are said to "enter the interior."</gloss>

# The Triple Burner has no form but only a name; it resides in the chest and corresponds to the diaphragm.

<gloss>The Triple Burner <term>San Jiao; a functional system in Chinese medicine responsible for fluid metabolism and energy distribution</term> consists of the Upper, Middle, and Lower sections.</gloss>

The Upper Burner is located below the heart and at the upper opening of the stomach; its primary role is intake rather than excretion. The Middle Burner is located in the center of the stomach; it does not move up or down but governs the "rotting and ripening" <note>the breakdown and fermentation process of digestion</note> of water and grain. The Lower Burner is located below the navel at the upper opening of the bladder; its role is the separation of the clear from the turbid fluids, and it governs excretion rather than intake. These are the Triple Burners. They govern all the various forms of Qi <note>vital energy</note> throughout the body. While they have the names "Upper," "Middle," and "Lower," they possess no physical shape. The Triple Burner channel belongs to the Hand Lesser Yang <note>Shaoyang</note> and serves as an "external hollow organ" <note>Fu</note>. 

In his writings, Shuhe <note>Wang Shuhe, a famous 3rd-century physician known for the *Pulse Classic*</note> discussed the "Solid Organs" <note>Zang</note> and "Hollow Organs" <note>Fu</note> for all other systems, but when he reached the Triple Burner, he did not assign it a corresponding solid organ. The Heart is the Sovereign Fire <note>Jun Huo; the primary heat of the body</note>, while the Triple Burner is the Ministerial Fire <note>Xiang Huo; the fire that supports the Sovereign Fire</note>. These two stand in an interior-exterior relationship. Both have names but no physical forms. Thus, the Triple Burner is said to reside in the chest and corresponds with the diaphragm. 

Why does it correspond with the diaphragm? The Vital Gate <term>Mingmen; the "Gate of Life," believed to be the source of the body's fundamental heat</term> also carries Ministerial Fire and is located below the navel. The Heart Protector <note>the Pericardium</note> is above the diaphragm, while the Vital Gate is below the diaphragm. The sections above and below the diaphragm do not lose touch with one another but respond to each other. Therefore, the area one and a half inches below the navel is called the Sea of Qi <note>Qihai</note>, and the center of the navel itself is also considered a Sea of Qi. The *Classic* <note>The *Huangdi Neijing* or Yellow Emperor’s Inner Canon</note> states:
"Above the Lesser Yang <note>Shaoyang</note>, Heat-Qi governs it; within it, the Reverting Yin <note>Jueyin</note> appears."

<gloss>The essential Qi of the Gallbladder relies on the surplus of the Liver.</gloss>

**The 53rd Difficulty** states: The Classic says that those who experience the **Seven Transmissions** will die, while those who experience **Interval Transmissions** will live. What does this mean?

The answer is: The **Seven Transmissions** occur when a disease is passed to the organ that the current one "overcomes" <note>original: "所勝" (suo sheng); this refers to the "destructive cycle" of the Five Elements, where one element controls or suppresses another, such as Fire overcoming Metal.</note>. **Interval Transmissions** occur when a disease is passed to the "child" organ <note>original: "其子" (qi zi); this refers to the "generative cycle," where one element supports the next, such as Fire creating Earth (ash).</note>.

Because an organ cannot withstand being damaged a second time <note>Once the cycle of destruction completes and returns to the already weakened starting organ, the body's vital energy is considered exhausted.</note>, it is said that the **Seven Transmissions** lead to death.

**Interval Transmissions** refer to passing the disease to that which the organ "gives birth to" <note>the generative cycle (相生, xiang sheng)</note>. Suppose a disease of the **Heart** transmits to the **Spleen**, the **Spleen** transmits to the **Lungs**, the **Lungs** transmit to the **Kidney**, the **Kidney** transmits to the **Liver**, and the **Liver** transmits back to the **Heart**. This is the **mutual transmission between mother and child** <term>Mother-Child Relationship|A core TCM concept where each element/organ is the "mother" of the next in the cycle; for example, Wood is the mother of Fire.</term>. It moves in a continuous circle and begins again, like a ring without an end. Therefore, it is said that the patient will live.

## The Fifty-fifth Difficulty
<note>The *Nan Jing*, or *Classic of Difficulties*, is a foundational medical text likely compiled in the 1st or 2nd century CE. It uses a question-and-answer format to explain complex theories of diagnosis and anatomy.</note>

The **Fifty-fifth Difficulty** states:
In cases of disease, there are **Accumulations** <note>original: 積 (jī)</note> and there are **Gatherings** <note>original: 聚 (jù)</note>. By what means can one distinguish between them?

The answer is: **Accumulations** are the product of **Yin energy** <note>original: 陰氣 (yīn qì)</note>; **Gatherings** are the product of **Yang energy** <note>original: 陽氣 (yáng qì)</note>. 

Therefore, Yin is deep-seated and hidden, while Yang is floating and moving. Where energy <term>Qi|The vital life force or "breath" that circulates through the body; its stagnation is thought to cause disease.</term> becomes stationary and piles up, it is named an **Accumulation**. Where energy congregates and collects, it is named a **Gathering**.

For this reason, **Accumulations** are produced by the **Five Solid Organs** <note>The *Zang* organs: Heart, Liver, Spleen, Lungs, and Kidneys. These are "Solid" because they store vital essence and are associated with Yin.</note>, and **Gatherings** are formed by the **Six Hollow Organs** <note>The *Fu* organs: Stomach, Small Intestine, Large Intestine, Gallbladder, Bladder, and the Triple Burner. These are "Hollow" because they transmit and digest, and are associated with Yang.</note>.

**Accumulations** are Yin energy: 
* Their onset occurs in a **fixed, constant location**. 
* The pain they cause does not leave its specific region. 
* Above and below, they have a defined beginning and end. 
* To the left and right, they have finite limits.

**Gatherings** are Yang energy: 
* Their initial onset has **no fixed root or source**. 
* They move up and down, stopping or lingering at various points. 
* The pain they cause has **no constant location**. 

These are what we call **Gatherings**. Thus, by these characteristics, one can distinguish and recognize the difference between **Accumulations** and **Gatherings**.

*   The **Stomach** is the Fu of Water and Grain.
*   The **Bladder** is the Fu of Fluids.

The Large Intestine is the Fu corresponding to the Lungs.

**The question is asked:** The "Ten Transformations" state that for the <term>Five Viscera and Six Bowels: the internal organs of the body in traditional Chinese medicine</term>, the sequence of flow through the transport points—from the **Gushing** to the **Uniting** points—all begins with the **Well** point. What is the meaning of this?

The answer is: The **Well** point corresponds to the **East** and the season of **Spring**. This is the time when the <term>Myriad Things: a traditional term for all living beings and phenomena in the universe</term> begin to emerge into life. Among all creatures that walk or breathe, and all those that fly or crawl—indeed, among all things destined to live—there are none that do not begin their life in **Spring**.

### The Sixty-Fourth Difficulty

The **Sixty-Fourth Difficulty** states: "The Ten Variations" also says that the **Yin Well** is Wood, while the **Yang Well** is Metal; the **Yin Spring** is Fire, while the **Yang Spring** is Water; the **Yin Stream** is Earth, while the **Yang Stream** is Wood; the **Yin River** is Metal, while the **Yang River** is Fire; and the **Yin Uniting** is Water, while the **Yang Uniting** is Earth. Since Yin and Yang are different in this regard, what is the meaning behind it?

The answer is: This is a matter of **Firmness** and **Softness**.

The **Yin Well** corresponds to **Yi-Wood**, and the **Yang Well** corresponds to **Geng-Metal**. The **Geng** of the Yang Well represents the **firm** aspect of **Yi**; the **Yi** of the Yin Well represents the **soft** aspect of **Geng**. Because **Yi** is Wood, it is said that the **Yin Well** is Wood. Because **Geng** is Metal, it is said that the **Yang Well** is Metal. All the other points follow this same principle.

<note>
This text explains a fundamental rule in acupuncture theory regarding the <term>Five Shu-points: five specific points on each meridian used to balance the body's elements</term>. To a student, it might seem confusing that a "Well" point on a Yin meridian is "Wood," but a "Well" point on a Yang meridian is "Metal." 

The explanation relies on the <term>Ten Heavenly Stems: a system of ten characters used in ancient Chinese chronology and medicine to represent the Yin and Yang aspects of the Five Elements</term>. The stems form "marriages" or pairs:
1. **Jia (Yang Wood) & Ji (Yin Earth)**
2. **Yi (Yin Wood) & Geng (Yang Metal)**
3. **Bing (Yang Fire) & Xin (Yin Metal)**
4. **Ding (Yin Fire) & Ren (Yang Water)**
5. **Wu (Yang Earth) & Gui (Yin Water)**

In the example provided, the **Yin Well** (Yi) and **Yang Well** (Geng) are paired. In Chinese philosophy, Yang is "firm" (剛) and Yin is "soft" (柔). By pairing them, the system creates a balance where every Yin point has a corresponding Yang partner of a different element that "completes" it.
</note>
