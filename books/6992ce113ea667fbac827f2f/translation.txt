## Affairs of Men: Volume Eight

### Diagram of the Tiger-Wing Array
<note>original: "Huyi Zhen" (虎翼陣). This formation is one of the classic "Eight Formations." It is designed to mimic the predatory power of a tiger, typically emphasizing strong flanking units that can "pounce" on an enemy's sides.</note>

### Diagram of the Bird-Soaring Array
<note>original: "Niaoxiang Zhen" (鳥翔陣). Symbolizing a bird in flight, this formation emphasizes agility and the ability to swiftly expand or contract the wings of the army to respond to changing battlefield conditions.</note>

### Diagram of the Snake-Coiling Array
<note>original: "Shepan Zhen" (蛇蟠陣). A defensive and reactive tactical layout. Like a coiled serpent, the formation is compact and difficult to penetrate, yet it can strike out from any direction if provoked.</note>

### Diagram of the Array for Complete Security in Subduing the Barbarians
<note>original: "Pingrong Wanquan Zhen" (平戎萬全陣). This was a massive, historically significant tactical layout developed during the Song Dynasty (960–1279 CE). It involved a complex arrangement of infantry, cavalry, and war wagons designed to defend the empire’s borders against nomadic cavalry forces.</note>

### Diagram of the Female Array
<note>original: "Pin Zhen" (牝陣). In the <term>Yin-Yang: The concept of dualistic, complementary forces</term> framework of Chinese military theory, a "female" formation is often concave or hollowed, designed to yield to an enemy's advance and then envelop them.</note>

### Diagram of the Male Array
<note>original: "Mu Zhen" (牡陣). Conversely, a "male" formation is convex or protruding. It is an offensive shape designed to pierce through the enemy's center with a concentrated "head" or vanguard.</note>

### Diagram of the Square Charging Array
<note>original: "Chongfang Zhen" (衝方陣). A solid, geometric block of troops used for a heavy, direct thrust. The square shape provides stability on all sides while focusing momentum forward.</note>

### Diagram of the Chariot-Wheel Array
<note>original: "Chelun Zhen" (車輪陣). This formation is named for its rotational movement. It allows units to cycle in and out of the front lines, ensuring that exhausted soldiers are replaced by fresh ones in a continuous, rolling motion.</note>

### Diagram of the Net-Snare Array
<note>original: "Fuju Zhen" (罘罝陣). Named after the specialized nets used by ancient hunters to trap hares and deer, this tactical layout focuses on encircling the enemy and cutting off all avenues of escape.</note>

### Diagram of the Wild-Goose Flight Array
<note>original: "Yanhang Zhen" (鴈行陣). This formation mimics the V-shape or diagonal line of migrating geese. It is designed to provide a wide front for archery or to allow the army to pivot quickly to flank an opponent.</note>

### Diagram of the Square Array
<note>original: "Fang Zhen" (方陣). One of the most fundamental formations in ancient warfare, the square is a solid block of troops providing balanced defense on all sides, particularly effective against cavalry.</note>

### Diagram of the Snake Array of Mount Chang
<note>original: "Changshan She Zhen" (常山蛇陣). This refers to a legendary tactical concept mentioned in Sun Tzu's *Art of War*. The "Snake of Mount Chang" is characterized by total coordination: if you strike the head, the tail attacks; if you strike the tail, the head attacks; if you strike the middle, both head and tail respond.</note>

### Diagram of the Columned Square Array
<note>original: "Hanglie Fangzhen" (行列方陣). A variation of the basic square that emphasizes the precise alignment of horizontal rows and vertical columns, facilitating disciplined movement and internal rotation of troops.</note>

### Diagram of the Instant Square Array
<note>original: "Licheng Fangzhen" (立成方陣). The term <term>Licheng: Literally "immediately established" or "ready-made"</term> suggests a formation that can be deployed rapidly from a marching column into a defensive square when an ambush is detected.</note>

### Diagram of the Square Encampment <gloss>Another version</gloss>
<note>original: "Fang Ying" (方營). This illustrates the layout of a fortified camp rather than a battlefield formation. It shows how tents, supplies, and defenses are organized in a square to maintain order and security overnight.</note>

### Diagram of the Crescent Moon Encampment
<note>original: "Yanyue Ying" (偃月營). Named for the "reclining moon" shape, this curved layout is used for camps or formations to create a concave front that can draw an enemy in and then surround them from the "horns" of the crescent.</note>

### Diagram of the Order of March
<note>original: "Xingjun Cidi" (行軍次第). This is a logistical diagram showing the sequence of units—scouts, vanguard, central command, and the baggage train—ensuring the army remains organized while traveling through different terrains.</note>

### Diagram of the Shield Formations
<note>original: "Pai Tu" (簰圖). This diagram details the use of <term>Pai: Large, heavy shields</term> which were often lashed together or held in specific rows to create a "wooden wall," protecting infantry from arrow fire.</note>

# Principles of the Eight Formations

The calculation and derivation of the **Eight Formations** begins with the basic organization of squads and ranks, and culminates in the layout of the camp itself. The squad of five <note>original: "wu" (伍)</note> represents the numbers of generation and completion of the **<term>Five Elements<note>original: "Wu Xing" (五行). The fundamental phases of Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water that govern the natural world and, by extension, the "natural" order of a balanced army.</note></term>**.

The formations themselves are physical manifestations of the **<term>Eight Trigrams<note>original: "Ba Gua" (八卦). These are the three-lined symbols from the *I Ching* (Book of Changes) used to represent the fundamental principles of reality.</note></term>**, while the skirmishers <note>original: "you bing" (遊兵), literally "wandering soldiers" or mobile reserves.</note> correspond to the number of the **<term>twenty-four solar terms<note>original: "ershisi qi" (二十四氣). The traditional Chinese lunisolar calendar divisions used to synchronize with the seasons.</note></term>**. Therefore, five men constitute a squad, and ten squads form a company. With the addition of five flag-bearing troops, a company totals fifty-five men, reaching the final number of "generation and completion" <note>In ancient Chinese numerology, 55 is the sum of the numbers 1 through 10, representing the "Number of Heaven and Earth."</note>.

Eight companies form one **formation**, consisting of 440 men. Eight formations constitute one **division**, totaling 3,520 men; this is known as the **Minor Completion**, which possesses the flexibility to transform into two separate formations. Eight divisions constitute one **command** under a general, totaling 28,160 men; this is known as the **Intermediate Completion**. In this state, the eight formations are aligned and capable of diverse transformations, ultimately corresponding to the **sixty-four hexagrams** <note>The 64 hexagrams are the expanded combinations of the Eight Trigrams in the *I Ching*, representing all possible states of change.</note>.

Eight commands constitute one **army**, totaling 225,180 men <note>The text reads 225,180, though mathematically 28,160 multiplied by 8 is 225,280; such minor discrepancies are common in historical transcriptions.</note>. This is known as the **Great Completion**. Regarding such vast numbers, the Great General **<term>Han Xin<note>Han Xin (d. 196 BCE) was a famous military strategist and a founding hero of the Han Dynasty, known for his brilliance in managing massive troop deployments.</note></term>** famously remarked that, for him, **"the more, the better."** 

The deployment of the camp formations is as follows: with the **General’s Platform** as the center, four formations are arrayed to the left and four formations to the right. These are stationed in two layers. For a **Minor General’s** command, four divisions are arrayed to the left and four divisions to the right, likewise divided into two layers.

# The Current Dynasty’s Compendium of Ten Thousand Certain Tactics for Pacifying the Barbarians

Each squad in the front rank consists of fifty men. Each squad in the rear rank consists of thirty men. Together, five squads from the front and rear are combined to form a single "Point" <note>A "point" (點, diǎn) is a basic unit of command or a rallying post within the larger formation.</note>. Each complete formation covers one thousand four hundred and forty land divisions, spanning a square area of five *li* <note>original: 里 (lǐ). A traditional unit of distance; five li is approximately 2.5 kilometers or 1.5 miles.</note>. Each land division contains one war wagon and twenty-two soldiers; ten such land divisions constitute one "Point."

The **Concave Formation** <term>牝陣 (pìn zhèn): Literally the "Female" or "Concave" formation. In Chinese cosmology and military theory, "female" represents the receptive, hollow, or enveloping force, as opposed to the "male" (convex) piercing force.</term> described to the right is synonymous with the "Water Formation" of the Yellow Emperor’s Five Elements, the "Concave Formation" of Sun Tzu, the "Curved Formation" of Wu Qi, the "Soaring Dragon" of Zhuge Liang, and the "Crescent Moon" of Liu Yu.

When the black flag <note>original: 皂旗 (zào qí). In the Five Elements system, the color black corresponds to Water, reinforcing the connection to the Yellow Emperor's Water Formation mentioned earlier.</note> is raised and the drums are sounded, the troops shall take this position. Both the left and right flanks are positioned forward and high. When a marshaling army encounters valleys or streams, it is advantageous to adopt the Concave shape. In the Concave formation, the two wings are spread forward to facilitate "swallowing" <note>original: 吞掩 (tūn yǎn). A tactical term for enveloping and smothering an enemy force.</note> the enemy. This forces the opponent to rush their defenses while being attacked from three sides, which is sufficient to overcome a **Convex Formation** <term>牡陣 (mǔ zhèn): Literally the "Male" or "Convex" formation, characterized by a protruding or pointed vanguard.</term>.

The arrangement of the Concave formation is sharp at the front and heavy at the rear, resembling the tip of a sword. As the Concave wings spread to perform a pincer attack on the enemy's vanguard, even if that vanguard has endured a day of bombardment from firearms <note>original: 銃 (chòng). Refers to early hand cannons or muskets common in the Ming and Qing eras.</note>, the flanking wings will strike them from both sides. Thus, the Concave defeats the Convex...

## Victory Over Various Formations

The **Male Formation** <term>牡陣 (mǔ zhèn): Literally the "Convex" or "Yang" formation. In military theory, this is a protruding shape used to pierce or divide enemy lines, acting as the aggressive counterpart to the "Female" (Concave) formation.</term> described to the right is synonymous with the "Fire Formation" of the Yellow Emperor’s Five Elements, the "Male Formation" of Sun Tzu, the "Sharp Formation" of Wu Qi, and the "Soaring Bird" of Zhuge Liang. 

When the vermilion flags <note>original: 朱旗 (zhū qí). In the Five Elements system, red/vermilion corresponds to Fire, matching the Yellow Emperor’s Fire Formation mentioned above.</note> are raised and the drums are sounded, the troops shall take this position. The formation is low in the front and high in the rear. It is used to take advantage of vulnerabilities and create chaos among the enemy. Depending on the terrain, it is advantageous to adopt a "Female" (Concave) posture <note>This suggests a tactical flexibility where the formation can transition between convex and concave shapes to counter the enemy.</note> to defeat a **Pointed Square** formation. 

In a **Pointed Square** formation, the front is crowded while the rear is sparse; those fighting are pressed together, while those in the interior are redundant. The Classics state: "He who receives the assistance of the terrain shall flourish; he who loses the assistance of the terrain shall perish." <note>original: 經曰得地助者興失地助者亡 (Jīng yuē dé dì zhù zhě xīng shī dì zhù zhě wáng).</note> Therefore, the Male Formation defeats the Pointed Square.

The **Pointed Square Formation** <term>衝方陣 (chōng fāng zhèn): A rectangular or "Thrusting Square" formation designed for heavy penetration of enemy lines, often compared to a battering ram.</term> described to the right is synonymous with the "Wood Formation" of the Five Elements, the "Pointed Square" of Sun Tzu, the "Straight Formation" of Wu Qi, and the "Breaking the Thrust" of Zhuge Liang.
