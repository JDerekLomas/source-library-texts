*It is right to believe, Most Serene King, that those ancient Sages and philosophers of old were not moved to the contemplation of Nature and the inquiry into the sources and principles of things in vain. Instead, they were driven by the same impulses that we, in this age, ought rightly to obey, and so they delved into the investigation of causes. For indeed, since mortals were being overwhelmed on all sides by necessities and the hardships of the seasons and weather, and were continually experiencing barrenness <note>In this context, it refers to agricultural failure and famine.</note>, and were being miserably afflicted by new and unusual disturbances of the <term>Elements</term> <note>In the 16th century, the "Elements" (Earth, Air, Fire, Water) were believed to be the building blocks of all physical matter.</note>—earthquakes, floods, and plagues affecting both animals and humans—the more prudent men of higher intellect began to search for a way to meet these calamities with healthy foresight, as Nature is instinctively inclined toward safety and preservation. While they were actively engaged in this inquiry, they discovered that there is a connection of causes: that one thing depends upon another, and that in a wondrous order, later things come from prior ones, lower things from the higher, and weaker things from* <note>original: "à poten-" (likely *à potentioribus*); the text continues "from more powerful ones" on the following page.</note>

Thus, four sources, nurturers, and guardians immediately became apparent, from whose kind and fertile treasures everything necessary for the use of this life would be supplied. Conversely, from the "silences" <note>A poetic way of describing periods where celestial influence is absent or unfavorable.</note> and hostile dissolutions of these sources, there would occur hopeless harvests, corruptions, plagues, destructions, and infinite disasters. The only task that remained was for men to discern, out of such a great abundance and universality of things, which things agreed with one another; and once this "harmony" was grasped, they had to determine by what laws effects were coupled to their causes.

This was an arduous task, requiring immense observation. Therefore, to manage the difficulty, they divided the work into "provinces" <note>Meaning specialized fields of study or disciplines.</note>. Some men primarily considered the births, transformations, successful yields, failures, and corruptions of grain, vines, trees, herds, bees, and similar things. These men studiously noted the properties of **Animals** and **Plants**—especially domestic ones that provide food and clothing—the nature of seeds, the suitability of various locations, and the associations of the sky and the **stars**, from which the fertile opportunities of the seasons are gathered. Having grasped the causes of fertility, they led a blessed life with a rich confidence in their crops and an undoubting hope for their flocks.
