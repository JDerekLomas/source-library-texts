# Preface

->To the<-

## Wise Lover of the Golden Art.

<note>The "Golden Art" refers to Alchemy, specifically the pursuit of the Philosopher's Stone.</note>

Since it is in no way to be expected of wise, sensible, and upright persons that they would bring forth foolish, impossible things that run contrary to Nature, nor present such things to others as believable and true—much less write and teach extensively about them—it might seem to many a strange and absurd matter that one should here anew bring to light and promote for printing such an extensive and detailed description of the so-highly doubted Golden Art of transforming lesser metals into gold and silver. 

For what can be more beautifully depicted and described than that which is found here (as the title states): the <term>AUREUM VELLUS</term> <note>Latin: "Golden Fleece." In alchemical literature, the quest of Jason and the Argonauts was often interpreted as an allegory for the preparation of the Philosopher's Stone.</note>, that Golden Fleece so highly praised by the ancient Philosophers and conquered with much toil? How a golden treasure and cabinet of art is thereby opened, wherein the writings and books of the most prominent, excellent, select, magnificent, and proven authors—gathered from the ancient, surviving, and withheld relics and monuments of the philosophers of former and modern times—are contained and most faithfully published from the originals. 

The first editions, printed in the year 1598 at Rorschach on Lake Constance and at Basel in 1604, have become so rare and difficult to obtain that the publisher had to procure them at extraordinary expense. This was done to awaken even greater pleasure in the lovers of this science (after he had previously released so many other books of the same kind). All of this, however, might [be viewed] by untimely critics—who very quickly despise what they cannot understand or grasp, or who, because they are on the wrong path, [seek] this jewel...

...to snatch [this knowledge] / have perhaps turned out / and all hope of a successful conclusion and possession of these secrets / has already been given up as lost) might be viewed as an unnecessary / indeed, seductive labor / especially since after so much effort and unspeakable costs / which the lovers and seekers of the <term>Philosopher's Stone</term> <note>The legendary substance capable of turning base metals into gold and granting eternal life.</note> have applied everywhere / nevertheless nothing has come to light / that would grant the unbelieving world a glimpse of its possibility / since the many legends and histories of transformations occurring here and there are always entangled with such doubtful circumstances / as if everything were composed and fabricated out of fables and little tales / by gold-greedy minds. Added to this is the insurmountable difficulty of such a vexing undertaking / since nowhere is the matter described so clearly and distinctly / that one could make a reliable calculation of the profit from it / while all the highly praised gold-processes often do not even pay for the lead used for testing / or bring the slightest advantage. The words which the philosophers and teachers of the art have expressed in their writings to that end / are entirely similar to riddles / as they themselves remind us / that where they name a thing clearly / something else entirely is meant / for everything must be taken and judged not according to the outward letter / but according to the inward understanding. He who therefore, without reason / like the blind / presumes to hit the mark in this difficult investigation / what kind of luck will he have to expect from such errors? That all circumstances have not been described so clearly by those experienced in this art / arises from no malice or wicked intent / to lead others behind the light or to deceive and cause them harm / but rather Divine Providence and the foresight to govern the world according to the highest wisdom / has introduced this wonderful order (as is well reminded here on pages 24 and 58) that men should not all be equally rich and of the same fortune. For otherwise a theater of luxury / vice and immorality would emerge from this / since no one would show the slightest compliance to another / nor would anyone be compelled to recognize a higher or more worthy person. # Preface

God alone grants knowledge of this matter to anyone; and it happens only to those who are seekers and lovers of Divine Truth. It is not for the greedy who deal in deceit, nor for the idle who wish to grow rich in their sleep and have no desire to work, and even less for those souls given over to lust. To all of these, Heaven is barred. Even if they possessed all the <term>Hermetic books</term> <note>Refers to the legendary writings of Hermes Trismegistus, the foundational texts of alchemy</note> to be found in the world, and set up ever so many <term>laboratories</term>, melting, digesting, and distilling furnaces for that purpose, they would achieve nothing with them, but would only be brought into greater confusion.

"But to what end," (an inexperienced person might burst out in impatience) "do all the heaped-up writings of so many <term>alchemists</term> serve? It would be better not to touch or read any of them; for since one can gain no understanding from them to produce gold or silver, it would be much better to keep such artistic feats silent than to publish such shapeless processes and offer them publicly for sale."

However, just as there are two kinds of seekers (as already mentioned)—*Digni & Indigni* <note>Latin: Worthy and Unworthy</note>—the former will nevertheless behold a light in the deepest darkness, by which they can direct their work and investigation, even while the latter grope around in the dark at high noon, seizing *quid pro quo* <note>Latin: "this for that"; a mistake or substitution of one thing for another</note>, taking one thing for another. It is enough that the knowers of truth understand one another, while the so-called vain <term>ash-blowers</term> <note>A derogatory term for amateur or false alchemists who merely blew on the coals without understanding the theory</note> with their Babylonian language—where none can guess the other's meaning—continually expand their numbers. Since they can achieve nothing according to perfection, they turn to sophistry, where refined <term>calamine</term> <note>Zinc ore used in making brass</note> and zinc must give the color of gold, and adulterated tin and copper must resemble silver.

The guideline for extricating oneself from this deceptive labyrinth will therefore primarily aim at first distinguishing well whether those who have passed themselves off as masters of the golden art and written books about it were of sound mind, and have not perhaps fooled themselves with vain imagination. Of these, an incredible multitude exists, who, when they have scarcely begun, pledge body and soul with great oaths that they shall not fail.

Secondly, whether they have attained a complete experience of the matter and have not merely remained stuck in theory. Thirdly, whether they show a sincere spirit; for though they knew the art, they had no desire to mislead others through false teaching, nor did they offer themselves up for hire, whether to great lords or other wealthy persons. And fourthly, whether they properly refrain from boasting and grand bragging—claiming they can immediately produce millions with their <term>tincture</term> <note>The "Philosopher's Stone" in liquid form, capable of transmuting metals and curing disease</note> and infallibly cure all diseases—and keep within the bounds...

# Preface

...not overstep the bounds of modesty. All of those [true masters] can certainly be trusted with confidence, and no misfortune need be feared from following them. On the contrary, the greatest number of daring <term>gold-makers</term> <note>original: Goldmacher; often used disparagingly for those seeking only material wealth</note> ultimately end in ignorance, presumption, deceit, and boasting, as daily experience testifies. In this <term>gilded (not yet golden) century</term> <note>original: "Seculo de aurato, nondum aureo"; a play on words suggesting an age that looks like gold but lacks its true value</note>, they have made themselves so hated that they are regarded by everyone more as useless <term>coal-burners</term> <note>a derogatory term for failed alchemists who wasted fuel without results</note> than as true <term>gold-artists</term>.

The authors cited and collected in this book of the <term>Golden Fleece</term> <note>The "Golden Fleece" is a classic alchemical metaphor for the Great Work or the Philosopher's Stone</note> generally display the four aforementioned qualities (albeit mixed with errors). They are presented as the most excellent, experienced, and proven writers, who at the very least knew such <term>particulars</term> <note>specific chemical transmutations of individual metals</note> that can later provide a secure guide to the <term>Universal</term> <note>the universal medicine or stone that affects all things</note>. Because they base themselves on the <term>experience</term> of the ancient Egyptians, Chaldeans, and Arabs, they are to be esteemed much higher than the blindly guessed conjectures of untimely newcomers. 

Therefore, for the better illumination of the matter, it may not be improper to add a brief report on these mentioned <term>practitioners</term>, regarding how their <term>operations</term> agree with the truth or depart from it. This is not so that the reader of these writings, as a lover of the art, should immediately form a firm hope of becoming a possessor of the <term>Golden Fleece</term> or being clothed with the wool of the <term>Martial Ram</term> <note>an alchemical reference to iron or the beginning of the work in the sign of Aries</note>; for the laws of this <term>most beneficent Order</term> do not permit this, nor would the authors who wrote of it claim or approve it. Rather, it is merely to prove that the golden art walks on firm feet; for whoever takes the right path according to their admonition may at least reach a small goal of the golden <term>triumphal chariot</term>.

The entire work is divided into five different <term>treatises</term> to this end, so that the distinction between the artistic efforts may be better recognized, and the apprentice may have the opportunity to seek out the most suitable method. 

The fortunate Salomon Trismosin (in Greek, the "triple tower") <note>The author likely derives "Trismosin" from "Trismegistus" or "three-fold"</note> makes the beginning here, from whom it is said Theophrastus Paracelsus learned the foundation of his science. His travels with the three discovered powerful <term>tinctures</term> are worthy of contemplation. The <term>Red Lion</term>, the <term>Mercurial Water</term> (in which gold is to be reduced to its first <term>matter</term>), the <term>Black Eagle</term>, and its oil for <term>cementing</term> <note>a process of refining or hardening metals</note> are incomparable in their effect, if such a result follows as is attributed to them. Remarkable is the downfall of the Venetian nobleman on page 4, who perhaps abused this art. The <term>medicinal power</term> to renew life on page 9 surpasses all other means.
