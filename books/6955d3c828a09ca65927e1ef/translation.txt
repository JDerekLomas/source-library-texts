### A GENERAL
# PREFACE
### TO THE
## Abridgment of Mr. Boyle’s
## Philosophical Works.

---

The *philosophical writings of* Mr. Boyle <note>Robert Boyle (1627–1691), a founding member of the Royal Society and a pioneer of modern chemistry.</note> *have, from their first appearing in public, brought honor to his country and earned him general esteem throughout the world. The novelty, variety, dignity, and usefulness of the various subjects he treats—along with the easy and familiar manner in which they are handled—recommend his work to all of humanity. Mr. Boyle accommodates himself as well to the unlearned and the novice as to the philosopher and the scholar; his whole scope and design being, with the utmost candor and simplicity, to communicate those numerous and important discoveries which cost him infinite effort and application to make.*

*On the other hand, as Mr. Boyle was the introducer—or, at least, the great restorer—of mechanical philosophy <note>The theory that natural phenomena can be explained by the motion and interaction of physical particles.</note> among us, he endeavored to express himself in the most full and detailed manner. Consequently, he has spun out his works to what now—now that philosophy is more generally understood—appears to be an immoderate length. Furthermore, because his numerous pieces were all published occasionally and in a miscellaneous manner, it is difficult to organize them into an order suitable for a quick and convenient reading. Unless one takes the pains to collect, organize, and regularly study his excellent writings as they require, the value placed upon them—though indeed great and widespread—falls vastly short of what they merit. This high reputation proceeds more from the reports of a few diligent men than from any personal and actual knowledge that the majority, even of philosophers <note>original: "philosophers"; here meaning natural scientists or scholars of "natural philosophy"</note>, have of them. But those who have been most familiar with *Mr. Boyle* agree that no author deserves better of mankind, or merits to be more generally read, than he. 

In order to secure him this general readership, nothing seemed more helpful than to organize and condense his works into a proper and regular abridgment; therefore, I was requested to attempt one. For, despite the *Epitome* produced by *Mr. Boulton* <note>Richard Boulton published an earlier, four-volume abridgment of Boyle's works in 1699-1700, which was criticized for its lack of depth and poor organization</note>, a well-organized and concise collection of all *Mr. Boyle's* philosophical writings has been generally desired and declared, by beyond-reproach judges, to be greatly lacking. It was the opinion of such people that principally convinced me to take on this task; and I shall consider my labor well spent if I have performed it to their satisfaction.

The purpose of an abridgment, as I understand it, is to clearly present the entire substance of an author without including anything unnecessary.

How far I have achieved this goal in abridging the philosophical works of *Mr. Boyle* is not for me to decide. It will be expected, however, that I provide some account of my methods in this process.

Since *Mr. Boyle's* philosophical writings appeared to be a miscellaneous collection of essays on a great variety of subjects, it was judged convenient, in the first place, to individually categorize them under the respective general headings where they naturally belonged. By doing this, the scope and purpose of each separate piece might immediately become clear, and the meaning and direction of the whole collection, once arranged this way, could be better understood.

I assumed a further advantage would come from placing them in such an order that those which were introductory or fundamental might stand first to prepare the way for the rest. In this arrangement, all the essays might naturally follow one another, mutually explain each other, and, as much as possible, prove, confirm, and lead into one another. But as Mr. *Boyle* never designed to write a complete body of philosophy, but only to bestow occasional essays on those subjects to which his genius or inclination led him; it is not to be expected that even the most exquisite arrangement should ever reduce them to a methodical and uniform system, though they afford abundant materials for one.

In the next place, observing that Mr. *Boyle’s* manner was ceremoniously to address particular pieces to particular persons; to use large preparatory discourses before he entered upon his design; and always to write in a copious, diffusive, and circumstantial style; I determined to leave out in the abridgment whatever was merely personal, or had no relation to the argument; and to contract his words into as small a compass as appeared consistent with the clarity <note>original: "perspicuity"</note> requisite in philosophical writings. And I should be sorry if any person, though a stranger to Mr. *Boyle’s* discoveries or modern philosophy, should not, by proper application, fully understand his doctrine in the abridgment; or not be enabled by it to go through all his experiments, processes, and operations in the very manner wherein he himself delivers them.

And because some exceptions have been taken to Mr. *Boyle’s* originals on account of the passages that frequently occur in different languages from that in which he wrote, and the quaint, antiquated, or uncouth expressions he sometimes uses; it seemed convenient to make him wholly *English*; to throw out the harsher words and phrases; and so far to alter the style that it should not offend a purely philosophical ear. To do more than this was deemed an unnecessary labor. For, as I was all along to preserve the sense of the author pure from any foreign turn or tincture, it seemed most expedient to employ his very words wherever they appeared concise, expressive, and unexceptionable.

And these are the views I had, with regard to the author, in this undertaking.

*Having now organized* Mr. Boyle*’s philosophical works under distinct general headings, I judged it would be acceptable if each were preceded by a short account of the doctrine arranged under it; this way, the reader might come prepared to consider the material.*

*To render this abridgment more useful and satisfactory, it was judged necessary to add, by way of notes, the later discoveries and improvements in the principal subjects treated by* Mr. Boyle. *It was far from my design in this to write a commentary upon the author; that would be a task for some great master of natural and experimental knowledge. Conscious of my own inabilities, I designed no more than to transfer from the great* Sir Isaac Newton, *and some few other eminent and successful inquirers into nature, such details as tended to illustrate, confirm, or improve the doctrine and discoveries of* Mr. Boyle.

*What has been collected from various pieces by our author and brought together as a general preface to his philosophical writings will render it unnecessary for me to explain their specific nature, method, and design, or to answer the objections to which they may seem liable. All of this is done there by the author himself in a manner superior to any I could attempt. I shall, therefore, only further observe regarding my own part that I have, through the whole course of the work, consulted the advantage of the reader as well as the reputation of* Mr. Boyle.

It is *an equal regard for both that leads me here to dwell a little upon the excellencies of the author and the advantages to be gained from a reading of his works.*

Mr. Boyle *starts his reader at the elements, or fundamental principles of things, and with exquisite judgment conducts him through all the regions of nature to furnish him with objects upon which to exercise his faculties. Being first concerned with making him a general philosopher, he leaves him prepared for any further inquiry he shall think fit to make into the works of nature or art.*
