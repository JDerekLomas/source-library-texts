# Diagram of the Kidneys
<note>original: "腎圖" (Shentu). In traditional Chinese medicine, the Kidneys are considered the "Root of Life," storing the body's essential essence or "Jing."</note>

The kidneys consist of two organs, weighing approximately one <term>jin</term> and one or two <term>liang</term> <note>original: "一斤一二兩"; these are traditional Chinese units of weight. Historically, one jin was roughly 600 grams, subdivided into 16 liang.</note>. Their shape is like stone eggs, and they are attached to the spine at the fourteenth vertebra <note>Counting from the top of the spine downward, the fourteenth vertebra corresponds to the second lumbar vertebra (L2) in modern anatomy.</note>, each positioned one and a half <term>cun</term> <note>original: "寸"; an anatomical "inch" used in acupuncture to measure distances on the body based on the patient's own proportions.</note> away from the midline.

In the *Suwen* (Basic Questions) <note>One of the two parts of the *Huangdi Neijing* (Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon), the foundational text of Chinese medicine.</note>, the "Treatise on the Secret Canon of the Orchid Chamber" <note>original: "靈蘭秘典論" (Linglan Midian Lun); a chapter describing the twelve organs as "officials" in a government.</note> states: "The kidneys are the officials of 'producing strength' <note>original: "作強之官" (Zuoqiang zhi guan); this refers to the kidneys' role in providing physical stamina, structural integrity of the bones, and reproductive vitality.</note>, and from them, skill and ingenuity <note>original: "伎巧" (Jiqiao); this encompasses both physical dexterity and the mental capacity for creative thinking.</note> originate."

The "Chapter on the Fundamental Organs" <note>original: "本藏篇" (Benzang pian); a chapter from the *Ling Shu* (Divine Pivot) that discusses how the size and position of organs affect a person's constitution.</note> states: 

If the kidneys are small, the organ is secure and difficult to injure. If the kidneys are large, one is prone to illness, suffering from lower back pain and an inability to bend or look up <note>original: "俛仰" (fuyan); the physical movement of bowing the head/bending the torso and raising the head/straightening up.</note>; such kidneys are easily harmed by external pathogens. 

If the kidneys are positioned high, one suffers from pain in the back and along the spine <note>original: "背膂" (beilü); specifically the muscles and structures flanking the spine.</note> and cannot easily bend or look up. If the kidneys are positioned low, there is pain in the lower back and tailbone <note>original: "腰尻" (yaokao); "kao" refers to the sacrum or coccyx area.</note>, an inability to bend or look up, and a predisposition to "fox hernia" <note>original: "狐疝" (hushan); a traditional term for an inguinal hernia, so named because it was thought to retract like a fox hiding in its den.</note>. 

If the kidneys are firm, one does not suffer from lower back or back pain. If the kidneys are brittle, one suffers from "wasting-thirst" disease <note>original: "消癉" (xiaodan); a condition characterized by intense thirst and weight loss, similar to modern diabetes.</note> and is easily injured. 

If the kidneys are upright and aligned, the body is harmonious and the organs function smoothly, making them difficult to injure. If the kidneys are tilted or slanted, one suffers from pain in the lower back and tailbone.

### External Signs of the Kidneys

Those with dark-toned skin <note>In the Five Elements theory, the color black corresponds to the Water element and the Kidneys.</note> and fine skin texture have small kidneys; those with coarse skin texture have large kidneys. 

Those with high-set ears have high kidneys. Those with a depression behind the ears have low kidneys. Those with firm ears <unclear>have</unclear> firm kidneys. Those with thin ears that are not firm have brittle kidneys. Those whose ears are well-proportioned and sit forward near the jawbone <term>jawbone</term> <note>original: "牙車" (yache); an archaic term for the mandible or lower jaw.</note> have upright kidneys. Those with one ear tilted higher than the other have slanted kidneys.

# **Spirit Seal**, **Spirit Ruin**, **Spirit Storehouse**, **Middle of Chest**, and **Mansion of Point** each lie two inches from the midline, spanning six inches from top to bottom.

These six points—including **Mansion of Point**—are located two inches beside the **Jade Pivot** <note>original: "璇璣" (Xuanji), a point on the midline Conception Vessel</note>. If you locate them according to this method, you will achieve success.

<gloss>In my humble opinion, **Yin Metropolis** <note>original: "陰都" (Yindu)</note> is five-tenths of an inch <term>分 (fen): a proportional unit equal to one-tenth of a "cun" or Chinese inch</term> beside **Central Venter**; **Communicating Valley** <note>original: "通谷" (Tonggu)</note> is five-tenths beside **Upper Venter**; and **Dark Gate** <note>original: "幽門" (Youmen)</note> is five-tenths beside **Great Palace Gate**. However, from **Pubic Bone** <note>original: "橫骨" (Henggu)</note> to **Middle Pouring** <note>original: "中注" (Zhongzhu)</note>, each point is situated one and a half inches from the midline. **Vitals Point**, **Shang Bend**, **Stone Pass**, **Yin Metropolis**, **Communicating Valley**, and **Dark Gate** are each only five-tenths of an inch from the midline. From **Stepping Corridor** <note>original: "步廊" (Bulang)</note>, **Spirit Seal**, **Spirit Ruin**, **Spirit Storehouse**, and **Middle of Chest** up to **Mansion of Point**, the distance increases to two inches from the midline. The curvature of the meridian follows this specific path. Master Xu’s books on acupuncture <note>likely referring to the Ming Dynasty scholar Xu Feng, author of the *Complete Collection of Acupuncture and Moxibustion*</note> are in error when they describe these as forming only two straight lines. In total, there are twenty-seven points on each side, making fifty-four points in all.

# Song of the Kidney Organ

The Kidneys are split into two; they harmonize together with the Bladder. <gloss>The kidneys consist of two organs hanging opposite each other. However, the right one is the **Gate of Life** <note>original: "命門" (Mingmen)</note>; there is a distinction here between the elements of Water and Fire. The Gate of Life takes the **Triple Burner** as its...</gloss>

...partner organ. Yet its Qi is connected to the Kidneys; furthermore, the Triple Burner has a name but no physical form. <gloss>The Bladder is a **yang organ** <term>腑 (fu): a "hollow" organ, such as the stomach or bladder, that processes and moves substances rather than storing vital essence.</term>; its pairing with the Kidney and the Gate of Life is appropriate.</gloss>

Flourishing in Winter, its essence belongs to the Water element; its position is fixed in the North, without deception. <gloss>The Kidney belongs to the Water element and flourishes during the Winter season. # The Forty-First Problem

**The Forty-First Problem** states: The **Liver** alone has two lobes <note>original: 兩葉 (liǎng yè). In Chinese, the character for "lobe" is the same as the character for "leaf," reinforcing the connection to the Wood element.</note>. To what does this anatomical structure correspond?

Spring is when the **Myriad Things** <note>original: 萬物 (wànwù); a traditional philosophical term referring to all things in existence.</note> first begin to sprout and take life; at this stage, they are still young and delicate. Their "intent" <note>original: 意 (yì); here referring to the internal direction or budding consciousness of a living thing.</note> has not yet found a fixed place to settle or attach.

In terms of the body’s energetic cycles, the Liver is still close to **Supreme Yin** <note>original: 太陰 (tàiyīn); the phase associated with deep internal storage and the end of Winter.</note> and is not yet far from **Supreme Yang** <note>original: 太陽 (tàiyáng); the phase associated with full expansion and the peak of Summer.</note>. Because it exists in this transitional state between the two extremes, it is as if it still possesses **"two hearts"** <note>original: 兩心 (liǎng xīn); a metaphor for a dual nature or a state of indecision and transition.</note>. Therefore, it is formed with two lobes, which also corresponds to the way a young plant puts forth its first leaves.

# The Thirty-Third Difficulty

The Thirty-third Difficulty of the *Classic of Difficult Issues* <note>original: 三十三難 (Sānshí sān nán). A foundational text of Chinese medicine formatted as a series of 81 questions and answers.</note> states: The **Liver** is green and corresponds to **Wood**, while the **Lungs** are white and correspond to **Metal**. If you place the **Liver** in water, it sinks, yet **Wood** normally floats in water. If you place the **Lungs** in water, they float, yet **Metal** normally sinks in water. What is the meaning behind this?

The answer is that the **Liver** is not "pure" **Wood**. In the system of the Ten Heavenly Stems <note>The Ten Heavenly Stems are an ancient system used to track time and the movements of the Five Phases.</note>, the Liver is associated with <term>Yi-Jue</term> <note>original: 乙角 (yǐ-jué). 'Yi' is the second Heavenly Stem (Soft Wood), and 'Jue' is the musical note associated with Wood.</note>. It is the "soft" counterpart to <term>Geng</term> <note>Geng (庚) is the seventh Heavenly Stem, representing Hard Metal.</note>. On a large scale, we speak of **Yin** and **Yang**; on a small scale, we speak of **Husband and Wife**. 

The **Liver** releases its subtle **Yang** energy and absorbs subtle **Yin** energy. Its "intent" or natural inclination is attracted to **Metal** <note>In the "Husband and Wife" theory of the Five Phases, the "Soft Wood" (Liver/Yi) is cosmically attracted to "Hard Metal" (Geng).</note>. Furthermore, it travels primarily through the **Yin** paths of the body. Therefore, the **Liver** sinks when it meets water.

The **Lungs**, likewise, are not "pure" **Metal**. They are associated with <term>Xin-Shang</term> <note>original: 辛商 (xīn-shāng). 'Xin' is the eighth Heavenly Stem (Soft Metal), and 'Shang' is the musical note associated with Metal.</note>. They are the "soft" counterpart to <term>Bing</term> <note>Bing (丙) is the third Heavenly Stem, representing Hard Fire.</note>. In terms of **Husband and Wife**, the **Lungs** release their subtle **Yin** energy to "marry" and approach **Fire**. Because the **Lungs'** natural inclination is toward **Fire** and they travel primarily through the **Yang** paths of the body, they float when they meet water.

Why is it, then, that when the **Lungs** are "ripened" <note>original: 熟 (shú). This can mean "cooked" or "matured." In this medical context, it refers to a state where the organ's temporary energetic attractions are stripped away.</note> they return to sinking, and when the **Liver** is "ripened" it returns to floating? By this, we know that <term>Xin</term> (Soft Metal) must eventually return to its root, <term>Geng</term> (Hard Metal), and <term>Yi</term> (Soft Wood) must eventually return to <term>Jia</term> <note>Jia (甲) is the first Heavenly Stem, representing Hard Wood.</note>.

# Song of the Various Points of the Governing Vessel

On the **Governing Vessel** <note>original: 督脈 (Dū mài). Known as the "Sea of Yang," this channel runs up the spine and over the head to the mouth.</note>, **Gum Junction** <note>original: 齦交 (Yínjiāo)</note> is situated in the area inside the upper lip. **Exchange End** <note>original: 兌端 (Duìduān)</note> is located exactly at the very tip of the upper lip. The shape of the head is high in the north <note>referring to the back and crown</note> and slopes down toward the south <note>the face</note>. First, measure the distance between the front and back hairlines. This space is divided into twelve <term>proportional inches</term> <note>original: 寸 (cùn). One *cun* above the hairline is the position of **Upper Star** <note>original: 上星 (Shàngxīng)</note>. Three and a half *cun* above the hairline is **Frontal Vertex** <note>original: 前頂 (Qiándǐng)</note>. At the center, five *cun* above the hairline, lies **Hundred Meetings** <note>original: 百會 (Bǎihuì)</note>. <gloss>Located in the center of the vertex, within the hair whorl, it is deep enough to hold a bean. Now, if the total measurement is said to be twelve *cun*, why does the actual count of points only reach eleven *cun*? *   The **Triple Burner** <term>original: 三焦 (sān jiāo); the system responsible for the transit of energy and fluids</term> is called the **Official of Irrigation** <note>This title refers to its role in maintaining the body's "waterways" and ensuring proper drainage.</note>. 
*   The **Bladder** is the **Official of the Provincial Capital** <note>original: 州都之官 (zhōudōu zhī guān); a metaphor for a central reservoir where fluids converge.</note>.

This is because the **Lower Burner** <term>original: 下焦 (xià jiāo); the lower division of the torso, including the kidneys, intestines, and bladder</term> acts like a **drainage ditch**, storing and discharging to regulate the body's water levels. However, within the *Ying* <note>The cooling, physical, and fluid aspects of the body.</note> of the Lower Burner, there exists a spark of *Yang* <note>The warming, active, and transformative aspect of energy.</note>. It is from this spark that energy ascends through the Middle and Upper Burners, and thus the **Defensive Qi** <term>original: 衛氣 (wèi qì); the body's protective energy barrier</term> is born.

### The Thirtieth Difficulty
<note>original: 三十難 (Sānshí nán). This refers to the 30th chapter of the *Classic of Difficulties*, a seminal Han dynasty medical text that clarifies complex points of the *Yellow Emperor’s Inner Classic*.</note>

The **Thirtieth Difficulty** states:

The movement of **Nutritive Qi** <note>original: 榮氣 (róng qì); also known as Ying Qi, this is the refined energy derived from food that flows within the blood vessels to nourish the body.</note> always follows the **Defensive Qi** <note>original: 衞氣 (wèi qì); the "fierce" or "unrefined" energy that circulates on the periphery of the body to protect against external illness.</note>; they do not separate from one another.

The Classics state that humans receive their energy from **grain** <term>original: 穀 (gǔ); here meaning food and drink in general</term>. When food enters the **Stomach**, its essence is transmitted to the **five Zang organs** <term>the solid, "storing" organs: Heart, Liver, Spleen, Lungs, and Kidneys</term> and the **six Fu organs** <term>the hollow, "transmitting" organs: Small Intestine, Large Intestine, Gallbladder, Bladder, Stomach, and Triple Burner</term>. All of these organs receive this energy.

The **clear** portion <note>the most refined and subtle essence</note> becomes the **Nutritive Qi**, while the **turbid** portion <note>the more robust and active essence</note> becomes the **Defensive Qi**.

The **Nutritive Qi** travels inside the vessels, and the **Defensive Qi** travels outside the vessels. They circulate around the body without ceasing. After fifty revolutions <note>traditionally, the Qi is said to complete 50 full cycles through the body's meridians in a 24-hour period</note>, they meet again in a grand convergence.

**Yin and Yang** <note>representing the internal/Nutritive and external/Defensive energies respectively</note> penetrate one another like a ring that has no beginning or end. Thus, we know that the **Nutritive** and **Defensive Qi** follow one another.

<summary>This text explains the origin of the body's energy from food and distinguishes between the internal Nutritive Qi and the external Defensive Qi, describing their inseparable, cyclical movement.</summary>
