# PRINCE MAURICE,

## Landgrave of Hesse, etc., <note>The "etc." refers to Maurice's additional titles, which included Count of Katzenelnbogen, Diez, Ziegenhain, and Nidda.</note> my most gracious Lord.

Great Prince, both the immense greatness of your name and your singular kindness <note>original: "clementia"</note> toward me have had such power that they have not only made me mindful of such great favors, but have even made me bold. For no interval of time or place ought to snatch the memory of your virtues from me, nor could it prevent me from rendering to you an account of my leisure and my duty. Indeed, ever since I arrived at this University <term>Academy (original: Academia): A term for a place of higher learning; here it likely refers to the University of Marburg, which Maurice transformed into a center of Reformed learning.</term> by your authority and counsel, I have applied myself not only to be diligent in the duty that my loyalty required of me, but also to satisfy your expectations and recommendation—by which you easily surpassed both my own weakness and the opinion of many others. Therefore, I had to strive with greater effort, both because of your remarkable judgment of me (which made me feel somewhat modest, given my insignificance <note>original: "tenuitate mea"</note>) and because of the difficulty of this duty of mine, to which I am scarcely equal. I wished for the reason of my leisure to be clear to this University, and to ensure that you would not regret your recommendation. Having entered this arena of studies and labors, while I reflect on my progress and bring forth now one thing and now another, this <term>disputation (original: disputatio): A formal academic discourse or thesis defended in public.</term> concerning Public Law <note>original: "Iure publico"</note> happened to emerge from my work. While the difficulty of this study is great enough to deter some from such a praiseworthy task, its dignity has such power over me that it leads me toward the greatness of so important a subject—

*Indeed, many are so impatient that unless they expect the greatest hope of profit, they do not even "deserve heaven"—as the saying goes <note>original: "ut ille dicebat" (as that man said); likely a reference to a classical proverb or a specific author such as Seneca, suggesting that people only strive for high goals if there is a reward.</note>—to apply their minds to such a barren study. *For you, who sit at the helm of the State, cannot be ignorant of the laws of the Empire, of Religion, of wars, the treaties of allies, the <term>secrets of state (original: arcana): A central concept in Clapmarius's work, referring to the hidden strategies or "state secrets" used by rulers to maintain power.</term>, the method of delivering an opinion, the laws of decreeing or reporting, and three hundred <note>The number "three hundred" (Latin: trecenta) was often used idiomatically by Roman and Renaissance writers to mean "countless" or "an infinite number."</note> other matters of that kind, without the greatest detriment to the Commonwealth. Farewell and live long, most excellent of Princes, the immortal glory of the German name. ### IV.

Tacitus says in the third book of his *Annals*: *"Great,"* he says, *"was the appearance of that day, on which the Senate examined the favors granted by their ancestors, the treaties of allies, the decrees of those who were powerful even before Roman might prevailed, and the very religion of the divine powers."* <note>original: "Magna... introspexit"</note> Truly, these things savor of public law.

### V.

<margin>Chapters of sacred law.</margin>
*The chapters of sacred law are generally these: The laws of Religion, of rites, of sacrifices, of the Augurs, of funerals, of temples, of the spirits of the dead <note>original: "Manium" (The Manes), the souls of deceased ancestors worshipped as protective deities.</note>, and of oracles.* Regarding these, fragments from Varro <note>Marcus Terentius Varro (116–27 BC), a scholar who wrote extensively on Roman antiquities and religion.</note> survive, and histories abound with examples; if only there are those who can explain them clearly.

### VI.

Tacitus beautifully uses the terms *sacred things of the kingdom* and *sacred things of an embassy*; others use *sacred things of war, sacred things of treaties, and sacred things of the household gods*. So too, the jurists call the laws of Majesty, and those things allowed by the law of the kingdom, the *Sacred of Sacreds*. <note>original: "Sacra sacrorum"</note> Nevertheless, all these are not strictly part of sacred law, but of public law.

### VII.

<margin>Chapters of public law.</margin>
*The chapters of public law are these: The laws of Empire, of Sovereignty, of Magistrates, of the Senate, of the Assemblies, of coinage, of the Orders <note>The "Orders" refers to the social classes of Rome, such as the Senators and the Knights (Equites).</note>, the laws of the citizens' liberty, of voting, of markets, of taxes, of tributes, of the Treasury <term>Aerarium: The public treasury of the Roman state, separate from the Emperor's private funds.</term>, of the Census, the laws of treaties, of embassies, of wars, of victory, and other things of this kind.*

Emperor, Lord, Caesar, King, Prince—and the many other words for sovereignty—were in reality one and the same thing. No matter how much Pliny <note>Pliny the Younger, who in his "Panegyricus" tried to distinguish the 'Princeps' (first citizen) from a 'Dominus' (master/tyrant).</note> may argue otherwise. This refers to the political maneuvering of the early Roman Emperors who maintained absolute power while appearing to respect traditional Republican titles.</note>

->IX.<-

However, there are differences between Majesty, Supreme Command (*Imperium*), or <term>the sovereign power</term> <note>original: τὸ κύριον (*to kyrion*), a Greek term for the supreme authority in a state.</note>, Power, Lordship, Force, Dignity, Authority, the Principate (or the Beginning), Counsel, Necessity, and Dominion. Nevertheless, many writers incorrectly confuse most of these terms with one another.

->X.<-

<margin>*In whose hands the sacred law rested among the Romans.*</margin>
From the beginning, sacred law was in the hands of the Kings; later, it was transferred to the high Senate (*although learned men disagree on this point*). When the Republic was changed, just as with all other things, the Emperors transferred matters pertaining to divine affairs and religion to themselves. Tacitus and Josephus <note>Flavius Josephus (c. 37–100 AD), a Romano-Jewish historian.</note> write about this. There also exists a legal opinion by Demetrius Chomatenus <note>A 13th-century Byzantine archbishop and expert in canon law.</note> supporting this view.

->XI.<-

Nevertheless, under Augustus, Tiberius, and a few others, the Roman Senate still held great authority regarding sacred law. This is evident from that <term>Decree of the Senate</term> <note>original: "SCto." abbreviation for *Senatus Consulto*.</note> stating that Christ our Savior should not be counted among the gods. <note>The author refers to a famous tradition, cited by early church fathers like Tertullian, that Tiberius proposed the divinity of Christ to the Senate, who then rejected it.</note> Likewise, the Priests (*Pontifices*) were to see which traditions of the Soothsayers <note>original: "Aruspicum" (Haruspices), diviners who interpreted the will of the gods from the entrails of sacrificed animals.</note> should be retained and strengthened; however, under the remaining Emperors, this authority was meager.

In the Roman Republic, the Priests never held a rightful legal title of authority over the Emperors, nor even formerly over the Kings, as even Otto of Freising <note>original: "Frisingense." A 12th-century bishop and historian.</note> testifies. # Disputation I.

<margin>
How it was mixed under Augustus.
</margin>
...[liber]ty, though the writer from Angers <note>Referring to an author from Angers, France, likely a legal commentator or historian.</note> denies this. Therefore, what Tacitus said of Nerva applies even more truly to Augustus: that he mixed things once considered incompatible—the Principate <note>The early period of the Roman Empire where the ruler styled himself as "First Citizen."</note> and liberty.

### XIV.

For this reason, Augustus acted wisely by rejecting the titles of Lord, King, and Dictator, and instead took the title of *Princeps* <note>First Citizen</note>. He also wisely compelled the Senate through the exercise of "tribunitian power" <note>The legal authority of a Tribune of the Plebs, which allowed the Emperor to veto legislation and hold personal sanctity.</note>. It was in this same spirit that Gregory the Great sharply warned the Patriarch of Constantinople not to call himself "Universal Lord," but rather "Servant of the Servants of God" <note>original: *Servum Servorum*. A title still used by the Pope to emphasize humility despite holding supreme authority.</note>. The conduct of Vitellius <note>Roman Emperor during the "Year of the Four Emperors," 69 AD.</note> was foolish by comparison; when his Empire was in peril, he issued an edict insisting he be called "Caesar," a title he had previously despised.

### XV.

Tacitus truly writes in the first book of his *Histories*: *There was no other remedy for a country in discord than for it to be ruled by one man.* <note>original: "Non aliud... regeretur."</note> Pomponius the Jurisconsult, Pliny, and Cassius Dio all testify to the same. Therefore, it was rightly and with the best legal justification that *Augustus took under his command all things exhausted by civil discord.* <note>original: "Augustus cuncta discordiis fessa sub imperium accepit."</note> I am not certain, however, if the same can be said of Julius Caesar.

### XVI.

For even if we grant that Caesar had a just cause for waging war against Pompey—which he certainly did—he nevertheless had no cause for overturning the Republic and seizing the empire for himself. Fabius Maximus, Tiberius Gracchus, and others acted much more correctly when they set aside their private hatreds for the sake of the public good.

These are the formal administrative rules governing the imperial household and palace.</note>. The former should not be made public...

### X V I I I.

*Julius Caesar* said of *Sulla* that because he had resigned the Dictatorship, he "did not know his letters" <note>original: *literas nescivisse*. A play on words suggesting Sulla was politically illiterate or foolish to give up absolute power.</note>. The same could be said of *Caesar* himself, for he dismissed his Praetorian guards. For this reason, the most learned *Savile* <note>Sir Henry Savile (1549–1622), an English scholar and translator of Tacitus.</note> calls him a "solecism" <note>In this context, a political blunder or error in judgment.</note> and a "bad grammarian." How much more correctly did *Tiberius* station guards at the funeral of *Augustus*, however much he was mocked for it by the people.

### X I X.

Among the *Romans*, the *Flamen Dialis* <term>the high priest of Jupiter, who was subject to many strict religious taboos</term> was once not permitted to go into a province with military command; nor was the priest of Mars <note>referred to here as *Martiali*</note>, as recorded in *Valerius Maximus*. Therefore, the same *Valerius* says that the supreme command "yielded to religion." This was done much more submissively than the way the "spiritual and civil swords" <note>The medieval doctrine of the 'Two Swords,' representing the power of the Church and the power of the State.</note> are used today, as *Otto* <note>Otto of Freising (c. 1114–1158), a bishop and chronicler who wrote on the history of the two powers.</note> testifies, whose words I cannot help but quote here:
> "Those priests," he says, "seem to be entirely at fault who attempt to strike at a kingdom with their own sword—which they themselves hold only by the grace of Kings—unless perhaps they think to imitate David, who first struck down the Philistine by the power of God, and afterwards cut his throat with the Philistine's own sword."
Nevertheless, *Arnold of Brescia* <note>A 12th-century reformer and rebel who argued that the clergy should possess no temporal power.</note> was too harsh in saying that bishops who hold temporal commands and royal rights cannot be saved; for this reason he was burned, according to *Gunther* <note>Gunther of Pairis, a Cistercian monk and poet who chronicled the deeds of Frederick Barbarossa.</note>.

We believe that Emperors were safer with two than with one. Nonetheless, *Agrippina* was no less right [in her reasoning], if the Praetorian cohorts <term>the elite bodyguards of the Roman Emperor</term> were to be ruled by one...
