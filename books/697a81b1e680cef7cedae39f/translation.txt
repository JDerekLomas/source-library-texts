# I.

NOTHING is more common today—and indeed has been in almost every age—than for not only many **political thinkers** <note>original: *Politicis*</note>, but even certain **theologians**, to flatter Kings and other Great Men. Above all, they seek to strengthen an absolute and free power over their subjects. To this same end, they misuse the authority of Holy Scripture, so that the **conscience of Princes** might be made more unburdened, and the subjects more strictly bound to a rule comparable to **tyranny**, to be endured with a patient spirit. I do not in the least blame those who, otherwise most zealous for legitimate liberty, defend even the harsh power of Kings, not through the vice of flattery, but solely with the aim of cutting down any rebellious attempts "in the blade" <note>original: *in herbâ*; a proverbial expression meaning to stop something before it has a chance to develop, similar to "nipping it in the bud."</note>, as they say, and not allowing them to grow in any way. However, I would not wish it to be called the **Royal Law** <note>original: *Regium Jus*</note> or **Regal Right**; for if that term is rashly seized upon, it makes those ruling in this age the slaves of eternal destruction hereafter. I now wish to examine more closely two passages of Holy Scripture which are pressed into service here; and first indeed, that famous **Royal Law** which seems to be set forth in the first book of **Samuel**, *chapter 8*, and which, according to the **Vulgate version** <note>The standard Latin Bible translation used in Western Europe for centuries.</note>, reads as follows:

> 11. *This will be the law of the King, who is to rule over you: He will take your sons, and place them in his chariots; and he will make for himself horsemen and runners for his chariots.*

Those whom we usually call the Seventy Translators <note>The Septuagint, the earliest Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible (3rd–2nd century BCE).</note> render the phrase there as the **decree of the king** <note>original Greek: δικαίωμα τοῦ βασιλέως (*dikaioma tou basileos*)</note>. For the word for "statute" <note>original: נמוסא (*nimusa*)</note>, like many other Aramaic words, is a loanword from the Greek; in both its spelling and its meaning, it signifies the same thing as "law" <note>original Greek: νόμος (*nomos*)</note>. The excellent Monarch of Great Britain, **King James**, published a booklet several years ago—originally with his name withheld—titled *On the Law of Free Monarchies*. <note>See Thomas Rosa, in his *Account of the Virtues of King James*, page 50.</note> This work is now included among the collected *Royal Works*, where on page 184 he appears to judge that the constitution of the monarchy among the Jews, and this very law regarding it, is a model to which all Christian and well-ordered Monarchies can, and indeed must, be conformed; as it was founded by God Himself and sanctioned by a most sacred oracle issued from His own mouth.

3. 4. But it is generally the case that I consider truer, or at least more probable, the opinion of those who think no **Right of the King** <note>original Latin: *jus Regis*</note> is established here. Instead, they believe that everything Samuel says in that passage should be referred to the wicked habits and customs of neighboring Kings. This was the view supported by Mr. **Matthias Hafenreffer** <note>A prominent Lutheran theologian (1561–1619) known for his work in dogmatics.</note> while he was alive—a man of all-encompassing learning and especially true piety, who deserves the highest reverence. He even defended this position laboriously in a certain publicly held dissertation.

Thus, the discourse here seems to be not about a privilege or a legal right, but about **tyranny and injustice**. Even legitimate power, if it is supreme and perpetual, is not seldom accustomed to harass its subjects in this way. For we humans possess such a weakness of mind that, when granted great power, nothing is more quickly lost than the moderation of that power. <note>See my *Politics*, Book 1, Chapter 9, Number 24, Verse 1.</note>

So too does **Doctor Marta** <note>Giacomo Antonio Marta, a legal theorist and advocate for papal jurisdiction.</note> write in his *Treatise on Jurisdiction*, Part 1, Chapter 35, Number 1 and following: he states that when Samuel had consecrated Saul as King by God's command, he composed a book concerning matters of the **Prince’s Majesty**. However, it is recorded by the Hebrews that successor Kings burned that book so that they might exercise tyranny over their subjects without any restraint of law. For this reason, Marta believes **Melanchthon** <note>Philip Melanchthon (1497–1560), a key leader of the Protestant Reformation and a colleague of Martin Luther.</note> is deceived in thinking that the rights of Kings consist of those things recorded in the eighth chapter of Samuel. Instead, those verses are more correctly understood as describing what Kings *will do*—not by a legitimate law of reigning, but by abusing their power.

**Johannes Molanus** <note>A Catholic theologian (1533–1585) who wrote on the ethics of legal promises.</note> agrees in his work *On Keeping Faith with Heretics*, Book 4, Chapter 14. Likewise, **George Buchanan** <note>A Scottish historian and humanist (1506–1582) who argued for limited monarchy.</note> in *On the Right of the Kingdom among the Scots* <note>original: *de Jure regni apud Scotos*</note> (page Bb. 8 in my edition) says: we must be careful lest we think that those things which the Lord detests in the lives of tyrants were granted by Him to Kings as a right.

And similarly **Johannes Althusius** <note>A Calvinist political philosopher (1563–1638) often called the father of modern federalism.</note> argues at length in his *Politics*, Chapter 19, Number 51 and following. He thinks Samuel is speaking there about unjust, immoderate, and unnecessary taxes and services, which are exacted according to the whim of the King rather than for the utility of the **Commonwealth** <note>original: *Reipublicae*</note>. He considers that verse 28 <note>The author cites verse 28, but 1 Samuel 8 ends at verse 22. This is likely a reference to verse 18, which describes the people crying out to God.</note> implies this when it says: that therefore the Israelites [would cry out] to the **Lord** <note>original: *Jehovam*</note> for relief from these calamities...

5. There are many arguments and reasons that could be brought forward to strengthen this opinion, of which I will present several here: (1) This section is said to be intended only to strike fear into the Jews, as **Rabbi Judah** says, according to **Drusius** <note>Joannes Drusius (1550–1616), a Flemish Protestant scholar of the Old Testament and Hebrew.</note> in his *On the More Difficult Passages of Samuel, ibid.* From the final purpose of a speech, we investigate its intent and meaning; that purpose extends, restricts, and declares the entire arrangement, and must be principally considered, as stated by **Tiberius Decianus** <note>An Italian jurist (1509–1581) influential in criminal law.</note> in *Response 41, beginning by following the order of doubts under number 21, book 1.* The goal of this proposition by Samuel is **discouraging**, consisting of the evils that could happen on the occasion (though not by right) of this petition for a king. Samuel establishes or defines nothing; he only threatens the evils or injuries to be feared from a King. (2) Samuel speaks from the mouth of an angry God—an argument from *verses 7 and following of the said chapter 8 of Samuel*—and God says this in express words in **Hosea chapter 13, verse 11**:

> I gave you a King in my anger, and I took him away in my wrath.

Therefore, the power of the future King is announced to the people as a punishment for their recklessness. Nor is the following conclusion valid: "A Prince cannot be punished by his subjects; therefore, when he unjustly harms them, he does not sin against them." For being the victim of an injury is a different matter from being able to avenge it; it does not follow that if the latter is removed, the former is taken away. See the notes of **Emanuel Tremellius** <note>A Jewish-Italian convert to Protestantism and a noted Hebraist (1510–1580).</note> on that Psalm 51, and **Lord Johann Arndt** <note>A German Lutheran theologian (1555–1621) whose work *True Christianity* became a classic of devotional literature.</note> in his *Exposition of the Psalter, Psalm 51, sermon 2, number 2.* To God alone was the "blind crime of the house" <note>original: "cæcum domus scelus," a quote from Virgil's *Aeneid*, Book 1, referring to hidden treachery.</note> revealed. David had indeed sinned against **Uriah**, his most faithful soldier, and against the entire army—whom he had ordered into danger for the sake of killing Uriah—but while he was weighing how much he had despised and offended God by this crime, he was so tormented and torn in his soul that he felt only one thing, complained of only one thing, and said to the Lord: *Against you, against you only have I sinned.* And he meant: "The fact that I have so despised your word, and so greatly provoked you—this, I say, kills me, tears me apart, and tortures me."
