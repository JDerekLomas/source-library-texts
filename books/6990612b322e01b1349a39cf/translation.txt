# TO THE MOST BLESSED FATHER
## AND LORD, LORD GREGORY XIII
## SUPREME PONTIFF <note>original: "PONTIFICI MAXIMO" — The formal title for the Pope</note>

->FRANCISCO PEÑA.<-

<image-desc>A large floriated and historiated decorative initial 'D' featuring a figure holding a book, surrounded by intricate scrollwork and foliage.</image-desc>

*While the pious princes of the Christian Commonwealth <note>original: "Reipub. Chriſtianæ" — refers to the collective body of Christian nations under the influence of the Church</note>, powerful on land and sea, strive to bravely defeat the enemies of the Catholic religion in open warfare with much blood of soldiers shed—so that they may protect the unity of the Church, the authority of the Apostolic See, and your power, Most Blessed Father, from their bold and impious attempts—there are those who, in leisure and peace within private walls, refute the wicked opinions of those same violators of sacred religion by writing. They arm, teach, or interpret rights and laws to either convert or coerce them, so that when they see themselves pressed and restrained both from afar and at hand—since they are not kept to their duty by the love of virtue—they may at least be turned away from wrongdoing by the bitterness of punishments and the magnitude of the penalty. Among these latter ones, although I may hold the lowest place in both sharpness of mind and brilliance of learning, I nevertheless most willingly attempt everything that seems most convenient and suitable for repressing the impious audacity of heretics and restraining their monstrous depravity <note>original: "prauitatem" — literally crookedness or distortion of faith</note>. This will be faithfully witnessed, as I hope, one day by some of my works on the punishments of heretics which I have been diligently contemplating from the beginning of the rising Church until these times. It is witnessed now in part by that labor, of whatever quality, which I happily undertook last year, when I turned all my study and industry toward illuminating the Directory for Inquisitors by Nicholas Eymeric. It is indeed a distinguished work, venerable for its age, in which the main points of the highest religion are contained; those things which lead to the coercion and extirpation of heretical iniquity are piously and learnedly prescribed, and whatever is required to safely exercise the sacred office of the Inquisition is demonstrated with skill and method. Since this work has been printed in Rome, complete in all its parts, it pleased me to dedicate it to Your Holiness primarily because the author, who defends the Christian religion and shows how to bravely defeat and restrain heretics, should be kindly received by Your Holiness—who has been divinely appointed as the head of the Christian Commonwealth—so that he may be defended from so many bands of enemies and bring no small utility to the affairs of the Catholic faith. I hope this will most certainly happen, as no one is ignorant, Most Blessed Father, that although you are distracted by the mass of many and great affairs of the entire Christian Commonwealth, you nevertheless most vigilantly care for what pertains to the faith with singular providence. You direct all care and concern especially toward ensuring that the cause of religion remains intact, without the storms of new opinions, and that the uncorrupted and inviolate faith is fostered and spread everywhere. That this may be further achieved, I pray to God the Best and Greatest, whose business in His Church you handle most prudently and ardently, that He may preserve Your Holiness in health for a long time.*

# THE LIFE OF NICHOLAS EYMERIC
## BY THE SAME FRANCISCO PEÑA
->BRIEFLY COMPILED.<-

---

<image-desc>A woodcut emblem or printer's mark depicting a heraldic shield with vertical stripes (paly), likely representing the arms of the Crown of Aragon. The shield is surmounted by an ornate crown and framed by decorative foliate scrollwork.</image-desc>

Nicholas Eymeric was born in Catalonia, a distinguished province of Tarraconensis Spain <note>A Roman name for the region of northeastern Spain</note>, according to the certain opinion of learned men; but many think it uncertain which city raised him. For some make him a native of Perpignan, others of Elne, and there are those who say he was from Barcelona. There is a constant report that he was born of a noble lineage, tracing his origin to the ancient Eymeric family, which remains noble and distinguished in that principality today. He flourished primarily during the times of Popes Urban V and Gregory XI, and King Peter IV of Aragon, who received the surname "the Ceremonious," around the year of Our Lord 1360. 

At that time in the kingdoms of Aragon, Nicholas Rossell was famous for his learning and holiness; he was a Master of Sacred Theology in the Dominican Order <note>original: "instituti Dominicani"</note> and Inquisitor General in the entire dominion of the King of Aragon. He was created Cardinal of the Title of Saint Sixtus by Pope Innocent VI. When it was sought who would be his successor in such a great office of the Inquisition, this other Nicholas of ours, surnamed Eymeric, was found. He was a man endowed with learning and skill (as Sebastian Olmeda testifies in his chronicles on the beginning and progress of the Order of Friars Preachers <note>The Dominicans</note>), and he was appointed Inquisitor General of heretical depravity in those same kingdoms, as he himself testifies in his work (part 2, question 10). 

For in this man, besides an illustrious knowledge of theology, there was much expertise in Canon Law and a far from common knowledge of civil laws. This easily placed him above the literati of his age, since these skills are especially necessary for exercising the office of the Inquisition. Eymeric was created Inquisitor General around the year of Our Lord 1358 or 1367, as we gather clearly from part 2, question 47 of his work. 

For the business of the faith, he left Spain and set out for Avignon, where the Apostolic See <note>the Papal court</note> then resided. He was kindly received by Pope Gregory XI and appointed as his Chaplain (as he himself says). From the Pope, he frequently obtained many things necessary for the more convenient exercise of the office of the Holy Inquisition regarding a variety of business and cases; this is indicated, among other things, by a certain rescript <note>a formal written decree or reply</note> which he himself recites in part 3, question 10. 

Finally, when he was already an old man and adorned with much learning and experience, he published a distinguished work which he called the *Directorium Inquisitorum* <note>The Directory for Inquisitors</note>, a very fitting name, because its primary purpose is to provide the rules by which inquisitors may be directed and instructed in recognizing and uprooting heresies. In it, he gathered almost everything regarding the business of the Inquisition and the punishments of heretics from various places and authors; in this matter, he imitated (as we have written elsewhere) most learned men. This labor was once very necessary due to the great scarcity of books; today, however, it is also very useful and suited to the studies of Inquisitors. 

I find it recorded that this work was composed by him in Avignon. Some say it was compiled in Aragon, but nothing certain remains. He had many adversaries, but this is not surprising, for such is the condition, or rather the misfortune, of learned men. Virtue also has its opposing and obstructing vices; and the higher the towers are, the greater the blasts of wind that shake them. As the poet rightly put it: *lightnings strike the highest mountains* <note>original: "feriunt summos fulmina montes," a quote from Horace</note>. This is the great and final praise of the man: that he held all heretics in sharp hatred and detested all heresies; but matters regarding the propagation of the Christian faith and the Catholic religion, he cultivated with the greatest religious devotion and piety. And such indeed was the life of the man, as far as we have been able to gather from the monuments of antiquity.
