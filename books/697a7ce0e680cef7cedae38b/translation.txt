§. 2. Common principles must be sought in the pure disciplines, namely Algebra, Geometry, and Arithmetic.

§. 3. But the **specific principles** derived from these common ones are as follows:

1. Water, when left to its own liberty <note>meaning when it is unaffected by external forces other than gravity</note>, affects a spherical shape; that is, it strives to remain in equilibrium, or always at an equal distance from the center of the earth.

2. Water enclosed in a curved pipe <note>original: *fistulæ recurvæ*; this refers to a U-shaped tube or siphon</note>, held perpendicularly upright and of the same width throughout, maintains equal heights.

3. Water has a certain proportion of weight relative to other bodies, both those that are lighter and those that are heavier.

§. 4. **POSTULATES**, which must be granted before we proceed to the demonstrations <note>mathematical or logical proofs</note>, are as follows:

1. That water cannot be compressed or expanded by weight or by any mechanical force <note>The author here asserts the incompressibility of water, a standard assumption in classical hydrostatics</note>.

2. That water, with the addition of heat, can be thinned <note>made less dense</note>, or indeed entirely reduced to vapor.

3. That water can be condensed <note>made more dense</note> by means of cold.

4. That some liquids can be frozen by cold and take on the nature of solid bodies.

5. That some solid bodies, by means of heat, can be liquefied and take on the nature of water <note>meaning a liquid state</note>.

Thus, the *sixth hour of the Preparation* would be none other than the *third hour of the day*. This was the opinion of the Blessed *Augustine <note>St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430 AD), one of the most influential theologians in Western Christianity.</note> in his Harmony of the Evangelists, book 3, chapter 13*, where he states that the third hour of the day and the sixth hour of the preparation are one and the same. He argues that John does not mean the sixth hour of the day, but the sixth hour counted from the start of the Preparation—the preparation for the sacrifice of the Lord (which is the true Passover for us). *Ludovicus Ramirez* argues similarly in his *On the Pentecontarch, chapter 38*.

Of all these explanations, none would please us more than the idea that the "third hour" refers to the third quarter of the day, and the "sixth hour" refers to the sixth of the twelve parts of the day. However, this opinion is significantly hindered by the fact that no ancient author ever used the word *hour* to mean a "quarter" of the day (that is, a three-hour block). Therefore, I greatly fear that some error has crept into the text through the carelessness of a scribe.

My mind leans most strongly toward the theory that a scribe confused the numeral symbols for 6 and 3 <note>original: the *episēmon* ϛ (6) and Γ (3). In ancient Greek notation, these characters were visually similar and easily mistaken by copyists.</note>. This is also the opinion of Saint Jerome <note>The 4th-century scholar famous for the Vulgate translation of the Bible.</note>—if he is indeed the author—in his commentary on *Psalm 77*; it is likewise held by others mentioned by Theophylact <note>An 11th-century Byzantine archbishop and prolific biblical commentator.</note> in his notes on *John, chapter 19*.

### Chapter XII. On the Hour at which [Our Savior was Crucified]

But even so, the matter is left in doubt: should *Mark* be corrected by *John*, or should *John* be corrected by *Mark*? Those whom *Theophylact* <note>A prominent 11th-century Byzantine Archbishop and biblical commentator.</note> praises have embraced the latter opinion. *Petavius* <note>Denis Pétau (1583–1652), a French Jesuit theologian and world-renowned expert on chronology.</note>, in *Book 12 of his On the Doctrine of Time, chapter 19*, reports that he possesses an unpublished work by *Peter of Alexandria* <note>A 4th-century Bishop and martyr.</note> which testifies that in the original manuscript of *John*, which was preserved at Ephesus, it was written that it occurred **"at about the third hour"** <note>original Greek: "ὥρᾳ ἦ ὡσεὶ τρίτῃ"</note>. If this were established as certain, the dispute would already be decided.

Now it is worth considering whether we should instead listen to those who follow the first opinion—such as *Thomas Cajetan* <note>A 16th-century Italian cardinal and philosopher.</note> and *Melchior Canus* <note>A Spanish Dominican theologian of the same era.</note> in their commentaries on the second chapter of Luke. To me, it seems more likely that *Mark* ought to be amended. And I think those who are willing to reflect will easily agree with us on how many things the Evangelists say occurred between the time Christ was led from Caiaphas to Pilate and the time he was crucified at Golgotha. 

For (to say nothing of the second accusation and the condemnation by the Jews carried out in the morning, as we have already said), He was accused by the Jews before Pilate, interrogated himself, sent to Herod, sent back to Pilate, and so many attempts were made by Pilate to have Him released, before He was finally sent outside the city. I am so far from thinking these things could have happened within a three-hour window that it is a wonder they occurred even within six hours, especially with Pilate weaving together so many delays. 

Add to this that *Matthew* also acknowledges that the sun failed at the sixth hour. How much more likely is it that Christ was crucified at that same hour, and the darkness followed shortly after, than that the sun failed three hours after Jesus was crucified? You may see, however, that *Nonnus* <note>A 5th-century Greek poet who wrote a poetic paraphrase of the Gospel of John.</note> held this latter view. But these words of the Evangelists indicate otherwise: **"Now it was about the sixth hour, and when the sixth hour had come, darkness fell over the whole land until the ninth hour."**
