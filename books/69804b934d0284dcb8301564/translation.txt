# To
## THE READER.

*<term>Although</term> <note>original: "Hoewel"; the text begins with a decorative initial letter 'H' featuring floral and architectural scrollwork.</note> it is well known to the lover of <term>poetry</term> <note>original: "dichtkunste"</note> in what manner poets of old have been accustomed to expand and portray their histories; nevertheless, since many—trained only in our mother tongue and being less practiced in the <term>colors of eloquence</term> <note>original: "verwen der welspreeckenheit"; a rhetorical term referring to the use of metaphors and stylistic flourishes to "paint" a scene with words.</note>—might easily stand amazed when they hear and see a sacred history presented more extensively than it was described by the sacred pen of the <term>Evangelists</term> <note>Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John, the traditional authors of the four Gospels in the New Testament.</note>; so it did not seem inadvisable to me to point out to them how the poet, like the painter, is free to set up his work according to demand and propriety, and to clothe it richly. For to unfold a history, one needs not only to embellish the conditions and appearances of places and persons, but also other circumstances that best suit them. One must therefore not think that the painter wishes to convince the viewer that his drawing and brush followed the life of ancient histories so closely that all his inventions and preparations did not differ from the matter itself. Whoever considers this freedom of art to be grounded in reason will also calmly grant it to the poet, who by right may graze so much more widely as thoughts can reach higher and deeper than the eyes can see; provided that in <term>biblical subject matter</term> <note>original: "bybelstoffe"</note> he generally stays within the bounds of a respectful <term>piety</term> <note>original: "godtvruchtigheit"</note> and <term>modesty</term> <note>original: "zedigheit"</note>.*

We, in order to follow from afar in the shadow of the ancient Stage-poets, chose from the difficulties that befell the sons of the Patriarch Jacob, the brothers in Dothan; and we preferred to take material from Moses' infallible history <note>The biblical account in the book of Genesis.</note> rather than from Worldly history or some Heathen fiction: since holy histories, above all others, always bring with them a certain divine majesty and venerable dignity, which are nowhere so much required as in tragedies. # S U M M A R Y.

*Joseph, the son of the patriarchs Jacob and Rachel, was sent by his father from the vale of Hebron toward Shechem to see how his brothers and the livestock were faring. On the way, while he was wandering about, he met a man—perhaps an Angel <note>In the biblical book of Genesis, Joseph is redirected by a mysterious "certain man," whom many commentators historically identified as the angel Gabriel.</note>—who set him on the right path and pointed toward Dothan. When his brothers saw the messenger approaching from a distance, they conspired together (out of hatred and enmity because of his reports on them, and out of jealousy over his dreams, as well as the great love their father bore for him above his other sons) to kill him and throw his corpse into a pit. Reuben, the eldest brother, opposed this and urged them not to stain their hands with his blood, hoping thereby to save the hated youth from the danger of death. Meanwhile, as Joseph arrived, his multi-colored coat <note>original: "veelverwigen rock" — This is the famous "coat of many colors," a symbol of Jacob's favoritism.</note> was stripped off, and he was lowered into a dry well. This being settled, they saw while they were eating a group of Ishmaelites and Midianites passing by from Gilead with their camels, laden with balm, myrrh, and spices, on their way to Egypt. On this occasion, Judah advised sparing Joseph’s life and selling him to these strangers, which was done. Reuben, who was not present at the time, later returned to the pit; finding his brother missing, he tore his clothes in grief and lamented to the other brothers. To cover up their deed, they slaughtered a goat and smeared the coat with blood, leading their old father to believe that a wild beast had devoured Joseph.*

*The tragedy begins at dawn and ends after midday. The Chorus of Angels speaks the prologue. The scene is set in Dothan, a region in Canaan.*
