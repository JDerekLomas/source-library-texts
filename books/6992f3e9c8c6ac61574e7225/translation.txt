# The Old Preface to the Terrestrial Map

As a youth, I studied the classics and learned of the mountains and rivers of the Nine Provinces <note>original: "九州" (Jiuzhou); an ancient name for the regions of China</note>. Upon reading the *Records of the Grand Historian* and seeing how Sima Qian <note>The father of Chinese historiography</note> traveled throughout the world, I was filled with deep admiration. Later, I climbed Mt. Kuaiji, sailed across Lake Dongting, and traveled freely through the regions of Jing and Xiang. I surveyed the Huai and Si rivers, crossed the lands of Han, Wei, Qi, and Lu, and followed the tracks of carts through Yan and Zhao until I reached the capital itself. From there, by imperial decree, I performed sacrifices at Mt. Songgao, went south to the Tongbai Mountains, and further south to Mt. Zhurong until I reached the sea.

Everywhere I went, I questioned the elders about local lore, searched for ancient ruins, and examined how the borders of prefectures and counties had changed over time. I contemplated the names and realities of the mountains and rivers, testing them against the stone inscriptions of the *Map of the Tracks of Yu* <note>original: "禹迹圖" (Yuji Tu); a famous 12th-century map</note> in Ganyang and Anlu, and the *Map of the Unified Empire* from Jian’an. I realized then that the works of my predecessors were riddled with errors.

I resolved to exhaust my heart and strength to correct them. I consulted the *Commentary on the Water Classic* by Li Daoyuan of Wei, the *Tongdian* <note>An encyclopedic history of institutions</note>, the *Records of the Nine Realms* from the Yuanfeng era, and the current *Great Unified Record* of the imperial library. I meticulously compared ancient and modern accounts and measured distances. Even when I discovered discrepancies, I did not dare rely solely on my own judgment. When scholar-officials of the court were sent on missions to the four corners of the empire, I would always ask them to verify details with the provincial governments and gather the words of many. I drew maps for each location and eventually merged them into one.

Beginning in the year *Xinhai* of the Zhida era [1311] and ending in the year *Gengshen* of the Yanyou era [1320], my work was finally completed. Within it, rivers and mountains are intricately woven like embroidery; cities, roads, and passages are connected in every direction, both straight and winding. Everything is laid out with the utmost precision. As for the lands southeast of the surging seas and northwest of the deserts—though various foreign tribes occasionally arrive to pay tribute—they are so remote and isolated that there is little evidence to examine. If the accounts were not detailed, I could not include them; if they were detailed but untrustworthy, I could not believe them. Therefore, I have left those areas blank for now.

Alas! I have traveled the four directions since I was a boy, and now my hair is turning gray. I have researched and discussed almost everything. This map is the ambition of my entire life and the result of ten years of labor. I hope that those who view it in the future will know that I did not produce this lightly.

Written on the Winter Solstice of this year by Zhu Siben, also known as Benfu, of Linchuan.

# Preface to the Enlarged Terrestrial Atlas

In my youth, I suffered from a frail and sickly body. Finding the *Internal Classic* <note>original: "素問" (Suwen); a foundational Chinese medical text</note> and other medical books difficult to understand, I developed a great interest in the arts of nourishing life. I once sat silently in a room, reducing my food and drink, but within ten days I became even more emaciated. I consulted a physician, who said: "Your illness is a blockage of energy; medicine and acupuncture cannot work quickly enough." He took a *Diagram of the Hall of Light* <note>An anatomical map of acupuncture points</note> and pointed to the "Ren" and "Du" meridians, the extraordinary vessels, and the various *yin* and *yang* channels of the hands and feet. He performed moxibustion according to the pressure points and followed it with herbal decoctions and nourishing meats. After several months, I recovered.

I thanked the physician, saying: "How could the theory of root causes and urgent treatments be false?" Later, as I traveled to and from the capital, I heard friends discuss the 'urgent' and 'strategic' situations of the world. I began to study the historical traces of how famous men of the past governed the land. My only regret was that I could not hear the full details regarding the terrain's difficulty, or how the prefectures and counties were linked. Without knowing the physical reality, one cannot investigate where to advance or retreat, what to take or what to abandon—much less discuss success and failure.

I once surveyed the maps and records of the empire, but they were a chaotic mess. Some were overly detailed, others sparse, and most were inaccurate in their distances. Out of a hundred maps, not one could be easily relied upon. After three years of searching, I happened to acquire the map by the Yuan dynasty scholar Zhu Siben. His map used the method of **calculating miles through a grid system**, making the physical reality reliable. By following his method of dividing and combining, the east and west were balanced and there were no contradictions.

Consequently, I used everything I had seen to expand upon what he had not finished. I enlarged his work into dozens of maps. Because I could not carry the originals everywhere, I kept copies in the mountains. Without outside help, I spent more than ten winters and summers to complete this. The human body has meridians, organs, and hidden passages, each with different names that cannot be guessed; yet even a common physician using moxibustion or needles can hit the correct point and trigger a reaction. Is this not because the measurements on the medical chart are grounded in evidence?

Zhu's grid system seems to have originated with him. Zhu was a man from Linchuan, profoundly learned and widely traveled. His own preface states this map took ten years of effort; if he had not seen it with his own eyes or reached it with his own feet, he did not dare write it down. This can be called the height of diligence. His grid system is a feat of clever thinking that others cannot match. However, the official histories of the counties do not record his name, and his maps are rarely seen. Perhaps his original work has been lost? Alas, who knows if my own maps will not also be considered "superfluous objects" in the future? I began to regret my labor, realizing that since my body was already ill, I had misapplied my mind and lost track of what was "urgent." I have written this preface and stored the atlas away as a way to reflect on my own faults.

I noted that Zhu’s original map was seven feet long and wide, making it difficult to roll and unroll. I have now used the grid system to reorganize it into a book format. Respectfully, I observe that:

## The Great Ming Shines Like the Sun in the Heaven

Our imperial influence knows no borders, far surpassing both ancient and modern times. To observe our virtue, I have created one **General Map of the Terrestrial Realm**, showing the capital regions and foreign lands, with the people and the defense installations supporting each other according to established laws. I have created sixteen maps of the **Two Metropolitan Areas and Thirteen Provincial Administrations**, showing how princes and dukes establish defenses—never forgetting danger in times of peace. To maintain the great boundary between China and the borderlands, I have created nine **Frontier Maps**.

Mountains and valleys can hide illness; sometimes there is unrest, and rebellious forces hide like snakes and tigers. The merit of governance lies in "cleansing" these areas; thus, I have created five maps of **Tao River, Songpan, Qianzhen, and Mayang**. I have recorded the governance of the Yellow River and the Grand Canal, creating three maps of **The Sea, the Grand Canal, and the New Canal**, showing how the flow of water is managed. To prepare for unexpected hardships, such as those at the Sanggan River or the Zhaling Pass during difficult times, I have created three maps to show the limits of governance.

The four seas are joined; from the northern shores to the deserts, from the Huai to the Xu rivers, the water and land routes are prepared for the annual transport. Foreign kings from the four directions come to pay tribute to the Middle Kingdom. I have concluded the work with four maps of **Korea, the Ryukyu Islands, Vietnam, and the Western Regions**.

Wherever the maps were too complex to be joined together, I have created sixty **supplementary maps**. The names and shapes of mountains, rivers, and cities are intertwined; words cannot express everything, so I have used twenty-four **symbols** to simplify the text. Any corrections or omissions are for the reader to observe.

# General Map of the Territory, Part Two

The territory of the <term>Great Ming</term><note>The Ming Dynasty, 1368–1644</note> reaches <term>Choson</term><note>Korea</note> in the east and <term>Jiayu Pass</term><note>The western end of the Great Wall</note> in the west. To the south, it borders the sea, and to the north, it meets the desert. The winding roads of the empire extend for more than ten thousand <term>li</term><note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters</note> in every direction.

There are two metropolitan regions (<term>North and South Zhili</term>), 22 Prefectures, 34 Sub-prefectures, and 210 Counties. There are 13 Provincial Administration Commissions, overseeing 118 Prefectures (plus 19 <term>loose-rein</term><note>Jimi: frontier regions under indirect control</note> districts), 153 Sub-prefectures (plus 47 loose-rein districts), and 928 Counties (plus 6 loose-rein districts). In total, there are 159 Prefectures, 234 Sub-prefectures, and 1,114 Counties. The total distance recorded is 69,556 *li*. The population counts 9,352,015 households and 58,550,801 individuals.

The combined summer and autumn taxes yield 26,085,916 <term>shi</term><note>Also known as a picul; a unit of volume for grain</note> of rice and wheat.
The two granaries at the capital and Tongzhou receive 4,000,000 *shi* of grain annually via the <term>Grand Canal</term>.
The various granaries in <term>Nanjing</term><note>The secondary capital</note> transport grain annually.

# Guizhou Atlas, Part 2

The **Guizhou Provincial Administration Commission** <note>The primary civil administrative body of the province during the Ming dynasty.</note> is governed from Guizhou. It consists of one Left Provincial Administration Commissioner, two Left Administration Vice-Commissioners <gloss>within which one is requested for general management</gloss>, and three Right and Left Assistant Administration Commissioners <gloss>assigned respectively to the Qing’an Circuit, the Xinfu Circuit,</gloss> <gloss>and the Haitong Circuit</gloss>. It oversees 8 Prefectures <gloss>with 3 subordinate Departments, 6 Counties, and 2 Anshun Offices</gloss> and 1 Pacification Commission <gloss>overseeing 1 Chief’s Office and 4 Departments</gloss>.

The province contains a total of 79 Townships, 18,957 Households, and 112,110 Individuals. The total tax revenue in rice and wheat is 148,948 <term>dan</term> <note>A "dan" is a traditional unit of dry measure, often translated as a "bushel," roughly 60 kilograms.</note> and a fraction. 

<gloss>The Huguang Provincial Administration Commission and other offices handle 102,410 dan and a fraction of grain in kind (actual crops). Commuted grain (paid in currency) totals 30,723 taels of silver. Land rent and additional grain levies collected by the Administration Commission total 39,223 dan. The proportions of actual grain versus commuted payments vary, totaling 129,235 dan, 5 dou, 5 sheng, 9 ge, 4 chao, 5 cuo, 5 gui, 5 su, and 5 li <note>These are increasingly small subdivisions of the dan measurement</note>. Autumn grain rice accounts for 9,471 dan and a fraction. The combined rice and wheat total is 158,910 dan and a fraction. Excluding the 30,723 taels of commuted rice, the actual grain in kind stands at 112,100 dan and a fraction.</gloss>

<gloss>A total of 2,952 tax grains are allocated for prefectural patrols and police coordination.</gloss> 259 strips of hemp cloth are collected from the "Cave People" <note>original: "Dongman," a historical term for local ethnic minorities</note>. The brokerage tax silver totals 136,046 strings and 4,388 coins. Tax silver totals 329 <term>taels</term>, 9 <term>qian</term>, 3 <term>fen</term>, 3 <term>li</term>, and 9 <term>hao</term>.

**Guizhou Regional Military Commission** <gloss>Wen Xingkui</gloss> <note>Likely the name of the official in charge at the time of writing.</note>. They oversee 18 Guards <gloss>including 3 subordinate Departments and 3,093 registry entries</gloss> and 2 Defense Battalions. The total number of cavalry and infantry officers and soldiers under this commission is 37,417.

**Provincial Surveillance Commission**: One Surveillance Commissioner, four Vice-Commissioners, and two Assistant Commissioners <gloss>One Vice-Commissioner for the Qing’an Circuit and four for the Inspection Circuits</gloss>.

# Jia Rang’s Three Strategies for River Management

->centered text<-
Relocating the people to defend against the river—this is the humane way. This began in the Warring States period, when the states of Qi, Zhao, and Wei used the Yellow River as their border. Since the lands of Qi were low-lying, they constructed dikes twenty-five <note>approx. 8 miles</note> away from the river's main channel.
<-

This was not the river's natural course, yet it allowed the waters space to wander. When the floods receded in due time, they left behind rich, silty soil which the people then farmed. In previous generations, people had built houses and established businesses there, but living in such a "simmering cauldron" <note>an area prone to sudden inundation</note> was bound to lead to trouble. Where the water hits with full force, one should let it flow north into the sea. Once this work is established, the river will be settled and the people at peace; there will be no disasters for a thousand years. This is called the **Superior Strategy**.

**The Middle Strategy:** This involves digging many irrigation channels. In times of drought, the people's <term>stone weirs (埭)<gloss>low dams or barrages used to regulate water flow for irrigation or navigation</gloss></term> are opened; in times of flood, these <term>weirs</term> are closed. By directing the water north into the sea, once the work is established, the river will be settled and the people at peace for a thousand years. This is called the Middle Strategy. This should be the norm. However, simply using wood and mud to repair old dikes without increasing their thickness—constantly spending money on thin, failing structures—will inevitably lead to a breach in the end. This is the **Lowest Strategy**.

# Ouyang Xiu Says:

<note>Ouyang Xiu (1007–1072) was a preeminent historian and statesman of the Song Dynasty.</note>

If the river is managed by merely patching old dikes with wood and mud, without doubling their thickness, the effort is thin and the costs are endless. Eventually, they will surely burst; this is the very lowest of strategies. One must understand the nature, principles, history, and flow of the river, as well as the path of what has already been abandoned. Since ancient times, it has been difficult to restore a river to its original course. Implementing a new policy requires determining where the upper reaches are low or blocked. This is the natural order of things. Thus, patching old dikes with wood and mud—spending without end on structures that are too thin—will only result in a later collapse. It is not that we cannot act, but if we do not follow the natural flow, the upper reaches will eventually breach because the old channels are silted up and the water cannot move. A wise person, when faced with a task where success is not guaranteed, must weigh the pros and cons, choosing the path with the most benefits and the fewest harms. This is still better than a path with many harms and few benefits.

# Ouyang Xiu Says:

Regarding the defenses of the Zhizheng era <note>1341–1368, the final years of the Yuan Dynasty</note>, there are three main considerations for the river. First, the damage to various prefectures; second, the diversion of the river's flow; and third, the "new lands," the "old channels," and the deepening of the river at Heyang. The "new lands" refer to the straight sections of the river. Because they are straight, they are excavated. The old channels have high and low points. The high points must be leveled to encourage the water toward the low points. If the high points are not leveled, they block the water and cause stagnation; one must worry that such blockages will lead to breaches and disasters. If the body of the river is straight, it can be guided. Where it is narrow, its fury must be lessened. By managing one bank as the primary defense, there must be materials to resist the current. There are five names for these defensive measures: leveling the high and low points, using dikes, and using "stone boats" <note>baskets or boats filled with rocks</note> to imprison the river's rage. Manage one bank as the primary bank. There are methods of <term>fascine construction (紉築)<gloss>bundles of brushwood and earth used to reinforce riverbanks</gloss></term> tied to old structures. Where the river body is straight, guide it forward. Where it is narrow, it is difficult for it to receive the water. In such methods, one uses boats, stones, "moon irons" <note>curved iron reinforcements</note>, and dikes. Where the new river enters the source of the old channel, it is said that where the water carves a path, the water will recede; if the water is blocked, the river will become one. There are fascines, there are "chime-mouths" <note>narrow openings</note>, there are "dragon mouths" <note>breaches or narrow gaps</note>, and "broken mouths."

## Song Lian Says:

<note>Song Lian (1310–1381) was a lead historian of the early Ming Dynasty.</note>

When dealing with a river thousands of miles long, torrential and difficult to manage, if one wishes to use a single standard to vent its angry momentum, there is absolutely no logic in that. If you divide its water in half, making one stream into two, you divide its power. The key to managing the river lies entirely in this principle.

<margin>
In the previous dynasty, Minister of Works Song Li and Chen Xuan, the Marquis of Pingjiang, managed this by only channeling the Wen River and building locks. They did not redirect the Yellow River, and on the north bank, they built dikes and fascines at a cost of millions. This was a defense...
</margin>
<margin>
...against the river moving north, just as one defends against bandits. For over a hundred years, even in times of drought or flood, the water did not overflow the "Pan-bo" <note>a local area</note> or reach the capital late. There was never the disaster of a total blockage...
</margin>
<margin>
...the problem of non-circulation. However, if the flow of the Wen River springs is too weak early in the season, the lakes will silt up. The proposal to divert the Yellow River might address this, but...
</margin>
<margin>
...since the national plan depends on this, we must seek a perfect solution. I believe that leading the Qin River is a better remedy, even if it increases labor and costs, to provide water to the river and then expand the plan.
</margin>
<margin>
In drought, restrict the water so it flows east into the Grand Canal, which is easier to control and thus a safer plan. As for the area below the two rapids of Xu and Dai, one must rely on the Yellow River's water to enter before expanding the plan.
</margin>
<margin>
At that time, one must carefully dredge and maintain defenses, considering the height of the land and the direction of the flow, weighing the urgency and importance to guide the river according to its natural inclination.
</margin>

## Liu Tianhe Says:

<note>Liu Tianhe (1479–1546) was a Ming official known for his expertise in border defense and water conservancy.</note>

When the river water arrives, it crashes and breaches; when it recedes, it fills with silt and is ruined. The lock gates are widened by the force, the river channel is obstructed—is the harm to the water sources small? The previous breach at Zhangqiu and the silting of the "New River" at Miaodaokou are warnings. Some debaters use the metaphor of "using the Wolf Soldiers to eliminate internal bandits" <note>original: 引狼兵以除內冠</note>.

<note>The "Wolf Soldiers" metaphor suggests that bringing the dangerous Yellow River water into the Grand Canal to flush out silt is like hiring fierce, uncontrollable mercenary troops to kill local thieves—you might get rid of the thieves, but the "wolves" might destroy everything else.</note>

<summary>This page outlines the classic "Three Strategies" of Jia Rang for Yellow River management, emphasizing that land reclamation and heavy diking are inferior to letting the river follow its natural course. It includes commentaries from Song and Ming scholars who argue for dividing the river's power and caution against using the volatile Yellow River to clear silt in the Grand Canal.</summary>
