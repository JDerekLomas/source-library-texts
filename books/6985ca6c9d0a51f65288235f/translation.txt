The discord that reigns among known systems proclaims that the inspection and exact comparison of <term>Monuments</term> <note>original: "Monumens" — In this context, the author refers to any physical remains of the past, including ruins, coins, and written records.</note> alone is a poor guide: that these Monuments show us what the men of the first centuries did, without enlightening us on the motives that led or determined them to do it; that the lack of light regarding these motives does not even allow us to glimpse whether the materials match the purpose given to them, whether we are missing any, or whether those that seem best matched in a systematic comparison do not actually leave a void in their true place, from which they might have been moved. Is it not evident that, for want of a common link, these innumerable materials remain as isolated, as scattered, and as silent—despite our attempts to bring them together—as they are in the state of dissemination in which they are held by the darkness of time and the forgetting of primitive institutions?

Discouragement seemed likely to arise from these reflections, and above all from the uselessness of the efforts of those who preceded us in this kind of meditation. Let us not suppose, however, that these means were always so necessary that man could never choose, or even err in his choice: that he was always driven in this regard by an invariable and necessary cause; this would be a false and illusory reasoning. Such are the riches of Nature that she always presents to Man a multitude of means, and it is through this that she provides the opportunity for his intelligence and his wisdom to develop: thus, we observe an infinite variety in the <term>industry</term> <note>In this 18th-century context, "industry" (industrie) refers to human ingenuity, skill, and the application of craft, rather than large-scale factories.</note> of different Peoples. Indeed, it is the consideration of the means employed by each People that reveals the difference in their position and their genius; it enables us to judge in advance, by the nature of the means they put to work, the success they were destined to have. It is this same consideration that becomes the source of the perfection to which we rise from century to century, whether by making better use of means already employed, or by substituting them with means more fertile in their development.

The transmission from century to century of this mass of discoveries became more cumbersome: but a division of these immense riches soon took place. The Professions were categorized, and the total store of knowledge was preserved, on one hand, by the perpetuity of real or artificial needs; and on the other hand, by each individual’s adoption of the labor of one of these classes, destined in common to compensate for all privations.

Thus every Art which answers the primary needs, such as Language, Agriculture, etc., contains today both what it has always been and what it has become through human industry. Its fundamental elements have remained the same; they are found there in their entirety: and the Monuments relating to them are merely landmarks <note>original: "points de reconnoissance"</note> which alert us to the moments when improvements were made.

By following these principles, we have become increasingly convinced that in working to decipher Antiquity through Monuments alone, one would do nothing more than stir a heap of rubble and throw the pieces alternately and successively upon one another; whereas, by considering them solely as witnesses to the inseparable needs of humanity and the means employed to fulfill them, these principles—these primitive and necessary facts—have summoned these innumerable Monuments one by one, and they have come, so to speak, to arrange themselves in their true place. This Work shall be titled:

## THE PRIMITIVE WORLD analyzed and compared with the Modern World, or Researches on the Antiquities of the World.

It is the **PRIMITIVE WORLD**, because it treats the Origins of the World and its Antiquities from its beginnings until the historical times of the Greeks and Romans—that is to say, until at least the 8th century before our Era, a time when a general revolution occurs from China to Italy, and when enlightenment begins to develop with a remarkable explosion. <note>The author, Court de Gébelin, is referring to what modern historians often call the "Axial Age," a period of significant cultural and philosophical shifts across Eurasia.</note>

This World is **ANALYZED**, for we review therein its language, its writing, its customs, its habits, its laws, its religion, and its extent; and we trace back to the origin and causes of these fundamental objects.

It is **COMPARED TO THE MODERN WORLD**, in that we demonstrate that its language is our own, and that most of our knowledge comes from this primary source.

This **PICTURE** is divided into two parts, corresponding to two Classes to which the whole relates.

In the first Class, we consider the Primitive World relative to **WORDS**.

In the second, we consider it relative to **THINGS**.

We shall then develop the considerations that should convince our Readers of the certainty of the success of these researches and the advantages that will result from them.

# SECOND OBJECT.
## UNIVERSAL GRAMMAR.

**WHAT** we have said until now regarding the origin of language and writing has had as its object only words taken one by one, and without being considered as a whole. But it is not enough to consider the origin and the value of words in themselves: it is also necessary to examine in what manner it has been possible to reunite them, so that they might form complete pictures; so that they might paint not only the object of our ideas, but also the very assembly of our ideas. <note>The author uses the metaphor of "pictures" (tableaux) to argue that language is a visual-logical representation of the human mind's internal structure.</note>

In this respect, the Rules of language must be as necessary as the words themselves: they must be given by the nature of the pictures offered by our ideas, and be nothing more than the results thereof. Without this, our discourse would paint nothing, or it would paint imaginary objects.

It is therefore these immutable Rules with which we occupy ourselves here, and which form Universal Grammar, because they belong to all places, all times, and all Languages; and because when one possesses them for one Language, one possesses them for all—for the Language of the most savage People, as well as for that of the most enlightened People, who will have carried the art of Speech to the highest degree of perfection. <note>This concept of "Universal Grammar" was a popular 18th-century philosophical pursuit, suggesting that all human languages share a single underlying logical structure reflecting human reason.</note>

If these Rules were the effect of chance, of caprice, or of usage, they would be absolutely different according to time and place: their uniformity therefore demonstrates the contrary; and that being as necessary as the primitive words of Languages, they have the same source.

From then on, Universal Grammar, instead of being a heap of variable and metaphysical Rules, is entirely in action: it becomes a sequence of Observations that are simple, clear, natural, easy to grasp, and all necessary.

# THIRD OBJECT.

## PRIMITIVE DICTIONARY.

**F**ollowing the origin of language and writing, and after Universal Grammar, there follows necessarily—and before the subdivision of language into a multitude of tongues—the Dictionary of the Primitive Language.

It will serve as the verification of everything we have said regarding the two preceding Objects, and it will become the key to all languages.

An imaginary or incomplete key, some will say—those who, never having delved into these matters, suppose that because the Ancients left us no dictionary of their language, it is impossible to supply one. But this key will appear very simple, very natural, and very evident to those who understand that the first of all languages, composed of necessary and representative words <note>Gébelin argues that primitive words were not arbitrary, but "representative" because their sounds naturally imitated the objects or actions they described.</note>, could never have been lost: it exists in all languages, and there is no impossibility in extracting it from them, provided one has established Principles by which one can distinguish a primitive word from one that is not.

These Principles are neither arbitrary nor new: they are the same ones by which so many great minds have seen that it was necessary to analyze languages by tracing them back to their roots. This is how we have been given the roots of the Latin language, those of the Greek language, and those of the Hebrew language: even the roots of the Chinese language, under the name of *Keys* <note>original: *Clefs*. In the study of Chinese, "keys" refers to radicals, the structural components used to categorize characters in a dictionary.</note>.

What were these roots, if not the remains of the primitive language preserved among these peoples, and the source of all their words?

There is, therefore, no difference between this primitive dictionary and the dictionaries of roots of all these peoples, except that each of the latter is of the smallest possible utility, providing understanding of only a single language; whereas the one we announce to the Public will be of the greatest possible utility, leading to the understanding of all languages, and by a path just as short: for it requires just as much pain and effort to learn two thousand roots that provide the key to only a single language as if they provided the key to all.

This primitive dictionary will even be much less difficult to learn, because it will be much less numerous <note>Gébelin suggests that the "true" root language is much smaller and more logical than the complex modern languages derived from it.</note>: and let no one be surprised by this.

### AUGUR, *Augur.*

The origin of this word, so significant to the Romans, is still completely unknown: no one has even provided a passable etymology for it. It was correctly seen to be composed, 1st, of the word **BIRD** <note>original: "AVE"</note>; but the second Root has been indecipherable even for the Latins themselves. SERVIUS, in his commentary on *Aeneid V*, derives it from **to carry** <note>original: "gero"</note>; ISIDORE from **to chatter** <note>original: "garrio"</note>; others from **taste** <note>original: "gustus"</note>; OVID (*Fasti I*) from **to increase** <note>original: "augere"</note>. But one sees by the passage itself that he intended less to give a true etymology of the word than to flatter Augustus.

VOSSIUS <note>Gerardus Johannes Vossius (1577–1649), a renowned Dutch classical scholar.</note> gravely discusses all these absurdities and finally decides in favor of **to carry** <note>original: "gero"</note>. One feels they are reading the discussions of Barbarians, or of children, regarding objects they cannot see at all. They all chase frivolous etymologies here, leaving the beautiful and brilliant truth by their side. It is from **TO WATCH/OBSERVE** <note>original: "CURO"</note> that **AUGUR** came: from **curo**, whose proper meaning is *to look, to see, to observe*. **AU-GUR** therefore signifies, word for word, *he who observes the Birds*.

Is this not the very idea of the word Augur? Is this name, which seemed made by chance, not a vivid depiction of the idea it was destined to represent? Such is the utility of etymologies: they give life to corpses; they make the study of words as interesting as it is, without their help, off-putting and insipid.

### POST, *After*.

This is a preposition, yet nothing is less understood than the origin of most words of this kind, which do not designate specific names. ## ETYMOLOGICAL DICTIONARY

### OF THE GREEK LANGUAGE.

If the Hebrew language deserved—by its utility and by the number of those who study it—that one should facilitate its understanding by means of its etymological dictionary; the same holds true for the Greek language. As the most perfect model of languages, cultivated by all who aim to achieve a knowledge of beauty, to refine their taste, to expand their ideas, and to place themselves in a position to portray them with as much force as grace and harmony, we could not neglect it in a work of this nature. We believe, therefore, that we are providing an essential service to Literature by tracing back to the origin of this language—itself linked to most of our European languages—and by showing from which sources this interesting People <note>The Ancient Greeks</note> drew its words, and how they managed to extend, embellish, and diversify this primary foundation, making it disappear, in a sense, through the manner in which they made it their own.

Scholars had very clearly perceived that this immense mass of words composing the Greek language was reduced to a certain number of radicals <term>radical: the core root or base part of a word from which other words are derived</term>; and collections were made of them that were as good as they could be for the times in which they were produced.

# NINTH OBJECT.

## ETYMOLOGICAL DICTIONARY

### OF PROPER AND APPELLATIVE NAMES.

Beyond the words used to express our ideas or to converse with one another, there exists a multitude of Names intended to designate particular Beings: such are the names of Peoples, Empires, Heroes, Gods, Cities, Rivers, Mountains, and so on.

These names, which appear to be given by chance and without any relationship to language, were nonetheless meaningful from the moment they came into existence. It would even be against all reason for them not to be; it is unthinkable that men who chose the words necessary to paint their ideas with such accuracy would fail themselves and the nature of things when it came to the words by which they were to designate themselves and the objects surrounding them.

If they no longer seem meaningful, it is because as Languages changed, the value of these names ceased to be known; people no longer knew to which <term>radical words</term> <note>The basic, primary words from which others are derived; the "roots" of the language.</note> they should be related; and with their source being unknown, their meaning could no longer be restored.

The efforts that a great number of Scholars have made at various times to rediscover the value of Proper or <term>appellative names</term> <note>Nouns that describe a class of objects or provide a descriptive title, as opposed to unique proper names.</note> have, in fact, only served to spread more obscurity over this subject and to foster the belief that if these words ever had any meaning, it was lost forever.

But the uselessness of their research stemmed only from the method they followed, or rather from the fact that they were still walking here by chance and without principles.

To find the value of a name, one must always have two points of support, two objects of comparison: the name itself and the nature of the Being it designates. This is the only way to arrive at a certain conclusion. From the moment these two things are known, it is no longer difficult to discover, among all the words that have some relationship to the Name whose origin is sought, the one that was its source.

# FIRST OBJECT.
## SYMBOLIC AND ALLEGORICAL GENIUS
### OF ANTIQUITY.

THE Symbolic Genius <note>In this context, "Genius" refers to the prevailing spirit, character, or essential nature of an era</note> of Antiquity consisted in animating the whole of Nature, personifying all inanimate or moral Beings, and presenting the instructions one wished to give to men as accounts of past events: painting the most elevated, respectable, and important objects in the form of physical Figures.

What Allegory says is never what it truly means; it appears to present us with one Object, while it has another, even more interesting one, in view: this is the definition given by the Ancients. From this comes the word itself, *Al-legory.* (1)

Composed of two Greek words, <term>ALL</term> (other) <note>original Greek: "allos"</note> and <term>AGOREuein</term> (to harangue, to speak) <note>original Greek: "agoreuein"</note>, *Allegorizing* meant, word for word, to speak or express oneself through comparison—to say something different from what one seemed to be saying. The Orientals <note>A term used by 18th-century scholars to describe the peoples of the Near East and Asia, such as the Hebrews, Persians, and Arabs</note> likewise use a word for the same purpose that carries the whole idea of resemblance and comparison, which is rendered as "Parable" and "similitude" regarding its nature, and as "Proverb," "Sentence," "Maxim," etc., regarding its importance and the weight it must carry.

Allegory set the tone for the entirety of Antiquity; it created its Fables, presided over its Symbols, animated Mythology, incorporated itself into the most respectable truths, and formed the bulk of the most august Ceremonies. Everything bore its imprint: it was, in a sense, the unique language of primitive Antiquity. It is the language of all the ancient Peoples of whom some Monuments remain: that of the Celts, the Scythians, the Etruscans, the Phoenicians, the Indians, the Chinese, the Chaldeans, etc.

Clear and intelligible at the time it was used—when people knew very well where they stood regarding it—it has become an inexhaustible source of riddles as that knowledge weakened and the understanding of the primitive Language was lost. # GENERAL PLAN

Here, History and the Primitive Language <note>The "Primitive Language" refers to the 18th-century linguistic theory that all human languages descended from a single, perfect original tongue spoken by the first humans.</note> will offer each other mutual aid. This analysis of Languages does not serve solely to better understand the Monuments of the Ancients: it goes even beyond these Monuments, for which it compensates, and it gives us the very History of the human Mind <note>original: "Esprit humain"</note> and its developments within each Nation. The History of Peoples becomes a matter of calculation <note>The author suggests that history can be studied with the same precision and logical deduction as mathematics or the physical sciences.</note>: consequently as sure as it was uncertain, and as luminous as it was obscure.

From this will result the perfection of this Work, in which Antiquity will appear **ONE**, in Language, and in Traditions, and in morals, and in Laws, etc. It will thus reunite the advantages of etymological <term>Etymology: the study of the origin of words and the way in which their meanings have changed throughout history.</term> and historical discussions, without having the dryness of the former, nor the sterile abundance of the latter.

# FOURTH OBJECT.

## CUSTOMS AND MANNERS.

**T**o the knowledge of History is indispensably joined that of Customs and Manners: this is even more interesting than History itself, because it provides a more just and exact idea of Peoples. History only presents us with their revolutions: it is the external portrait of Peoples, if one may use that expression; but through their customs, one penetrates into their interior, into their ideas, and into their way of thinking. It is in their customs that Peoples are portrayed, just as an individual is reflected in his habits: thus, History necessarily follows the path of Customs and Manners.

Through Customs, Peoples triumph over the strangest revolutions: they are an unshakable foundation to which even the most intractable Conquerors are forced to submit, while those who hold to nothing are always the prey of the first innovator powerful enough to place himself above the customs and manners of his age or his Nation.

In this regard, we shall show that all Nations, at the moment they each arrived in their respective Lands, possessed exactly the same manners and the same customs; that being already linked to one another by language and Traditions, they remained so in all these respects.
