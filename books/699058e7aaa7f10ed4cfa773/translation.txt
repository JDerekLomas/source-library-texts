In counting the number of <term>ultimate atoms</term> <note>The authors refer to "Anu," the smallest subatomic particles they believed they could perceive through clairvoyance</note> within a chemical element's atom, we did not count them all individually, one by one. For instance, when we counted the ultimate atoms in sodium, we dictated the number found in each convenient group to Mr. Jinarâjadâsa; he then multiplied the totals, divided by 18, and announced the result. 

For example: sodium (see Plate I) is composed of an upper part—divisible into a globe and 12 funnels—a lower part similarly divided, and a connecting rod. We counted the number in the upper part: the globe contained 10; the number in two or three of the funnels was 16 each; and there were 12 funnels in total. We did the same for the lower part and found 14 in the connecting rod. Mr. Jinarâjadâsa calculated: 10 + (16 x 12) = 202; therefore: 202 + 202 + 14 = 418. When divided by 18, this equals 23.22 recurring. 

By using this method, we protected our counting from any bias or <term>pre-possession</term> <note>preconceived notions or expectations</note>, as it was impossible for us to know how the various numbers would turn out after addition, multiplication, and division. Elements grow denser in descending order; thus hydrogen is an invisible gas; chlorine is a denser gas visible by its color; bromine is a liquid; and iodine is a solid—all, of course, when temperature and pressure are normal. By lowering the temperature and increasing the pressure, an element which is normally gaseous becomes a liquid, and then a solid. Solid, liquid, and gaseous are three interchangeable states of matter, and an element does not alter its fundamental constitution by changing its state. 

So far as a chemical "atom" <note>The author uses "atom" to refer to the standard chemical element, while "ultimate atom" refers to the smaller sub-atomic particles they claim to have discovered through clairvoyant observation.</note> is concerned, it does not matter whether it is taken for investigation from a solid, a liquid, or a gas. However, the internal arrangements of these "atoms" become much more complicated as they become denser and denser. This is seen by the complex arrangements required by the presence of the 3,546 ultimate atoms contained in the chemical "atom" of gold, compared with the simple arrangement of the 18 ultimate atoms of hydrogen.

According to the <term>lemniscate</term> <note>A figure-eight shaped curve used here to describe the periodic arrangement of elements.</note> arrangement, we should begin with hydrogen as the head of the first negative group. However, because it differs entirely from the other elements placed with it, it is better to consider it by itself. Hydrogen is the lightest of the known elements and is therefore valued as 1 in ordinary chemistry, with all other atomic weights being multiples of this. We value it as 18, because it contains eighteen ultimate atoms—the smallest number we have found in any chemical element. Therefore, our "number weights" are calculated by dividing the total number of ultimate atoms in an element by 18 (see p. 349, January issue).
