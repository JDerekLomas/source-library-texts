# TO THE MOST ILLUSTRIOUS AND EXCELLENT
### MAN, MR. ROBERT FLUDD,
### most celebrated Doctor of Medicine and Philosophy,

### JOHANNES KEPLER, MATHEMATICIAN,
### *An Apologetic Letter.*

---

**L**ATE, Excellent Man, did your *Analytical Demonstration* <note>Original: *declaratio Analytica*. This refers to Fludd's "Demonstratio quaedam analytica" (1621), which attacked Kepler’s mathematical approach to cosmic harmony.</note> reach my hands: and it found me entangled in inextricable business: so that if I had passed it over entirely untouched, no one who is aware of my affairs could impute the stubbornness of silence to me as a mark of pride.

Nature, however, conquered, and the habit I have maintained until now; it conquered, I say, the weariness of writing and reading: nor did I think this contest dishonorable; nor did I judge that the eager reader should be frustrated: finally, as for this bit of profit which the printers seem to aim for, by provoking and inciting authors against one another, I have undertaken to handle it for them—as far as in me lies—only through a display of honesty approved by all good men. And without further preface, I shall run through the demonstration itself—which was already long enough before, since in it you repay me a hundredfold for my *Comparison* <note>Kepler refers to the Appendix of his 1619 work *Harmonice Mundi*, where he first compared his mathematical harmonies to Fludd's more mystical, alchemical ones.</note>—using as concise a style of speech as I can; and I shall dissolve those things in it that seem to me to stand in the way.

<margin>Page 3.</margin>
In the title of the preface, I wonder what necessity drove you to respond, to whom I previously promised nothing, asked nothing, and objected to nothing. The material of your demonstration and its sentiments are rather those of one rebuking: as if you were striving in a fit of temper to drive away with words and push from his place someone standing next to you in public, though he has not uttered a word.

I thought myself established on a podium <note>Original: *pulpito*; here meaning a public stage, lecturer’s chair, or a position of intellectual authority.</note> while I was writing my *Harmonic* work, and adding the comparison at the end: and behold, you call me to a judgment of morals. I shall indeed respond to the objections, but from the podium: honor demands the former, equity permits the latter. You judge that *no one should rightly cover their own errors with the obscurity of different colors*; you recognize a hardened and bold brow in those who dare such things. This beginning of your criticism is certainly laudable; I readily agree. *You deny that one's own deeds or writings should be extolled through self-love and vain glory.* I assent; you tax public vices with authority. It is in the public interest that the studious youth, as well as all writers, should hear you. *You judge it unfair for anyone to sell themselves to the people through the detriment of another's praise and the depression of an honorable reputation: to trumpet the heraldry of their own works through the injuries and disgrace of others, with an unjust mouth.* This is altogether most unfair, and contrary to divine and human precepts. *Judgment concerning published books should not be snatched away from the readers by the disputants.* Plainly, I say, it is just and holy to forbid this. *You say you have written simply, sincerely, and wished to please the people;* I praise that. *You deny that you have disparaged anyone secretly or openly.* I am satisfied. I prefer to have affected no one, to have spoken no witty insult against anyone, to have gnawed at no one's reputation with the rusty tooth of envy; to the Cynic man- <note>The text breaks off here at the end of the page; the catchword "ribus" suggests the next page continues with "moribus" (manners/morals).</note>

I seem to accuse you of ignorance, sharply, though secretly. This is so "secret," Robert, that you are as far off as heaven is from earth. You exercise your right when you explain more broadly what was said truly but concisely by me (for I did not know more about your editions). I even allow that this may be added to my comparisons: that I spent more years on my work than you spent weeks on yours. Indeed, in the year 1617, having set out for Tübingen for business, I had nothing ready except a few small pages containing the raw material of Book III, which I showed to friends; I explained my purpose in writing: I reviewed each thing I had been meditating on for 20 years, and what remained to be investigated. Add the remaining comparisons, the particulars omitted by me, if you like, for the benefit of your readers. I place no importance on the number of words as a point of distinction. Regarding the division of the <term>Harmonic string|Harmonica chorda: The monochord, a single-stringed instrument used to mathematically demonstrate musical intervals</term>, I used such a wealth of words as I judged necessary to explain the foundations of Music. Furthermore, on the <term>Harmonic means|medietatibus Harmonicis: The mathematical ratios used to find the middle terms between two musical notes</term>, I used as much brevity as I possibly could; because little weight was placed on them for this type of philosophical contemplation, and the means followed naturally once the extremes were established—that is, the Harmonies themselves, composed among each other, whose origin I taught in Chapter 2. Yet I do not see how I can be said to have written "superficially" about the Harmonic means, since I both provide the definition according to the opinion of the ancients and tear it down from its very foundations, as it very frequently produces non-Harmonic numbers—a name without a reality. Therefore, our common reader must be further warned to look again and again whether Robert describes his Harmonic means according to this ancient definition; for he says he has handed them down most copiously, and indeed in more elegant words. If what I say is true (for his book is not at hand), then indeed Robert, with that abundance of words, will have wandered from the truth along with the ancients. Therefore, the reader must take care not to agree with his statements until Robert has legitimately refuted my reasons opposed to the ancients. Regarding Robert’s other specific comparisons, let it be the judgment of the discerning reader what each of us has truly said about our own treatment. For if I instruct my reader on my own text, it is only fair that Robert be allowed the same in his. And look at what Robert says: that I dealt with the divisions of the string in too many words; yet all this abundance was not enough to give Robert any light regarding the discovery of the <term>Diapason|The interval of an octave, the most fundamental harmony</term>, about which, led now by regret, he says I have dealt only obscurely. Yet I believe I have clearly derived the bisection of the string from the ratios of the circular diameter; let the reader see if he can find any further light from Robert in this part. I will not be envious, for it is a pleasure to offer shared efforts in educating readers. Therefore, I will also gladly accept a greater order from you in my new chapter, which you think was written in a more confused manner, if the reader can recognize a greater order in yours. Nor will I deny to the discovery of the musical keys <note>original: "clauium b h," referring to B-flat and B-natural</note> a greater clarity than in my own work, or its own commendation; provided that the association of the pentagon and hexagon (in my second chapter) did not suffice for the reader for this purpose—as long as he admits he found so much light in Robert.

As for your lengthy excuse, Robert, for your way of writing about Harmonic matters without this "Analysis": I would like you to remember that it was never accused by me. I use a long discourse and many words, specifically so that I may teach; you contract things into briefs, so that you may commit to memory what someone else has taught before you. Your brevity does not follow unless there is a prior abundance from another, and unless another has fashioned the matter at length. ### TO ANALYSIS IV.

I satisfy you in a single word: *We compare things either to show agreement or to show a difference*. I have, therefore, rightly compared your ideas with mine so that my reader might know that there is more of a difference between us than an agreement. But what point is there in playing with words? I called your Music "artificial" based on your own book, for you distinguish *Natural* from *Artificial* in your own way. To me, there is no legitimate craft <note>original: "artificium"</note> of Music, nor one accepted by the ear, that lacks foundations shown by Nature. Therefore, I reveal the causes of natural things even when I am disputing the origin of artificial Music.

But see what a difficult man you are, through whom I am not even allowed to be modest; you are so suspicious that you twist every single word into the opposite meaning. I wrote that I pass down "few things" regarding the inventors of Music; is this to accuse you of superfluity? ### TO ANALYSIS V.

<margin>In how many ways time is considered in sounds.</margin>
Regarding the Times <note>original: "Temporibus," referring to duration and rhythm</note> necessary for artificial Music, I praise you for philosophizing. However, a worthy question arises here in which the reader may exercise themselves. You define sound correctly, Robert, through motion, and you do not allow time to be separated from motion. Ptolemy and his commentator Porphyry <note>Two of the most important ancient authorities on music theory and harmonics</note> followed this diligently. Time, therefore, is present in sounds in two ways: first, as it forms the essence of sounds, or the differences caused by heaviness and sharpness <note>i.e., low and high pitch</note>; second, as it happens to those sounds already formed, as an external measure. Deep sounds have slower tremors; high sounds have faster ones. For example, if a duration of time is set, such as the interval between the contraction and expansion <note>original: "systolen & diastolen"</note> of a human pulse; let two strings be struck which make an Octave <note>original: "Diapason"</note>. If that string which gives the deep sound trembles within the prescribed space of time—let us say it trembles fifty little vibrations <note>original: "vibratiunculas"</note>—the other string, which sounds the high note in consonance, will tremble one hundred times. I touched upon this speculation in Book III, folio 14, in a wonderful experiment. You see, Robert, a deception arising from the ambiguity of the word *Time* has been the cause of your wondering. If I had not touched upon this first consideration of time, I could have argued that I had assumed it along with the difference of the sounds themselves in pitch and depth. My text, therefore, speaks of the other respect, in which time is an external measure of sounds, as does yours, which I am comparing. For in the former respect, time is not arbitrary, but adheres necessarily to the depth or sharpness; but in this latter respect, it is entirely arbitrary. For this reason, among other causes, it is called "artificial Music." For as much as exists in any matter by the choice of the craftsman, all of that is credited to art; but as much as art imitates nature, and by its own choice...

The art itself carries its own laws; so much of the skill is judged to be in the craftsmanship of goodness itself. For example, in all the voices of <term>figured song<note>original: "cantus figurati." This refers to polyphonic music where different voices have different rhythms, as opposed to simple plainchant.</note></term>, the artist can establish short sounds; yet if he assigns long measures to the **Bass** and short ones to the **discant** <note>The highest voice in a polyphonic composition.</note>, here he looks toward nature. For we have said that low sounds are related to long durations even in the formation of their very essence, and high sounds to short ones. But if, on the contrary, he occasionally commands the Bass to flutter about in short measures, he is engineering an extraordinary "seasoning," using a plan drawn from the flourishes of his art and from his own arbitrary freedom. I touched upon this in Chapter 16, page 83, regarding the alternation of consonances.

When you weigh these things, I shall indeed wonder if you can bring forward any further excuse for why, at the end of this analysis, you denied that the various dimensions of **Times** in length and shortness are arbitrary. For you are speaking of this very respect of time as a measure.

Therefore, as far as concerns this respect of Time—for which we are accustomed to use the word "Times" rather than "Time"—I, in turn, wonder, *Robert, why you wonder that I have written nothing at all about times in my work on Harmony. You interpret these words to mean that I consider the investigation of Times to be frivolous.*

For I ask you: when I defined **Song** in Chapter 13, did I not insert a small part into the definition that would encompass the measures of times? Can anything be considered frivolous that the person who is said to consider it so includes in the definition of the thing itself? Or did I not, in turn, in the explanation of that small part on page 65, in the paragraph on **Measures**, indicate the cause of my brevity clearly enough to the readers? Namely, that it was not my purpose in this work to dispute about Measures. Why do you wonder that I did not dispute more? Accept the reason: it was not my purpose. Although even in Book IV, page 125, I added a few small points which **Poetics** has in common with artificial **Music** regarding Measures: because in that place it was my purpose to explain the variety of subjects in which Harmonies exist.

<margin>The <term>tactus<note>The steady beat or pulse in music, similar to a modern conductor's "downbeat."</note></term> has no place in planetary Harmonies.</margin>
The arguments you bring to prove that one writing about the Harmonies of the Planets must dispute about time are drawn from the first respect of time, about which we have no discussion in this present section of comparison. Indeed, I compare low sounds to the motions of **Saturn**, as it is the slowest planet—that is, the one which covers very little of its journey in a long time. But for how long Saturn maintains that same lowness of sound—the same, I say, and uniform slowness of its motion—this pertains nothing to celestial Harmony. For in that harmony, every degree of slowness, if we act most accurately, is momentary. Thus, it is not possible for a human to secure a uniformity of Saturn’s motions lasting for any length of time; nor is it <unclear>possible</unclear> <note>original: "ἐνδεχομένον" (endechomenon). Kepler uses the Greek term to mean "permissible" or "fitting" within the logic of the system.</note>, once the continuous change of the interval between the Planet and the source of its motion has been established. This change of interval is necessarily followed by a continuous intensifying and relaxing of the motions, except at the <term>Apsides<note>The points in an orbit where a planet is closest to (perihelion) or farthest from (aphelion) the sun, where its speed momentarily stabilizes before changing again.</note></term>. Therefore, the measure of time has no place in celestial motions when contemplated harmonically, insofar as they are contemplated harmonically. An aspect <note>A specific angular relationship between planets, such as a square (90°) or trine (120°)</note> does not exist in the heavens, but is formed on Earth. If you substitute a *solar spirit* here, then say why this solar spirit does not move the air except at those moments in time when the rays of any two planets are configured on the earth? By what means does this Spirit of the sun investigate what becomes of the planets' rays on the earth? How much easier it is for us to place the seat of the Spirit there where it perceives, and where it works according to what it perceives. You do the former, I do the latter. I recognize the powers of a wisdom acting everywhere, and all living faculties, whether in the Earth, or in plants, or in Man. But as for "dispositions" in the heavens, or a *solar spirit*, or a wisdom acting everywhere, I know of none according to which sublunary Nature <note>The realm of the Earth, located "below the moon"</note> might be stirred. For it is stirred according to certain dispositions of rays on the earth; this is established by perpetual experience. But those dispositions are by no means present in the heavens for the very reason that they are present on the earth. You say, *The Spirit of the Sun gives life and soul to the planets.* What advantage does your opinion have over mine? At every moment, you bring forth a new soul of the planets from the solar spirit, through which two planets from their celestial seats might stir up weather in the sublunary World—and that according to a prescribed aspect on Earth. What great convolutions these are! How much more probably do I place this spirit (if you wish to call it that) permanently there where the law of acting is present to it, and where its work is—namely, in sublunary nature? You call the air the *lower spirit of the Soul of the World*. I will not contend with you on this matter; whether that which I have called the *Earth's soul* is itself a certain separate soul, or rather the Spirit of another *Soul* of a higher order. It is as if someone were to place the seat of the *Soul* in the heart of a living being; in the eye there is not the Soul itself, nor that great one, nor a separate one of a lesser order, but at least a spirit full of the character of that Queen sitting in the heart, having gone out from there. Let it be so, I say; let this soul of mine, which I attribute to the earth, be nothing other than the spirit of the Soul of the World seated elsewhere.

This efficacy in sublunary <note>The region "below the moon," meaning the Earth and its atmosphere.</note> Nature is a greater work than could be performed by the coarse air, which we so easily exclude from empty spaces.

From where, you continue, *the power of radiations is in the air.* Why, you ask, is it in there? Is it because the celestial Planets receive a soul from the sun? For that is what is read in your discourse just before this "wherefrom." And yet there is no need for a Soul in the Planets for the power of the radiations being formed on earth to come into the air. For if this were the case: that soul of the planets would necessarily have to travel with the radiations themselves all the way down to the earth. But this cannot happen. For there is no power of radiations in the air, unless some Geometric conformity in the aspect or angle of two radiations is to be observed. Who, then, would be the messenger to indicate this all the way from Earth to Saturn and Jupiter, so that from there he might lead down the souls—either each planet's own or a soul common to both—to the earth, so that the soul, being present here, might *procure power for the air*? You might rather have said that the light of the planets itself, from which the radiations consist, is this Soul, just as you previously made our air into the spirit of the World Soul. For me, everything will follow more easily by placing a certain spiritual faculty in the Earth.

The *soul of the Earth,* you say, *is from the air and in the air, rather than the movements of the air proceeding from the Earth’s soul.* I do not fight this; indeed, I publicly profess this very thing: that this faculty of the earth I speak of, which is similar to that of a living animal, also goes out in its own way into the surrounding air. There, as circumstances arise, it gives rise to locusts, cicadas, and other insects. But to place its chief seat in the air, and grant it only excursions into the earth: this seems to me just as if someone were to say the chief seat of even the vegetative soul in a human consists of those vapors exhaling from the body and clinging to the folds of the body, where it gives rise to lice and similar things.

<margin>How the air gives life.</margin>
You continue, *Air gives life to the waters and the earth, and to all things composed of them.* I would like to know, do you contend that Aristotle stands with you? He certainly attributes all those things to the heat of the sun; the air is merely an instrument to him. And what life do you say is in the waters and the earth? Is it not the fish in the former, and the plants in the latter? I, for my part, once drank in an obscure saying from your Chemists <note>Alchemists; Kepler often engaged with alchemical texts even while critiquing them.</note> that it is the benefit of Salt, not of air, that the sea teems with monsters, that the earth produces plants without seed, and that a pool, warmed by the heat of the Sun, produces mice. How will the air give life to others, which it does not have itself? Unless, under the word "air," you thrust upon us something more divine than this vapor surrounding us, thinned out from moisture.

<margin>Why do fish have a bladder distended with air?</margin>
I know that fish contain air in a twin bladder, but that is an instrument of motion and rest for them, and a kind of natural suspension from the surface of the water. Thus, this air remains enclosed in these bladders even when the soul has departed and the lifeless body now hangs from the belly which contains this bladder; whereas before, by the benefit of the soul, it would strive to lean upon the belly and be supported by it.

So much does the instrument differ from the motor, and the air from the soul. I also know that animals retain their soul by the benefit of air, and expire when it is cut off; yet the air is not the soul itself, nor does it give life when inhaled. Rather, it ventilates the flame of life burning in the hiding places of the heart, and prevents it from suffocating itself with its own soot. Once suffocated, or if it is not burning, the air itself never ignites it of its own accord—much less is it life itself, or something joined by life. Nothing similar can be said of plants; here the air defends no part of life, not even as an instrument: except insofar as the life of a plant is more similar to fire and flame than to air. And yet, according to you, air even gives life to the earth.

That the motion of the air proceeds from a soul of the earth is a statement somewhat foreign to my philosophy. It is more accurate to say that the air is generated by the soul of the earth; for this soul, stirred by subterranean heats and aided from without by the heat of the sun, thins the subterranean humors into vapor. It does this in the same way that a human soul triggers both sweat and evaporation when irritated by a mithridatic <note>A "mithridatic" refers to a complex medicinal compound used as an antidote or stimulant, named after Mithridates VI of Pontus.</note>, or when boiling over from the motion of the body. Now, once the vapors exhale, the remaining motions—of weight and lightness, of heat and cold, of density and rarity—follow naturally by their own momentum. 

But let us also hear one of your arguments; for so far, you were speaking mere opinions. *If the soul of the earth stirs up tempests in the air,* you say, ***then it destroys and tears apart its own body.*** Does this seem absurd to you, Robert? Have you never bitten your own tongue in a headlong error? Have you never dug furrows into your skin with your fingernails? Or perhaps it was not your soul that performed this, but your teeth and nails acting apart from the soul? But you return to your opinions: ***The soul of the world is universal, from which tempests, comets, etc., proceed.*** I raise no objection regarding comets, for I do not think they arise from the Earth, and even less from our air—or certainly very few of them do. Concerning tempests, grant me reasons, or confess that you have said nothing.

<margin>Page 17.</margin>
For, you say, ***the air is a passive thing, the proper [medium] of that World Soul, and more suited to it than to humans.*** What? If I were to say the same about the Earth’s soul: surely this familiarity would be more likely between neighbors, between equals, than that the soul of the vast Universe should have so much business in these narrow reaches of our air that it would swim and act in them so abundantly. Truly, I also deny this: that the air is more suited to receiving the actions of the Earth’s soul than the humors <note>In ancient and early modern medicine, "humors" were the primary fluids of a body—here Kepler applies the concept to the Earth's internal fluids.</note> of the Earth's body. What do you seek? The air is outside; the humor is within. In the humor, the heat stirred up by the soul finds something to act upon; the air, already thinned, has escaped its force. Thus, fire has an action upon wood, but it has none upon ash.

I would also like to know what that ***likeness of the mundane soul is, which sometimes rains down from the heavens, which does not become a harmonic configuration, as I teach, but is something fortified by it.*** I would like to know what the ***magnetic exhalation of the earth*** contributes toward receiving the spirit, that is, the air. Do you mean that the earth is exhausted by that exhalation, and being thirsty for it, refreshes itself by drawing in the air? A magnetic exhalation, Robert, is not material, but potential; for it is the formal power of a body aspiring to union with something like itself. But if the earth has need of air for its generations, this will be more easily supplied to it from within, by humors thinned by heat.

<margin>Why marbles sweat when rains are imminent.</margin>
I disagree with many of your opinions, Robert. Here is a pleasant problem: *Why do marbles sweat when rains are coming on?* You answer that the air, already moist, enters the stone and dissolves the salt of the marble. Weigh the marble, then; and report how much lighter it has become, if it has sent out so much salt. I, for my part, hope I shall satisfy the reader better. It is not only marble, and it does not always perform this, but only then when cold has struck deep into the marble and all dense objects and clings there tenaciously; then, the moist and warm vapor arriving suddenly is resolved into water upon meeting the cold, and it adheres to the surface of the marble. For warm things are rare; when they are condensed by the encounter with cold, they take up a narrower space; and so the neighboring warm parts follow after, lest a vacuum be created. Thus it happens that the warm air is carried toward the marble. Therefore, this example does not prove that air enters into the earth, if it merely adheres to the outside.

You draw another argument from rainy conditions, and you think the supply of the sea would fail if the matter came from there and not from the air. I, on the contrary, think the supply of air would fail much sooner. For as I once wrote truly in my *Optics* <note>Referring to Kepler's 1604 work, *Ad Vitellionem paralipomena, quibus astronomiae pars optica traditur* (The Optical Part of Astronomy).</note>, there is more matter in a single drop of water than in a very large space of air—even one that would fill an entire room. Therefore, I hope for greater agreement from those who believe that rains are generated not from the external air, as you do, but from the condensation of vapor still creeping from its subterranean matrix. For it was subterranean humor, resolved into vapor by subterranean heat stirred by the Soul of the Earth; and exhaling into the cold air, it immediately returns to the form of water. Thus, the soul of the earth still stands firm.

...that the air receives celestial influences and radiations, and pours those it has received into the bowels of the earth. The air does indeed receive—that is, it admits—radiations: for nothing hinders them, not even the opacity of the earth; for the invisible power of a ray is a more penetrating force than visible light. An example of this is the magnetic force, which breathes through plates of gold. Yet the air does not receive radiations in such a way that you could distinguish each one by its geometric formations. For air is matter, it is a body; whereas the geometric beauty of an angle, which forms the "aspect," <note>In astrology, an "aspect" is the specific angular distance between two planets (like 60 or 90 degrees) which was believed to exert influence over the Earth.</note> is an <term>entity of reason</term> <note>Original: *ens rationis*. But as for the kind of "reception" you propose, your "transfusions" prove it: you speak of a liquid, not of the expressibility of proportions, which is a geometric matter. It is almost as if some material were raining down from heaven, which you call "influence."

You had concluded your point, and lo, you open a new argument in turn: *The Earth is a passive element; therefore it does not act upon the active air.* Take back what you have set down; I will not fight you on it. But is that body of the Earth therefore also lacking in an "animal faculty," <note>The "animal faculty" refers to the animating power or soul (*anima*) that allows a body to move or react.</note> by whose efficacy subterranean vapors are stirred up to mix and disturb your air? The Earth is a passive element even when it is in a human body, yet it is not prevented from being so by the human soul.

Then you also raise a question for me: are those angles <term>physical</term> or <term>mathematical</term>? A manifold ambiguity has confused you, so that you rise up against me here thoughtlessly and in vain. Certainly, those lines of radiation are not "nakedly mathematical," for they are luminous, and light is a physical thing; nor do they lack their own dimensions, since stars are bodies and they shine from every point. Indeed, I will further increase your objections with additions of the kind you have not even thought of. I say they are not even individual angles, as they are otherwise drawn in Mathematics for the sake of demonstration; for the whole sublunary nature, as great as it is, is everywhere full of <term>sextile</term> angles <note>An angle of 60 degrees.</note> when two planets make a sextile, or <term>squares</term> <note>An angle of 90 degrees.</note> when they make a square; because of which, nature can be compared in a way to a cloth, with the weft and the warp running through it everywhere. For rays descend from all planets to every single point in the entire world. On so many counts, then, I grant you that the radiations are physical. But all these things, in the "aspect," still only have the character of matter or a subject; there remains the *form*—that is, the logic of the aspect, derived from Geometric proportion—a thing purely mathematical, purely intellectual, and which can be received or imprinted upon no body, whether it be solid like earth and metals, or fluid like water and your air. This distinction, I say, which separates a sextile from a <term>septile</term> <note>An angle of about 51.4 degrees, which Kepler considered "inexpressible" and thus ineffective in astrology.</note>—so that the former is effective and the latter is not—can by no means move any corporeal matter. It requires an animal faculty which is thus far a participant in Geometry—and an innate one at that, not one acquired through reasoning and schooling.

But I do not understand that reasoning of yours: *In the beginning, the spirit of the Lord was carried and moved over the waters, not in the darkness.* <note>A reference to Genesis 1:2.</note> Therefore, the angles of visible rays were made in the air, not in the earth. I see indeed where you are heading. My "Soul of the Earth" lies hidden within; but if you prove that the radiations remain outside, then my philosophy is stabbed through; the Soul of the Earth will not be moved by the aspects; and therefore, since its function (from which I wished to demonstrate the soul) ceases, there will not even be any Soul of the Earth. This, I say, is your target, I see it; but how you are aiming for it, I do not grasp. If visible angles are in the air, those same angles will also be in the earth, because the distance from the center to the circumference of the air is not considered significant in the radiations of the planets—not even in those of the moon, more than what can be accounted for in the space of two hours. But, you say, the opacity of the earth hinders them. Granted regarding the *light*, yet I deny it regarding the *power*; I give the example of the magnet: its radiation is not hindered by the solidity of gold.

But why do you deny the angles of radiation in the earth? Because *the spirit of the Lord was not under the earth* (you say *not in the darkness*, not considering that light did not yet exist then) *but was carried over the waters*—namely, in the place of the air—*in the beginning.* As if it truly matters to the radiations where the spirit of the Lord is said to have been carried! The planets radiate now that the heavens have been established; your text refers to the time before the heavens were established. And you speak of angles, not as a daily and transitory accident of individual radiations, but as a substance lasting from the beginning, as if the angles were "made" in the air. Finally, Robert, we shall leave that for the Theologians to examine...

<margin>Why does dissonance offend?</margin>
But this is precisely what must be compared to the Harmony of voices. For the efficacy found in geometric angles is equivalent to harmony in voices; what is inefficacy there is dissonance here. For although the prefixes *Con-* <note>original: "Con-", meaning "with" or "together"</note> and *Dis-* <note>original: "Dis-", meaning "apart" or "away"</note> suggest a contrariety, there is no true conflict between harmony and dissonance. Rather, it is a simple contradictory opposition—not only in the Idea (between the single angle of Geometric beauty and the infinite angles that are not beautiful) but also in the effect of pleasure or offense to the ears. For if you pay close attention, the sense of hearing is no more offended by the collision of sounds if they are dissonant than if they are consonant; nor does it suffer any physical pain from dissonance that is the opposite of pleasure. Instead, the mind feels pain because of dissonances only because it carries within itself the related ideas of consonant sounds. Since the mind desires these—having "tasted" them before—it is contracted with sadness when deprived of them. It is the inner soul that suffers, not the sense of hearing positioned at the threshold of the mind. Just so with a child: a true hunger or thirst is one thing, but the craving to eat cherries placed before his eyes is another. Unless those cherries refreshed the memory of a sweetness tasted long ago, their mere absence would not torture the boy. Having established this, nothing prevents us from linking dissonance with inefficacy, as if it were a mere negation, not the positive position of the opposite of efficacy.

<margin>Why are the aspects multiplied?</margin>
You consider it superfluous, Robert, to observe more than the traditional five aspects. First, it is never superfluous to describe the causes of appearing things. If, at a time when there are no traditional aspects, I see Nature specifically moved, it is a task worthy of a philosopher to ask why nature is moved on this particular day. It is a worthy opinion if a philosopher sees that on that day there is a "semi-sextile" <note>An angle of 30 degrees between two planets.</note> between good planets, and perhaps concludes that the semi-sextile should be included in the count. It is a philosopher’s diligence to watch for other similar occasions. And so it happened. For the efficacy of this aspect was testified to by meteorological observations <note>original: "Meteoroscopii"</note> even before the geometric causes were discovered; indeed, this semi-sextile exercised my mind significantly until I could establish the causes of the aspects in such a way that this one could also be included, since I saw it had the testimony of observation. Again, it is not superfluous, Robert, once the causes for the efficacy of the other aspects are found, to see whether those same causes might reveal others not yet proven by observation, and to what degree. By that same measure, one can assign them a level of efficacy even without prior observation. For the causes of the aspects do not exclude the decile, tridecile, or the sesquiquadrate; <note>Minor astrological aspects: Decile (36°), Tridecile (108°), and Sesquiquadrate (135°).</note> yet they only admit them at the most distant degree, after the other known ones. It pertains to the knowledge of these causes not to pass them over in silence, even if we are not strictly forced by the necessity of explaining observations to watch them. Small and obscure things can be omitted in experiments, but they cannot be ignored when elucidating causes. Thus, we pass each other here without a clash. However, those aspects we measure by the Tenth-parts (*Decatemoria*), Twelfth-parts (*Dodecatemoria*), or Eighth-parts (*Octoëmoria*) of the Zodiac are not to be called mere fractions or trivia.

I had said that the rays of the planets meet on the Earth, and if this meeting is Harmonic, then rains fall. You counter: *they meet first in the air.* There is no distinction of "before" and "after," Robert, where the flow of light is instantaneous. Or else say "first" meaning higher than the center of the Earth. You continue: *they receive their mixtures in the air, not on the earth.* I do not grasp what the rays "receive" here or there; you say "mixtures." But the meeting <note>original: "coitus," used here in the sense of rays intersecting</note> itself is the mixture—the only kind I can grant you. I had said they are always engaged in this conjunction and mutual intersection; so what does your speech mean, that they *at last receive a mixture*, when they always have it, if you like? Or do you say they "receive" in the air that which allows the mixture to become an "aspect"? But this happens to these mixtures in the air and on the earth at one and the same time. "Whence," you say, "a cloud is generated in the air before it rains." This is certainly true: there is first a cloud, then rain. Nor did I exclude the cloud when I spoke of rain. But if I examine your "whence," I find you attribute the generation of the cloud from the air itself to the presence of the aspect in the air. Yet you are singing the same old song that I exploded with reasons in *The Harmony of the World*, Book IV, on the very page (158) from which you plucked my words to refute them. Attack my reasons, Robert, by which I establish my dogma, and I will be more ready afterward to brush aside your objections.

# THE HARMONY OF THE WORLD
### FIVE BOOKS, OF WHICH:

The first is **GEOMETRIC**, regarding the origin and demonstrations of Regular Figures, which constitute Harmonic Proportions.

The second is **ARCHITECTURAL**, or concerning **FIGURED GEOMETRY**, regarding the Congruence of Regular Figures <note>The way shapes fit together without gaps, known today as tessellation.</note> in a plane or a solid:

The third is properly **HARMONIC**, regarding the origin of Harmonic Proportions from Figures; and regarding the Nature and Differences of things pertaining to song, in opposition to the Ancients:

The fourth is **METAPHYSICAL**, **PSYCHOLOGICAL**, and **ASTROLOGICAL**, regarding the mental Essence of Harmonies and their kinds in the World; especially regarding the Harmony of rays descending from the celestial bodies to the Earth, and its effect on Nature or the sublunary <note>Referring to the region below the moon, i.e., the Earth and its atmosphere.</note> and Human Soul:

The fifth is **ASTRONOMICAL** and **METAPHYSICAL**, regarding the most perfect Harmonies of the celestial motions, and the origin of Eccentricities from Harmonic proportions.

The Appendix contains a comparison of this Work with Book III of the *Harmonies* by the celebrated Ptolemy <note>Claudius Ptolemy (c. 100–170 AD), the Greco-Roman mathematician and astronomer whose earlier work on music theory Kepler critiques and builds upon.</note>, and with the Harmonic speculations of Robert de Fluctibus, called Fludd, an Oxford Physician, which were inserted into his work on the Macrocosm and Microcosm.
