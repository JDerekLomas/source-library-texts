# ERASMUS of Rotterdam, to his dear William Thale, sends greetings.
<note>original: "S.D." for Salutem Dicit. William Thale was an English student who studied under Erasmus and later became a diplomat.</note>

<margin>To weigh matters with sharp seriousness</margin>
Indeed, my sweetest Thale, you have both sharply perceived and seriously—and truly—judged that it matters a great deal by what method and in what order you do something. This carries the greatest importance, as in all other things, so especially in the study of good literature. Do we not see that enormous weights are lifted with very little effort through skill <note>original: "arte," referring to mechanical skill or engineering</note>, which otherwise could not be moved by any amount of brute strength? Just as in war, it does not matter so much with how many troops or with how much force you fight, as it does with how well-organized an army and with what degree of order you engage. And those who know the shortcuts <note>original: "semitas compendiarias," literally "shorter paths"</note> reach their destination much more quickly

<margin>Twofold knowledge</margin>
In principle, all knowledge is twofold: of things and of words. Knowledge of words comes first, but knowledge of things is more important. But some, as they say, hasten toward the knowledge of things with “unwashed hands” <note>original: "ἀνίπτοις... χερσὶν" (aniptois... chersin), a Greek proverb for approaching a sacred or difficult task without proper preparation or the necessary tools—in this case, language skills</note>, neglecting the care of speech. For since things are only known through the signs of words, it is necessary that one who is not skilled in the power of speech will be blind, wandering, and raving in matters of substance as well. Indeed, in both categories, the best things should be learned immediately, and from the best teachers. For what is more foolish than to learn with great effort things that you must later unlearn with even greater effort? For nothing is learned more easily than what is true; but once false notions have stuck in the mind, it is amazing how they cannot be uprooted.

### Which authors should be read first, according to Quintilian.

If this harmony can be achieved, there remains a question that is not so difficult: which authors should be read by beginners? Some have approved of lesser authors, because they seemed easier to understand. Others preferred a more flowery style, as if it were better suited to nourishing the minds of the young. I, however, would want the best authors to be read immediately and always—yet specifically those who are the most clear and polished. For instance, I would recommend Livy <note>Titus Livius (64 BC – 17 AD), a historian known for his monumental history of Rome.</note> for boys, and even more so Sallust <note>Gaius Sallustius Crispus (86–35 BC); though his style is more concise and difficult than Livy's, he was highly regarded for his historical depth.</note>; though Sallust is a greater authority of history, some progress in learning is needed to understand him. Cicero <note>Marcus Tullius Cicero (106–43 BC), the Roman orator whose prose became the standard for "Good Latin."</note> (as it seems to me, at least) is both pleasant for beginners and clear enough; he can be not only helpful but even loved. Then, as Pliny <note>Likely Pliny the Younger, a protégé of Quintilian who advocated for the imitation of Cicero.</note> instructs, one should choose whoever is most like Cicero. However, I think two styles must be especially avoided by boys: first, that no one who is too much an admirer of antiquity should lead <note>original: "du," a catchword for the next page, likely "ducat" (should lead).</note> them into the reading of the Gracchi <note>The brothers Tiberius and Gaius Gracchus, whose 2nd-century BC speeches were considered rugged and archaic by later Romans.</note>, Cato <note>Cato the Elder, famous for a very blunt, unpolished style of Latin.</note>, and others like them...
