# Foreword

The Pacioli Society’s publication of this new English version of Friar Luca Pacioli’s treatise on accounting commemorates the 500th anniversary of its initial publication in Venice in 1494. Renaissance mathematician Luca Pacioli’s codification of the “Venetian” accounting method was part of a much larger work entitled *The Collected Knowledge of Arithmetic, Geometry, Proportion, and Proportionality* <note>original Italian/Latin: "Summa de Arithmetica, Geometria, Proportioni et Proportionalita". This massive volume was one of the first mathematical texts printed in a vernacular language rather than Latin.</note>. The 300-page *Summary* represents the collected knowledge of mathematics of the time. It also includes the now celebrated 26-page guide for merchants on sound accounting and business practices. Pacioli entitled this guide *Particulars of Reckonings and Writings* <note>original Latin: "Particularis de Computis et Scripturis". This section is widely considered the first printed treatise on double-entry bookkeeping.</note>.

Pacioli’s how-to manual, the *Summary*, was a great success. As Edward A. Fennell points out in his captivating monograph on Pacioli’s life and work, *Figures in Proportion: Art, Science and the Business Renaissance* [Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales, 1994], the *Summary* was a publisher’s dream, taking advantage of the new market for self-help books and the prestige of an established scholar and teacher. Pacioli’s publisher, Paganino de Paganini <note>A prominent printer in Venice and later Toscolano, known for his innovative typography and for printing the first Koran in Arabic.</note>, took the *Summary* into a second printing. The section on accounting was in such demand that it was extracted from the *Summary* and published separately. This version found its way to Northern Europe, where it was read and translated into other languages as early as 1543.

Pacioli’s *Summary* was equally celebrated for its contribution to organizing knowledge in the field of mathematics. Pacioli’s work attracted the attention of Renaissance intellectuals, most notably those in the court of Lodovico Sforza <note>Known as "Il Moro," the Duke of Milan was one of the most powerful and extravagant patrons of the Italian Renaissance.</note> in Milan. This illustrious group of scholars and artists included the architect Bramante, the composer Josquin des Pres and the artist Leonardo da Vinci. Pacioli was invited to join this intellectual circle in Milan and share his knowledge of mathematics and proportion. Luca Pacioli, the protégé of Piero della Francesca <note>A renowned painter and mathematician; he taught Pacioli the secrets of perspective and Euclidean geometry.</note>, became the tutor of Leonardo da Vinci. Thus, Piero’s influence on Pacioli’s knowledge of mathematics and proportion was brought to bear upon the work of Leonardo, then in the midst of painting *The Last Supper*. Pacioli and Leonardo went on to collaborate in Pacioli’s second masterwork, *On the Divine Proportion* <note>original Latin: "De Divina Proportione". This work explored mathematical ratios in art and architecture; Leonardo provided the illustrations of polyhedra.</note>, written by Pacioli and illustrated by Leonardo.

A distinctive and crucial aspect of the *Summary* is the language in which it was written: not Latin, the language of scholars and lawyers, but Renaissance Italian, the language of the street. Luca Pacioli, one of the great teachers and mathematicians of his time, was trying to reach the common man—the stonecutter, the architect, the merchant, the banker. He was convinced that a grounding in mathematics was essential to success in many walks of life, including commerce.

Jeremy Cripps’ translation joins a distinguished field of English translations. What further distinguishes Cripps’ work is his insistence on applying a modern interpretation to Pacioli’s writing. Professor Cripps, a linguist and a practitioner and teacher of accounting, believes his work most honestly reflects the style in which Pacioli would have written had he used 20th-century accounting and business idiom <note>Cripps prioritizes the "spirit" of the merchant's manual, translating archaic Venetian terms into modern equivalents like "ledger," "journal," and "balance sheet."</note>.

While this may be a controversial translation of Pacioli’s treatise on accounting, Professor Cripps’ interpretation brings Pacioli’s material to life. In this accessible and truly contemporary writing, Professor Cripps captures not only the literal impact of Pacioli’s work but the spirit of the man behind it. The work enriches not only our understanding of Luca Pacioli’s contribution to the art and practice of accounting but of Renaissance commerce itself.

'Fra' is a title for a member of a mendicant religious order, such as the Franciscans.</note> five hundred years ago.

# I n t r o d u c t i o n

**F**ive hundred years ago, in November 1494, one of our world's earliest printed texts included a section on accounting. Printing from loose metal type <note>Often referred to as "movable type," a revolutionary technology popularized in Europe by Johannes Gutenberg around 1440.</note> had just begun, and the publishing industry was in its infancy. Naturally, publishers preferred important texts on marketable topics. In their judgment, internal control, management accounting and the simple elegance of the double-entry system <note>A system of bookkeeping where every entry to an account requires a corresponding and opposite entry to a different account, ensuring the accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Equity) remains balanced.</note> were essential material for readers in the world's then-greatest financial center: Venice.

Five hundred years later, the ideas printed in that accounting manual continue to provide the guidelines for recording economic activity in *all* the world's great financial centers. The author of that first accounting manual is Luca Pacioli. The *Summary of Arithmetic, Geometry, Proportions, and Proportionality* <note>Original Latin title: *Summa de Arithmetica, Geometria, Proportioni et Proportionalita*.</note> — which includes this first published discourse on accounting — is a work of genius.

For hundreds of years, Pacioli's work — often plagiarized — stood alone as a text for young accountants. Pacioli had identified key information and key internal controls needed to manage the economic activity of a single entity. As accounting technology advanced, as new financial centers evolved and as English emerged as the language of commerce, other writers incorporated Pacioli's work into new texts. Seldom is Pacioli acknowledged, or are the authors even aware of his contribution. Indeed, Pacioli's ideas are so pervasive and deeply rooted that today we consider them "generally accepted accounting principles." Sad to say, even in our universities, we forget to identify the man so rightly honored as the "Father of Accounting."

Luca Pacioli's ideas, found in his accounting manual and other great books, provide the fundamentals young people must appreciate if they are to have a successful career in business. As those in the paintings of his friend Leonardo da Vinci <note>The famous Renaissance artist and scientist; Pacioli taught Leonardo mathematics, and Leonardo illustrated Pacioli's book *De divina proportione*.</note>, Luca Pacioli's perspectives are deep and wonderful. To communicate those perspectives and ideas most beneficially, Pacioli — a distinguished academic — avoided writing in scholarly Latin, then the language of the elite. Instead, he chose to write his ideas in the vernacular <note>The common language spoken by ordinary people in a particular country or region.</note> of Renaissance Italy. He wanted to share his knowledge and insights with real people — and especially with young accountants.

## PACIOLI'S LANGUAGE

Renaissance Italian, the language of the *Summary* <note>Referring to the *Summa*.</note>, developed during the 15th century from classical Latin, current in Pacioli's time. Although as elegant as Latin, Renaissance Italian was far less formal. It had no fixed grammar or disciplined syntax or structure; even the spelling of words was flexible. The beauty of the language was the way it enabled ideas to be shared among the people. It was because of its quality of communication that Pacioli preferred Renaissance Italian to Latin.

The first definitive version of embryonic modern Italian is usually accorded to Dante <note>Dante Alighieri (1265–1321), whose *Divine Comedy* helped establish the Tuscan dialect as the standard for the Italian language.</note>. His work marks the beginning of at least 150 years of Italian language renaissance.

Writers in this period include Petrarch and Boccaccio from the late 14th century, Lorenzo de Medici and Pacioli during the 15th century, and Machiavelli into the 16th century. Their language combines a classical Ciceronian <note>A style of formal prose modeled after the Roman orator Cicero, characterized by balanced sentences and refined vocabulary.</note> vocabulary with Italian vernacular in an extraordinarily rich exercise of individuality.

Some of the character and individuality of the language is lost to us because we are not familiar with all the meanings in Pacioli’s text. For example, the subtitle of his manual, “particulars of accounts” <note>original: “pticularis de coputis,” an abbreviation of *Particularis de computis et scripturis*.</note>, includes an abbreviation. Abbreviations occur throughout the text, and words are shortened to such an extent that it is impossible to ascertain accurately what word was intended. At the beginning of Chapter 21, for example, “com” may be an abbreviation of “purchase” <note>original: “compra”</note> or “companies” <note>original: “compagnie”</note>. Pacioli also makes frequent use of ampersands, “etc.” and phrases such as “so and so” and “so on,” which add pace to his writing but require contemporary interpretation. Words are also divided to wrap around the end of lines, probably because of technical limits in the early stages of printing.

Punctuation is almost nonexistent and includes inverted commas for more than one purpose. As with other vernaculars, a knowledge of the spoken language should provide sufficient understanding of punctuation — but we are listening five centuries later. Words are spelled inconsistently and perhaps with special meaning. We cannot know. Quite often, letters are obviously omitted, in some cases with such consistency that the existence of a common shorthand speedwriting is suggested. The diversity is at once attractive and upsetting: attractive because the language is so exciting; upsetting because the very diversity adds a barrier to our understanding.

### TRANSLATION RISKS

In any work of art, there is an integral coherence between the language and the content. Loss is a risk when the language is changed. That risk exists with any interpretation of Pacioli’s original text. My translation, however, is quite different from the three previous English translations in this respect: my emphasis has been placed on integral coherence, that is, the form at some expense to literal translation.

To this end, certain of Pacioli’s medieval terms have been replaced with contemporary idioms and terms. In connection with the making of payments, for example, Pacioli describes the borrower’s “oath,” which I rephrase as “promise to pay.” I have adapted certain medieval accounting terminology to contemporary financial terminology. Thus, Pacioli’s “charges for exchange” <note>Medieval fees paid to money-changers or market authorities.</note> made by a taxing authority in the marketplace readily adapts to modern sales tax. Also, medieval word order differs from the word order of classical Latin, modern Italian and English, so some of Pacioli’s medieval phrases have been paraphrased.

Certain pieces of text and word descriptions have been condensed and streamlined, in each case the subject of a footnote. I have also changed the schematic representation, particularly of the accounting book entries. I have shown such entries as they might appear in a modern text on accounting. Finally, I have used a single currency, again in an effort to present data in a contemporary manner.

## PRIOR TRANSLATORS

The three prior English translations of Luca Pacioli omitted “numerous foreign terms and ancient names” because they were “unattractive and tiresome to follow.” My interest in international accounting and enjoyment of an atlas made investigation of both terms and names intriguing. Many of the foreign terms led me to the roots of modern-day economic activity, especially in the areas of <term>documentary credits</term> <note>A financial guarantee where a bank ensures a seller receives payment from a buyer once specific shipping documents are provided.</note> and <term>exchange controls</term> <note>Government regulations that limit the purchase or sale of foreign currencies.</note>. The ancient names also charted the way to interesting discoveries.

Prior translators, who did not accept the original text as a language separate from classical Latin or modern Italian, found Pacioli’s language “presented some difficulties.” They believed that “what is written must necessarily be interpretive.” From a different language viewpoint, I agree that interpretation is inevitable.

Each prior translator in turn criticized Pacioli’s style and his reiteration of salient points. They found his use of the vernacular “vulgar.” In contrast, I find beauty in the original text. Sinews of real energy, excitement and expectation interact synergistically as words from ancient Rome and Renaissance Italy react etymologically. It is a mighty accounting experience, getting closer to Pacioli’s original text, its meaning and, therein, the abundant accumulated evidence of accounting as a <term>liberal art</term> <note>Historically, the branches of knowledge—such as arithmetic, logic, and rhetoric—considered essential for the education of a free person.</note>.

Pacioli, with a rare creativity, if not always gently, blends the language of the academic and the artisan. Pacioli sets aside needless communication barriers that have no place in the art of accounting.

## THE CHOICE

For the translator who loves and respects the author and his work, the most difficult problem is the choice of the language and its layout when presenting the translation. It is a choice between fundamentally conflicting poles: literal translation and free translation.

This classic choice is as problematic as the choice facing the knight in Chaucer’s “Tale of the Wife of Bath” <note>A famous 14th-century story from Geoffrey Chaucer's *Canterbury Tales* where a knight must answer what women most desire.</note>. The knight, bent on learning what woman is most desirable, must choose between a wife who is “foul and old” but absolutely faithful and a wife who is “young and fair” but faithless. In much the same way, translators who wish to write a fine translation are forced to choose between the author’s text and attractive prose. Changes in order and cultural improvisations at the expense of literal adherence to a beloved author’s text are inevitable.

Professor Francis William Newman <note>A 19th-century scholar known for his literal, if sometimes eccentric, translations of classical works.</note>, in his translation of the *Iliad* <note>original: "Ilias", the ancient Greek epic poem attributed to Homer.</note>, described this difficult choice. The translator may “retain every peculiarity of the original . . . with the greater care, the more foreign it may happen to be.” Alternatively, the translator should focus on the reader. The reader should, if possible, “forget that it is a translation at all, and be lulled into the illusion that he is reading an original work.” Horace <note>A leading Roman lyric poet during the time of Augustus.</note>, translator into Latin, advised that “even a faithful translation does not have to be word for word.”

The work of Luca Pacioli must be set in 20th-century America, far from the civilization of Renaissance Italy. My contemporary translation is from a language I have called <term>Renaissance Italian</term> <note>The author's term for the fluid, non-standardized Italian used during the 15th century.</note>, a language whose rules of grammar are free spirited and never clearly defined. For all its beauty, that language is a dead language and, as Howard W. Clarke <note>A 20th-century classical scholar and author of studies on Homer.</note> puts it in *The Art of the Odyssey*, we can never know “quite what to be faithful to.”

### NEW FOCUS

My contemporary interpretation of Pacioli’s <term>Summa</term> <note>Short for *Summa de arithmetica, geometria, proportioni et proportionalita* (1494), the first printed work to describe the double-entry bookkeeping system.</note> is distinctly different from previous interpretations. The vocabulary and style are deliberately contemporary, following Pacioli’s lead in writing in <term>vernacular</term> <note>The everyday language spoken by people in a particular region, as opposed to a formal or dead language like Latin.</note> Renaissance Italian, not formal Latin. The presentation of accounting entries is also deliberately contemporary. New readers of Pacioli should be encouraged by the contemporary format. A contemporary language and format are more readable. New and younger readers of Pacioli ought to be no more limited by the constraints of 15th-century printing and accounting than 15th-century readers needed to be limited by the constraints and conventions of classical Latin.

My purpose is to present an important part of our accounting heritage and to extend that heritage to the widest possible readership. Pacioli’s text is important in so many ways. It captures a historical moment in the development of ideas. It helps accountants document significant traditions in accounting. It provides a benchmark against which advances in accounting practices and techniques may be measured.

Prior interpreters of Pacioli were not focused on accounting as a college subject and <term>liberal art</term> <note>A field of study intended to develop general intellectual capacity (like grammar, logic, or rhetoric) rather than just professional or technical skills.</note>. Yet Luca Pacioli, a Renaissance professor of mathematics, saw the practical application of algebra across the humanities as an interdisciplinary opportunity. A simple application of basic algebraic theory gives ordinary users the freedom to control — and the art of maintaining — their financial transactions in meaningful and significant order. In setting forth the core concepts of modern accounting and blending those concepts into an economic communications powerhouse, Pacioli demonstrates accounting not as a technical skill but as a liberal art. I hope that the addition of informative footnotes goes far in illustrating Pacioli’s liberal art.

The ideas Luca Pacioli wrote down for us seem clear and simple, and, for all the difficulty of interpretation, his language seems to be so, as well. Pacioli is direct. He writes with pace. He reiterates important points, as will any good professor, emphasizing certain principles delightfully and managing to include numerous essential details for the student in a remarkably short text. Pacioli’s style is also simple to listen to (accountants “audit” what they read) <note>The word "audit" comes from the Latin *audire*, meaning "to hear," because accounts were originally verified by listening to them being read aloud.</note>. Pacioli seems to think naturally. His writing flows easily from one subject to the next in a fine and easy order, and the text is never boring.

The muse of Luca Pacioli’s language may be silent, yet I hope that an accountant, as aged as the author when he wrote, knows how the algebra of accounting sounds. I hope that a teacher knows something of the ways a teacher wrote. May my focus on the message of the author — the meaning of accounting — give me an empathy for the material so that my translation may prove both faithful and fair. I hope my contemporary interpretation of Pacioli’s manual will fill young accountants with great joy and excitement at the discovery of this great man and encourage a wider knowledge of our accounting heritage.

->Jeremy Cripps<-
->Heidelberg College<-
->Tiffin, Ohio<-
->March 1994<-

### NEW FOCUS

My contemporary interpretation of Pacioli’s <term>Summary</term> <note>Short for the *Summa de arithmetica, geometria, proportioni et proportionalita* (1494), the first printed encyclopedia of mathematics and the first work to document the double-entry bookkeeping system.</note> is distinctly different from previous interpretations. The vocabulary and style are deliberately contemporary, following Pacioli’s lead in writing in the <term>vernacular</term> <note>The everyday language spoken by people in a particular region—in this case, 15th-century Italian—as opposed to formal Latin used by scholars.</note> Renaissance Italian, not formal Latin. The presentation of accounting entries is also deliberately contemporary. New readers of Pacioli should be encouraged by the contemporary format. A contemporary language and format are more readable. New and younger readers of Pacioli ought to be no more limited by the constraints of 15th-century printing and accounting than 15th-century readers needed to be limited by the constraints and conventions of classical Latin.

My purpose is to present an important part of our accounting heritage and to extend that heritage to the widest possible readership. Pacioli’s text is important in so many ways. It captures a historical moment in the development of accounting, transitioning from informal records to a structured system that remains the foundation of modern finance.

# CHAPTER 1

## GOOD BUSINESS ACCOUNTS

->*The universal approach to keeping a Journal and a Ledger, as practiced in Venice and throughout the world.*<- <note>original: "a la vineziana e de ogni altro"</note>

Three things are needed by anyone who wishes to carry on business carefully:
1) Cash <term>cash: the original "pecunia numerata" refers to ready money, property, and wealth, as well as the credit needed to acquire them</term> and/or credit
2) A good accountant <term>accountant: one who keeps records of what is owed and what is owned</term>
3) Proper internal control <note>original: "bello ordine," literally a "beautiful order" or system that puts transactions in their proper place</note>

*Cash and/or credit*
The most important of these three is capital (that is, assets, cash, or any cash equivalents). As the saying goes, "Substance is the one thing most necessary." <note>original Latin: "Unum aliquid necessariorum est substantia"</note> Without capital, a business ceases to be a going concern <note>literally "to carry on doing business in the same way"</note>.

Many without capital of their own but whose credit was good have managed to finance large transactions, using just their credit (which they faithfully kept), and some have also become very wealthy. You may be acquainted with many of these throughout Italy.

In the business world, nothing is considered more important than the word of a reputable businessman. Promissory notes are honored and accepted based on a businessman's reputation. Confidence—faith <note>Italian: "fede"</note> in reputable businessmen—is what makes the market effective. This is not really strange, for the faith of a Christian in God works in much the same way to make religion effective.

*A good accountant*
The second necessity in business is to employ a good accountant or to have a good accounting system so as to understand the business. Prerequisite to being a good accountant is a practical understanding of elementary mathematics. <note>Pacioli had already included the rules and functions of arithmetic in previous sections of his larger work, the *Summa*.</note> For the person who lacks elementary mathematics, there is no point in reading further!

*Proper internal control*
The third and last necessity to achieve success in business is to have a system of internal control. With a system of internal control, business transactions are recorded in such a systematic way that one may understand at a glance the state of their affairs.

# CHAPTER 2

## THE INVENTORY

### *The nature of an inventory and how it should be conducted.*

<note>The text begins with a decorative woodcut initial letter B, featuring intricate interlace and knotwork patterns.</note>Begin with the assumption that every businessman has a specific goal when he enters into commerce. That goal is one he pursues with great spirit. This objective—shared by every merchant who intends to be successful—is to make a lawful and reasonable profit. Therefore, businessmen should begin their records with the name of God <note>original: "In nome di Dio"</note> and the date AD,¹ marking every transaction so they always remember to act ethically² and, while at work, remain mindful of His Holy Name.

To begin the process, the businessman must take an <term>inventory</term> <note>original: "inventario"; a detailed list of all assets and liabilities to establish the starting capital of a business.</note> in the following manner: Always record on a separate sheet of paper, or in a specific book, all <term>assets</term>, whether they be <term>personal property</term> <note>original: "mobili"; movable goods such as furniture, clothing, or stock.</note> or <term>real estate</term> <note>original: "stabili"; fixed assets like buildings and land.</note>. You must begin with the items of highest value and those most easily lost:

- First, personal property: cash, jewels, silver, and similar valuables.
- Then, real estate: such as houses, lands, lakes, meadows, and ponds (which are permanent and cannot be easily lost).
- Finally, all other items must be listed one after another.

In these inventory records, you must always include a descriptive heading followed by the day, the year, and the specific location for every item listed.

It is best to complete the entire inventory in a single day; otherwise, the lengthy process of counting and listing may interfere with the daily management of the business.

# CHAPTER 4

## IMPORTANT DOCUMENTS

->A useful note to businessmen.<-

In this way, you will make a formal record — an <term>inventory</term> <note>A detailed list of all property, goods, and contents of a business.</note> — of all your <term>assets</term> <note>Items of value owned by a person or company, which can be used to settle debts.</note>. Detail everything, item by item, regardless of how many items there are and regardless of their condition or whereabouts. Include assets deposited in banks, assets on loan; register all assets. Record all the appropriate details, identification marks, names, and surnames. Be as detailed as possible. The sheer volume of inventory items requires attention to detail, as every business executive knows. The proverb is right that says, “It takes more to make a good merchant than to make a <term>Doctor of Laws</term> <note>A scholar who has achieved the highest academic degree in legal studies.</note>.” <note>original: "Piu ponti a fare un bo mercatate che a fare un doctore de leggi." Literally: "It takes more bridges (or points) to make a good merchant than to make a Doctor of Laws."</note>

Who can say what will happen to a businessman? Businessmen operate on land and sea, in peace and war, during boom and recession, in health and plague. During all these times, they need to know the right decision to make in markets and at international industrial fairs. So, in this sense, the businessman, like the early bird, <note>Pacioli contrasts the rooster with the nightingale. The rooster, he says, is on vigil day and night all year long, while the nightingale sings beautifully only during summer nights.</note> must remain on the lookout for change all year long.

There is a saying that “though the businessman’s head has a hundred eyes, yet there are still not enough for all his duties.” Those in the know repeat the saying: they are familiar with competition in the international marketplace. <note>Pacioli lists the major trading centers of the era, including Venetians, Florentines, Genoese, Neapolitans, Milanese, and many others across Italy.</note> The largest competitors, the cities of Venice and Florence, have adopted rules and regulations for commerce that include the warning that “the law helps those who help themselves.” <note>original Latin: "Vigilantibus et non dormientibus Jura subueniunt"; literally, "the law helps those who are awake and not those who are sleeping."</note>

In church, too, we sing of help coming to those who are awake, not to those asleep. <note>original Latin: "Nocte surgentes vigilemus omnes"; translated as "Rising at night, let us all keep watch." This is a hymn often attributed to St. Gregory the Great.</note>

# CHAPTER 5

## THE INTERNAL CONTROL SYSTEM

->*Its meaning for business — what it includes — the three principal books of account.*<-

**S**ection Two of this book describes how a business should be organized and controlled. <note>The original Italian uses the word "dispone," meaning the disposition or orderly arrangement of the accounts.</note> For the sake of clarity, this part is longer than Section One. Section Two is therefore divided between:

(i) general requirements for a large-scale business <note>original: "corpo o vero monte de tutto el trafico"; literally the "body or mountain" of all trading activity, referring to high-volume commerce.</note>
(ii) particular requirements for the sole proprietor or shopkeeper <note>original: "corpo o vero de botega"; referring specifically to the organizational needs of a retail shop.</note>

First of all, businesses require an accounting system. After taking an inventory, three books of account are needed for proper accounts:

(i) The <term>Memorandum</term> or day book <note>original: "Memoriale." This was the first book where transactions were jotted down in a rough format as they occurred.</note>
(ii) The <term>Journal</term> <note>original: "Giornale." A chronological record of transactions formatted for entry into the ledger.</note>
(iii) The <term>General Ledger</term> <note>original: "Quaderno." The "large book" or master record that organizes all transactions by account.</note>

Many small businesses use only the last two, the Journal and the General Ledger. First, the Memorandum is described, followed by descriptions of the contents and layout of the other two books.

Next, I define the Memorandum.

---

1. "Arrangement"; <note>original: "Dispone"</note>; literally, "the disposition."
2. "The body or total volume of all trade"; <note>original: "Corpo o vero monte de tutto el trafico"</note>; that is, business open to whatever is appropriate.
3. "The body or shop-based business"; <note>original: "Corpo o vero de botega"</note>; that is, business open to shop owners.
4. "Memorandum"; <note>original: "Memoriale"</note>; nowadays, in effect, loose leaf, being made up of individual data transaction vouchers.
5. "Journal"; <note>original: "Giornale"</note>; nowadays, loose leaf in the form of a computer printout analyzing the day's vouchers.
6. "Ledger" or "Notebook"; <note>original: "Quaderno"</note>; nowadays, loose leaf, the basis of the audit trail.

### CHAPTER 6

## THE MEMORANDUM OR SCRAP BOOK

->*The Memorandum, also known as the scrap book¹ and the day book.²*<-
->*Its purpose, who should make entries and what entries should be made.*<-

The purpose of the Memorandum or day book³ is to record all business transactions, regardless of size <note>original: "piccole e grandi," meaning both small and large dealings</note>, in chronological order. The businessman records in the day books, in detail, every business transaction, purchase or sale, day by day and hour by hour <note>original: "a giorno per giorno e ora per ora"</note>, and every other transaction, every "jot and tittle," at the time it takes place.

As with the inventory record, so with the day book, no detail should be omitted. The questions “who, what, why, how, when and where” need to be answered. But note that it is not wise to enter any contract details or confidential information in the day book.

The day book is kept because of the sheer volume of transactions. In the businessman’s absence, his agents, assistants or other employees should make entries, since a successful businessman never wants his employees to have to stop work. Businessmen and employees are frequently absent on business, perhaps in the marketplace, on visits, at a promotion; other employees who may not be well trained in bookkeeping are left at the home office. Those remaining must continue to sell and make collections and pickups, in order to satisfy customers, just as their employer would wish. Therefore, employees, not just their employer, must enter the details in the day books of every transaction that takes place. They should record details of the weights they make and the money they take in from cash sales or receivables. However, there is no need to standardize every entry into a single currency in the day book. This may be done later in the Journal and Ledger. <note>In the Renaissance, merchants dealt in a chaotic variety of local and foreign coins; the Memorandum served as a rough record of exactly what was received before it was converted into a standard "money of account" for the formal books.</note>

Whoever transfers entries from the day book to the Journal puts transaction details in order before entering them in the Journal. Then, when the owner returns, he will review the Journal entries and make any appropriate adjustments. That is why the books are so important for large-volume businesses. The day books provide complete double entry and offer full explanations, yet accumulate simple totals.

---

1. <term>Squartafoglio</term>: Literally, "The fourth book" in reverse order of use, before the day book, or Memorandum, the Journal and the Ledger. This scrap book or “loose-leaf or household expense notebook” might be kept in addition to the Memorandum or day books, for similar entries (sales or purchases) and then noted in the Memorandum prior to posting to the Journal or Ledger. The modern equivalent would be the sales day book.
2. <term>Vachetta</term>: Literally, "a little cow," referring to a book bound in cowhide leather, which was durable for daily use, as opposed to a loose-leaf book.
3. Here, <note>the author, Luca Pacioli</note> repeats the other names by which the Memorandum book may be known.
4. Be they “small or large” <note>original: "piccole e gradi"</note>.
5. Or, more poetically, “day by day, hour by hour” <note>original: "a giorno per giorno e ora per ora"</note>.

As with other accounting records, when the Memorandum <term>Memorandum: a preliminary book where transactions were first recorded before being transferred to the Journal</term> is filled up or at the year-end, <note>Depending on a particular business's policy, some close all their books at the year-end; others, not wishing to waste unused pages, only close books when they are full.</note> a distinguishing mark should be made on the book’s cover. Among Christian businessmen, it is also common to mark the initial account books with the sign of the cross. <note>Pacioli provides an interesting description of the practice of using the cross on the first account book: "From that glorious sign from which every spiritual enemy of ours flees, and the entire infernal crowd deservedly trembles before the sign" (original Italian: "De quel glorioso segno dalql fuggi ogni nostro spiritual nemico: e la caterua ttuta infernal meritamente trema del segno").</note> The books that follow may be marked in alphabetical <note>They may also have been marked in numerical order, but alphabetical order was most widely used in keeping records of Journals and Ledgers right into the 20th century.</note> order. The pages of account books should also be numbered. Businessmen of experience know that this is necessary even though other businessmen do not number pages. These other businessmen say that since all transactions are entered chronologically by date, additional numerical control is not necessary. This would be true if daily transactions could always be limited to a single page, but this is not the case for large-volume businesses when more than one page may be needed per day. Then, without numerical control, a cunning person could remove a page from a series of pages having the same date, and the fraud would go undetected. For these and other reasons, <note>Other reasons include the identification of days when there is less than a page of activity and to control loose-leaf records.</note> numerical control of the pages of all books of account is wise.

### CHAPTER 7

## AUTHENTICATION OF BUSINESS BOOKS AND RECORDS

->*By whom and why.*<-

It is the established practice <note>This historical commentary suggests that the formal registration of business records predates modern auditing and company law, showing that the accounting profession has long valued transparency and standardized regulation.</note> in certain regions for all accounting books and records to be officially registered. The merchant takes these books and presents them to a specific commercial officer <term>commercial officer: a public official, such as the city consuls mentioned by Pacioli, responsible for overseeing trade and legalizing documents</term>. <note>original: "como sonno consoli nela cita de perosa" — such as the consuls in the city of Perugia.</note> The officer is informed that these are the specific volumes in which your employees intend to record every detail of your business transactions. At this time, you must also declare the type of currency and the system of measures <note>original: "cioe a l de picioli: o uero a L de grossi: o uero a duc e L etc." — that is, whether using small pounds, large pounds, or ducats and lire.</note> that will be used in the entries.

The businessman should then transcribe these registration details onto the very first page of each account book. This should include the names of the specific employees who are authorized to make entries. This record must be formally authenticated by the officer. If there are later changes or if new employees are authorized to handle the books, these updates must also be registered at the commercial officer’s bureau. The merchant must carefully verify that his own copies match the official records, including:

- the date the registration took place
- the specific books and the identification marks they bear <note>referring to the crosses or letters described on page 12</note>
- the total number of pages in each book
- the names of the staff members keeping the books
- the name of the commercial officer presiding over the record

Finally, the merchant should request that the commercial officer sign each book and apply the official seal of his office. This serves to authenticate your records, providing legal weight <note>original: "fede" — faith or legal trust</note> in the event of future court proceedings, where your books might be required as evidence. <note>This remains a vital practice in many parts of the world. For example, similar bureaucratic procedures for authenticating every accounting record were still strictly required in Milan, Italy, as recently as 1980.</note>

This practice is highly commended, as are the jurisdictions that maintain such organized systems for registering companies.
