# Yellow Essence
<note>original: 黃精 (*Huangjing*); *Polygonatum*. This plant is a cornerstone of the <term>Materia Medica</term> (本草, *Bencao*), categorized as a "superior" herb. It was famously believed by ancient herbalists and Daoist seekers to nourish vital essence and promote longevity; legends even suggested that consuming it could allow a person to transcend the need for common food.</note>

Most importantly, the artist has rendered the thick, knotted rhizome (the underground stem) emerging from the soil, as this is the part of the plant used for its potent medicinal and life-extending properties.

## Yellow Essence

**Yellow Essence** grows in mountain valleys and is found throughout both the north and south of the country. Those specimens found on **Mount Song** and **Mount Mao** are considered to be of the highest quality.

<note>original: 嵩山 (Mount Song) and 茅山 (Mount Mao). Mount Song in Henan is one of the Five Sacred Peaks of China, while Mount Mao in Jiangsu is a historic center of Daoism, famous for its medicinal herbs and spiritual cultivation.</note>

In the third month of the year, the shoots emerge, reaching a height of one to two feet or more. The leaves resemble those of bamboo but are shorter, growing in opposite pairs along the stalk. The stems and stalks are soft and brittle, somewhat resembling the branches of a gardenia; they are yellow at the base and red at the tips. In the fourth month, the plant produces small, bluish-white flowers shaped like bean blossoms. The seeds are white and resemble broomcorn millet, though some varieties do not produce seeds at all.

The root resembles young ginger and is yellow in color. One should harvest the roots in the second month of the year, steaming them and then drying them in the sun before use.

<note>The preparation method of steaming and sun-drying (known as *paozhi*) was essential in traditional pharmacy to enhance the herb's tonic properties and remove its "raw" harshness.</note>

This plant is known by many names:
*   **Drooping Stems** <note>original: 委蕤 (*Weirui*)</note>
*   **Immortal's Surplus Grain** <note>original: 仙人餘糧 (*Xianren Yuliang*)</note>
*   **Dog Lattice** <note>original: 苟格 (*Gouge*)</note>
*   **Rabbit Bamboo** <note>original: 菟竹 (*Tuzhu*)</note>
*   **Hanging Pearl** <note>original: 垂珠 (*Chuizhu*)</note>
*   **Horse Arrow** <note>original: 馬箭 (*Majian*)</note>
*   **White Bletilla** <note>original: 白及 (*Baiji*). Note that in modern herbalism, *Baiji* refers to a different plant, but it was historically used as a synonym here.</note>
*   **Chicken Lattice** <note>original: 雞格 (*Jige*)</note>
*   **Savior of the Poor** <note>original: 救窮 (*Jiuqiong*)</note>
*   **Deer Bamboo** <note>original: 鹿竹 (*Luzhu*)</note>
*   **Multi-storied Building** <note>original: 重樓 (*Chonglou*)</note>

It is also colloquially known as **Pen-tube Vegetable**.

**Flavor and Nature:**
Its flavor is sweet, its nature is neutral, and it is non-toxic.

**Primary Functions:**
It primarily serves to **tonify the middle and boost qi** <note>A technical term in Chinese medicine meaning to strengthen the digestive system and increase vital energy.</note>, dispel wind-dampness, and soothe the **five viscera** <note>The Heart, Liver, Spleen, Lungs, and Kidneys.</note>. Taking this herb over a long period of time will make the body feel light and extend one's lifespan.

## Sweet Flag

Sweet Flag grows in the ponds and marshes of **Shangluo** <note>Modern-day Shangzhou in Shaanxi province</note> and in **Yandao** within the **Shu Prefecture** <note>The region of modern Sichuan</note>. Today it is found in many places, but the varieties from **Chizhou** <note>Modern-day Anhui</note> and **Rongzhou** <note>Modern-day Yibin, Sichuan</note> are considered to be of the highest quality.

Its green leaves reach a length of about one or two feet; the center of the leaf features a prominent ridge, giving it a shape like a sword. It is said to have no flowers or fruit <note>While the plant does produce a flower spike (spadix), ancient Chinese botanical texts often categorized it as flowerless because its flowering is inconspicuous or rare in certain climates.</note>. The roots should be gathered in the fifth and twelfth months and dried in the shade; currently, it is common practice to harvest them on the fifth day of the fifth month <note>The Dragon Boat Festival, a holiday where Sweet Flag is used rituals to ward off pestilence.</note>.

The roots are coiled and winding with nodes, shaped like a horsewhip. A single large root will put forth three or four lateral roots; the nodes on these side roots are particularly dense. Roots that possess nine nodes per inch are considered superior, though some varieties may have twelve nodes per inch. 

When first harvested, the roots are soft and hidden <note>Referring to their state in the mud or their initial texture</note>, but they become firm and solid after being dried in the sun. When broken open, the center is a pale reddish color. When chewed, it has a pungent, aromatic flavor and leaves behind very little residue. 

Many people cultivate this plant in dry sand and stones; if transplanted during the <term>Twelfth Lunar Month</term> <note>original: 臘月 (*làyuè*); the final month of the traditional lunar calendar</note>, it is especially easy to keep alive. It is also known by the name **Sun-Bright** <note>original: 昌陽 (*chāngyáng*)</note>. Its flavor is pungent and its nature is warm. It is used for long-standing <term>Wind-Dampness Numbness</term> <note>original: 風濕痺 (*fēngshībì*); a traditional diagnosis for chronic joint pain and rheumatism caused by environmental factors.</note>.

# Sancai Tuhui, Volume on Grasses and Trees, Part 1

**Ginseng** <note>original: 人參 (Renshen)</note> grows in the mountain valleys of **Shangdang** <note>A region in modern-day Shanxi province, historically famous for producing the highest quality ginseng</note>, as well as in the various prefectures of **Liaodong**, **Hedong**, and **Qinshan**. It is also found in **Hebei**, **Huaiyang**, and **Fujian**; the variety from the latter regions is known as **Silla Ginseng** <note>original: 新羅人參; Silla was an ancient Korean kingdom, and Korean ginseng has been a prized trade commodity for centuries</note>. However, none of these varieties are as excellent as the ginseng from **Shangdang**.

The root is shaped like that of the *Siler* plant <term>Fangfeng (Saposhnikovia divaricata)</term>, but it is moist and solid. In spring, the sprouts emerge; in the third or fourth month, it produces tiny flowers like millet grains, with thread-like stamens of a purplish-white color. After autumn, it bears seeds. Those roots that take on the shape of a human are considered to possess a divine spirit. The variety produced on **Mount Tai** is quite distinct, having green stems and leaves but a white root.

In the **Yangtze** and **Huai** regions, there is a variety called **"Earth Ginseng"** <note>original: 土人參 (Tu Renshen)</note>. Its leaves are small and spoon-shaped, resembling the **Balloon Flower** <term>Jiegeng (Platycodon grandiflorus)</term>, and it has an extremely sweet and pleasant flavor.

According to local lore, there is a way to test for genuine **Shangdang Ginseng**: have two people walk together, one with a piece of ginseng held in their mouth and the other without. After they have walked about three to five *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance; 5 li is approximately 2.5 kilometers or 1.5 miles</note>, the person who did not have the ginseng will certainly be panting heavily, while the one who held the ginseng in their mouth will breathe normally. This proves the ginseng is authentic.

Ginseng is also known by the names **"Human Bit,"** **"Ghost's Parasol,"** **"Divine Grass,"** **"Human Tenuity,"** **"Two Essences,"** and **"Blood Ginseng."** Those shaped like a human are particularly spiritual.

**Flavor and Nature:**
Sweet in taste. Its nature is slightly cold or slightly warm, and it is non-toxic.

**Primary Uses:**
It is primarily used for deficiencies in the *Qi* <note>Vital life force or energy</note> of the <term>Five Viscera</term> <note>The Heart, Liver, Spleen, Lungs, and Kidneys</note>, as well as for exhaustion, injuries from overwork, and general physical depletion. Long-term consumption makes the body feel light and extends one's years.

# Asparagus <note>original: 天門冬 (Tianmen Dong); specifically the species *Asparagus cochinchinensis*</note>

**Asparagus** grows in the mountain valleys of **Fenggao** <note>A historical county located near Mount Tai in modern-day Shandong Province</note>, though it is now found everywhere. In spring, it produces climbing vines as thick as a decorative hairpin, reaching heights of over ten feet. Its leaves resemble those of **fennel**; they are extremely sharp, fine, sparse, and smooth. Some varieties possess backward-facing thorns, while others are rough to the touch but lack thorns entirely. Those with leaves resembling fine, scattered needles of the **silk cedar** <note>likely referring to the Japanese Cedar or a similar conifer</note> are all classified as Asparagus.

In summer, it produces white flowers, though some varieties bear yellow ones. In autumn, it produces black seeds along the sides of its roots and branches. After the **Dog Days of summer** <note>original: 入伏 (rufu); the hottest period of the year</note>, the flowers disappear and the seeds develop in a hidden manner. Its roots are white or yellowish-purple, as thick as a finger and two to three inches long; the larger roots are considered superior. Those produced in the **Luoyang** region have large leaves and thick stems, while those from **Lingnan** <note>The far southern regions of China, such as Guangdong and Guangxi</note> do not flower.

This plant is also known as **Xiao**, **Diandong**, or **Dianji**. Its name varies significantly across the **Five Sacred Mountains of China**:
*   At the **Eastern Peak** (Mount Tai), it is called **Yinyanghuo** <note>This name usually refers to Epimedium (Horny Goat Weed), but here it is recorded as a regional synonym for Asparagus</note>.
*   At the **Central Peak** (Mount Song), it is called **Tianmen Dong**.
*   At the **Western Peak** (Mount Hua), it is called **Guansong**.
*   At the **Northern Peak** (Mount Heng in Shanxi), it is called **Wubuyu** <note>Literally translates to "No Illness Uncured"</note>.
*   At the **Southern Peak** (Mount Heng in Hunan), it is called **Baibu** <note>This name usually refers to the Stemona plant, but is used here locally for Asparagus</note>.

On the mountain banks of **Jingling**, it is called **Dianji**. Those grown in the shaded northern slopes of the Northern Peak are considered of the highest quality.

The flavor is bitter and sweet; its nature is neutral but very cold, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat sudden "wind-dampness" <note>A traditional category of ailments often manifesting as joint pain or seasonal illness</note> and partial paralysis. It strengthens the bone marrow, <gloss>stabilizes the Qi of the lungs and aids urination</gloss>, and if taken over a long period, it is said to prolong one's life.

# Licorice <note>original: 甘草 (Gancao); specifically the species *Glycyrrhiza uralensis*, often called Ural Licorice</note>

->[Illustration: A detailed botanical woodcut featuring three distinct specimens of the Licorice plant. The drawing highlights the plant's compound leaf structures and sturdy, upright stems, framed within a traditional decorative border.]<-

**Licorice** grows in the mountain valleys and plains of **Hexi** <note>The Hexi Corridor in modern-day Gansu Province</note> and **Shangjun** <note>A historical commandery located in what is now northern Shaanxi Province</note>, though it is now found in many regions. In spring, the plant sends up sprouts that reach two to three feet in height. Its leaves resemble those of the **indigo plant** <note>original: 青黛 (qingdai)</note> or a **bean plant**; the stems are covered in a bark that is purple-red in color.

In the fifth month <note>of the lunar calendar; roughly mid-summer</note>, it produces flowers that look like bean blossoms, appearing in shades of purple and white. Its seed pods are similar to those of a **pea** <note>original: 豌豆 (wandou)</note> but are smaller in size. The roots can grow to a length of three or four feet, varying in thickness. They are characterized by a reddish outer skin and yellow inner flesh; the flavor is remarkably sweet.

Because of its unique properties, this herb is also known as **Guolao** <note>literally "The Imperial Tutor" or "State Elder"</note>. This title is a metaphor for the plant's function in pharmacology: much like a senior statesman who harmonizes a royal court, Licorice is used to balance the diverse properties of other medicinal ingredients, moderating toxicity and ensuring all the herbs in a formula work together effectively.

# Licorice

**Licorice** grows in the mountain valleys of **Xichuan** <note>Historical Western Sichuan</note>, **Jisha Mountain**, and **Shangjun** <note>A historical commandery in northern Shaanxi</note>. Today, it is found throughout the Shaanxi and Hedong <note>The region east of the Yellow River, modern-day Shanxi</note> prefectures.

In the spring, it produces green sprouts reaching one or two feet in height. Its leaves resemble those of the **Scholar Tree** <note>original: 槐 (huai); *Sophora japonica*</note>. In the seventh month <note>of the lunar calendar; roughly late summer</note>, it blooms with purple flowers that resemble millet. It produces seed pods similar to **small beans** <note>original: 卑豆 (beidou)</note>. The roots can reach three or four feet in length. The bark is reddish with horizontal ridges; beneath these ridges grow many fine, fibrous roots.

The roots should be harvested in the second and eighth months on **Chu days** <note>original: 除日 (churi); specific auspicious days in the traditional calendar used for medicinal gathering</note>. They should be sun-dried for ten days until cured. To prepare the herb, remove the **rhizome head** <note>original: 蘆頭 (lutou)</note> and the red outer skin, then dry it in the shade. 

There are several varieties of this herb. Those that are firm and possess a clear, solid grain when cut are considered the highest quality. Varieties that are light, hollow, have vertical fibers, or are sold as fine scraps are unsuitable for medicinal use. 

Licorice is known by several alternative names, including **Honey-Sweet** <note>original: 蜜甘 (migan)</note>, **Beautiful Herb** <note>original: 美草 (meicao)</note>, **Honey Herb** <note>original: 蜜草 (micao)</note>, and **Dew Herb** <note>original: 露草 (lucao)</note>. 

Its flavor is sweet and its nature is balanced. It neutralizes the toxicity of various drugs and harmonizes all other medicines, ensuring they work effectively. For this reason, it is nicknamed the **State Elder** <note>original: 國老 (Guolao); an honorific title for a senior statesman who resolves disputes and brings harmony to the court</note>. It is used to supplement the body after fatigue or injury, replenish essence, nourish the **qi** <note>vital life force</note>, and strengthen the sinews and bones. It also resolves ailments characterized by extreme cold or heat, and is used extensively in many medicinal formulas.

# Rehmannia

**Rehmannia** <note>original: 地黃 (Dihuang); Rehmannia glutinosa</note> grows in the river valleys and marshes of Xianyang; those harvested from yellow soil are of the best quality. It is now found in many regions, though the variety from Tongchuan is considered the most superior.

In the second lunar month, the leaves emerge and spread across the ground. They resemble the leaves of the **Plantain** <term>original: 車前 (Cheqian); a common medicinal weed</term>, but the surface is wrinkled and not glossy. Taller plants reach over a foot in height, while shorter ones are only three or four inches tall. Its flowers resemble those of the sesame plant and are reddish-purple, though some plants produce yellow flowers. The fruit forms a capsule similar to that of **Forsythia** <term>original: 蓮翹子 (Lianqiaozi)</term>; the seeds are very fine and brown in color.

The roots resemble human fingers and are entirely yellow, though their thickness and length are not uniform. The roots are harvested in the second and eighth lunar months. If the roots are steamed until they are thoroughly soft and then dried in the sun, they are called **Processed Rehmannia** <term>original: 熟地黃 (Shu Dihuang)</term>. Those dried in the shade are called **Raw (Dried) Rehmannia** <term>original: 生地黃 (Sheng Dihuang)</term>.

Physicians distinguish the quality of the roots by soaking the freshly harvested batch in water. Those that float are called "Heavenly Yellow" <note>original: 天黃 (Tianhuang)</note> and are unfit for use. Those that half-sink are called "Human Yellow" <note>original: 人黃 (Renhuang)</note> and are considered secondary in quality. Those that sink completely to the bottom are called **"Earth Yellow"** <note>original: 地黃 (Dihuang)</note> and are the very best.

The plant is also known as "Earth Marrow" <note>original: 地髓 (Disui)</note>, *Xia* <note>original: 芐</note>, or *Ba* <note>original: 芭</note>. It is used to treat palpitations caused by fright, consumptive fatigue, the vomiting of blood, nosebleeds, and uterine bleeding or blood circulation issues in women. 

When dried in the sun, the herb’s nature is balanced; when dried over a fire, its nature is warm. One must strictly avoid using iron vessels when preparing this medicine. <note>Traditional Chinese pharmacy often warns against iron contact for certain herbs to prevent "tainting" the medicinal essence or causing adverse chemical reactions.</note>

# Atractylodes
<note>original: 术 (Zhu). Specifically refers to the rhizome of Atractylodes, a staple in Chinese herbalism known for its ability to "strengthen the spleen" and dry dampness.</note>

**Atractylodes** grows in the mountain valleys of Mount Zheng in Hanzhong and Nanzheng <note>Ancient administrative regions located in what is now southern Shaanxi province</note>. While it is now found in many places, those harvested from **Mount Song** or **Mount Mao** <note>Mount Song in Henan and Mount Mao in Jiangsu are historically renowned as the best sources for this herb</note> are considered of the highest quality.

In summer, it produces purple-blue flowers that also resemble the prickly thistle; some varieties may produce yellow or white flowers. After the peak of summer heat <note>original: 入伏 (rufu); the "Dog Days" or the three ten-day periods of the hottest season</note>, the plant bears seeds, and by autumn, the sprouts wither. The root resembles ginger but has fine secondary roots growing along the sides; the skin is black while the inner flesh is yellowish-white and contains a purple resinous liquid.

The roots are harvested during the second, third, eighth, and ninth lunar months and then sun-dried. Both the fresh and dried forms are utilized, though it is most common to harvest them in the eighth month. This plant is also called **Mountain Ginger** <term>original: 山薑 (Shanjiang)</term> or **Mountain Link** <term>original: 山連 (Shanlian)</term>.

Its flavor is bitter yet sweet, and its nature is warming. It is primarily used to treat all manner of "Wind" disorders <note>In traditional medicine, "Wind" (Feng) refers to a category of illnesses ranging from the common cold to tremors or joint pain</note>, exhaustion from overwork, "Cold Qi" <note>Conditions believed to be caused by internal cold, often manifesting as pain or poor circulation</note>, and abdominal bloating. It supplements the lower back and knees, clears phlegm, treats edema, and promotes urination. It is also effective for stopping chronic vomiting or acid reflux and treating weakness in the tendons and bones. Before use, it should be soaked in **rice-water** <note>original: 米泔 (migan); the water used to wash rice, used here to temper the herb's potency</note> for one night.

# Dodder Seed
<note>original: 菟絲子 (Tusizi); the seeds of *Cuscuta chinensis*. It is a parasitic plant famous in traditional medicine for "tonifying" the kidneys and improving eyesight.</note>

**Dodder Seed** grows in the river valleys and fields of Korea. Between those from Korea and those from Wangu <gloss>Ju</gloss> <note>Wangu refers to a historical region in modern-day Henan province; the gloss "Ju" likely completes the historical place name</note>, which is superior? 

In summer, it produces sprouts that look like silk threads. These creep and spread over other grasses and trees. In the sixth or seventh months, it bears seeds that are extremely fine, resembling silkworm eggs and colored like yellow clay. In the ninth month, these are harvested and dried in the sun. They are most effective when prepared with wine.

There are two varieties: the yellow and fine type is called **Red Cord** <term>original: 赤綱 (Chigang)</term>, while the lighter-colored and larger type is called **Rabbit’s Coil** <term>original: 菟纍 (Tulei)</term>. Their medicinal effects are identical.

# Ox-knee (Continued)
<note>original: 牛膝 (Niuxi); *Achyranthes bidentata*.</note>

**Ox-knee** grows in the river valleys of Huanei <note>a historical region in modern Henan Province</note> and in Linqu. Today, it is also found in the regions of Jiang-Huai <note>the lands between the Yangtze and Huai Rivers</note>, Fujian, Guangdong, and Guanzhong <note>the Wei River valley in Shaanxi</note>. However, none are as high in quality as the authentic variety produced in Huaizhou.

In the spring, it produces sprouts with stems two to three feet high. These are bluish-purple with nodes that look like the knees of a crane or an ox; it is named for this resemblance. The leaves are pointed and round, growing in pairs opposite each other. Flowers emerge from the nodes in the form of spikes. In autumn, it bears very fine seeds.

There are two varieties of this plant: those with purple stems and large nodes are considered "male," while those with green, slender stems are "female." The roots are harvested in the second, eighth, and tenth months and should be dried in the shade. Roots that are exceptionally long, large, supple, and moist are considered the best. The stems and leaves may also be used on their own.

It is also called <term>Hundredfold</term> <note>original: 百倍 (Baibei); likely referring to the plant's potent medicinal efficacy or its ability to strengthen the body many times over.</note>. Its flavor is **bitter** and sour, and its nature is neutral. It is primarily used to treat "cold dampness" <note>a traditional medical category of ailment characterized by joint pain and stiffness</note> and to strengthen the sinews and bones.

## Motherwort Seed
<note>original: 茺蔚子 (Chongweizi); the seeds of *Leonurus japonicus*. Today, the plant is more commonly known as "Mother-Benefitting Herb" (Yimucao), but this historical entry focuses specifically on the seeds.</note>

<note>The accompanying woodcut illustration depicts the Motherwort plant, showing its characteristic deeply lobed leaves and the whorls of small flowers and seeds that cluster around the stem at the leaf axils.</note>

**Motherwort Seed** is also known by the names **Beneficial Mother** <note>original: 益母 (Yimu)</note> and **White Flower Herb**. It grows in the wild fields and along the banks of ponds. The plant features a square stem and grows to a height of three or four feet.

### Motherwort Seed
<note>original: 茺蔚子 (Chongweizi); the seeds of *Leonurus japonicus*.</note>

**Motherwort seeds** grow by the seashores, in ponds, and in marshes, though today they are found everywhere. We carefully note that the *Classic of Poetry* <note>original: 毛詩 (Mao Shi); specifically the Mao version of the *Shijing*</note> mentions this plant in the line: "In the valley is the *Tui*."

Guo Pu <note>276–324 AD; a famous commentator and polymath</note> stated that the Motherwort plant resembles the <term>Perilla</term> <note>original: 荏 (Ren); *Perilla frutescens*</note>, having a square stem and white flowers that bloom between the nodes. Lu Ji <note>261–303 AD; a prominent scholar and writer</note> observed that both the *Han Odes* <note>a version of the *Classic of Poetry* compiled by Han Ying</note> and the *Three Lexicons* <note>original: 三蒼 (San Cang); a collection of early Chinese dictionaries</note> identify this *Tui* as Motherwort. It is said that the philosopher Zengzi <note>a disciple of Confucius famed for his filial piety</note> was moved to deep reflection upon seeing it. Liu Xin <note>c. 50 BC – 23 AD; a Han Dynasty scholar</note> also referred to it as "Foul Weed," which is simply another name for Motherwort. Today, it is seen very frequently in gardens and fields, and its appearance matches Guo Pu’s description.

Flowers grow at every node along the upper parts of the leaves. The seeds resemble <term>Cockscomb</term> seeds <note>original: 雞冠子 (Jiguanzi); *Celosia cristata*</note> and are black in color. The stem is shaped with four square edges. The plant is harvested in the fifth month; it is also said that the seeds should be gathered in the ninth month. In medical formulas, it is rare to see the seeds themselves being used.

The plant is also known as **Motherwort** <note>original: 益母 (Yimu); literally "Benefitting Mothers"</note>, **Benefitting Vision**, **Great Bundle**, and **True Wei**. Its flavor is **pungent** and sweet; its nature is slightly warm and slightly cold, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to brighten the eyes, benefit the <term>Essence</term> <note>original: 精 (Jing); the fundamental vital energy or reproductive power</note>, remove "water vapor" <note>referring to edema or fluid accumulation</note>, and treat "blood reversal" <note>blood stasis or abnormal circulation</note>, extreme heat, headaches, and irritability.

## Lilyturf

**Lilyturf** <note>original: 麥門冬; *Maimendong* (*Ophiopogon japonicus*). The name literally translates to "Wheat-Gate Winter."</note> grows in the river valleys of Hangu <note>An ancient mountain pass of great strategic importance in modern-day Henan.</note>, as well as on hillsides, in fertile soil, among rocks, and in long-abandoned sites. Today, it can be found nearly everywhere. Its leaves are green and resemble sedge grass; they grow over a foot long and do not wither throughout the four seasons. The roots are yellowish-white and possess fibrous rootlets formed like a string of beads. Because the root tubers resemble the grains of **hull-less barley** <term>穬麥; *kuangmai*</term>, it was given the name "Wheat-Gate Winter."

In the fourth month of the lunar calendar, it blooms with pale red flowers similar to those of the **water pepper** <note>original: 紅蓼; *hongliao*</note>. Its fruit is bluish-green and round like pearls. Among the varieties found in the Jiangnan region <note>The region south of the Yangtze River.</note>, those with larger leaves have seedlings resembling the **orange day-lily** <term>鹿葱; *lucong* (literally "deer onion")</term>, while those with smaller leaves resemble leeks. There are three or four varieties of different sizes, but their medicinal functions are similar. Some say that those harvested from the Wu region <note>Modern-day Jiangsu and Zhejiang provinces.</note> are especially superior.

The roots are harvested in the second, third, eighth, and tenth months. In the ancient state of Qin it is named **Sheep Leek**; in Qi it is called **Love Leek**; in Chu it is known as **Horse Leek**; and in Yue it is called **Sheep Qi**. Another name for it is **Yu’s Reed** <note>original: 禹葭; *Yujia*. Referring to the legendary Great Yu.</note>.

---

It is also named **Yu’s Surplus Grain** <note>original: 禹餘糧; *Yuyuliang*. Note: In other texts, this name often refers to a mineral (limonite), but here it is listed as an alias for Lilyturf.</note>. Its flavor is sweet and balanced, or slightly cold, and it is non-toxic. It is used to treat exhaustion and injury, and it serves to **tranquilize the ethereal and corporeal souls** <note>original: 安魂定魄; *an hun ding po*. In traditional thought, these are the two aspects of the human spirit that must be harmonized for mental health.</note>. It stops thirst, helps a person put on healthy weight, treats seasonal infectious diseases and heat-induced mania, relieves headaches, and stops coughing.

# Duhuo and Qianghuo

**Duhuo** <note>original: 獨活; *Angelica pubescens*</note> and **Qianghuo** <note>original: 羌活; *Notopterygium incisum*</note> originate in the river valleys of Yongzhou <note>An ancient province covering parts of modern Shaanxi and Gansu.</note>, or in Longxi, Nan’an, and Shuhan. Those produced in the Shuhan region <note>The ancient state in the Sichuan Basin; modern Sichuan province.</note> are considered the finest quality. 

In the spring, the sprouts emerge with leaves resembling green hemp. In the sixth month, the plant blooms in clusters, producing flowers that are either yellow or purple. At the time the seeds form, the plants with yellow leaves are those found growing amidst rocks, while those with green leaves are found growing in fertile soil.

This herb is unique because it remains steady and does not sway when the wind blows, yet it moves of its own accord when there is no wind at all; for this reason, one of its names is the **Self-Swaying Grass** <note>original: 獨搖草; *Duyaocao*</note>.

The roots are harvested during the second and eighth months of the lunar calendar and should be dried in the sun. The *Classic of Materia Medica* <note>original: 本經; *Benjing*, referring to the *Shennong Bencao Jing*, the foundational text of Chinese herbology.</note> states that these two substances belong to the same class. However, contemporary people distinguish them: the variety that is purple in color with dense nodes is considered **Qianghuo**, while the yellow variety that forms large clumps is **Duhuo**.

Another account states that **Duhuo** grows in the northern parts of Yizhou in western Sichuan. It is slightly white in color, hollow, and large in shape, though its medicinal uses are similar to those of **Qianghuo**. 

It is also known by the following names:
*   **Qiang-Green** <note>original: 羌青; *Qiangqing*</note>
*   **Envoy Protecting the Qiang** <note>original: 護羌使者; *Huqiang Shizhe*</note>
*   **Envoy of the Barbarian King** <note>original: 胡王使者; *Huwang Shizhe*</note>

The flavor of the herb is sweet and balanced, or slightly warm, and it is non-toxic. It is used to treat all types of "wind" disorders <note>In traditional Chinese medicine, "wind" (feng) refers to a category of ailments including strokes, tremors, and wandering joint pain.</note>, contractions and spasms of the sinews and bones, and weakness or wasting of the four limbs. It is also effective for dizziness, brightening the eyes, treating red and painful eyes, internal exhaustion, damage from overwork, and "cold qi" <note>A condition of internal stagnation or chills.</note>.

# Shengma (Ascending Hemp)

**Shengma** <note>original: 升麻; *Cimicifuga* species, also known as Bugbane or Black Cohosh.</note>

The medicinal herb **Shengma** is found in the river valleys and mountain marshlands of the Hanzhong region <note>Modern-day southern Shaanxi province.</note> and the areas surrounding the capital.

The plant's sprouts emerge in the spring. Its leaves are green and bear a striking resemblance to those of the hemp plant <note>original: 麻; *ma*</note>, which is why it is called "Ascending Hemp." The stems grow to a height of three or four feet. During the sixth month, it blooms with clusters of small, delicate flowers that are white or slightly tinged with purple. 

The root is the part used for medicine. It is typically harvested in the second or eighth month of the lunar calendar. The exterior of the root is black and rough, often described as having "bird-like eyes" <note>Referring to the small pits or scars left on the rhizome where previous years' stems have withered away.</note>. The interior is light, greenish-white, and has a porous, spongy texture.

The *Classic of Materia Medica* <note>The *Shennong Bencao Jing*.</note> notes that while the plant resembles hemp, its nature is to "ascend" <note>original: 升; *sheng*</note>. This refers to its medicinal property of lifting the clear energy (*qi*) of the body and treating conditions where the internal organs or energy have "sunk." It is frequently used in formulas alongside **Duhuo** and **Qianghuo** to clear heat and detoxify the system.

# Shengma (Bugbane Rhizome)

**Shengma** <note>original: 升麻; *Cimicifuga* species, often called Bugbane or Cohosh. The name literally translates to "Ascending Hemp" due to its hemp-like leaves and the "ascending" nature of its medicinal effect.</note> grows in the river valleys and mountain wilderness of **Yizhou** <note>An ancient province covering parts of modern Sichuan and Chongqing.</note>. Today, it can be found throughout the prefectures and commanderies of **Shuhan** <note>Sichuan region</note>, **Shaanxi**, and **Huainan**. Among these, the variety sourced from the rivers of **Shu** <note>Sichuan</note> is considered the most superior in quality.

In the spring, the plant sends up sprouts that reach a height of approximately three *chi* <note>About three feet or one meter.</note>. The leaves are green and bear a close resemblance to those of the **hemp plant**. During the fourth and fifth months, it produces flowers that look like clusters of white millet. After the sixth month, the plant bears black fruit. 

The root is purple, resembling the root of the **Artemisia** <note>Wormwood</note> plant, but it is covered in numerous fine whiskers. The roots are harvested during the second and eighth months of the lunar calendar and are then dried thoroughly in the sun.

Another name for this herb is **Zhouma** <note>original: 周麻; "Universal Hemp."</note>. Its flavor is described as both sweet and bitter; its essence is balanced and slightly cold, and it is entirely non-toxic.

**Shengma** is primarily used to neutralize "the hundred toxins." It is effective against the "hundred evils and demonic possessions" <note>In ancient medical texts, terms like "ghosts" or "evils" (*guimei*) often referred to acute infectious diseases, sudden strokes, or severe environmental pathogens that modern medicine might categorize as viruses or bacteria.</note>. Furthermore, it treats **wandering wind** <note>A term for migrating pains or skin rashes that appear and disappear quickly.</note>, swellings caused by heat-toxins, sore throats, and ulcerations of the mouth.

**Plantain Seed** <note>original: 車前子; *Cheqianzi*</note> grows in the marshy plains, hills, and along the roadsides of Zhending <note>A historical prefecture in modern-day Hebei Province</note>. Currently, it is found everywhere throughout the regions of the Yangtze and Huai rivers, as well as the northern territories near the capital. 

In early spring, the leaves sprout and spread across the ground, shaped like the head of a spoon. In plants that have grown for several years, the leaves can reach lengths of over a foot. The flower stalks resemble a mouse's tail; the flowers themselves are very fine, greenish with a slight reddish tint. The seeds produced are similar to those of the Draba plant <note>original: 葶藶; *Tingli*</note> and are a reddish-black color. 

Traditionally, the plant was to be harvested on the fifth day of the fifth lunar month and dried in the shade. Nowadays, people harvest the young shoots in the fifth month and the seeds during the seventh and eighth months. Some people cultivate it in their own gardens and orchards; this practice is especially favored in the Shu region <note>Modern-day Sichuan province</note>. People in the north sometimes take the roots, dry them in the sun, and sell them as Purple Aster <note>original: 紫苑; *Ziyuan*</note>, which is a serious error. 

Regarding its usage, we carefully note that the "South of Zhou" section of the *Classic of Poetry* says: "Gather, gather the plantain" <note>original: 芣苡; *Fuyi*</note>. The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, dating back to the 3rd century BCE</note> states that the *Fuyi* is the *Maxi*, and the *Maxi* is the *Cheqian* (Plantain). 

The scholar Guo Pu noted: "The modern Plantain grass has large leaves and stands in the middle of the road with long spikes; it flourishes by the wayside. In the region east of the Yangtze, it is called 'Toad’s Clothing' <note>original: 蝦蟆衣; *Xiamayi*</note>." 

Lu Ji observed: "The *Maxi* is also known as 'In Front of the Cart' <note>Cheqian</note> or 'Blocking the Path' <note>Dangdao</note>. It delights in growing within the tracks left by oxen; therefore, the people of Youzhou call it 'Ox-Tongue Grass' <note>original: 牛舌草; *Niushecao*</note>." 

Its nature is cold and non-toxic. It promotes urination, eliminates "damp-numbness" <term>shibi: a traditional diagnosis involving pain, swelling, or heaviness in the joints caused by environmental moisture</term>, brightens the eyes, and treats redness and pain in the eyes.

# Muxiang (Costus Root)

**Muxiang** <note>original: 木香; *Aucklandia lappa*</note> originates from the valleys of Yongchang. Nowadays, it is only found arriving on foreign ships at the port of Guangzhou; it is not produced anywhere else. Tao Yinju <note>Tao Hongjing (456–536 CE), a renowned Daoist scholar and physician</note> stated that this is the same as **Qingmuxiang** <note>original: 青木香; Green Wood Fragrance</note>.

The root clusters are large and resemble those of the eggplant. The leaves are similar to those of the *Rumex* <note>original: 羊蹄; literally "Sheep’s Hoof," commonly known as Dock</note> but are longer and larger. Its flowers are like chrysanthemums, and the seeds are yellowish-black. There is also a variety with leaves resembling mountain yams that produces purple flowers. The roots and sprouts may be harvested for medicinal use at any time of the year. Those specimens that are shaped like **dry bones** are considered to be of the highest quality.

In the region between the Yangtze and Huai rivers, there is a variety known as **Tu-qingmuxiang** <note>Local Green Costus</note>, which is unsuitable for medicinal use. In the gardens of Wang Chang of the Former Shu <note>A 10th-century regional kingdom</note>, this plant was once cultivated; it was said that the sprouts grew three to four feet high, with leaves eight or nine inches long that were wrinkled, soft, and hairy, bearing yellow flowers. These are likely also a variety of **Tumuxiang** <note>original: 土木香; Local Costus/Elecampane</note>.

Another name for this herb is **Mixiang** <note>original: 密香; Honey Fragrance</note>. Its flavor is acrid, its nature is warm, and it is non-toxic. It is used to treat all manner of *qi* <note>Vital energy or internal pressure</note> imbalances in the chest and abdomen, as well as "sudden turmoil" <note>original: 霍亂; *Huoluan*, a term for acute vomiting and diarrhea similar to cholera</note> and dysentery. It is also used to stabilize pregnancy, strengthen the spleen, and aid digestion. Furthermore, it treats carbuncles and fistulas, cold pain in the bladder, vomiting, and acid reflux.

## Chinese Yam <note>original: 薯蕷; *Shuyu*. Now commonly called *Shanyao*.</note>

**Chinese Yam** grows in the valleys of Mount Song <note>original: 嵩高; *Songgao*, one of the Five Great Mountains of China</note>. Today, it is found everywhere, though those harvested from the Northern Capital or Siming are considered the finest.

In spring, the sprouts emerge with purple stems and green leaves. The leaves have three pointed lobes, resembling the **Morning Glory** <note>original: 牽牛; *Qianniu*</note>, but they are thicker and have a glossy luster. In summer, the plant opens tiny white flowers that closely resemble those of the jujube tree. In autumn, fruit grows among the leaves, shaped like small bells <note>These are the aerial tubers or bulbils, often called "yam beans" today</note>.

The roots are harvested during the second and eighth months of the lunar calendar. However, people nowadays often harvest them in winter and spring. When the outer skin is scraped away, those that are pure white are of the highest quality; those that appear greenish-black are unsuitable for use. They should be dried in the sun before being prepared as medicine.

In the southern regions, there is a variety that grows in the mountains with roots as thin as a finger. These are extremely firm and dense. When scraped and added to boiling soups, they remain in solid pieces and do not dissolve; their flavor is considered even more precious and delicious.

This plant is also known as **Mountain Taro** <note>original: 山芋; *Shanyu*</note>. In the ancient regions of Qin and Chu, it was called **Jade Extension** <note>original: 玉延; *Yuyan*</note>, while in Zheng and Yue, it was known as the **Earth Lord** <note>original: 土諸; *Tuzhu*</note>.

Its flavor is sweet, its nature is warm, and it is non-toxic. It aids the <term>Five Viscera</term> <note>The functional systems of the Heart, Liver, Spleen, Lungs, and Kidneys</note>, strengthens the sinews and bones, bolsters the will, and calms the spirit. It is primarily used to treat nocturnal emission and forgetfulness.

# Water Plantain

**Water Plantain** <note>original: 澤瀉; *Zexie*</note> grows in the ponds and marshes of Runan <note>A historical commandery in modern-day Henan province.</note>. Today, it is also found in the regions of Shandong, the Yellow River valley of Shaanxi, and the Yangtze-Huai River area, though the specimens from Hanzhong are considered the finest.

The shoots sprout in the spring, typically in shallow water. Its leaves resemble an ox's tongue, and it grows upon a single, long stem. The plant is harvested at the end of autumn and then dried in the sun. This material is extremely prone to rot and insect damage; therefore, it must always be stored in airtight containers.

Those produced in Hanzhong are large and long, featuring a distinct bifurcation <note>original: 兩岐; a split or fork</note> at the tail end; these are considered the highest quality. The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, dating back to the 3rd century BCE.</note> refers to this plant as *Xi* <note>original: 蕮</note>. It is also known by the names *Kui* <note>original: 藈</note>, *Shuixie* <note>original: 水瀉; "Water Drainer"</note>, *Jixie* <note>original: 及瀉</note>, *Mangyu* <note>original: 芒芋; "Miscanthus Yam"</note>, and *Huxie* <note>original: 鵠瀉; "Swan Drainer"</note>.

Its flavor is sweet, its nature is cold, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat kidney deficiency and involuntary ejaculation. It also treats the "five types of strangury" <note>original: 五淋; *wulin*. A traditional category of urinary disorders involving pain, urgency, or blockage.</note>, clears heat from the bladder, and promotes the opening of the body's water passages.

# Red Sage

**Red Sage** <term>Danshen</term> <note>Salvia miltiorrhiza</note> grows in the river valleys of **Mount Tongbai** <note>located in modern Henan province</note> and **Mount Tai**. Today, it is also found throughout the prefectures of **Shaanxi**, **Hedong** <note>the region "East of the Yellow River," modern Shanxi</note>, and **Suizhou**.

In the second month of the lunar calendar, the sprouts emerge, reaching a height of about one foot. The stems are square-angled and green in color. The leaves grow in opposite pairs, appearing similar to **mint** but covered in fine hairs. In the third month, the plant produces reddish-purple flowers that resemble those of the **perilla** <note>original: 蘇; *Su*</note> plant. The roots are deep red, as thick as a finger, and over a foot in length; a single sprout typically produces several roots.

The roots are harvested in the fifth month and dried in the sun. Some sources suggest that those harvested in the third month are of superior quality, while those gathered during the peak of summer are often hollow and of poor quality.

It is also known by the following names:
*   **Cicada-Repelling Grass** <note>original: 却蟬草; *Quechancao*</note>

## Yuanzhi

**Yuanzhi** <term>Thin-leaf Milkwort</term> <note>Polygala tenuifolia</note> grows in the area of Mount Tai and the river valleys of Jurong. Today, it is also found throughout the prefectures of Heshan and Jingxi.

The root is yellow and shaped like the root of an artemisia plant. The sprouts are known as **Xiaocao** <term>Small Grass</term>; they resemble **Mahuang** <term>Ephedra</term> but are green in color. They also look like the leaves of the Chinese pea, though some specimens have leaves resembling the **Daqing** <term>Indigo-woad</term> but smaller. 

The plant flowers in the third lunar month with white blossoms. The roots can reach a length of one foot. In the fourth month, the roots and leaves are gathered and dried in the shade for medicinal use.

Specimens produced in **Sichuan** <note>original: 泗川; likely referring to the Si River region or a variant of Sichuan</note> produce red flowers, and both their roots and leaves are larger than those found elsewhere. Those from **Shangzhou** have roots that are black. According to popular tradition, the **Yuanzhi** from **Yimen** <note>An ancient name for Kaifeng, the Song Dynasty capital</note> is considered the highest quality.

In ancient texts, both the **Yuanzhi** (the root) and the **Xiaocao** (the sprouts) were used interchangeably. However, modern physicians primarily use the root and rarely utilize the "Small Grass" sprouts. 

This herb is also known by the names **Jiwan** <term>Thorny Aster</term>, **Yaorao** <term>Winding Vine</term>, or **Xicao** <term>Slender Grass</term>.

**Properties and Usage:**
*   **Taste:** Bitter.
*   **Nature:** Warm.
*   **Toxicity:** Non-toxic.

It is used to treat the spirit and combat **forgetfulness** <term>jianwang</term>. It settles the **ethereal and corporeal souls** <term>hun-po</term> <note>In traditional thought, these represent the mental and physical aspects of the soul</note>, ensuring the individual does not become confused. It also strengthens the **Yang path** <note>A reference to male vitality and reproductive health</note> and is primarily used to treat "dreaming of evil" <note>likely referring to night terrors or disturbing, spirit-related nightmares</note>.

# Gentian Root

Gentian root grows in the mountain valleys of Qiqu <note>An ancient district in modern-day Linqu, Shandong</note> and Yuanju <note>An ancient district in modern-day Heze, Shandong</note>; today, it is also found along common roadsides. Its perennial root is yellowish-white, and from the base, it sends out more than ten secondary roots that resemble **Achyranthes** <note>original: 牛膝 (Niuxi); another medicinal root known for its knee-like joints</note>. The shoots grow straight upward to a height of over one *chi* <note>A traditional Chinese foot, approximately 12 inches</note>.

In the fourth month of the lunar calendar, the leaves sprout; they are thin, and the stalks resemble small bamboo twigs. In the seventh month, it blooms with flowers like morning glories, shaped like hand-bells or wind-chimes, and colored a deep, vivid bluish-green. After winter, the plant bears seeds and the shoots then wither. 

The roots should be harvested in the second, eighth, eleventh, and twelfth months and dried in the shade. Another name for this plant is *Lingyou* <note>original: 陵游</note>. Its flavor is bitter, its nature is "greatly cold," and it is non-toxic. It is used to treat **fright-disorders** <note>original: 客忤 (kewu); a traditional diagnosis for sudden illness or seizures in children believed to be caused by external shocks</note>, childhood nutritional or *qi* ailments, and delirium during high fevers. It is also used for skin sores and scabies, to "brighten the eyes," and to soothe irritability.

# Asarum

Asarum <note>commonly known as Fine-Leaf Wild Ginger or *Xixin*</note> grows in the mountain valleys of Huashan <note>One of the Five Great Mountains of China, located in modern-day Shaanxi Province</note>.

The roots of this plant are remarkably thin and possess an intensely pungent, spicy flavor, which is the origin of its name: "Fine-Spicy" <note>original: 細辛 (Xixin); *Xi* means fine or thin, and *Xin* means spicy or pungent</note>. In the classic pharmacopoeias <note>original: 本草 (Bencao)</note>, it is recorded that the best variety comes from the Huazhou region.

The leaves are heart-shaped, resembling those of a mallow but thicker and a deeper green. The flowers grow close to the ground; they are dark purple and shaped like small, half-closed bells. The root system is a dense cluster of fine, fibrous threads. When harvesting, one must be careful to select roots that are "true"—they should be extremely thin, light in color, and have a scent so sharp it clears the senses.

The roots should be gathered in the ninth month of the lunar calendar and dried in the shade. It is a standard practice to remove the "heads" or rhizomes before use, as they are known to cause nausea. 

In terms of medicinal properties, Asarum's flavor is pungent and its nature is "warm." It is a vital herb for dispelling **Wind-Cold** <note>A traditional diagnosis for ailments caused by external cold, characterized by chills and body aches</note>. It is primarily used to treat:
*   Stubborn headaches that feel as though they are "in the bone."
*   Toothaches and "tooth-wind" <note>Acute dental pain or gum inflammation</note>.
*   Nasal congestion and the loss of the sense of smell.
*   "Retained fluid" in the chest that causes coughing and difficulty breathing.

Because of its potent and piercing nature, it is said in medical circles that one should never use more than half a *qian* <note>A traditional unit of weight, roughly 1.5 to 2 grams</note> of this herb in powder form at one time, as its power to "open the orifices" is exceptionally strong.

Asarum <note>commonly known as "Fine-Leaf Wild Ginger" or *Xixin*</note> grows in the mountain valleys of Mount Hua <note>Huashan, one of the Five Sacred Mountains of China</note>, though today it can be found in many regions. Dendrobium grows in the mountain valleys of Lu’an <note>Modern-day Anhui Province</note> on rocks situated near water. Today, it is also found in the prefectures of Jingzhou, Guangzhou, Wenzhou, and Hezhou; however, the variety from the southern regions <note>Guangnan</note> is considered of the highest quality. 

Most often found deep within mountain valleys, the plant sprouts its shoots in the fifth month. The stems resemble the joints of bamboo, and small, delicate leaves emerge from between these joints. It blooms in the seventh month and bears fruit in the tenth month. Its roots are slender, long, and yellow in color. 

In the seventh and eighth months, the stems are harvested. They are then soaked in a solution of mulberry ash water <note>A traditional alkaline solution made from the ashes of mulberry wood, used to preserve the plant's color and "prepare" its medicinal properties</note>, which turns the stems the color of gold, after which they are dried in the shade for use. Some practitioners suggest washing the stems with wine and steaming them until fully processed <note>literally "nine parts complete"</note>, noting that the ash water treatment is not always necessary.

In the Jiangnan region <note>south of the Yangtze River</note>, two distinct varieties exist:
*   One variety resembles a cluster of barley with a single leaf growing at the tip; this is known as **Ginseng Dendrobium** <note>original: 参斛 (*Shenhu*)</note>.
*   The other variety is as thick as a sparrow’s thigh; this is known as **Sparrow-Thigh Dendrobium** <note>original: 雀髀斛 (*Quebihu*)</note>.

Only those specimens that grow specifically on rocks are considered superior; those found growing on oak trees are called **Wood Dendrobium** <note>original: 木斛 (*Muhu*)</note> and are unsuitable for medicinal use. 

This herb is also known by the names **Forest Orchid** <note>original: 林蘭 (*Linlan*)</note>, **Forbidden Growth** <note>original: 禁生 (*Jinsheng*)</note>, **Wild Orchid** <note>original: 杜蘭 (*Dulan*)</note>, and **Stone Marrow** <note>original: 石遂 (*Shisui*)</note>. 

**Medicinal Properties:**
The flavor is sweet, and it is non-toxic. It is used to treat weakness and softness in the waist and legs of men, to strengthen the *Yang* <note>referring here to male vitality and reproductive health</note>, and to dispel skin numbness caused by <term>Wind-Bi</term> <note>A Traditional Chinese Medicine term for "Wind-Numbness," a condition where external "Wind" and "Dampness" cause pain or loss of sensation in the muscles and skin</note>. It also treats long-standing coldness deep within the bones, addresses internal "void" or deficiency-related damage, tonifies the kidneys, and restores the "essence" <note>original: 精 (*Jing*); the fundamental life force or reproductive energy in Chinese medicine</note>.

**Baji** <note>Also known as *Bajitian*; botanical name *Morinda officinalis*.</note> grows in the mountain valleys of Ba Commandery <note>Modern-day eastern Sichuan and Chongqing</note> and Xiapi <note>Modern-day northern Jiangsu</note>. In genuine Baji, although the interior is purple when broken, there are subtle white specks like flour, and the texture is somewhat dull.

# Caltrop Seeds

Caltrop seeds grow in the river valleys and marshes of Xianyang, as well as along roadsides everywhere. The *Erya* <note>The oldest extant Chinese dictionary, dating back to at least the 3rd century BCE, which categorizes plants and animals.</note> states that the caltrop is the "large shepherd's purse" <note>original: *daqi* (大薺)</note>. Guo Pu <note>A prominent scholar and commentator from the Jin Dynasty (276–324 CE) known for his natural history observations.</note> says it resembles the shepherd's purse but with finer leaves; it is commonly called "old shepherd's purse" and has a pungent taste.

Chen Cangqi <note>A Tang Dynasty pharmacologist (c. 681–757 CE) famous for his work *Bencao Shiyi* (Supplement to the Materia Medica).</note> argues that the "large shepherd's purse" should actually be identified as the Japanese Hop <note>original: *lülu* (葎蘆), *Humulus scandens*</note> and not the caltrop. The caltrop is large and flat, whereas the Japanese Hop is fine and round; the two substances are quite distinct. Furthermore, the *Erya* already contains its own entry for the Japanese Hop, referring to it simply as *lü*. The commentary there states that its fruits and leaves both resemble mugwort and it is also known as "dog shepherd's purse." Generally, because both plants are categorized within the broad family of shepherd's purse-like herbs, many people fail to distinguish between them clearly, which leads to such confusion.

Harvest the plant during the fourth and fifth months and dry it in the sun. It is frequently used in both ancient and modern medicinal formulas for the eyes. Its other names include *Ci* <note>meaning "thorns" or "to puncture," referring to its spiked fruit</note>, "Large Thorn," "Horse Tether," and "Large Shepherd's Purse." It has a pungent taste, is slightly warm in nature, and is non-toxic. It is primarily used to **brighten the eyes** <term>Mingmu: a traditional therapeutic goal of improving visual acuity and clearing redness or irritation</term>, **remove "numbness"** <term>Bi: a category of ailments characterized by pain, heaviness, or restricted movement in the joints and muscles, often caused by environmental "wind" or "dampness"</term>, and treat pain in the chest, abdomen, and lower back.

# Yarrow Seeds

The seeds of the Yarrow plant grow in the valleys of Shaoshi Mountain <note>Shaoshi Mountain is the western peak of the sacred Mount Song in Henan Province.</note>. Today, they are found near the White Tortoise Shrine in Shangcai County, Cai Prefecture. The plant grows in clusters resembling mugwort, reaching heights of five or six feet. A single root typically produces ten to twenty stalks; the most prolific plants can produce between thirty and fifty stalks. These stalks grow remarkably straight and slender from the very beginning, which is what distinguishes them from other varieties of common mugwort.

After the autumn season, flowers emerge from the tips of the branches. They are reddish-purple in color and shaped like chrysanthemums. In the eighth and ninth months of the lunar calendar, the seeds are harvested and dried in the sun to be used as medicine. The stalks themselves are used for <term>divination<note>original: 筮 (shì); refers specifically to the practice of casting yarrow stalks to consult the *I Ching* or Book of Changes.</note></term> to question the spirits and discern good or bad fortune.

Master Chu <note>Likely referring to Chu Shaosun, a Han dynasty scholar who provided supplements to the *Records of the Grand Historian* regarding divination.</note> said: 

"Wherever a Yarrow plant reaches a full hundred stalks, a divine tortoise will surely be found guarding it from below, and azure clouds will frequently hover above it." 

It is also said in tradition: 

"When the world is at peace and the 'Kingly Way' <note>The Confucian ideal of benevolent governance.</note> prevails, the Yarrow stalks will grow to ten feet in length and the clusters will reach a full hundred stalks."

The flavor of the seeds is bitter and sour; its nature is balanced and non-toxic. It is primarily used to <term>replenish vital energy<note>original: 益氣 (yìqì); a traditional Chinese medicine term for boosting the body's fundamental life force.</note></term> and to improve the eyesight.

# Resurrection Fern

<term>Resurrection Fern</term> <note>original: 卷柏 (juǎnbǎi), literally "Rolled Cypress." This is likely *Selaginella tamariscina*, a lycophyte known for its ability to survive desiccation by curling into a ball.</note> grows in the valleys of <term>Mount Chang</term> <note>A historical region located in modern-day Hebei province.</note>. Today, it is also found in various prefectures throughout the regions of Guanzhong, Shaanxi, Yi, and Yan.

The perennial root is purple in color with many fibrous rootlets. In the spring, it produces sprouts that resemble cypress leaves but are finer and more fragmented. They curl up like a **chicken's foot** and are greenish-yellow in color. The plant grows to a height of three to five inches and bears no flowers or seeds. It mostly grows atop rocks.

It is harvested in the fifth and seventh months and dried in the shade. When preparing it for use, one must remove the lower parts that were close to the rock and covered in sand or soil.

It is also known by the following names:
*   **Ten Thousand Years** <note>original: 萬歲 (wànsuì)</note>
*   **Leopard’s Foot** <note>original: 豹足 (bàozú)</note>
*   **Seeking Thigh** <note>original: 求股 (qiúgǔ)</note>
*   **Intertwined Seasons** <note>original: 交時 (jiāoshí)</note>

**Properties:** Its flavor is pungent and sweet. Its nature is balanced between warm and level, or slightly cold, and it is non-toxic.

**Functions:** It calms the heart, removes <term>facial blemishes</term> <note>original: 面疢 (miànchèn), referring to spots, acne, or skin discolorations.</note>, and treats <term>head wind</term> <note>original: 頭風 (tóufēng), a traditional term for chronic, stubborn headaches or migraines believed to be caused by "wind" pathogens.</note>. It warms the <term>water organ</term> <note>original: 水臟 (shuǐzàng), a reference to the Kidneys in traditional Chinese anatomy.</note>. 

**Usage:** When used raw, it **breaks up blood stasis**; when toasted, it **stops bleeding**.

### Sichuan Lovage <note>original: 芎藭 (*Xiōngqióng*); *Ligusticum striatum*</note>

**Sichuan Lovage** grows in the valleys of Mount Wugong, in the Li Valley, and on the West Ridge. **Miwu** <note>original: 靡蕪</note> is the name for the seedling of the Sichuan Lovage plant. It grows in the rivers and marshes of Wei Prefecture as well as in Yuanju. Today, it is found abundantly in the mountains of the Guanzhong and Shaanxi regions, Shu Prefecture <note>modern Sichuan</note>, and the Jiangdong region. However, the variety sourced from the rivers of Shu is considered the superior grade.

Its seedlings emerge between the fourth and fifth months <note>late spring and early summer</note>. The leaves resemble those of **celery**, **coriander**, or **shechuang** <note>original: 蛇牀 (*shéchuáng*); *Cnidium monnieri*</note>. As the plant matures, it grows into clusters with thin stems. The leaves are intensely fragrant; if planted in courtyard gardens, their sweet scent fills the paths. People in the Jiangdong and Shu regions harvest the leaves to make a fragrant beverage, which is said to be able to stop diarrhea.

In the seventh or eighth months, the plant produces white flowers. The roots are hard, slender, and yellowish-black in color. They are harvested in the third or fourth months and dried in the sun. Roots that are heavy, solid, and shaped like a **sparrow’s brain** are called **Sparrow Brain Lovage** <note>original: 雀腦芎 (*Què nǎo xiōng*)</note>; these are considered the most potent in medicinal strength.

The plant is also known by the names **Huqiong** <note>original: 胡窮</note> and **Fragrant Fruit** <note>original: 香果 (*Xiāngguǒ*)</note>. Its flavor is acrid and its nature is warm; it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat "wind-stroke" <note>a traditional medical category for sudden onset conditions like strokes or seizures</note> that enters the brain, headaches, "cold-numbness" <note>original: 寒痺 (*hánbì*); joint or muscle pain caused by cold and dampness</note>, muscle spasms, and both acute and chronic ailments. It is also used to treat wounds caused by metal blades, and for women suffering from blocked blood flow <note>amenorrhea</note> or infertility.

### Goldthread <note>original: 黃連 (*Huánglián*); *Coptis chinensis*</note>

**Goldthread** <note>literally "Yellow Linkage," referring to the interconnected nature of its rhizomes</note> originates in the river valleys of Mount Wu <note>a mountain range on the border of modern Chongqing and Hubei</note> and the Shu Commandery <note>modern Sichuan</note>. Today, it is also produced in the prefectures of Cheng, Mei, Xuan, Tong, Kui, Hang, and Yi. Among these, the varieties harvested from Cheng and Mei Prefectures are considered the highest grade, while those from Xuan Prefecture <note>in modern Anhui</note> are regarded as second-best.

The plant’s name is derived from the appearance of its roots, which are shaped like **linked beads** <note>original: 連珠 (*liánzhū*)</note>. Its seedlings grow to approximately one foot in height, featuring leaves that are remarkably stiff and leathery. 

In the first month of the lunar calendar <note>early spring</note>, the plant produces flowers of a pale yellow hue. By the third month, it bears fruit within small pods, which contain black seeds as tiny as grains of millet. The medicinal roots are traditionally harvested during the second and eighth months of the year.
