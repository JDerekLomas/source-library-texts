# PREFACE
## TO THE READERS.

THE Images made to signify a thing different from that which is seen with the eye have no other more certain nor more universal rule than the imitation of the records found in Books, on Medals, and carved in Marbles by the industry of the Latins and Greeks, or of those more ancient peoples who were the inventors of this artifice <note>The author refers to the "artifice" of iconography—the symbolic language of art.</note>. Therefore, it commonly appears that whoever labors outside of this imitation errs, either through ignorance or through too much presumption; these two stains are greatly abhorred by those who attend to the acquisition of some praise through their own labors. To flee, then, the suspicion of this fault, I have judged it a good thing—having wished to make of all these Images a greater bundle than that which could be gathered from the observations of the most ancient things, and therefore needing to invent many, and take many from the moderns, explaining each one with verisimilitude—to treat some matters regarding the method of forming and explaining <term>symbolic concepts</term> <note>original: "concetti simbolici"; the underlying ideas or allegories represented by visual figures.</note> at the beginning of this work, which perhaps with too much diligence is urged by many friends, and is expected by those whom I am under principal obligation to satisfy. Leaving aside, then, that image which the Orator uses, and which Aristotle treats in the third book of his *Rhetoric*, I will speak only of that which belongs to Painters, or to those who by means of colors or other visible things can represent something different from the thing itself, and which has conformity with the other; because, just as this [visual image] often persuades by means of the eye, so the other [spoken image] moves the will by means of words: and because this looks to the metaphors of things that exist outside of man, and those which are joined with him—

One part [of representation] is that which affirms or denies something about someone; the other is that which does not. With the former, those who compose *imprese* <note>original: "impreſe"; these were personal devices or heraldic-like emblems combining a motto and a picture to express a specific intent or principle</note> form their craft, in which a single concept is hinted at with few objects and few words; so too do those who make Emblems, where a greater concept is manifested with a larger quantity of words and objects. With the latter [the non-affirming part], the art of other Images is formed, which pertain to our discourse due to the conformity they have with definitions. These images solely embrace the virtues and the vices, or all those things that have an affinity with them, without affirming or denying anything. Because they are either pure privations or pure habits <note>In Aristotelian philosophy, a "habit" (habitus) is a settled disposition or quality, while "privation" is the absence of a quality that should naturally be present</note>, they are appropriately expressed through the human figure.

For just as man is the measure of all things, according to the common opinion of Philosophers and of Aristotle in particular—almost as the definition is the measure of the thing defined—so likewise the accidental form <note>The outward appearance or non-essential characteristics of a thing</note> that appears externally on him can be an accidental measure of definable qualities, whatever they may be, whether of our soul alone or of the entire composite [of body and soul]. Therefore, we see that in our context, something cannot be called an "Image" if it does not have the form of a human; and it is a poorly defined image when the principal body does not perform, in some way, the office that the "genus" <note>The broad category in a formal definition</note> performs in a definition.

Among the number of other things to be observed are all the essential parts of the thing itself; and of these, it will be necessary to examine the dispositions and qualities minutely.

**Disposition** in the head will be the posture—high or low, cheerful or melancholy—and various other passions that are revealed, as if in a theater, in the appearance of the human face. ...a harmony so concordant that, in explaining it, it gives satisfaction to recognize the conformity of things and the good judgment of the one who knew how to arrange them together in such a way that a single, perfect, and delightful thing results.

Such are almost universally all those works of the Ancients, and also those of the Moderns who do not govern themselves by chance. And because physiognomy <note>The study of facial features as an indication of character</note> and colors are considered by the Ancients, everyone may guide themselves in this according to the authority of Aristotle. One must believe, according to the opinion of the Learned, that he alone suffices in this—as in all else—to represent what many others say on the subject. We will often omit explaining these points individually, as it is enough to state once or twice among so many things gathered together what would otherwise need to be manifested in each part if they were distinct. This is so that from the many, one may choose the few that are most relevant, or so that all together they create a composition more similar to the "description" used by Orators and Poets than to the strict "definition" of the Dialecticians <note>Logicians or philosophers who use formal reasoning</note>. This avoids creating obscurity and boredom in the process of organizing, explaining, and committing many different images to memory.

In those matters, therefore, where the "ultimate difference" <note>The specific defining characteristic that distinguishes one thing from all others.</note> can be demonstrated, if any such thing is found, this alone is sufficient to make the image praiseworthy and of the highest perfection. When these are placed together, they show the same thing that the single specific trait would have contained.

Furthermore, when we distinctly know the qualities, causes, properties, and accidents of a definable thing through this method, we must then seek a similitude <note>A symbolic likeness or comparison.</note> in order to create the image, as we have mentioned regarding material things. For example, if for the similitude of <term>Fortitude</term> <note>Strength of mind that enables a person to encounter danger or bear pain or adversity with courage.</note> one paints a Column, it is because in buildings the column supports all the stones and the entire structure above it without moving or wavering; thus we say that such is fortitude in man, for sustaining the weight of all the troubles and difficulties that fall upon him. Likewise, for the similitude of <term>Rhetoric</term> <note>The art of persuasive speaking or writing.</note> one uses the Sword and the Shield; because just as the Soldier defends his own life and attacks others with these instruments, so the Rhetorician and the Orator, with their arguments or <term>enthymemes</term> <note>original: "entimemi"; a rhetorical syllogism or argument where one premise is implied rather than stated.</note>, maintain favorable positions and strike back against opposing ones.

For instance, if to denote Magnanimity <note>Greatness of soul and nobility of spirit.</note> we were to take the Lion, in whom this quality is greatly revealed. *and of the declaration; and these two types of metaphor <note>original: "similitudine"</note> are the nerve and the strength of a well-formed image; without which, just as the image lacks much difficulty, it remains insipid and weak.*

*This is not well-noted by some modern artists, who represent contingent effects to show essential qualities—as they do when painting Despair as a man hanging himself by the throat, or Friendship as two people embracing, or similar things of little ingenuity and little praise. It is indeed true, as I have said, that those accidents which necessarily follow the thing signified in the Image will be praiseworthy to place in certain distinct and bare locations; particularly those belonging to physiognomy <term>physiognomy: the historical practice of judging a person's character or personality from their outer appearance, especially the face</term> and the habit of the body. For example, if one must paint Melancholy, Thought, Penitence, and other similar things, it will be well done to make the face dry and gaunt, the hair disheveled, the beard tangled, and the flesh not very youthful. But Pleasure, Delight, Joy, and every other thing similar to these should be made beautiful, lascivious, fresh, ruddy, and laughing. And although such knowledge does not have much place in the categorization of similar things, it is nonetheless used quite often.*

*However, this rule of accidents and effects already mentioned will not always follow; as in the painting of Beauty, which is a thing outside the comprehension of the predicables <term>predicables: a term from Aristotelian logic referring to the ways something can be described or categorized</term>. She is depicted as an old woman because in the years of old age, strength ceases and the power to act fails, as David demonstrates in Psalm 70 <note>Modern Psalm 71</note>, where he says: *Do not cast me off in the time of old age; when my strength fails, do not forsake me.* <note>original Latin: "Ne proijcias me in tempore senectutis, cum defecerit virtus mea ne derelinquas me."</note>

## ILL-GOTTEN GAIN.

**A MAN** dressed in the color of tree leaves when they are about to fall; the said figure shall be in the act of walking, and a hem of his garment shall be caught on a thorn, pulling a great tear, at which he turns to show the displeasure he feels; and in his right hand he shall hold a kite <note>A bird of prey known for scavenging</note> that is vomiting.

## FLATTERY.

A cheerful woman with a furrowed brow, she shall be dressed in iridescent cloth <note>term: cangiante; a shot silk or fabric that changes color depending on the light, symbolizing inconstancy.</note>, and the clothing shall be entirely painted with Chameleons; with her right hand she will hold a bellows to light a fire, and with her left, a rope.

Flattery, according to Cicero in the second book of the *Tusculan Disputations*, is a sin committed by a discourse of praise given to someone with the soul and intention of pleasing; or rather, it is a false persuasion and lying consent used by a feigned friend in conversation with someone, to make them believe things about themselves and their own affairs that are not true, and it is done for pleasure or for greed.

She is dressed in iridescent cloth because the flatterer is very quick at every opportunity to change face and words, and to say "yes" and "no" according to the taste of each person, as Terence demonstrates in *The Eunuch*:
> Whatever they say, I praise; if they deny it again, I praise that too. If someone says no, I say no; if he says yes, I say yes. <note>original: "Quicquid dicunt laudo: id rursum si negant laudo, Id quoque negat quis, nego: ait, aio."</note>

The Chameleons are placed there for the excessive following of the appetites and opinions of others: because this animal, according to what Aristotle says, transforms itself according to the changes of the times; likewise the flatterer considers himself perfect in his profession when he best conforms himself to applaud the habits of others for his own interest, even if they are blameworthy. It is also said that because the Chameleon is very timid, having very little blood in itself (and that only around the heart), it fears and transforms itself at every weak encounter. From this, one can see that flattery is a sign of little spirit and a low soul in those who practice it and in those who listen to it willingly; Aristotle says in the fourth book of the *Ethics* that *All flatterers are servile and abject men.* <note>original: "Omnes adulatores sunt serviles, & abiecti homines."</note>

The bellows, which is a most fit instrument for lighting a fire and for extinguishing lit lamps using only wind, lets us know that flatterers, with the wind of vain words, either ignite the fire of passions in those who listen to them willingly, or they extinguish the light of truth that others held—

The beautiful face is an indication of the first appearance of flattering words; and the other ugly face shows the hidden defects sent behind the shoulders.

Bees, according to Eucherius <note>Saint Eucherius of Lyon, a 5th-century bishop known for his allegorical interpretations</note>, are the true likeness of the flatterer, because they carry honey in their mouths and keep a piercing sting hidden, with which they often strike a man who does not notice it.

The dog with fawning caresses anyone who gives him bread, without any distinction of merit or demerit, and sometimes even bites those who do not deserve it—even the very person who gave him bread, should they happen to stop. Therefore, he greatly resembles the flatterer, and for this purpose Marc'Antonio Cataldi Romano <note>A Roman poet and contemporary of Ripa</note> took him in that sonnet:

### Joy.

A young girl with a garland of flowers on her head, because children are always joyful: and because in ancient public festivals everyone crowned themselves, both them and the doors of their houses, temples, and animals, as Tertullian mentions in the book *Concerning the Military Crown* <note>original: "de corona Militis"</note>; and with her right hand she holds a branch of palm and olive, in memory of Palm Sunday, and the joy with which Christ Our Lord was received with many branches of palms and olives.

## B I T T E R N E S S .

FOR Bitterness, some paint a woman dressed in black, holding with both hands a honeycomb, from which a wormwood plant is seen sprouting; perhaps because when we are in the greatest happiness of life, we then find ourselves in the greatest danger of the disasters of Fortune; or because all qualities are known through the knowledge of their opposite, one can then have perfect knowledge of sweetness when one has tasted extreme bitterness; therefore Ariosto said:

## A M B I T I O N .

## Ambition.

A young woman, dressed in green, with a tucked-up garment and bare feet; she shall have wings on her shoulders, and with both hands she shows herself confusedly putting many kinds of Crowns on her head, and she shall have her eyes bandaged.

Ambition, according to Saint Thomas <note>Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologica</note> 2. 2. q. 131. art. 2. is a disordered appetite for making oneself great, and for attaining Ranks, States, Lordships, Magistracies, and Offices, by whatever just or unjust occasion, virtuous or vicious means; whence it happens that one is said to be ambitious, as Aristotle says in the fourth book of the Ethics, who seeks honors more than is necessary, and where they are not needed.

## THE AMPLITUDE OF GLORY.

For this effect, the figure of Alexander the Great is depicted with a thunderbolt in his hand and a crown upon his head.
The ancient Egyptians understood the thunderbolt to represent the amplitude of glory and fame spread throughout the entire world; this is because nothing else produces a louder sound than the thunders of the air from which the lightning bolt emerges. For this reason, historians write that Apelles <note>A renowned painter of ancient Greece, active in the 4th century BCE</note>, the most excellent painter, wishing to paint the effigy of Alexander the Great, placed the thunderbolt in his hand so that it might signify the brilliance of his name, carried by the deeds he performed in distant lands and celebrated for eternal memory. It is also said that a thunderbolt appeared in a dream to Olympias, the mother of Alexander, which gave her an indication of the future amplitude and fame of her son.

### Friendship.

The three Graces <note>The Charites of Greek mythology, representing charm, beauty, and creativity</note> are shown naked, one of whom will be seen from the back, and the other two from the front, joining their arms together. One of them will have a rose in her hand, the other a die <note>a gaming cube</note>, and the third a branch of myrtle. From the images of these three Graces, good and perfect friendship is undoubtedly governed, according to what the ancients thought; for friendship has no other end than to help and benefit others, and not to be outdone in benevolence. And just as there are three Graces of the ancients, so there are three degrees of benefits held in friendship.

The first is to give things. The second is to receive from others. The third is to return the favor. And of the three Graces, one clasps the hand or the arm of the other, because the order of doing a benefit for another is that it must pass from hand to hand, and return to the benefit of the one who first performed it; in this manner, the knot of friendship keeps men tightly united among themselves.

These three Graces are represented naked because men should be of a free spirit toward one another, and released from all deceit. Only one turns her back, and two turn their faces, to show that a benefit should always be returned twofold to a friend.

They are represented with cheerful countenances, because such should be the appearance of one who does a benefit for another, and likewise those who receive it. They have a virginal appearance because friendship must not be contaminated by the vileness of any private interest.

The rose signifies the pleasantness which must always exist between friends, there being a continuous union of will between them.

The die signifies the alternating going and returning of benefits, just as dice do when one plays with them.

The myrtle, which is evergreen, is a sign that friendship must preserve itself the same, and never be diminished by any accident.

### Love of Fame.

A naked boy crowned with laurel with its branches and berries; he shall have in his right hand the act of offering the Civic crown, and in the left the Obsidional crown <note>Also known as the Grass Crown, the highest Roman military honor</note>, and upon a pedestal near said figure, there shall be distinctly placed those crowns that the Romans used as a sign of valor: namely, the Mural, the Camp, and the Naval crowns.
Aulus Gellius <note>A 2nd-century Roman author known for 'Attic Nights'</note> recounts that the golden triumphal crown, which was given in honor of a triumph to a Captain or an Emperor, was anciently made of laurel, and the obsidional was of grass, and was given only to those who in some extreme danger had saved the entire army, or had lifted a siege from around the army. The Civic crown was of oak, and the ancients crowned almost all statues of Jove <note>Jupiter</note> with oak, as if this were a sign of life; and the Romans used to give the garland of oak to whoever had defended a Roman Citizen from death in war, wishing to give the insignia of life to him who was the cause of another’s living. They also used to make this garland of holm-oak because of the similarity of said trees. The Mural crown was that which was given to the Captain, or rather the soldier, who had been the first to mount the walls of the enemy. The Camp crown <note>Latin: *Corona Castrensis*</note> was given to whoever was first of all others to mount inside the bastions and lodgings of the enemies. The Naval crown was given to him who was the first to board the enemy fleet. And these three were made of gold, and the Mural was with certain battlements made in the likeness of the walls where he had ascended. The Camp crown was made at the top in the manner of a bastion. The Naval crown had for ornaments the signs of the prows <note>Latin: *rostra*</note> of ships; and this is as much as was necessary to write on this subject for the convenience of Painters.

Summer, because of the great heat and the fully ripened grain, is represented with a red chest and flanks, and with ears of corn.

## THE RATIONAL AND BLESSED SOUL.

A most graceful MAIDEN; she shall have her face covered with a very fine and transparent veil, her clothing clear and shining, a pair of wings upon her shoulders, and a star at the crown of her head.

Although the soul, as the theologians say, is an incorporeal and immortal substance, it is nonetheless represented in the best way that man—bound to his bodily senses—can comprehend it through imagination; no differently than one is accustomed to represent God and the Angels, even though they are also incorporeal substances.

She is depicted as a most graceful maiden because she was made by the Creator (who is the source of all beauty and perfection) in His likeness.

Her face is veiled to denote that she is—as Saint Augustine says in his book *On the Definition of the Soul*—a substance invisible to human eyes, and the substantial form of the body, in which she is not evident except through certain external actions by which she is understood.

The clear and shining garment is to denote the purity and perfection of her essence.

The star is placed above her head because the Egyptians signified the immortality of the soul with a star, as Pierio Valeriano relates in the 44th book of his *Hieroglyphics*.

## A R R O G A N C E.

**A** WOMAN dressed in the color of verdigris <note>a bluish-green pigment formed on copper</note>; she shall have the ears of a donkey, hold a peacock under her left arm, and with her right hand held high, she shall point her index finger.

Arrogance is the vice of those who, although they know themselves to be of little value, nevertheless, in order to appear great to others, take on the burden of difficult and important enterprises. St. Thomas says this in the Second Part of the Second Part, Question 112, Article 1: *The arrogant man is he who attributes to himself that which he does not possess.* <note>original Latin: "Arrogans est, qui sibi attribuit, quod non habet."</note> Therefore, she is rightly depicted with donkey ears, as this vice is born of ignorance and stupidity, which prevents one from foreseeing the outcome of undertakings entered into with poor judgment.

The peacock signifies that arrogance is a species of pride, and the raised finger represents the obstinacy of maintaining one's own opinion, however false and far from common sense it may be, by esteeming oneself greatly and despising others. In this same way, the ancients also depicted Pertinacity <note>stubbornness</note>, which is almost the same thing as ignorance.

## A R I T H M E T I C.

**A** WOMAN of beautiful appearance; in her right hand she holds an iron hook, in her left a whitened tablet, and on the hem of her garment shall be written EVEN & ODD. <note>original Latin: "PAR & IMPAR"</note>

Her beauty is an indication of the perfection of numbers, of which some Philosophers believed all things were composed; and God, from whom nothing can proceed that is not perfect, made everything in number, weight, and <unclear>in</unclear> measure <note>A reference to the Wisdom of Solomon 11:20</note>, and this is the true subject of Arithmetic.

The iron hook and the whitened tablet demonstrate that with those instruments, calculations are made regarding different kinds of existence and things composed by the number, weight, and measure of the Elements.

The motto *Even* and *Odd* declares what it is that gives all the diversity of occurrences and all demonstrations to this art.

### Arithmetic.

**A** woman who holds a number tablet in both hands, with another one on the ground near her feet.

## A R T.

**A** MATRON with a hand-crank and a lever in her right hand, and in her left, a flame of fire.

All the arts that use instruments and machines (which are many) reduce the force of their proofs to the demonstration of the circle; from it, they receive their logic and their stability. Therefore, Art is depicted with the hand-crank and the lever, which derive their force from the balance scale, and the scale derives its force from the circle, as Aristotle writes in the book of *Mechanics*.

The flame of fire is placed as the principal instrument of artificial things: because by hardening or softening materials, it makes them fit to be used by man in many industrious exercises.

### Art.

**A** woman dressed in green; in her right hand she holds a paintbrush and a chisel, and with her left, a stake driven into the ground, to which a young and tender plant is tied.

The paintbrush and the chisel signify the imitation of nature, which is seen expressed particularly in painting and sculpting; this is shown through the brush and the chisel. ### Avarice.

A poorly dressed woman, disheveled and barefoot; in her right hand she shall hold a toad, and with her left a iron-bound purse.

She is represented as a Matron <note>A mature, dignified woman</note>, because mature age properly possesses authority within itself; wherefore Cicero, in his book *On Old Age*, says: *The crown of old age is authority* <note>original: "Apex autem senectutis est auctoritas"</note>, and shortly after adds: *An honored old age possesses such great authority, that it is worth more than all the pleasures of youth* <note>original: "Habet senectus honorata præsertim tantam auctoritatem, vt ea pluris sit, quam omnes voluptates"</note>. From this it follows that: *the young are suited for obeying, and the old for commanding*, as Plutarch says in his *Political Precepts* <note>original: "ad parendum iuuenes, ad imperandum senes sunt accomodati"</note>.

# ICONOLOGY

->AUTHORITY<- <note>The header and context imply this section describes the personification of Authority.</note>

She is painted sitting, because sitting is proper to Princes and Magistrates; by this act, authority is shown, along with quiet and tranquility of soul. This is because matters requiring gravity should not be dealt with except through mature session <note>meaning a formal, seated deliberation</note>. Thus it happens with Judges, who, having the power and authority to decide, absolve, and condemn, cannot legitimately execute a sentence unless they are seated, as the law states in *Book 2, Section "in bonorum" of the Digest, "What order should be observed in the possession of goods."* <note>original: "legge 2. § in bonorum ff. quis ordo in bon. poss. seru."</note>

She is dressed in pompous and shining raiment, because such is the appearance of one who has power over others in the sight of men; furthermore, precious garments and stones in themselves demonstrate authority and honor in the wearer.

The keys denote spiritual authority and power, as Christ Our Lord and Redeemer most excellently demonstrated when, by means of them, He gave that supreme authority to Saint Peter, saying: *And I will give unto thee the keys of the kingdom of heaven: and whatsoever thou shalt bind on earth shall be bound in heaven: and whatsoever thou shalt loose on earth shall be loosed in heaven. Matthew, chapter 16.* <note>original: "Et tibi dabo claues regni Cælorum..."</note>

She holds these keys in her right hand, because spiritual power is the principal one, and more noble than all others—just as the soul is more noble than the body. There is no one who is not subject to that of the Supreme Pontiff, the Vicar of Christ on earth, who *is said to have the fullness of power* <note>original: "dicitur habere plenitudinem potestatis"</note>, according to the Canon in the chapter *qui fecit 2. q. 6.*

She holds her right hand raised with the keys lifted toward Heaven, to demonstrate that *all power is from God* <note>original: "Omnis potestas à Deo est"</note>, according to the Apostle Saint Paul in Romans, chapter 13. Therefore, he admonishes them that *every soul should be subject unto the higher powers.* <note>original: "Omnis anima potestatibus sublimioribus subdita sit."</note>

The scepter in the left hand shows temporal authority and power, as this is a thing known to everyone in itself. The books and the arms <note>weapons/armor</note> that are at her sides (to make this image more universal) represent two meanings: one demonstrates the authority of the scriptures and of the doctors <note>learned theologians</note>, and the other the authority of arms, which are placed on the left according to the saying of Cicero: *Let arms yield to the toga.* <note>original: "Cedant arma togæ" — meaning military power should yield to civil authority.</note>

## Which is Meekness.

->*Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth.*<-
<note>Matthew 5:5</note>

To be meek and humane means to consent to others in goodness and in honest service.

A young girl, holding in her arms a small and gentle lamb in an act of caressing it, with the motto taken from the Psalm: *The meek shall inherit the earth.* <note>Psalm 37:11</note>

For the same reason mentioned above, this figure shall also be made as a young girl.

The lamb signifies purity, simplicity, and meekness, not only in the profane Egyptian letters <note>hieroglyphics or ancient lore</note> but also in the sacred writings of the Christian Religion. Even the pagan augurs used the lamb in their sacrifices solely for the pleasantness of its pure and gentle spirit. Furthermore, Saint John the Baptist, a singular witness to Heavenly secrets, in order to manifest the meekness of Christ our Lord under a simple veil, said that He was a lamb who, with His own sacrificed blood, appeased the wrath of God for us.

## THE THIRD BEATITUDE
### And Mourning.

> Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted. <note>original: "Beati qui lugent, quoniam ipsi consolabuntur." (Matthew 5:4)</note> This implies weeping for one's own sins and those of one's neighbor, along with our miseries and theirs.

A young girl kneeling, with hands joined, weeping profusely; the motto says: *Present sorrow generates everlasting joy* <note>original: "Præsens loquutus, lætitiam generat sempiternam."</note>, and it is taken from St. Augustine.

Mourning, as it is understood here, is the displeasure that one may take through charity for each person’s faults and harms, as well as one's own. And the state of a young girl being perhaps the least guilty possible, there is no doubt she will be easily recognized as a sign of what would be necessary to say to anyone who wished to express the concept of this Beatitude through words; in which, through the motto, it is revealed that the reward for this sort of mourning will be a perpetual joy in the next life.

The kneeling posture and joined hands show that this weeping and this sorrow must be moved by a pious and religious cause, so that it may be called an act of true virtue—unlike the weeping of Democritus <note>A Greek philosopher often contrasted with Heraclitus; here the author suggests his "sorrow" for the world was rooted in pride rather than piety.</note>, which was born of ambition and the desire to appear the wisest and most deserving of all others.

> Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God. <note>original Latin: "Beati mundo corde, quoniam ipsi Deum videbunt." Matthew 5:8.</note>

->It is Mercy.<-

> Blessed are the Merciful. <note>original Latin: "Beati Misericordes." Matthew 5:7.</note> That is, those who have compassion for the miseries of their neighbors and, when able, relieve them.

A woman who, breaking a loaf of bread, offers a portion of it to each of two or three small children standing around her, with the motto of Saint Jerome:

Mercy is the virtue through which we feel sorrow for the miseries of others and assist their necessities according to our ability.

God is called merciful because he overlooks the sins of men for the sake of penance. A man is called merciful who easily inclines toward grieving for the miseries of others; it is almost the same thing as piety. It is not practiced except toward people who are needy, afflicted, and desperate due to some great misfortune, or for errors committed through their own fault, for which they feel sorrow and repentance. Such was Our Lord with the thief who was unfaithful, and He gave him Heaven; with the Samaritan woman who was immersed in lewdness, and He made her chaste; with the woman who was an adulteress, and He restored her honor; with Mary Magdalene who was a sinner, and He made her a Saint; with Saint Peter, to whom He forgave the sin of having denied Him, and furthermore gave him the keys of Heaven, justifying him. This is in addition to many examples read in the history of the Holy Gospel, where it seems Our Lord is depicted as nothing other than the true fountain of mercy. In imitation of Him, we must empathize with the ills of others and willingly bear our own tribulations, whether they come through our own fault or by His will.

## THE SEVENTH BEATITUDE.
->Being peaceful.<-

> Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called the children of God. <note>original: "Beati pacifici, quoniam filij Dei vocabuntur." (Matthew 5:9)</note>

A woman who holds under her feet several swords, helmets, shields, and other broken weapons; in one hand she holds an olive branch with the motto: *He broke the bow, the shield, the sword, and the battle.* <note>original: "Confregit arcum, scutum, gladium, & bellum." (Psalm 76:3)</note>

A very great degree of Blessedness belongs to those who not only delight in living in peace and quiet (which seems to be a universal desire of all men, and the reason why even war, though blameworthy in itself, is sometimes commended), but who, through tribulations, cause peace to be restored when it is lost—both for themselves and for others. This applies not only to the body against external enemies, but more importantly to the soul against the powers of hell.

And peace is depicted with weapons under her feet to show that it must be acquired and maintained through one's own virtue, in order to be all the more meritorious and commendable.

The olive is given as a sign of peace by the united testimony of the ancients and moderns. Thus we read that Aeneas <note>The hero of Virgil's Aeneid</note>, being about to disembark in the lands of Evander in Italy, in order to reassure the King's son who came toward him suspiciously, stepped forward with an olive branch in his hand, and the youth was immediately calmed. Beyond this, there are infinite other examples, for all of which this one suffices. The reward of these people is to be numbered among the children of God, chosen for eternal Blessedness.

> Blessed are they which are persecuted for righteousness' sake: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. <note>original: "Beati qui persecutionem patiuntur propter iustitiam, quoniam ipsorum est Regnum Cælorum." (Matthew 5:10)</note>

### BENIGNITY.

A WOMAN dressed in sky-blue starred with gold; with both hands she presses her breasts, from which a bounty of milk flows out, which various animals drink; on the left side there shall be an altar with a lit fire.

Benignity is not very different from affability, clemency, and humanity; it is principally exercised toward subjects, and is compassion guided by reason, interpreting the law without rigor. It is essentially what the Greeks call <term>epieikeia</term> <note>original Greek: ἐπιείκεια</note>, that is, a pleasant or equitable interpretation of the law.

She is dressed in starred blue in the likeness of the Heavens; just as the Heavens are said to be benign toward us, so too is a man called benign who, with a serene face, courteously grants favors to others without self-interest or worldly recognition, and who carries out a merciful justice.

She presses milk from her breasts, from which many animals drink, because it is the effect of benignity and charity together to lovingly scatter that which one has from nature, alluding to the saying of Saint Paul, who says: <term>Charity is kind</term> <note>original Latin: "Charitas benigna est" - 1 Corinthians 13:4</note>. This act further shows that when benignity is exercised toward subjects, as has been said, it must be preferred over the rigor of justice, according to Papinian the <term>Legal Consultant</term> <note>original Latin: "Iure Consulto"</note>; for benignity is the companion of justice itself, as Cicero well says in *On Ends* <note>De Finibus</note>, and therefore it must be praised and embraced by both, with Plutarch affirming in chapter 26: <term>He who does not praise benignity surely has a heart of adamant, or one consumed by iron</term> <note>original Latin: "Qui non laudat benignitatem..."</note>.

### L I E S.

**A** WOMAN wrapped and covered in her garments as much as possible; the clothing shall be white on one side and black on the other. She shall wear a magpie <note>original: "gaza," referring to the bird known for mimicry and theft</note> upon her head and hold a cuttlefish in her hand.

### C A L A M I T Y.

A sorrowful woman, dressed in black and in poor condition <note>The Italian "mal’in arneſe" implies being shabbily dressed or in a state of physical decline</note>, appearing weak and supporting herself upon a reed. ## CAPRICE

A youth dressed in various colors; on his head he shall wear a small hat similar to his clothing, upon which there shall be diverse feathers. In his right hand he shall hold a pair of bellows, and in his left, a spur.

Those are called "Capricious" who, with ideas different from the ordinary ones of other men, allow their own actions to be guided by them, but with a mobility from one thing to another within the same genre. By way of analogy, those ideas manifested in painting, music, or other modes that are far from the ordinary way are called "caprices." Inconstancy is demonstrated by his youthful age, and variety by the diversity of colors.

The hat with various feathers shows that these diversities of extraordinary actions are placed principally in the imagination.

The spur and the bellows show the capricious person ready to either puff up <note>The bellows symbolize "blowing" or inflating with praise.</note> the virtues of others or to prick their vices.

### CHARIOT OF VENUS.

**VENUS** is depicted as young, naked, and beautiful, with a garland of roses and of myrtle, and in one hand she holds a sea shell.
Venus was represented naked because of the appetite for lewd embraces, or rather, because those who always pursue lewd pleasures often remain stripped and deprived of every good; for riches are devoured by lewd women, the body is weakened, and the soul is stained with such ugliness that nothing beautiful remains.
The myrtle and the roses are consecrated to this Goddess because of the conformity that their scents have with Venus, and for the incitement and vigor that myrtle provides to lust; thus the comic poet Futurio <note>likely a reference to Pherecrates or a similar classical source cited via Boccaccio</note>, while portraying the prostitute Digone, says:

> *Bring me myrtle, so that I may*
> *With more vigor, wield the arms of Venus*

The sea shell that she holds in her hand shows that Venus was born from the sea, as is recounted at length by many.
Her chariot, according to Apuleius, is pulled by doves, which (as is written) are exceedingly lewd, nor is there any time of the year in which they do not remain together in their amorous pleasures.
And Horace, Ovid, and Statius say that Venus is pulled by swans, to demonstrate that the pleasures of lovers are similar to the song of the swan, which is all the sweeter the closer that animal is to dying; because the lover enjoys all the more, the more he suffers in love.
To make this figure somewhat different, Giraldi <note>Lilio Gregorio Giraldi, a prominent Renaissance mythographer</note> writes that Venus is represented, as I have said, atop a chariot pulled by two swans and two doves, naked, with her head encircled by myrtle, and with a flame at her breast; in her right hand a ball, or rather a globe, in the shape of the world, and with the left three golden apples, and behind her are the three Graces, with their arms entwined.
The globe shows Venus to be the ruler and preserver of the universe.
The three apples are in memory of the Judgment of Paris, in praise of her singular beauty.
The Graces are the handmaidens of Venus, who easily entice and corrupt souls that are not well-established in virtue.

### CHARIOT OF JUPITER.

**J**upiter is depicted as cheerful and benign, forty years of age; and on the ancient medals of Antonino Pio and Gordian he is shown naked, but to give him somewhat more grace and to cover his private parts, we will place across his shoulder a blue cloth woven with various flowers.
In his right hand he holds a spear, and in his left a thunderbolt, standing upright upon a chariot drawn by two eagles.
He is painted naked because, as Alexander of Aphrodisias <note>A 2nd-century Greek philosopher and commentator.</note> recounts, in ancient times the images of the Gods and Kings were made naked to show that their power was manifest to everyone.

The chariot is drawn by two eagles, not only to show how they are dedicated to Jupiter, but also to denote his high and noble thoughts, his liberality, and finally his being helpful to others; and for this reason, from "helping" <note>The Latin *iuvare* (to help) was traditionally thought to be the etymological root of *Iuppiter* (Iuvans Pater / Helping Father).</note> it is said that he was called Jupiter.

## F I R E.

**Vulcan** was placed by the ancients to represent fire, and it was customary to paint him naked, ugly, soot-stained, and lame, with a sky-blue hat on his head, holding a hammer in one hand and tongs in the left.

This image shall stand upon an island, at the foot of which there is a great flame of fire, and in the midst of it various kinds of arms; and said island is placed with beautiful grace upon a chariot pulled by two dogs.

**Boccaccio**, in the book *Genealogy of the Gods*, says that fire is of two kinds: the first is the element of fire, which we do not see, and this the Poets often call **Jupiter**; and the other is "elemented" fire <note>Physical fire as manifested in our world.</note>, which we use on earth, and by this is understood the figure of Vulcan. The first is ignited in the air by the very swift circular motion of the clouds and generates thunder; the second is the fire that we light with wood and other things that burn.

He is painted ugly because he was born so, and by his father, who is said to be Jupiter, and his mother **Juno**, he was cast down from Heaven, so that he fell onto the **Island of Lemnos** in the Aegean Sea—which is why he is painted beside the aforementioned Island—and from that fall he remained lame and crippled. Hence he is mocked by the Gods in the Banquet that **Homer** feigns at the end of the first *Iliad*, where he says in his own language:

> *And unquenchable laughter arose among the blessed Gods, as they saw Vulcan bustling through the house.* <note>original: "Immensus autē ortus est risus beatis Dijs, Ut viderunt Volcanū per domū ministrantē."</note>

For no other reason than because he limped, a ridiculous imperfection in a person when they move and perform some action of exercise; nevertheless, from this very imperfection, **Giovan Zarattino Castellini**, my friend—truly a gentleman of wit and fine letters—took lovely material for praise in this epigram of his.

### CHARIOT OF DAWN

A young girl of such beauty as poets strive to express with words, composing her of golden roses, purple, dew, and similar charms; and this shall be as far as the colors and complexion are concerned.

As for her clothing, it must be considered that just as she has three states and three distinct colors, she also has three names: Dawn <note>original: "Alba"</note>, Vermilion, and Orange <note>original: "Rancia"</note>. Thus, for this reason, I would give her a garment down to the waist that is white, thin, and as if transparent; from the waist to the knees, an overgarment of scarlet with certain slashes and knots that imitate those reflections in the clouds when she is vermilion; and from the knees to the feet, the color of gold, to represent her when she is orange. Note that this garment must be slit, starting from the thighs, to show her bare legs, and both the garment and the overgarment should be moved by the wind, creating folds and fluttering.

The arms should also be bare, with a complexion of roses, and she shall scatter various flowers with one of her hands, because at her appearance all those that were closed by the night open up.

She shall have wings of various colors on her shoulders, demonstrating with them the speed of her motion, for as soon as she is extinguished by the solar rays, she quickly vanishes.

On her head, she shall wear a garland of roses, and in her left hand a lighted torch, which signifies that morning splendor by which we see the sky turn white before the sun rises; or else, let a Cupid <note>original: "Amore"</note> be sent before her carrying a torch, and another after her who wakes Tithonus <note>In mythology, Tithonus was the mortal lover of Aurora</note> with another.

Let her be seated on a gilded chair atop a chariot pulled by the horse Pegasus <note>original: "cauallo pegaseo"</note>, because Dawn is a friend to poets and all scholars; or by two horses, one of which shall be a shining white color and the other a shining red. The white horse (according to what Boccaccio recounts in Book 4 of the *Genealogy of the Gods*) denotes that as Dawn is born from the Sun, that brightness of the sky called Aurora proceeds; and the red horse denotes the beginning of the morning, where the vapors rising from the earth are resisted by the coming of the Sun, and Dawn departs, so that from the coming of the Sun and the departure of Dawn, the sky turns red.

## CHARIOT OF CERES

From Boccaccio, in the *Genealogy of the Gods* book 8, Ceres <note>The Roman goddess of agriculture and grain</note> is described as a woman atop a chariot pulled by two most ferocious dragons: on her head she wears a garland of ears of wheat, as Ovid says in the *Fasti*:

> She placed the crown of grain-ears upon her hair. <note>original Latin: "Imposuitque suae spicea serta comae."</note> And in another place:
> Golden Ceres, her hair bound with ears of corn. <note>original Latin: "Flaua Ceres spicis redimita capillos."</note>

She holds a small bundle of poppies in her right hand, and a lit torch in her left.

The aforementioned animals <note>the dragons</note> are given to her to demonstrate the winding furrows made by oxen while plowing the earth (which is what Ceres signifies); or perhaps to denote the serpent driven away by Eurylycus from the Island of Salamis, which, having saved itself in the temple of Ceres, remained there forever as her minister and servant.

The garland of wheat ears signifies that Ceres is the earth, a full and generous producer of grain, and the poppy signifies her fertility.

By the burning torch, I believe we should understand the time of Summer, when the rays of the Sun burn most intensely, ripening the corn; it also refers to when the brush and stubble of the fields are burned, causing the contrary humors <note>In Renaissance science, "humors" were fluids or vapors; here referring to excess moisture in the soil</note> around the earth's surface to evaporate, and by this effect, the earth becomes rich and yields a very great abundance.

# FAMINE.

A GAUNT woman, poorly dressed; in her right hand she holds a willow branch, in her left a pumice stone, and beside her she shall have a lean cow.

Famine is depicted as thin to demonstrate the effect of the lack of things necessary for human life; because money, which is usually spent widely in happier times, is transferred almost entirely into the hands of a few during sterile seasons. Thus, the poor easily become gaunt and poorly dressed due to the scarcity <note>The Italian word "carestia" implies both a lack of food and high prices.</note> of bread and money.

The pumice stone and the willow plant are sterile, and sterility is the principal cause of Famine; however, at times it also arises from the insatiable greed of certain merchants, who are accustomed (defrauding nature) to afflict the poor people with their deceptions.

The lean cow is depicted nearby as a sign of famine; this meaning was shown by Joseph in the Holy Scriptures when he interpreted Pharaoh's dream <note>Referencing Genesis 41, where seven lean cows represent seven years of famine.</note>.

# CHARITY.

A WOMAN dressed in a red garment, who holds a burning heart in her right hand and embraces a child with her left.

Charity is a habit of the will infused by God, which inclines us to love Him as our ultimate end, and our neighbor as ourselves; thus the Holy Theologians describe it.

And she is depicted with the burning heart in hand and the child in her arms to note that charity is an effect, both pure and burning, within the soul toward God and toward creatures. The heart is said to burn when it loves because, when the spirits are moved by some worthy object, they cause the blood to draw back to the heart, which, being altered by its warmth, is said to burn by way of metaphor. Therefore, the two disciples of Christ Our Lord said that their hearts burned within them while He spoke <note>Referencing the Road to Emmaus, Luke 24:32</note>; and this metaphor has since been commonly usurped by poets for lascivious love.

The child is depicted in conformity with the saying of Christ: *Whatsoever you did for one of the least of these brothers of mine, you did for me.* <note>original: "Quod uni ex minimis meis fecistis, mihi fecistis." Matthew 25:40</note>

The red clothing, through its resemblance to the color of blood, shows that true charity extends even to the shedding of blood, according to the testimony of St. Paul.

### Charity.

A woman dressed in red, who has a flame of burning fire atop her head; she will hold a child in her left arm whom she is nursing, and two others shall be playing at her feet, one of whom shall hold the said figure's right hand in an embrace.

Without charity, a follower of Christ is like the dissonant harmony of a discordant cymbal and a disproportion (as St. Paul says); therefore, charity is said to be a "dear unity," because it unites us with God and with men in love and affection, which, by increasing our merits, in time makes us worthy of Paradise.

### C H A S T I T Y .

A WOMAN with a veiled face, dressed in white, standing in the act of walking; with her right hand she holds a scepter, and with her left, two Turtledoves.

Chastity, as Saint Thomas affirms in the *Summa Theologica* <note>original: "2. 2. quest. 151. art. 1."</note>, is the name of a virtue, named from the "chastisement" of the flesh or concupiscence <note>strong sexual desire</note>, which renders a man entirely pure and without any carnal stain.

Her face is veiled because it is characteristic of the chaste person to restrain the eyes; for, as Saint Gregory narrates in his *Moralia*, the eyes must be repressed as if they were kidnappers leading one toward sin.

The white clothing denotes that chastity must be pure and clean from every stain, as Tibullus says in the second book, first Elegy <note>original: "Epistola prima"</note>:

> Chaste things please the gods; come with pure garments, and with pure hands take the water from the fountain. <note>original Latin: "Casta placent superis, pura cum veste venite, Et manibus puris sumite fontis aquam."</note>

> If you take away idleness, Cupid's bows are ruined. <note>original Latin: "Otia si tollas periere cupidinis arcus."</note>

The turtledoves are, as Pierio Valeriano reports in the 22nd book of his *Hieroglyphics*, the symbol of chastity, because the turtledove, once it has lost its mate, never joins with another again.

The scepter signifies the dominion that the chaste person has over themselves; for although the flesh is the primary enemy of the spirit, nevertheless, when the spirit is willing, it can never be cast down or defeated by the flesh. > He said, "I shall die before you have your way with me." <note>original Latin: "Ante ait morian quam sit tibi copia nostri."</note>

Rather than miserably overflowing into the vice of carnal desires.

A woman who holds a legal petition in her left hand, and with her right hand cancels it with a pen; beneath her feet, there shall be several books.

### Clemency and Moderation.

#### From the Medal of Vitellius.

A seated woman, with a laurel branch in one hand, and in the other she holds a staff slightly away from her.

Clemency is a virtue of the soul that moves a man to compassion, making him easy to forgive and ready to assist.

She is depicted seated to signify gentleness and tranquility.

The staff shows that she has the power, but does not wish to use, rigor; thus one might well say, alluding to the current Papacy:

-> Let a thousand Severuses yield to one Clement. <-
<note>A wordplay on the names of Popes or Emperors, contrasting "Severe" with "Clement."</note>

And one could also say what Ovid says in the 3rd book of *Letters from the Black Sea*:

*No God is more moderate than our Prince; he tempers the strength of his own justice.*
<note>original Latin: "Principe nec nostro Deus est moderatior ullus / Iustitiæ vires temperat ille suas."</note>

The laurel branch shows that with it, those who had offended the Gods were purified.

### C O G N I T I O N.

A woman who, while seated, holds a lighted torch, and nearby she shall have an open book, which she points to with the index finger of her right hand.

The lighted torch signifies that, just as our physical eyes need light to see, so our internal eye—which is the intellect—requires the external instrument of the senses to receive the knowledge of intelligible forms. This is shown particularly through the sense of sight, represented by the light of the torch; for as Aristotle says: *Nothing is in the intellect that was not first in the senses.* This is further demonstrated by the open book, because through either seeing it or hearing it read, the knowledge of things is formed within us.

### Knowledge of Things.

A woman who holds a rod or a scepter in her right hand and a book in her left; from which we understand that the knowledge of things is acquired through the attentive reading of books, which constitutes a dominion of the soul.

## COMEDY.

A woman of mature age and noble appearance; in her hand she shall hold the <term>tibia<note>original: "tibia" - an ancient flute or reed instrument</note></term>, and on her feet the buskins. In the arrangement of her hair there shall be many twists and a great entanglement of knots, with this motto: *I describe the customs of men.* <note>original: "Describo mores hominum"</note>

## OLD COMEDY.

A laughing woman, old, but with a wrinkled and unpleasant face; she shall have white and disheveled hair, and clothes that are torn, patched, and varied with many colors. With her right hand she shall hold some arrows, or rather a whip; before her there shall be a monkey reaching out a covered basket to her. Opening said basket from one side with her left hand, the woman reveals various ugly and poisonous animals—namely, vipers, asps, toads, and the like.

One speaks of "Old Comedy" to distinguish it from the "New," which succeeded it and differed in many respects. The Poets in the schools of Old Comedy delighted the people (who at that time held the height of government) by speaking and recounting witty, ridiculous, sharp, and biting things, in blame and derision of the injustice of Judges, the avarice and corruption of <term>Pretors<note>Roman magistrates or administrative officials</note></term>, the wicked customs and misfortunes of Citizens, and other such things. Later, this license was reformed, and the silliness of laughter and buffoonery was entirely removed by New Comedy (as the changing fortune of the state and government, and the more ingenious and wise invention of men, required). It constrained itself to certain laws and more civil decency, through which the subject, the speech, and also the arrangement of the play became very different from what the aforementioned Old Comedy used to be. The reader may see these differences in full between the two in the *Poetics* of <term>Scaliger<note>Julius Caesar Scaliger (1484–1558), a famous Renaissance scholar and physician</note></term>, in the first book titled *History*, chapter 7. The office of Old Comedy, therefore, being to draw the vices and actions of men into laughter and foolishness, the figure has been made with such a face and form as will be explained step by step.

The torn and patched clothes represent both the subject matter she dealt with and the persons who performed such representations; for in these plays, there were no Royal personages as in Tragedy, nor citizens of account as in the <term>togata<note>Roman comedy about Roman life</note></term> or <term>pretextata<note>Roman drama based on historical themes</note></term> comedies of the Romans.

By the various colors of her clothing is demonstrated the diversity and inconsistency of the many things she put together in one composition, and also the varied style, mixing together different kinds of things.

The monkey reaching out the basket shows the foul imitation through which she made manifest the vices and ugliness of others. These are demonstrated by the filthy and poisonous animals which she, with laughter and silliness, reveals to the people; an example of which can be seen in the *Curculio* of <term>Plautus<note>Titus Maccius Plautus, a Roman playwright whose comedies often featured clever slaves and social satire</note></term>.
