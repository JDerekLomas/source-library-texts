# FOREWORD

---

The *Institutes* of Cassian are less famous than his *Conferences*: they lack the same breadth, variety, and that dramatic staging which introduces us to the lives of the ancient solitaries <note>The "Desert Fathers," early Christian hermits living in the Egyptian wilderness.</note> and allows us to witness their conversations. However, they also offer great interest to all those who desire to acquire Christian perfection; they are perhaps even suited to a greater number of people due to the more general character of their teaching, and we know of spiritual directors who prefer them to the *Conferences*, which are merely a further development of the former.

This first work by Cassian was written around the year 416. Its purpose is clearly explained in the preface. The Bishop of Apt <note>Castor of Apt, who requested this guide for his newly founded monastery in Provence.</note> wished to establish a monastery in his province, and he requested from Cassian the advice and traditions necessary to train his monks. Cassian obeyed and summarized in his work everything he had learned during his travels.

<summary>This preface introduces John Cassian's "Institutes," explaining its practical purpose as a foundational guide for monastic life written at the request of the Bishop of Apt.</summary>

He takes advantage of ancient rules, the writings of Saint Basil <note>Saint Basil the Great (c. 330–379), a Greek bishop who established influential rules for communal monastic life.</note> and Saint Jerome <note>Saint Jerome (c. 347–420), known for translating the Bible into Latin and his ascetic writings.</note>; he adds what his predecessors left in silence, and he does so simply, renouncing the extraordinary things he could recount to limit himself to what seems most useful and practical. He admires what he saw observed in Egypt and Palestine; but he proposes the mitigations necessitated by the difference in our climates.

Cassian's *Institutes* <note>original: "Institutions"; referring to the *De institutis coenobiorum*, a foundational text for Western monasticism.</note> are divided into twelve books and two very distinct parts. The first four books are very interesting from the point of view of monastic origins. They introduce us to the primitive regulations of the monasteries, and show us first the external life of the religious <note>In this context, "the religious" refers to monks or members of a religious order.</note>, the simplicity and symbolism of their clothing (I), then the offices of the day and night, the number of psalms and the manner of reciting them, the different hours during the week and on Sunday, the vigils and the fasts in use in the East and in Rome (II, III). Finally, the fourth book teaches us the interior life of the monasteries, the conditions and trials for the admission of the religious, the organization of work, the duties of obedience, and the penances
