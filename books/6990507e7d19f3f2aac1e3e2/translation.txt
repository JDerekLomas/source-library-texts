Secondly: Around the fourth day after the new moon and those following, certain bright points or small areas appear in the eastern part of the Moon—which is still dark—looking like cliffs or small islands separated from the already illuminated region of the Moon. Conversely, when the Moon is waning, there are certain dark parts in its western portion which lose the Sun's light later than some areas lying between them and the illuminated part of the Moon. This is a sign, therefore, that there are certain elevated parts on the Moon which, like our mountains, are illuminated earlier or later than the lower-lying parts. For this diversity cannot be attributed to a mere difference in density or rarity, since these very parts which were previously bright later become dark, and those which were dark become bright.*

*Thirdly: Just as on our Earth <note>original: "Tellure"</note> mountains, trees, and towers cast a shadow toward the West when the Sun rises, and toward the East when it sets—and as the Sun approaches midday those shadows decrease and sometimes vanish within the Torrid Zone <note>The tropical region near the equator where the sun can be directly overhead.</note>, only to grow again as the Sun moves away from the meridian—so it is on the Moon. During the entire time from the new moon to the full moon, the shadows of the new spots are carried toward the eastern part of the Moon, becoming smaller and more faint the closer it gets to the full moon. At the full moon itself they vanish, especially around the center of the Moon, because they are struck more directly and perpendicularly by the Sun's rays. Once the full moon has passed, however, the shadows of those parts begin again, but now moving toward the western region. They are always larger and more intense when those parts, located on the line dividing the illuminated part of the Moon from the dark, first begin to be illuminated or cease to be so. Likewise, when the Moon is full and has no latitude <note>referring to the Moon's ecliptic latitude or its position relative to the Earth's orbital plane</note>, hardly a trace of such shadows appears; but when the Moon then has a significant latitude, they do appear, and are cast either toward the North or the South, depending on whether the latitude is Southern or Northern. ### §. IV. On the nature and structure of the Moon.

<margin>The Moon is an opaque body.</margin>
From what has been said so far regarding the nature and structure of the Moon, which I chiefly wished to investigate, I establish these points:
I. The Moon is a dense and opaque body, suited for stopping and reflecting the light of the Sun. This is evident from what was said in Section I regarding the phases of the Moon, from which it is clearly manifest that the Moon borrows its light from the Sun and has no innate light of its own. The same is evident from eclipses of the Moon, when she, having entered the shadow of the Earth, is darkened either in part or totally. However, the secondary light <note>Now commonly called "Earthshine" or "the old Moon in the new Moon's arms"</note> which appears on the side facing us that is not illuminated by the Sun (when she is not full, and sometimes even during new moons) is light reflected from the Earth. For just as the Earth is illuminated by the Moon reflecting the light received from the Sun, so in turn is the Moon illuminated by the Earth reflecting that same solar light. Furthermore, the fact that an eclipsed Moon still reflects a certain dim light seems to arise from the rays of the Sun being refracted by the Earth's atmosphere toward the axis of the cone <note>The cone of the Earth's shadow</note> which the eclipsed Moon has entered.

<margin>The Moon is spherical, yet not geometrically so.</margin>
II. The Moon is spherical. This is evident from what was said in the same Section I regarding the phases of the Moon; for from them it is gathered that the Moon is illuminated spherically—that is, in exactly the same way that spheres are accustomed to be illuminated and viewed by us in different positions. Anyone can experience this method if they observe the successive illumination of gilded globes placed on the peaks of towers; or if one hangs an opaque sphere in a dark room and another person carries a light around him and the sphere from a distance, so that he is sometimes between the sphere and the light, sometimes the sphere is between the light and him, and sometimes in another position.
