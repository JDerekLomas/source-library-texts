## PREFACE.

THE *Economic Table* <note>original: *Tableau Oeconomique*</note> now printed in facsimile from an original copy of 1759 with Quesnay's manuscript notes, has only recently been rediscovered by Dr. Stephen Bauer, Austrian correspondent to the British Economic Association. In his well-known edition of the works of Quesnay in 1888, Prof. August Oncken of Berne, the Association's correspondent for Switzerland, was forced to refer to the first edition of the *Tableau* <note>French: "Table" or "Chart"</note> as lost beyond hope of recovery. The professor of history at Zürich, Dr. Alfred Stern, in his review of Oncken's *Quesnay* hazarded a conjecture that the *Tableau* might perhaps be found among the papers of Quesnay's chief disciple—the elder Mirabeau <note>Victor de Riqueti, Marquis de Mirabeau (1715–1789), a central figure in the Physiocratic movement</note>—in the National Archives <note>original: *Archives Nationales*</note> at Paris. Upon this hint Dr. Bauer examined the papers, and was fortunate enough to find the document now reproduced.

Editions of the *Tableau* with some alterations were published by Mirabeau in 1760 and 1763 and by Dupont de Nemours <note>Pierre Samuel du Pont de Nemours (1739–1817), a French economist and writer who later emigrated to the United States</note> in 1766 and 1767. The original edition, however, privately printed at the palace of Versailles, was issued in exceedingly small numbers and these appear to have been very carefully suppressed.

The manuscript of this first edition is still extant among the papers of Mirabeau. In 1759 this edition was reprinted with some slight modifications, for Quesnay's private use, only three copies being struck off, of which one, with Quesnay's annotations, was sent to Mirabeau. Making use of this copy Mirabeau composed his *Economic Table with its Explanations* <note>original: *Tableau économique avec ses explications*</note>, as an appendix to his prize-essay on agriculture for the economic <note>The text ends mid-sentence at the bottom of the page.</note>

In a more systematic manner it was recast by Quesnay in his celebrated *Analysis and Formula of the Economic Table* <note>original: *Analyse et Formule du Tableau Économique* (1766 and 1767)</note>. It is from the work here reprinted in the shape and type of the original document that all the later theoretical work of the Physiocratic school <note>The Physiocrats were a group of 18th-century French economists who believed that the wealth of nations derived solely from the value of "land agriculture" or "land development."</note> took root.

Though the *Economic Table* <note>original: *Tableau*</note> is followed by its own explanation, a few prefatory remarks may be found helpful in making clear its purpose and significance.

The French finances which had been drilled into some regularity by Sully <note>Maximilien de Béthune, Duke of Sully (1560–1641), a trusted minister to Henry IV known for his fiscal reforms.</note>, the great minister of Henry IV, had fallen into hopeless disorder during the long reign of Louis XIV (1643--1715), whose ambitious foreign policy, reckless magnificence, and costly administration created a burden of expense utterly disproportionate to the resources of the taxpayers. Matters were not much mended under the voluptuaries who succeeded him—the Regent Philippe, Duke of Orleans <note>original: *Philippe duc d'Orléans*</note>, and Louis XV (1715—1774). But even before the accession of the well-intentioned, though weak, Louis XVI, reformers took heart, and Quesnay, the physician of Louis XV, endeavoured to prove that the economic misery of the people might be relieved by measures which should at the same time improve the royal finances. The wealth of the king, he saw, depended upon the wealth of the people—the source whence it was drawn; and the wealth of the people, he thought, depended chiefly upon the profitable pursuit of agriculture—its principal industry. The most urgent needs of agriculture were capital and freedom. It had been starved of the first by the unprofitable consumption, year by year, of an unduly large share of the national wealth: it needed the second because it was impeded not only by its own burdens (aggravated as they were by the exemptions of privileged nobles and clergy) but also by the numerous and harassing taxes imposed upon commerce,

The nation, Quesnay thought, should cease to live upon its capital to the detriment of efficient production, and a greater portion of wealth should be devoted to the support of agricultural industry (productive expenses <note>original: *dépenses productives*</note>), a smaller to such relative superfluities as decoration (sterile expenses <note>original: *dépenses stériles*; Quesnay categorized any labor that did not produce a "net product" from the land, such as manufacturing or commerce, as "sterile"</note>); while a more equitable and economical principle of taxation should be adopted, by the levy of one tax and one alone—upon the net returns of agriculture (a single tax <note>original: *impôt unique*</note>),—in place of the cumbrous fiscal system in use. The economic activities of the nation freed from their former shackles were to be allowed free play. The beneficent operation of “natural laws” <note>The Physiocrats believed that there was a natural order to society and economics, much like the laws of physics, which would function perfectly if left alone.</note> would then ensure a maximum of productivity both of wealth and of taxes, while the simple taxation of wealth at its source would work the minimum of mischief. The statesman’s rule of thumb was to be "let things be" <note>original: *laissez-faire*; this term became the rallying cry for free-market capitalism, suggesting that government interference in the economy should be minimal.</note>; and his danger-post was, in the words of Quesnay’s motto, "poor peasants, poor kingdom; poor kingdom, poor king" <note>original: *pauvres paysans, pauvre royaume; pauvre royaume, pauvre roi*</note>.

The *Economic Table* <note>original: *Tableau Oeconomique*</note> essays, by analysis of the circulation of wealth, to show the importance of wise consumption in the accumulation of reproductive capital. It is assumed that agriculture, “as in England,” yields a return of 100 per cent. The further hypothesis is adopted of an equal division of expenses into those which are “productive” and those which are not, and the continued reproduction of the productive expenses is shown on the left of the table, the successive sections of unproductive expenses on the right. If the hypothesis be modified so as to allow the right hand column to preponderate at the expense of the left “it is seen that an opulent nation may very rapidly be ruined by [e.g.] excessive luxury of decoration.” Conversely a preponderance on the left will increase the national resources. Further assumptions as to the amount (300 livres <note>The *livre* was the primary currency of France from 781 to 1794.</note>) necessary for the support of an average family, and the number of ploughs needed for cultivation of the country (1 per 120 acres and as many men as there are ploughs)

These views, their novelty and influence, constitute one of the most important chapters in the history of economic and financial theory. For a fuller account reference should be made to Dr. Bauer’s forthcoming article in the *Economic Journal* for March 1895 (No. 17, Vol. v.), and to the authorities there quoted.

The *Economic Table* <note>original: *Tableau Oeconomique*</note> is perhaps best known to English readers from the reference which Adam Smith <note>Adam Smith (1723–1790) was the Scottish philosopher known as the "Father of Economics," whose work *The Wealth of Nations* was deeply influenced by the French Physiocrats.</note> makes to it in the *Wealth of Nations* and his quotation of the extravagant phrases of Mirabeau, who declared that the three great inventions in the history of the world “which have principally given stability to political societies” are the inventions of handwriting, of money, and the *Economic Table* <note>original: *Tableau Oeconomique*</note>.

A work which has in its time been regarded as the source and origin <note>original: *fons et origo*</note> of scientific economics is fitly reproduced in the present bi-centenary year of Quesnay’s birth. It will be remembered that had Quesnay lived long enough it was Adam Smith’s intention to dedicate to him the *Wealth of Nations*. The present publication can therefore hardly fail to interest the members of the British Economic Association.

The best thanks of the Association are due to Dr. Bauer, to Monsieur E. Castelot, its correspondent in Paris, for his care in supervising the photography of the manuscripts by M. Sauvanaud, and to Prof. H. S. Foxwell for superintending the engraving carried out by Mr. Dew-Smith of Cambridge.

It is from this perspective that one must view the state of Nations; because the cash reserve is always self-renewing in a Nation where wealth is continually renewed without decay. 

For about a century—that is, from 1450 until 1550—there was a great decrease in the quantity of money in Europe, as can be judged by the price of goods at that time; but this smaller quantity of cash was a matter of indifference to Nations, because the market value of this wealth was the same everywhere, and because, in terms of cash, their status was the same relative to their revenues, which were everywhere equally measured by the uniform value of silver. In this case, for the convenience of men, it is better for the value to compensate for the mass than for the mass to compensate for the value. One is led to believe that it was the discovery of America that brought a greater abundance of gold and silver to Europe; however, the value of silver had already fallen relative to goods, to the degree it is today, before the arrival of American gold and silver in Europe. ### OF MR. DE SULLY.

**O**ne sees in the preceding table that in the order of regular circulation of 600 million in annual revenue, these 600 million are obtained by means of 900 million in annual advances (*a*) which are distributed annually to four million heads of families. There are one million proprietors <note>Landowners who collect rent.</note>, whose expenditure is estimated on average at 600 for each (*b*) & three million heads of families, occupied in labor or lucrative employments who, each on average, receive 300 livres for their expenditure; but in this distribution it is supposed,

1°. That the entirety of the 600 million in revenue enters into the annual circulation & travels through its full extent; that no <term>pecuniary fortunes<gloss>Wealth held in cash or stagnant savings rather than being reinvested or spent in the economy.</gloss></term> are formed, or at least that there is a compensation between those that are formed & those that return into circulation; for otherwise these pecuniary fortunes would stop the course of a part of this annual revenue of the Nation, and would withhold the <term>money supply<gloss>Original: "pécul ou la finance." The liquid capital available in the Kingdom.</gloss></term> of the Kingdom, to the detriment of the reproduction of revenue and the well-being of the People.

2°. That a part of the sum of revenue does not pass to foreign countries without a return in money or in goods.

3°. That the Nation does not suffer a loss in its reciprocal commerce with foreign countries, even if this commerce were very profitable to the Merchants who gain from their fellow citizens in the sale of the goods they bring back; for then the increase in fortune of these Merchants is a deduction from the circulation of revenues, which is prejudicial to distribution & reproduction.

4°. That one is not deceived by an apparent advantage of reciprocal commerce with foreign countries, by judging it simply by the balance of sums in money, without examining the greater or lesser profit resulting from the goods themselves that were sold, & those that were bought: for often the loss is for the Nation which receives a surplus in money, & this loss turns to the detriment of the distribution & reproduction of revenues.

...come from the wealth necessary for the improvement of estates and for the cultivation of the land.

20°. That a Nation which has a large territory to cultivate, and the ease of conducting a large trade in **raw produce** <term>raw produce: (denrées du crû) unprocessed agricultural products like grain, wine, or wool, which the Physiocrats believed were the only true source of new wealth.</term>, should not extend the use of money and men too far into manufactures and the trade of luxury goods to the detriment of the labor and expenses of agriculture; for above all else, the Kingdom must be well-populated with **wealthy farmers** (a) <note>For the author, "rich farmers" are the essential entrepreneurs of the economy, as they have the capital to invest in modern, high-yield farming.</note>.

21°. That everyone should be free to cultivate in his field such productions as his interest, his means, and the nature of the ground suggest to him, in order to derive the greatest possible product from it; for one should not favor a monopoly in the cultivation of land, because it is prejudicial to the general income of the Nation. The prejudice that leads to favoring an abundance of **primary necessities** <note>Basic food staples like bread.</note> over those of lesser need, to the detriment of the market value of either, is inspired by short-sighted views that do not extend to the effects of reciprocal foreign trade which provides for everything, and which decides the price of the goods that each Nation can cultivate with the most profit. It is therefore the revenues and the **tax** <term>impôt: The author argues that taxes should ideally be a single levy on the "net product" of land.</term> which are of primary necessity to defend the subjects against famine and against the enemy, and to maintain the glory and power of the Monarch.

22°. That the Government be less occupied with the care of saving than with the operations necessary for the prosperity of the Kingdom; for very large expenses can cease to be excessive through the increase of wealth.

23°. That one should pay less attention to the increase in population than to the growth of income; for the greater comfort provided by large incomes is preferable to the more pressing needs for subsistence required by a population that exceeds its income; and there are more resources for the needs of the State when the people are in comfort, and also more means to make agriculture prosper (b).

Without these conditions, agriculture—which is supposed, in the **Tableau** <note>Refers to the "Tableau Économique," a famous diagram created by François Quesnay to show how wealth flows between different classes in society.</note>, to produce one hundred percent as it does in England—would be a fiction: But its principles are not uncertain.

(a) One should only focus on the manufacture of hand-crafted goods for which one has the raw materials, and which can be made with less expense than in other countries. And one must buy from abroad those hand-crafted goods that the foreigner can sell more cheaply than they would cost the nation to manufacture itself. Through these purchases, one encourages reciprocal trade; for if one wanted to buy nothing and sell everything, one would extinguish foreign trade and the advantages of exporting raw produce.

(b) The prevailing idea of war among Nations makes one think that the strength of States consists in a large population; however, the military part of a Nation can only subsist through the tax-paying part. Could one suppose that the great wealth of a State is obtained through an abundance of men? But men can only obtain and perpetuate wealth through wealth, and only as long as there is a proper proportion between men and wealth. A Nation always believes it does not have enough men, and one does not realize that there are not enough wages to support a larger population, and that men only abound in a country insofar as they find guaranteed earnings there to subsist.
