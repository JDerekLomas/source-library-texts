## PREFACE.

The *Economic Table* <note>original: "Tableau Oeconomique"; this was a visual diagram meant to show how wealth circulates through an economy, much like blood through a body.</note> now printed in facsimile from an original copy of 1759 with Quesnay's manuscript notes, has only recently been rediscovered by Dr Stephen Bauer, Austrian correspondent to the British Economic Association. In his well-known edition of the works of Quesnay in 1888, Prof. A. Oncken of Berne, the Association's correspondent for Switzerland, was forced to refer to the first edition of the *Table* as lost beyond hope of recovery. The professor of history at Zürich, Dr Alfred Stern, in his review of Oncken's *Quesnay* hazarded a conjecture that the *Table* might perhaps be found among the papers of Quesnay's chief disciple—the elder Mirabeau <note>Victor de Riqueti, Marquis de Mirabeau (1715–1789), a leading proponent of the Physiocratic school of economics.</note>—in the National Archives <note>original: "Archives Nationales"</note> at Paris. Upon this hint Dr Bauer examined the papers, and was fortunate enough to find the document now reproduced.

Editions of the *Table* with some alterations were published by Mirabeau in 1760 and 1763 and by Dupont de Nemours <note>A French economist and writer who later emigrated to the United States; his family founded the DuPont company.</note> in 1766 and 1767. The original edition, however, privately printed at the palace of Versailles, was issued in exceedingly small numbers and these appear to have been very carefully suppressed.

The manuscript of this first edition is still extant among the papers of Mirabeau. In 1759 this edition was reprinted with some slight modifications, for Quesnay's private use, only three copies being struck off, of which one, with Quesnay's annotations, was sent to Mirabeau. Making use of this copy Mirabeau composed his *Economic Table with its explanations* <note>original: "Tableau économique avec ses explications"</note>, as an appendix to his prize-essay on agriculture for the economic

It is from the work here reprinted in the shape and type of the original document that all the later theoretical work of the <term>physiocratic school</term> <note>The Physiocrats were a group of 18th-century French economists who believed that agriculture was the sole source of a nation's wealth and advocated for "laissez-faire" policies.</note> took root.

Though the *Tableau* <note>The "Tableau Économique" or Economic Table is a famous visual diagram representing the flow of money and goods in an economy.</note> is followed by its own explanation, a few prefatory remarks may be found helpful in making clear its purpose and significance.

The French finances which had been drilled into some regularity by Sully <note>Maximilien de Béthune, Duke of Sully (1560–1641), was the chief minister to King Henry IV and is credited with restoring French prosperity after the Wars of Religion.</note>, the great minister of Henry IV., had fallen into hopeless disorder during the long reign of Louis XIV. (1643–1715), whose ambitious foreign policy, reckless magnificence, and costly administration created a burden of expense utterly disproportionate to the resources of the taxpayers. Matters were not much mended under the voluptuaries who succeeded him—the Regent Philippe duc d'Orléans, and Louis XV. (1715—1774). But even before the accession of the well-intentioned, though weak, Louis XVI., reformers took heart, and Quesnay, the physician of Louis XV. <note>François Quesnay was a surgeon and court physician before turning his attention to "social health" and economics.</note>, endeavoured to prove that the economic misery of the people might be relieved by measures which should at the same time improve the royal finances. The wealth of the king, he saw, depended upon the wealth of the people—the source whence it was drawn; and the wealth of the people, he thought, depended chiefly upon the profitable pursuit of agriculture—its principal industry. The most urgent needs of agriculture were capital and freedom. It had been starved of the first by the unprofitable consumption, year by year, of an unduly large share of the national wealth: it needed the second because it was impeded not only by its own burdens (aggravated as they were by the exemptions of privileged nobles and clergy) but also by the numerous and harassing taxes imposed upon commerce.

The nation, Quesnay thought, should cease to live upon its capital to the detriment of efficient production, and a greater portion of wealth should be devoted to the support of agricultural industry (*productive expenses* <note>original: "dépenses productives"</note>), a smaller to such relative superfluities as decoration (*sterile expenses* <note>original: "dépenses stériles"; Quesnay used this term for manufacturing and commerce, believing they only reshaped wealth rather than creating it</note>); while a more equitable and economical principle of taxation should be adopted, by the levy of one tax and one alone—upon the net returns of agriculture (*single tax* <note>original: "impôt unique"</note>),—in place of the cumbrous fiscal system in use. The economic activities of the nation freed from their former shackles were to be allowed free play. The beneficent operation of “natural laws” would then ensure a maximum of productivity both of wealth and of taxes, while the simple taxation of wealth at its source would work the minimum of mischief. The statesman’s rule of thumb was to be **let it be** <note>original: "laissez-faire"; the principle of non-interference in economic affairs</note>; and his danger-post was, in the words of Quesnay’s motto, **poor peasants, poor kingdom; poor kingdom, poor king** <note>original: "pauvres paysans, pauvre royaume; pauvre royaume, pauvre roi"</note>.

The <term>Economic Table <note>original: "Tableau Oeconomique"; Quesnay’s famous visual diagram representing the flow of money and goods through an economy</note></term> essays, by analysis of the circulation of wealth, to shew the importance of wise consumption in the accumulation of reproductive capital. It is assumed that agriculture, “as in England,” yields a return of 100 per cent. The further hypothesis is adopted of an equal division of expenses into those which are “productive” and those which are not, and the continued reproduction of the productive expenses is shewn on the left of the table, the successive sections of unproductive expenses on the right. If the hypothesis be modified so as to allow the right hand column to preponderate at the expense of the left “it is seen that an opulent nation may very rapidly be ruined by [e.g.] excessive luxury of decoration.” Conversely a preponderance on the left will increase the national resources. Further assumptions as to the amount (300 <term>livres <note>a French unit of currency used before the adoption of the franc</note></term>) necessary for the support of an average family, and the number of ploughs needed for cultivation of the country (1 per 120 acres and as many men as there are ploughs)

This was a groundbreaking diagram created by Quesnay to show how wealth moved between different classes in society (farmers, landowners, and artisans).</note> is perhaps best known to English readers from the reference which Adam Smith makes to it in the *Wealth of Nations* and his quotation of the extravagant phrases of Mirabeau <note>Victor de Riqueti, Marquis de Mirabeau (1715–1789), a leading French economist and follower of Quesnay.</note>, who declared that the three great inventions in the history of the world “which have principally given stability to political societies” are the inventions of handwriting, of money, and the <term>Economic Table</term>.

It will be remembered that had Quesnay lived long enough, it was Adam Smith’s intention to dedicate to him the *Wealth of Nations*. The present publication can therefore hardly fail to interest the members of the British Economic Association.

It is from this perspective that one must view the state of Nations; because the monetary reserve is always being reborn in a Nation where wealth is renewed continually and without decay.

For about a century—that is to say, from 1450 until 1550—there was a great decrease in the quantity of money in Europe, as can be judged by the price of goods at that time; but this smaller quantity of reserve was a matter of indifference to the Nations, because the market value of this wealth was the same everywhere, and because, in relation to the reserve, their status was the same relative to their revenues, which were everywhere equally measured by the uniform value of silver. In this case, it is better for the convenience of men that value compensates for mass, rather than mass compensating for value. One is inclined to believe that it was the discovery of America that procured a greater abundance of gold and silver in Europe; however, the value of silver had already dropped against commodities to the level where it is today before the arrival of gold and silver from America in Europe.

### BY MR. DE SULLY.

**O**ne sees in the preceding table that in the order of the regular circulation of 600 million in annual revenue, these 600 million are obtained by means of 900 million in **annual advances** (*a*) <note>In Physiocratic theory, 'advances' refer to the capital or investment required upfront—such as seeds, labor, and animal feed—to generate agricultural wealth.</note> which are distributed annually to four million heads of household. There is one million of landowners, whose expenditure is estimated, on average, at 600 for each (*b*) and three million heads of household, occupied in labor or lucrative employment who, each on average, receive 300 livres for their expenditure; but in this distribution it is supposed:

1°. That the entirety of the 600 million in revenue enters into the annual circulation and traverses its full extent; that no **pecuniary fortunes** <note>The author is referring to the hoarding of cash, which removes money from the circular flow of the economy.</note> are formed, or at least that there is a compensation between those that are formed and those that return into circulation; for otherwise these pecuniary fortunes would stop the course of a part of this annual revenue of the Nation, and would withhold the **cash reserves** <note>original: "pécul"</note> or the finance of the Kingdom, to the detriment of the **reproduction** <note>The 'reproduction' of revenue refers to the cyclical regeneration of wealth through agriculture in the next harvest season.</note> of revenue and the well-being of the People.

2°. That a portion of the sum of revenue does not pass to foreign countries without a return in money or in goods.

3°. That the Nation does not suffer a loss in its reciprocal commerce with foreigners, even if this commerce were very profitable to the **Merchants** who gain from their fellow citizens in the sale of the goods they bring back; for then the increase in fortune of these Merchants is a deduction from the circulation of revenues, which is prejudicial to distribution and reproduction.

4°. That one is not deceived by an apparent advantage of reciprocal trade with foreigners, by judging it simply by the balance of sums in money, without examining the greater or lesser profit resulting from the goods themselves that were sold, and those that were bought: for often the loss is for the Nation which receives a surplus in money, and this loss turns to the detriment of the distribution and the reproduction of revenues.
