### PREFACE.

---

When, at the request of the governing body of the Sydenham Society <note>A medical publishing society founded in 1843 to disseminate important historical and scientific texts.</note>, I undertook to edit the works of the immortal discoverer of the circulation of the blood in English, I believed that the most important of these works were already available in our language in a form that would require little more from an editor than a careful revision of the text. I had unintentionally adopted the idea—quite baselessly originated by John Aubrey <note>original: "Aubrey"; refers to the 17th-century biographer known for his "Brief Lives," which contained many anecdotal and sometimes inaccurate claims.</note>—that Harvey was what is called an indifferent scholar, and that the English versions of his writings were the actual originals while the Latin versions were the translations. 

Because I had access to the handsome edition of Harvey’s works in Latin (revised by Doctors Lawrence and Mark Akenside and published by the College of Physicians in 1766), I had always referred to that edition whenever my studies led me to consult Harvey. Of the English versions, or any other edition, I knew little or nothing. 

However, upon proceeding to my new duty as English editor, I immediately saw that Harvey’s masterpiece, *On the Motions of the Heart and Blood* <note>original: "MOTIONS OF THE HEART AND BLOOD"; referring to the 1628 treatise "Exercitatio Anatomica de Motu Cordis et Sanguinis in Animalibus."</note>, far from having the character of an original English composition, must have been translated into English by someone with very little knowledge of the subject. I found the style to be extremely off-putting and full of flagrant errors. <header>PREFACE.</header>

By making my task a labor of love, I soon completed a new translation of the *Exercises on the Heart and Blood* <note>original: *Exercitatio Anatomica de Motu Cordis et Sanguinis in Animalibus* (1628)</note>, with equal pleasure and profit to myself.

The work on *Generation* <note>original: *Exercitationes de Generatione Animalium* (1651)</note> came next under review. I had heard it positively asserted that the existing English version of this book was the original—that it was Harvey’s own writing. Here, therefore, I expected my business as an editor would properly begin. But I had not gone through a couple of pages of the text before I encountered difficulties similar to those I had already experienced. It quickly became apparent that the claim mentioned above was erroneous. A little inquiry enabled me to discover that the English version of the *Exercises on Generation* was actually the work of a physician named Llewellen <note>Dr. Martin Lluelyn (1616–1682), a physician and poet</note>. Though it was not generally incorrect, there was, nevertheless, a great deal that I wished had been rendered differently. Furthermore, the scientific and professional language of two centuries ago looked strange to the eye and had an unusual sound to the ear. 

Anxious only to present the writings of Harvey to my colleagues in the most appropriate and attractive form possible—for he always appeared to me in his works, and with his contemplative look in his portrait, as a kind of divinity in medicine—I girded myself for the long and laborious enterprise of translating the work on *Generation* anew into our mother tongue. At length, I achieved my task, though not without difficulty.

The short paper on the *Anatomy of Thomas Parr* <note>Parr was a famous Englishman alleged to have lived to the age of 152; Harvey performed his autopsy in 1635</note> appears in the *Philosophical Transactions* <note>The scientific journal of the Royal Society</note> in English; however, it stands there as a translation. Having now translated so much myself, I thought it would be best to translate that also, and so it was accomplished.
