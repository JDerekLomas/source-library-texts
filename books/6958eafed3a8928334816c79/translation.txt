## PREFACE. &emsp; &emsp; &emsp; &emsp; &emsp; &emsp; PREFACE.

<margin>
"Come and see the works of the Lord, what wonders God has placed upon the earth: making wars to cease even to the end of the earth."
Psalm 45, verse 8. <note>Modern Bibles usually cite this as Psalm 46:8-9.</note>
</margin>

WHEN the Royal Prophet <note>King David, traditionally credited with the Psalms.</note> invites the faithful to consider the works of God and the wonders he has placed upon the earth; if he had not at the same time declared that he intended to speak only of those notable victories that the Israelites won over their enemies through the protection of the God of Armies, I would believe that he had wished to invite us to contemplate those rare marvels with which God has enriched the earth: I mean the plants, wonders indeed admirable and worthy of being looked upon for the help we receive from them, for the pleasures they give us, and for their almost infinite number, but which we very often neglect. I know that God has committed us to raise our eyes toward the Heavens from whence we must expect all our help; but these eyes he has made mobile, and he permits them to cast their gaze upon the earth, to admire there those rare wonders where his divine providence shines forth in a thousand and a thousand ways; so that, excited by so many different beauties found in plants, we might cry out in thanksgiving: that all that the earth—

I would believe that the Royal Psalmist and Prophet had exhorted the Faithful to marvel at PLANTS as stupendous wonders <note>original: "prodigia"</note>; were it not that the same Royal Psalmist and Prophet immediately thereafter alluded to the victories won by the mighty hand of the Israelite people, and wars driven to the borders of the enemies; when he calls the faithful to come and see the works of the Lord; what wonders God has placed upon the earth; for plants are wonders which, whether for their utility, or for their pleasantness <term>amœnitas: the aesthetic beauty or delightfulness of a landscape or garden</term> and their great number, are worthy of being weighed by men, but (alas) by the carelessness of men, they are often neglected by men. I know indeed that God gave to men a sublime countenance and faces turned upward to the stars, from whence comes all the salvation of men; yet he equipped them with mobile eyes, so that sometimes, cast down toward the earth, they might finally contemplate and marvel at the wonders—the plants, I say—monuments of immense providence, and moved by those marvelous wonders, they might justly and worthily sing to the praise of the Maker of wonders: Bless the Lord, all things that grow—
