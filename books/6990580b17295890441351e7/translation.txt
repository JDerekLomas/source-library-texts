**W**ith so many recent expansions of human knowledge and improvements in the presentation of the same, acoustics <note>original: "Akustik"; the branch of physics concerned with the properties of sound</note> has suffered the undeserved fate of being treated far more deficiently than other parts of natural history <note>original: "Naturkunde"; a term then used to encompass the natural sciences</note>. Excellent treatises on individual acoustic subjects can be found scattered throughout the writings of learned societies, but for the most part, these have either been completely ignored or have not been put to proper use; furthermore, there is not yet a single even mediocre work available covering the entirety of acoustics. ### regarding the history of my acoustic discoveries.

Since various people have shown interest during my oral accounts of the history of my discoveries, and some also while reading what was said about them in *Voigt’s Magazine for the Latest in Physics and Natural History* <note>Original: "Voigts Magazin für das neueste aus der Physik und Naturgeschichte," a prominent scientific journal of the late 18th century.</note> Vol. IX, Part 4, I have no hesitation in mentioning some of it here as well. I do this primarily to show that absolutely none of this was a result of chance, but rather of persistent striving. During the greater part of my life thus far, I had every reason to be dissatisfied with my fate—especially with the complete contradiction between my external circumstances and my inclinations. However, I have since found that everything was for the best; under a different course of fate, many things that may be useful for acoustics and its practical applications to the art of music <term>Tonkunst: the art of music or musical composition</term> would at least not have been discovered by me, and I would have had to forgo much subsequent applause, advantage, and pleasure. Nevertheless, I cannot advise another to yield so little to external circumstances, nor to abandon a career already entered upon—which, though not quite in accordance with one's inclination, promises certain advantages—in order to pursue entirely uncertain prospects; for a procedure of this kind would likely not have the desired success in most cases.

# XX

During several journeys undertaken with my *Euphon* <note>The Euphon was an instrument invented by Chladni in 1789 consisting of glass rods rubbed with wet fingers</note>, as well as during occasional stays in *Wittenberg*, I constantly endeavored to conduct and publish various new acoustic investigations. These were sometimes quite laborious; for example, a full three months were required just to conduct and organize the observations contained in my treatise on longitudinal vibrations <note>original: "Longitudinalschwingungen"</note>, which consists of only two sheets of paper.

A primary ambition of mine was to invent a keyboard instrument on which one could sustain any tone at will and allow it to increase or decrease in volume through more or less pressure. The Clavier (or clavichord), the pianoforte, the harpsichord, or indeed all instruments where the sound is produced not by friction but by striking, absolutely lack this quality. Consequently, slurs, syncopated notes, and long sustained passages—where the tones should increase in strength after the initial attack or continue with equal strength—cannot be properly performed on them. They belong, therefore (as *Horstig* correctly remarked in the *Musical Gazette* 1799, no. 24), not among the "singing instruments," but among the "percussive instruments" (or perhaps even more fittingly: "tinkling instruments").

On the organ, one can indeed sustain the tones, but one cannot make each individual tone increase or decrease in volume at will.

I wished, therefore, to produce a keyboard instrument where one would have the duration and the swelling or fading of every tone under one's control, just as with all string and wind instruments (on which, however, one cannot have full harmony unless several are played together).

A bowed-clavier <note>original: "Bogenflügel," a keyboard instrument where strings were vibrated by a continuous mechanical bow</note> does indeed possess this quality, but I did not wish to build one, because this had already been done by others, and because it is impossible to design such an instrument to be so small and compact <term>compendiös: small enough to be easily portable</term> that I could have conveniently taken it with me in my carriage on travels. Furthermore, I required that it should stay in tune and be as simple as possible, so that in the future it might become more widely used.

On a sea voyage from *Reval* <note>modern-day Tallinn, Estonia</note> to *Flensburg* in the year 1794, I shortened the boredom caused by contrary winds by reflecting further on the matter and sketching ideas for mechanical arrangements of this kind in my tablet. When I returned to *Wittenberg*, I conducted experiments which gave me some instruction; however, the matter was not yet ripe for execution. Finally, in May 1799, I discovered several more things necessary for this purpose, and in January 1800, I completed an instrument which, with a pleasant—

# XXII

To those who have asked out of interest, or who might yet wish to ask, whether I truly found the desired advantage on my travels, I answer: while each journey brought me no great riches, it nonetheless brought more benefit than harm. Given my modest pretensions <note>The author uses "Prätensionen" to mean his claims or expectations of status.</note> and simple needs, I can be well satisfied with the result. Perhaps I might have occasionally secured even greater profit had I been willing to employ some of that immodesty and intrusiveness which many a musical artist <note>original: "Tonkünstler"</note>—hardly to the honor of our art—allows himself to be guilty of. 

Furthermore, I find much that is rewarding in the kind reception I enjoyed from so many excellent individuals. These people certainly remain unforgettable to me, despite the fact that during my travels I noted down nothing more than the day of arrival in a place and the day of departure. Additionally, both in my travels and in my labors, I am greatly aided by a robust health, as well as an insensitivity toward many unpleasant things—a trait that has become a habit through my former circumstances and the thwarting of many of my wishes. Yet, this is balanced by an even greater receptivity to every kind of pleasant impression.

# Introduction.

Every possible movement is either a progressive, a rotating, or an oscillating movement, the latter of which is also called a vibrating movement <note>original: *motus vibratorius, oscillatorius, tremulus*</note>. This type of movement alone, under the conditions to be specified hereafter, acts upon the sense of hearing; all other types of movement, as far as is known, act upon it only indirectly, namely, insofar as a vibrating movement is induced in the surrounding bodies.

Audible vibrations are observed only in elastic bodies. Elasticity is that property of a body whereby, if the position of its parts is changed by an external force, it strives to restore their previous position. When a part of an elastic body has been displaced from its original position by an externally applied pull or impact, and this pull or impact ceases, the elastic body returns to its previous position or shape. It does so with increasing speed because, during this time, elasticity continues to act as an absolute force. When it has reached its former position, its attained speed has become greatest; it cannot, therefore, rest at this point, but continues in the same direction with decreasing speed until its speed becomes zero. However, it likewise cannot rest in this unnatural position, but returns from there in the same manner back to its original position with increasing speed, and beyond it with decreasing speed; and this back-and-forth

Movement continues for as long as it is not hindered by some internal or external resistance. A body can be elastic 1) through tension, like a string or any flexible body in general, 2) through compression, like air or any expansible fluid matter under the pressure of the atmosphere, 3) through its internal cohesion, such as a rod of glass, iron, wood, or a bell, and generally every rigid body.

## 3.

If a vibrating movement is to be audible, it is required:
1) That it happens quickly enough. It is commonly assumed that at least approximately 30 oscillations must occur in one second if a swinging movement is to be perceived by the human organs of hearing. <note>The author is describing the lower threshold of human hearing, modernly defined as roughly 20 Hz.</note>
2) That this movement be strong enough. The strength with which it acts upon the hearing can depend on the size and the elasticity of the vibrating body, on the force with which it is set into motion, on its distance, and on the conductivity of the materials through which the movement is propagated to the organs of hearing.
3) That the organs of hearing possess a suitable constitution for this purpose. These can be so different in humans and among various animal species that, for this reason alone, no absolute limit of audibility can be specified.

Even if a vibrating movement is not capable of acting upon the hearing, it still follows the exact same laws as audible oscillations, and is therefore not essentially different from them. If, for example, a tensioned string is so long that it makes 4 oscillations in a second, one can see the changes in shape and count the oscillations, but one hears nothing. If one continually shortens the string by half, so that it must gradually make 8, 16, and more and more oscillations in a second, one will only notice an effect on the hearing from approximately 32 onwards, although the very same laws of nature were in effect previously.

## 4.

Audible oscillations of an elastic body are called a **sound**. **Acoustics** is the study of **sound**.

## 5.

In the case of a sound, the vibrations (both in terms of the time intervals in which they occur and the changes in shape of the elastic body) are either uniform and determinable (through hearing as well as through other means known thus far) or they are not; in the former case, it is a *musical sound* <note>original: "Klang"</note>, in the latter, a *noise* <note>original: "Geräusch"</note>.

*Note.* Many writers, especially *Rameau* <note>Jean-Philippe Rameau (1683–1764), a French composer and influential music theorist</note> and others who followed him, have held quite incorrect concepts of a *musical sound* and the way it differs from a *noise*. They view a *musical sound*, which is something quite simple, as something very complex, and claim that one always simultaneously hears, in addition to the primary vibration corresponding to the number 1, others that correspond to the natural sequence of numbers 2, 3, 4, etc. <note>This refers to the harmonic series or overtones</note>. This error is also found in *Sulzer’s Theory of the Fine Arts*, in *Erxleben’s Natural Philosophy*, and in many other writings; *Rameau* and his followers founded their entire system of *music* upon it. However, such resonance <note>overtones</note>, as will be shown in the 9th section of the 2nd part, is not a universal property

## 6.

If, in the case of a musical sound, one considers only the speed of the vibrating motion and not the properties and shape-changes of the sounding body, it is called a *tone* <note>original: "Ton"</note>; specifically, a high *tone* if the vibrations occur quickly, and a low *tone* if the vibrations occur slowly. A *tone* is not absolutely high or low, but only in comparison with others where the vibrations occur slower or faster. A sequence of tones is called *melody*, a single appropriate coexistence of several tones a *chord* <note>original: "Accord"</note>, and a sequence of chords (or a coexistence of several melodies) is called *harmony*. *Music* utilizes the materials provided to it by *acoustics* in order to express and arouse sensations through the combination of tones into *melody* and *harmony*.

1. *Note.* If one wishes to express oneself correctly, one does not say: an instrument has a good *tone* <note>Ton</note>, but rather: a good *timbre* or *sound* <note>Klang</note>. Conversely, one does not say: a high or low *sound* <note>Klang</note>, but a high or low *tone* <note>Ton</note>.

### §. 7.
## Acoustics investigates:

1) The **time-ratios** of vibrating motions in general, without taking into account the properties and changes in shape of the trembling bodies; this part is called the **theory of tones** <term>Tonlehre: the mathematical study of musical intervals and frequencies</term>.
2) The **laws of vibration** of every elastic body.
3) The **propagation of sound**.
4) The **sensation** of sound by means of the organs of hearing.

This order will be followed in the present writing. One may fittingly call the **general theory of tones** the **arithmetic part**, the theories of the **laws of vibration** of elastic bodies and of the propagation of sound the **mechanical part**, and the theory of the **sensation of sound** the **physiological part of acoustics**.

**Note.** It is quite incorrect that the theory of sound is treated in most physics textbooks under the theory of **air**. Other elastic bodies are just as capable as air—and some even more so—of producing sound and propagating the sound of other bodies. It will therefore be more appropriate to treat this part of natural philosophy under the theory of **motion**, and specifically next to the theory of **pendulum swings**, with which it stands in close relation.

### §. 8.

The difference between one tone and another, or the variation in the number of vibrating motions occurring within the same period of time, is called an <term>interval</term> <note>from the Latin 'intervallum', meaning the space between two points</note> or a <term>tonal ratio</term>. One usually imagines every interval as ascending, so that a higher tone stands in a certain relationship to a lower tone used as the foundation. Thus, the first number of the ratio will be smaller than the second. If an interval is to be assumed as descending, so that one compares a lower tone with a higher one, this must be specifically indicated; in this case, the latter number of the ratio must be smaller than the first.

When a minor triad <note>original: "weiche Dreyklang," literally "soft triad"</note> consisting of the ratios 1, 6/5, and 3/2 is given to a root note, the scale belonging to it can be found in the same manner as previously shown for the major scale <note>original: "harten Dreyklange," literally "hard triad"</note>. ->A, B, C, D, E, F, G, A, B.<-

Now, however, the ear demands that to characterize every major and minor scale, the step from the seventh to the eighth tone must be only a large half-step, especially when ascending. Just as every major or minor scale consists of the triads <note>term: triad (Dreyklang) — a chord consisting of three notes: a root, a third, and a fifth</note> of the root, its fifth, and its fourth, so too—if the feeling of a key is to be maintained—the most natural progressions are those where the triad of the root moves to one of the other two triads from which the scale originated, or where one of those moves back to the root. This is because its combination with so many other already existing intervals would create too great a number of new tones, such that they could neither be properly grasped nor performed due to their proximity to other intervals. Since various writers, especially older ones, derive intervals from divisions of the octave, it will be necessary to mention a few more things regarding this. The division of a ratio consists of finding one or more mean proportional numbers between two numbers, thereby producing two or more smaller ratios from one larger one. The division of a musical ratio can be arithmetic, harmonic, or geometric.

Through **arithmetic division**, one obtains ratios where the differences between the terms are equal to one another. If a ratio is to be divided into two others, one places half the sum of both numbers between them. In the same way, by dividing the fifth 2 : 3, one obtains the major third <note>original: "große Terz"</note> 4 : 5 and...

### §. 31.

Since it has now been proven that the intervals—with the exception of the octave, which must retain its ratio of 1 : 2 as a mere repetition of an existing tone—cannot and may not be performed with complete purity, one must seek to bring every interval as close to perfect purity as is possible without noticeable detriment to the others. Just as every interval assumed in its arithmetic purity is generally either a bit too large or a bit too small for the maintenance of a usable <term>musical system (Tonsystem)</term> <note>The organized collection of tones used in a specific musical culture or theory</note>, so too is the small half-step <note>ratio 24:25, often called the chromatic semitone</note> somewhat too small for this purpose, and the large half-step <note>ratio 15:16, the diatonic semitone</note> somewhat too large. It is therefore necessary for a usable musical system to take the half-steps—whether they be large or small by origin—as larger than 24/25 and smaller than 15/16, and consequently to perform every raised interval at the same pitch as the neighboring lowered interval. If one therefore performs C-sharp and D-flat, D-sharp and E-flat, etc., in the same manner, the following 12 actual tones remain:

->c, c-sharp, d, d-sharp, e, f, f-sharp, g, g-sharp, a, b-flat, b, c,<-
->(d-flat) (e-flat) (f-flat) (e-sharp) (g-flat) (a-flat) (a-sharp) (c-flat) (b-sharp)<-

which constitute the musical system that is rightly and commonly accepted.

1. The comma 531441/524288, or 1.0136... in decimal numbers, by which 12 fifths are too high and 12 fourths are too low compared to the octave, is called the **Pythagorean comma**, and by some also the **ditonic comma**. It takes its name from the major third 81/64, increased by 81/80, which Pythagoras used and which was called the **Ditonus** <note>original: "Ditonus"; a "double tone" interval formed by two major seconds</note>, because it arises from the combination of 2 major whole tones 9/8; three such thirds exceed the octave by as much as this comma amounts to.

A twelfth part of the Pythagorean comma 531441/524288, or 1.0011... in decimals, is called a **Schisma** by some writers.

Eleven-twelfths of the Pythagorean comma constitute the **syntonic comma** 81/80 or 1.0123..., which appears most commonly in the combination of pure ratios and has already occurred here several times. It is arithmetically decomposed by Kirnberger <note>Johann Philipp Kirnberger (1721–1783), a theorist and student of J.S. Bach</note> and some others into the two commas 161/160 and 162/161.

The comma 2048/2025 or 1.0113..., which contains ten-twelfths of the Pythagorean comma, is called the **Diaschisma**.

The difference found in §34 between the product of three major thirds and the octave, which amounts to 128/125 or 1.0235..., is called the **lesser diesis**; it contains 21/12 of the Pythagorean comma.

The difference found in §35 between the octave and the product of 4 minor thirds, 648/625 or 1.0368, is called the **greater diesis**, and contains 32/12 of the Pythagorean comma.

It is an indubitable principle of experience that when one hears an interval which deviates only extremely slightly from an interval expressible by simpler numbers, one believes they are hearing the simpler one, and that this deception is all the more perfect the smaller the deviation. That such a deception of the hearing takes place is also very beneficial for us, because otherwise absolutely no usable music could exist, as was shown in § 30. Whether the interval that one actually hears can be expressed by whole numbers or only by irrational numbers makes no difference in the effect. Since the intention of a temperament can be none other than to make the necessarily distributed impurity as imperceptible as possible—and since in the current state of the musical art <term>Tonkunst: the art of music/composition</term> one must be able to make advantageous use of all intervals and all keys, and there is no reason to perform one interval or one key more purely or impurely than the others—it follows that equal temperament is most consistent with nature. In this system, although all intervals except the octave are impure, the deviation of each interval is so small due to the completely equal distribution of impurity that the hearing is nowhere offended, and every interval produces just as good an effect as if it were entirely pure.

2. **Note.** In the practical execution of tuning, it is sufficient if one tunes every <term>fifth</term><note>An interval of five scale degrees</note> slightly downward—so little that it is barely noticeable—and every <term>major third</term><note>An interval of four semitones</note> slightly upward, creating a "beat" or <term>oscillation</term><note>The pulsing sound heard when two notes are slightly out of tune with each other</note>. Namely, in a musical sound, the vibrations of the elastic body—or of the parts into which it divides itself throughout its entire extent in a very regular manner—are uniform and simultaneous. This cannot be claimed of a noise; rather, the trembling movements therein seem to be very heterogeneous, both in regard to their duration and in regard to their other characteristics. With a musical sound, one can judge the proportional number of vibrations, or the pitch <note>original: "Höhe des Tones"</note>, through the sense of hearing and compare it with other sounds, but this is not possible with a noise. ### 44.

Not all elastic bodies are capable of producing a musical tone; those which cannot, due to a lack of the necessary uniform elasticity <term>Elasticity: the ability of a material to return to its original shape after being deformed, essential for sustained vibration</term>, or due to various external or internal obstacles, do at least produce a noise when they are set into a sufficiently fast and strong trembling motion by a strike, a pull, or by friction (which acts here like a sequence of strikes or pulls occurring in an oblique direction). Every actual musical tone is capable of very different modifications, the essence of which is still entirely unknown, but which probably arise from some admixture of noise. Namely, if several human or animal voices, or several instruments, produce the same pitch, the effect can still be very different despite the same duration and strength of the tone, as well as often the same mode of vibration. The reason for this may lie:

1) In the nature of the sounding body itself. If sounding bodies differ only in regard to their material <term>Matter/Substance</term>, but perfectly agree with one another in regard to shape, mode of vibration, pitch, and the manner in which they are set in motion, one will nonetheless be able to notice a difference; as, for example, between steel and gut strings <note>Strings made from animal intestines, common before the widespread use of synthetic materials</note>, or between rods of metal and wood. To all appearances, this arises from a weak noise connected with the actual musical tone, which is stronger the tougher and less elastic the sounding body is, and the more obstacles to vibration are found in the heterogeneous consistency and in the various inhibitions and frictions of its internal parts. The shape of the sounding body and the variety of the modes of vibration usually contribute only little to a different effect of the tone.
