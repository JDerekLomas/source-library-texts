<margin>That the sciences were acquired through the activity of the mind.</margin>
MAN is the foremost of all things contained within the law and command of nature. Although he partakes of both the divine and the perishable, and enjoys the fellowship of both, he is nevertheless inflamed by a burning desire for higher things; he ought to follow only sparingly those things which are filthy, vile, and abject, which the impulse of beasts pursues. It would surely seem execrable and detestable if man allowed that part of himself which he received as celestial and divine to be so contaminated by the foulness of vices, or ensnared by the allurements of pleasures, or overwhelmed by the care and anxiety of mortal affairs, that, being oppressed and ruined, it could never at some point recover itself and lift itself higher. For those who hold to such a course of life that they are carried along ensnared only by the charms of the senses seem in no way superior to mute animals: since even beasts seek out and prepare the necessary protections for life, and possess the greatest care for both themselves and their offspring, as well as for pleasure.

<margin>The differences of the sciences arise from two causes.</margin>
But it is the mark of the most noble and wisest man to raise the mind, though it be covered by the stains of the body, to the contemplation of excellent things: so that he may perceive how much he alone excels among all these things, and, though entangled in these frameworks of the body, he may nevertheless, through the activity of the mind, become mindful of his origin and a sharer in divinity. This is the highest perfection of human life, this is the best and most greatly divine way of living. Those who were once distinguished by this were rightly held to be Gods <note>Fernel refers here to the "apotheosis" or deification of ancient inventors and healers like Asclepius</note>, and were treated with the most ample honors, both because they were admirable for their deep wisdom, and because they had conferred great and fruitful benefits upon mankind. From this, with the minds of others kindled and inflamed toward glory—as the impulse and intention of the mind was manifold, and as the things by the study of which they were captivated were various—many types of sciences were polished and perfected.

<margin>
Three types of Philosophy contemplated by the mind.
Natural philosophy, Theology and its subject matter, and Mathematics.
</margin>

...were established, which were begun partly out of necessity and partly from a zeal for learning. Some, admiring the harmony of nature's works and their extraordinary form and beauty, were enticed by the sweetness of those things which—leaning upon physical bodies—are changeable, mortal, and perceived by the senses. They scrutinized the origins and causes of these things with great labor and diligent vigilance, and thus founded **natural philosophy** <note>Also known as "physics" in the classical sense: the study of the physical world and its laws.</note>. Others, driven by a more sublime impulse of the soul, focused most keenly on divine matters—those things which are eternal, immutable, separate from matter, and beyond the grasp of any sense. So far as human frailty allows, they brought forth **theology** and the knowledge of the Gods. Others intervened, led by a middle type of contemplation, who established the **mathematical disciplines**: these are indeed illustrious and most certain in the subtlety of their demonstrations. **Plato** determined these to be the borders and intermediaries between divine and natural things, because they are eternal and immutable, yet positioned within a body and subject to the senses.

<margin>
The excellence of Contemplation.
</margin>

These are the three studies of wisdom celebrated for many centuries, born from the variety of things they treat, and formed by the contemplation of a single, higher mind. For they are not adapted to any comforts of life, nor to the allurements of pleasures, nor finally to any goods of fortune or the body; nor do they lower themselves to be despised in such great contempt. Instead, restrained within their own boundaries, they turn entirely within themselves as if in a circle. They are to be sought for their own sake and not for anything else mortal, primarily so that we may dispel ignorance and turbulent errors from our souls.

<margin>
The active Mind as the producer of arts.
</margin>

Furthermore, those who possessed a less exalted soul, neither born nor prepared sufficiently for philosophizing, likewise did not wish to pass an idle life or one sluggish without effort. Destined for either the comforts or pleasures of men, and moved by that part of the mind which they called <term>praktikēn</term> <note>original: "πρακτικήν". This refers to the "active" or "practical" intellect, as opposed to the "speculative" or "theoretical" intellect.</note>—that is, the **acting** mind—they gave birth to the lower disciplines, all comprised of prudence and art. For whoever among these placed all their zeal in shaping human character and the institution of living rightly, they made an entrance into the **moral** faculty, and into **civil** and **economic** matters, which are administered by governing prudence. To these is added **history**, the memory of deeds and the commemoration of antiquity; as well as **Geography**, the description of places and nations, without which history itself is neither illuminated nor shines forth. Furthermore, they stirred up all the **arts**, whoever pursued not the actions and characters of men, but their affections and works. First, so that they might express the perceptions of the mind through speech (for this is an "affection" because it is produced by the organ of the tongue, whereas a pure "act" of the mind needs no help from a bodily instrument), they arranged **Grammar**, which is the art of speaking correctly, and **poetry** bound by meter...

...constrained, and Rhetoric <note>original: "Rhetoricam"</note>, which is the doctrine of speaking with elegance and abundance, and Dialectic <note>original: "Dialecticam"</note>, which is the method of reasoning correctly. Medicine was discovered and refined for either maintaining or recovering the health of the human body; Agriculture for the necessity of life's nourishment; and Architecture for the construction of buildings. Thus weavers, smiths, goldsmiths, shoemakers, belt-makers, bakers, butchers, potters, painters, booksellers, and countless others have adapted their arts to the conveniences of mankind. Likewise, musicians, singers, flute-players, actors, gladiators, dancers, hunters, fishermen, bird-catchers, cooks, sausage-makers, pimps, jesters, and gamblers have celebrated certain skills for the delight and pleasure of men—skills which are either utterly foul or hardly noble. But since all philosophy examines and weighs the subject matter upon which it applies itself, it is the specific property of every art to implant a certain quality and faculty into a material that has come into the light by its own spontaneous nature—a quality which would not be added by itself without the mind or hand of man. For art does not provide the material, but rather confers the craftsmanship upon the material from the outside; this is either stable and firm, as in constructed houses, or fleeting and passing, as in the harmony of flute-players. These, therefore, are the pursuits of philosophy (which is the mother of all good arts), these the pursuits of prudence, and these the distinctions of the arts and the highest classes of disciplines, which both the diverse agitation of the mind and the variety of things they handle have separated as much as possible.

<margin>From whence the dignity of philosophy and the arts is to be regarded.</margin>
Now indeed, let us examine from this point how excellent and noble each art or discipline may be, and with how much splendor of dignity some outshine others. The action of the gods and of celestial minds is judged by the opinions of all to be the most excellent, because it applies itself deeply to the contemplation of the best things; but the action of beasts (which serve the necessity of life and are carried by every impulse toward pleasure) is humble, vile, and obscene. If we transfer these to human actions, all contemplation will seem most noble, supremely liberal, and high-born, and every kind of philosophy will be illustrious; however, all the industry of craftsmen—whose labor and work is not considered an arduous power of the mind—will be illiberal and sordid. Between these, however, art has taken its seat; and each will be placed in a position so much worthier in its kind, as it cultivates the mind with a more sublime contemplation than the rest, through which we are more intelligent and deserve to be called wise. This is especially true if that art does not build up hatred and offense toward men against itself, but is moderately fruitful and productive, both for all others

...structures, how can we spend a life that is blessed and tranquil? To Marcus Tullius <note>Marcus Tullius Cicero, the Roman statesman and philosopher</note>, the mere knowledge of nature and the contemplation of things is by no means pleasing; rather, it is considered idle, sluggish, altogether defective, and merely incomplete if it is not accompanied by the administration of affairs, and if it is not prepared for the benefit of mankind and the protection of society. If you examine all the advantages, faculties, and resources of all the arts, nothing is better than medicine, nothing more desirable, and nothing more excellent has been given to the human race. For if life is most dear to all mortals—if that light in which we live, breathe, and are joined together in pleasant fellowship is to be sought above all else—if it is the first and most outstanding gift attributed to living beings: how can the art which preserves, sustains, and protects that life not be the most outstanding of all? What can a person wish for more than that which repels the most infested enemies of the human race—namely, diseases and ill health? Which protects a safe life, free from all toil and faintness, for as long as possible? Just as no wealth or fortune is greater than a firm constitution of the body, so nothing is more laborious or calamitous than when it is weak and failing. Therefore, he who is able to help and assist those brought into such danger and crisis is so far from incurring their offense that he joins them to himself in the highest familiarity and friendship; and, as Hippocrates says, he is even revered as a deity <note>original: "pro numine"</note> for having restored such great riches, through which external fortunes may also be obtained and their use held pleasant and sweet.

Furthermore, turning to those things which medicine surveys by reason, you will understand with what noble and distinguished contemplation it pursues and investigates the natures of many things. For just as it explains and unfolds the admirable fabric and nature of man, so it inquires into and prepares for the rising and setting of all things, the very composition of all things from the elements, and the specific powers of animals and plants (though not absolutely, yet insofar as they are healthful or pestilential to man). Indeed, going deeper, it scrutinizes whatever is unearthed from the opened jaws of the earth; and rising from there, it observes the revolutions of the heavens and stars, and the virtues that flow and descend from them as moderators of the lower world. Moreover, because these things cannot be obtained except through a certain path of demonstration and reasoning, who would deny that the mathematical arts and logic <note>original: "dialecticam"</note> open the way to these? And even much more are rhetoric and grammar required, without which it is not only impossible to speak copiously and ornately, but one is not even permitted to speak correctly.
