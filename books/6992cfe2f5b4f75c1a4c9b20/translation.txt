In the twenty-third year of the <term>Kangxi</term> reign <note>1684; Kangxi was the fourth Emperor of the Qing dynasty</note>, the <term>Zheng clan</term> in Taiwan was pacified <note>Referring to the surrender of the Kingdom of Tungning, the last Ming loyalist stronghold</note>, and the maritime ban was broadly lifted. In the twenty-fourth year, following the petitions of provincial officials, Customs Houses were established at Jiang <note>Jiangsu</note>, Zhe <note>Zhejiang</note>, Min <note>Fujian</note>, and Yue <note>Guangdong</note>. Consequently, merchant ships from the interior were able to trade throughout the Southern Seas.

The Dutch, having assisted our forces during the pacification, were the first to request mutual trade. Other "seafaring foreigners" <note>original: "Bird Barbarians," a period term for Westerners whose ships seemed to appear like migratory birds</note> followed their lead and submitted, gazing with respect toward the imperial stars <note>original: "Dou Niu," referring to the North Star and the constellation Ox, symbolizing the Chinese Emperor</note>, arriving annually by sea from great distances. 

The English were the last to knock at our gates. Their nations are all located in the "Great Western Ocean" <note>the Atlantic</note>, vast and unfathomably distant. Since the Han Dynasty, the routes to the "Western Regions" reached only as far as the Southwestern Seas and stopped. However, if one examines the two chapters on the Western Regions in the *Book of Han* <note>The official history of the Han Dynasty, finished in 111 CE</note>, one can understand the relationship between the "Western Regions" and the "Western Ocean"...

Although these lands are far apart, their customs are not very different. The <term>Book of Han</term> <note>original: "Ban Shu," referring to the history of the Western Han Dynasty by Ban Gu</note> states that the King of <term>Jibin</term> <note>An ancient kingdom in the Kashmir or Gandhara region</note> ruled from the city of Xunxian. Its people are skilled at decorative carving and engraving, the construction of palaces, and the weaving of <term>woolen rugs</term> <note>original: "ji," a type of heavy woolen fabric or carpet</note> and fine embroidery. They take great pleasure in wine and food. They use gold, silver, copper, and tin to make vessels. In their markets, they use gold and silver as currency; the obverse of their coins depicts a man on horseback, while the reverse depicts a human face.

Among those whose temperament and products are similar to Jibin are the kingdoms of <term>Wuyi</term> <note>Alexandria Prophthasia, in modern-day Afghanistan/Sistan</note> and <term>Anxi</term> <note>Parthia, ancient Persia</note>. The coins of Wuyi specifically feature a human head on the obverse and a horseman on the reverse. Anxi also uses silver for currency; its coins depict the face of the King on the obverse and the face of his Consort on the reverse. 

Today, the nations of England, France, and Holland are highly skilled at manufacturing <term>pocket watches</term> <note>original: "lian biao," literally "chain-meters," referring to mechanical timepieces</note>, reaching the very limits of technical ingenuity. Their woven woolens and similar goods are of peerless refinement. The <term>foreign silver coins</term> <note>original: "yang qian," referring to the Spanish silver dollars and other European trade coins then circulating in China</note> they use are also cast in silver, with the reverse featuring either a human face or the face of a King. In this regard, their customs are identical [to those of the ancient Western kingdoms]. 

Furthermore, the <term>Book of Han</term> records that the people of Anxi write horizontally on <term>parchment</term> <note>original: "hua ge," literally "painted/marked leather"</note> to keep records. The commentator <term>Yan Shigu</term> <note>A famous Tang Dynasty scholar (581–645 AD)</note> noted that the writing of the various "Western Barbarian" nations and the people of <term>Linyi</term> <note>An ancient kingdom in central Vietnam</note> in the south is all written horizontally.

## General Treatise on the Maritime Kingdoms, Part II

How could such methods of conciliation and attraction fail to reach even the most distant places? 

Indeed, for **interpreters** <note>original: "xiangxu"; an ancient term for officials who translated the languages of distant "barbarian" tribes</note> to convey intentions and sincere feelings is no easy task; for masts and sails to brave the **South China Sea** <note>original: "zhanghai"; literally "the swelling sea," an ancient name for the South China Sea</note> where waves touch the sky is no pleasant journey. Yet, since the kingdom of **Huangzhi** <note>an ancient kingdom in the Indian Ocean, likely Kanchipuram in South India</note> first presented tribute during the Han dynasty, the historical records have never ceased. In the great metropolis of **Panyu** <note>modern-day Guangzhou</note>, since the Tang dynasty, foreign ships have gathered like clouds. 

It is evident that these distant races offer precious tribute because they regard the Central Lands as a **heavenly storehouse** <note>original: "tianfu"; a place of immense natural wealth and abundance</note>; these various foreign tribes **embrace our civilization** <note>original: "huaiba"; to be drawn to the cultural and moral influence of the Chinese empire</note> and seek trade to enrich themselves.

The Sage-Kings of old were well-acquainted with their customs; therefore, they bound them with grace and trust, and attracted them with **trade goods** <note>original: "huohui"; specifically referring to the exchange of gifts and commodities</note>. Thus the "barbarians" were filled with joy, and even elephants were brought as auspicious signs. This reduced the costs of border defense, so that war did not break out, and the surplus from trade taxes provided for military supplies. 

Looking broadly at ancient and modern times, the successes and failures of systems tailored to the era can be clearly recognized. Therefore, those who pursue profit with a voracious appetite—this is the common nature of these tribes. Those who use circumstances to secure treaties—this is as solid as the defenses of the Western regions. 

As **Mengjian** <note>the courtesy name of Ban Gu (32–92 CE), the famous historian and author of the Book of Han</note> said: "The Han has much wealth; they [foreigners] must trade to get what they want." Thus the kingdom of **Jibin** <note>original: "Kanbin"; likely a transcription variant for Jibin (Kapisa), a kingdom in the region of modern-day Afghanistan/Kashmir</note> sought to profit from imperial rewards and trade through its envoys.

# Illustrated Treatise on the Maritime Kingdoms, Volume 79
## General Treatise on the Seas, Part 3

**Page 33**

...The Edict ordered the Generals and other officials to conduct separate and detailed interrogations on each point and submit a full report. These instructions were issued for their information. 

**By Imperial Command.**

We [your ministers] have respectfully complied. We immediately supervised the departmental officials, who led interpreters to inquire about each item piece by piece over several consecutive days. According to the testimony of the **white barbarians** <note>original: "baiyi" (白夷). A term used by Qing officials for Westerners, specifically the British in this context.</note>:

From the capital city of **Great Britain** to the interior region of **Guangdong**, the journey depends entirely on the wind patterns. With favorable winds, it takes no more than three months to reach **Hong Kong**. If delayed, it varies between four or five months; at the absolute latest, it takes no more than six months to arrive. 

Regarding the places passed along the way, such as **France** <note>original: "Folanjia" (佛蘭機). Historically referring to the "Franks," this term was used for various European powers. Here, the text incorrectly lists it as a British possession.</note>, **Egypt** <note>original: "Jiyuju" (急欲舉). A phonetic rendering of Egypt.</note>, **Ceylon** <note>original: "Shilang" (士郎). Modern Sri Lanka.</note>, **Banda** <note>original: "Wanda" (萬達). Likely referring to the Banda Islands or a similar port of call.</note>, **Ratnagiri** <note>original: "Lasha" (刺沙). Likely a phonetic rendering of a coastal port, though "Lasha" is also the name used for Lhasa in Tibet; the context here is strictly maritime.</note>, **Colombo** <note>original: "Gulubi" (姑路庇). The capital of modern Sri Lanka.</note>, **Malacca** <note>original: "Lingmalege" (令馬勒格). A strategic port in the Malay Peninsula.</note>, and **Singapore**, these are all <term>territories belonging to Great Britain</term> <note>This reflects the Qing officials' attempt to map out the "string of pearls"—the British colonial outposts and naval bases that secured the route to the East.</note>. 

As for which other sovereign nations they might pass, it is difficult to provide the exact names. If the ships ran low on fresh water or food, they would approach any mountain or island visible near the sea's surface, using small boats to come ashore and gather water. They were unable to distinguish the specific names of all those various locations. 

Regarding **Kashmir**, that is **Bengal** <note>original: "Keshimier, ji Mengjia" (克食米爾, 即孟加). This reveals a significant geographic confusion in the interrogation report, conflating the inland northern region of Kashmir with the coastal eastern region of Bengal. Both were under British control or influence, leading the captives or the scribes to treat them as synonymous.</note>...
