## INTRODUCTORY REMARKS.
### 1. ANTIQUITY OF ARYAN POLITY.

The <term>Arthashâstras<note>Treatises on statecraft, economic policy, and military strategy.</note></term> have been in existence in <term>Aryâvarta<note>The "Abode of the Noble," referring to the northern part of the Indian subcontinent where the Indo-Aryan culture was established.</note></term> as a separate literature from very early times. The superiority of <term>Dharmashâstras<note>Treatises on duties, laws, and ethics (Sacred Law).</note></term> over <term>Arthashâstras</term> is referred to in the <term>Smriti<note>A category of Hindu texts "which is remembered," typically referring to legal and social codes.</note></term> ascribed to <term>Yâjñavalkya<note>A legendary sage and philosopher to whom several influential legal and philosophical texts are attributed.</note></term> in the following <term>shloka<note>A specific meter of verse used in Sanskrit poetry.</note></term> :—

> When two legal codes conflict, equity (justice) prevails based on established practice. However, the rule is that the Sacred Law (Dharma-shastra) is more authoritative than the Science of Statecraft (Artha-shastra). <note>original: "Smrityor-virodhe nyâyastu balavân-vyavahâratah | Arthashâstrâttu balavad-dharmashâstramiti sthitih ||" (2.21)</note>

The same is said in the <term>Bhavishya Purâna<note>One of the eighteen major Puranas (ancient texts), known for its prophecies about the future.</note></term> as quoted in the <term>Aparârka<note>A 12th-century legal commentary on the Yajnavalkya Smriti.</note></term> :—

> If there is a contradiction with the meaning of the Sacred Law, the Science of Statecraft shall be set aside. <note>original: "Smrityarthena virodhe tu arthashâstrasya bâdhanam ||"</note>

The <term>Nârada Smriti<note>An ancient legal text attributed to the sage Narada, focusing primarily on judicial procedures.</note></term> also remarks in the same way :—

> One should carefully conduct legal proceedings while ensuring there is no conflict between the Sacred Law and the Science of Statecraft. <note>original: "Dharmashâstrârthashâstrâbhyâm-avirodhena yatnatah | Sampashyamâno nipunam vyavahâragatim nayet ||"</note>
> Where there is a disagreement between the Sacred Law and the Science of Statecraft, one should reject what is stated in the Science of Statecraft and follow what is stated in the Sacred Law. <note>original: "Yatra vipratipattih syâd-dharmashâstrârthashâstrayoh | Arthashâstroktam-utsrijya dharmashâstroktam-âcharet ||" (1.37-39)</note>

In his well-known <term>Arthashâstra</term>, <term>Kautilya<note>Also known as Chanakya or Vishnugupta, the prime minister to Chandragupta Maurya and the traditional author of the most famous Artha-shastra.</note></term> (not later than 3rd century B.C.) says that a literature bearing this title was extant in his days, and that he compiled his treatise after seeing the works of his predecessors. His words are :—

> This single Artha-shastra has been composed by summarizing almost all those treatises on the Science of Statecraft which were established by previous teachers regarding the acquisition and protection of the earth. <note>original: "Prithivyâ lâbhe pâlane cha yâvantyarthashâstrâni pûrvâchâryaih prasthâpitâni prâyashastâni samhitya-ekamidam-arthashâstram kritam."</note>

->(Second edition, p. 1).<-

Hertel) has a reference to a <term>nîti<note>Policy, political ethics, or wise conduct.</note></term> maxim of <term>Brihaspati<note>In Hindu mythology, the teacher of the gods and a legendary founding father of political science.</note></term>.

> The well-organized [work] of <term>Vishnugupta<note>Another name for Kautilya/Chanakya.</note></term>, the "Winning of Friends" by <term>Bhargava</term>, and the "Distrust" of <term>Brihaspati</term>—these three comprise the foundations of political alliance. <note>original: "sukṛptaṃ viṣṇuguptasya mitrāptirbhārgavasya ca | bṛhaspateraviśvāso nītisandhistridhā sthitaḥ || 244 ||"</note> ॥२४४॥

Of that work, <term>Manu</term>, the son of the Self-Existent, separated the portion dealing with Sacred Law (<term>Dharma</term>) as a distinct work. <note>original: "tasyaika-deshikaṃ manuḥ svāyaṃbhuvo dharmādhikārikaṃ pṛthak cakāra || 6 ||"</note>

<term>Bhâsa<note>One of the earliest and most celebrated Indian playwrights.</note></term> also refers to a *Bârhaspatya Arthashástra* in his *Pratimâ Nâtaka*:—

Ravana:— "...I am of the <term>Kashyapa</term> lineage; I study the <term>Vedas</term> with all their limbs and supplements, the Law Code of <term>Manu</term>, the <term>Yoga</term>-treatise of <term>Maheshvara</term>, the Statecraft-treatise of <term>Brihaspati</term>, the Logic-treatise of <term>Medhatithi</term>, and the Ritual of Ancestors by <term>Prachetasa</term>." *Pratima Nataka* p, 79. <note>original: "rāvaṇaḥ—“... kāśyapa-gotro’smi sāṅgopāṅgaṃ vedam adhīye, mānavīyaṃ dharma-shāstraṃ, māheshvaraṃ yoga-shāstraṃ, bārhaspatyam artha-shāstraṃ, medhātither nyāya-shāstraṃ, prācetasaṃ shrāddha-kalpaṃ ca |”"</note>

In ancient times, he made my brothers learn the science of politics spoken by <term>Brihaspati</term>. *Vana Parva* 32. 61. <note>original: "nītiṃ bṛhaspati-proktaṃ bhrātṛn me’grāhayat purā | vana-p 32 61 ||"</note>

### IV. THE CODE OF MANU (MANAVA DHARMA SHASTRA).

Scholars like Max Muller and Buhler held that the <term>Dharmashâstra<note>A genre of Sanskrit texts concerning law, conduct, and social duties.</note></term> of Manu was originally a composition in the mixed style of <term>Sûtras</term> and <term>Shlokas<note>The standard poetic verse form in Sanskrit literature.</note></term>. Buhler tried his best to put forward this theory with such proofs as he could gather. One of his strongest proofs was a passage "They cite the Manava Sutra" <note>original Sanskrit: "mānavam sūtram udāharanti"</note> which he found in the <term>Vâsishtha Dharmashâstra<note>An ancient Indian legal text attributed to the sage Vasistha.</note></term>. Now it is sure that the original work of <term>Manu<note>The legendary first man and progenitor of humanity, to whom the primary code of law is attributed.</note></term>—had passed through the hands of several editors, as for example <term>Bhrigu</term>, <term>Nârada</term> etc., when it reached the compiler of the Vâsishtha Dharmashâstra and that a <term>Carana<note>A school or branch of Vedic study dedicated to a specific version of a Veda.</note></term> of the <term>Krishna Yajur Veda<note>The 'Black' Yajur Veda, one of the four primary Vedas, containing both ritual prayers and prose explanations.</note></term> namely the Mânava had also come into existence. It is, therefore, quite clear that a school of the Mânavas had come into existence even before the 3rd century B.C. The <term>Arthashâstra<note>A famous ancient Indian treatise on statecraft, economic policy, and military strategy.</note></term> of <term>Kautilya<note>Also known as Chanakya, the advisor to Chandragupta Maurya and the traditional author of the Arthashastra.</note></term> refers to a certain author of this very school. The views of the Mânavas represented in this *Arthashâstra* differ widely from the <term>Smriti<note>Literally "that which is remembered"; a category of Hindu texts including law codes and epics.</note></term> of Manu. It is, therefore, evident that the school consisted of authors who differed greatly. It was this great difference which troubled Buhler very much when he could find no reason as to why the teachings of the *Smriti* differed from the <term>Mânava Grihya Sûtra<note>A manual belonging to the Manava school that details domestic rituals and life-cycle ceremonies.</note></term>. ### IX. WAS BRIHASPATI A MATERIALIST?

No doubt, there was a great atheistic logician named Brihaspati; but was he the same person who wrote on statecraft and polity? The author Somadeva Suri (writing in the 10th century A.D.) says in his work *Yashastilaka*: **"Like the systems of Brihaspati, they do not depend on God."** <note>original Sanskrit: *Brihaspati-nitaya ivadevamatrika*. The term *adevamatrika* is a clever pun; in agriculture, it refers to land watered by rivers rather than "the gods" (rain), but here it implies an atheistic political system.</note> (p. 13, Nirnayasagar edition, 1901). His commentator, Shrutasagar Suri, adds: **"Like the systems of Brihaspati. Just as the systems of Brihaspati—the Charvaka scriptures—do not believe in a God distinguished as omniscient and so forth."** <note>original Sanskrit: *Yatha Brihaspati-nitayash-charvaka-shastrani devam sarvajnadivishesham na manyante*.</note>

So, according to this commentator, the <term>Nîtikâra</term> <note>A writer on ethics, law, or political science.</note> Brihaspati was the famous <term>Cârvâka</term> <note>An ancient school of Indian materialism that rejected the authority of the Vedas and the existence of an afterlife or deities.</note>. However, all the earlier quotations contradict this view. Brihaspati the politician, being associated with Manu and others of the theistic school, was certainly a believer in the divine. It appears that Somadeva and his commentator are confusing two different individuals named Brihaspati.

Thus, there have been at least three eminent persons known by the name Brihaspati in the literary history of <term>Bhârata</term> <note>India.</note>. First, and most well-known, was Brihaspati the writer on statecraft; second was Brihaspati the materialist (<term>Cârvâka</term>) logician; and third was the person who wrote the <term>Samhitâ</term> mentioned above.

## THE APHORISMS OF BRIHASPATI.

The text here edited with a translation was brought to notice in the course of a search for a celebrated treatise, also ascribed to a <term>Brihaspati</term> <note>A legendary sage in Indian tradition, regarded as the preceptor of the gods and the mythical founder of both political science and the materialist school of philosophy.</note>, namely the exponent of the <term>Worldly-Linked</term> <note>original: "Lokâyata"</note> or <term>Cârvâka</term> <note>A school of ancient Indian materialism that rejected the supernatural and emphasized sensory experience.</note> doctrine, the crude corporealism of India. The discovery of this work, to judge from the quotations in the *Compendium of All Philosophical Systems* <note>original: "Sarvadarshana-samgraha," a famous 14th-century survey of Indian thought</note> and elsewhere, would contribute notably to the entertainment of students of Sanskrit literature.

The present treatise is not devoid of interest, but the interest is of a different character. The work is in one respect unique, being an exposition of the Science of Royal Policy in <term>Sûtra</term> <note>Literally "thread"; a style of writing composed of short, aphoristic rules intended for easy memorization.</note> style. This Indian science may claim no ordinary place in the history of culture, since two of its offshoots <note>original: "succedanea"</note>, the policy in fable and the game of chess, still styled the Royal Game, have made the conquest of the world. In its pure form as a science of monarchical government it does not seem to have passed beyond the Indian sphere, that is the sphere dominated by Indian culture, including Further India, the Malay countries, Central Asia, and Tibet: for, though the Muslims <note>original: "Muhammadans"</note> have a science of government, which may go back to pre-Islamic Persia, it does not seem to betray an Indian inspiration. And Machiavelli’s *Prince*, if influenced at all, as is theoretically <note>original: "à priori"</note> quite conceivable, by oriental models, would derive rather from the Muslim than the Indian. The propagation of the policy in fable (the *Fables of Pilpay* <note>A common European name for the "Panchatantra," a collection of Indian animal fables used to teach worldly wisdom and politics.</note>) was first suggested <note>original: "adumbrated"</note> by Sir William Jones, in a sentence*

# The Aphorisms of Brihaspati
