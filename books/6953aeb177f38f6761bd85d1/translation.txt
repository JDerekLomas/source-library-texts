# Hekate-Selene-Artemis and Related Figures
## in the Greek Magical Papyri
## and on the Curse Tablets.

->By Theodor Hopfner (Prague).<-

In the Greek Magical Papyri¹ <note>These are a collection of papyri from Graeco-Roman Egypt containing magical spells, rituals, and hymns.</note> only the following seven major pieces address themselves exclusively to the syncretic <note>Syncretism refers to the merging or "blending" of different gods and beliefs into one.</note> goddess named in the title: A. *Apotropaic Inscription to Selene* <note>original: "Δέλτος ἀποκρουστικὴ πρὸς Σελήνην". "Apotropaic" refers to magic intended to turn away or ward off evil.</note> in the Paris Papyrus (Pap. IV Pʀ.), lines 2243–2347 (Volume I, pages 140–146 Pʀ.); B. *Erotic Binding Spell* <note>original: "Ἀγωγὴ". This type of spell was intended to "lead" or "compel" a lover to the practitioner.</note>, ibid. lines 2441–2621 (Vol. I, pp. 148–154 Pʀ.); C. *Slander Spell to Selene, also an Erotic Spell* <note>original: "Διαβολὴ πρὸς Σελήνην". This ritual involved "slandering" a person to the goddess to provoke her anger against them.</note>, ibid. lines 2622–2706 (Vol. I, pp. 154–158 Pʀ.); D. *Another Erotic Binding Spell* <note>original: "Ἄλλη ἀγωγή"</note>, ibid. lines 2709–2784 (Vol. I, pp. 158–160 Pʀ.); E. *Prayer to Selene* <note>original: "Εὐχὴ πρὸς Σελήνην"</note>, ibid. lines 2785–2890³; F. *Prayer* <note>original: "Εὐχὴ", namely to Selene again</note>, London Papyrus 121 (Pap. VII Pʀ.), lines 756–795 (Vol. II, pp. 34–35 Pʀ); G. *Claudian's Lunar Incense and the Rite of Heaven and the Great Bear for Lunar Incenses* <note>original: "Κλαυδιανοῦ Σεληνιακὸν καὶ οὐρανοῦ καὶ ἄρκτου τελετὴ ἐπὶ σεληνιακῶν". Though titled as a ritual for incense and the stars, it is actually another erotic binding spell.</note>, ibid. lines 862–918 (Vol. II, pp. 38–40 Pʀ.).

Selene is indeed explicitly called by her names Selene, or Mene⁴, in the invocations, prayers, and formulas of pieces A, B, C, E, and F. However, in several places, epithets <note>Descriptive titles or nicknames given to a deity.</note> appear alongside these names of the Moon Goddess that point to a syncretism with other goddesses, namely the **three-headed** <note>original Greek: "τρικάρανος"</note> and **triple-named Selene**⁵ <note>original Greek: "τριώνυμος Σελήνη"</note>, or the **triple-named Mene**⁶ <note>original Greek: "τριώνυμος Μήνη"</note>. And the fact that we are dealing with documents of the most highly developed syncretism (which, incidentally, is self-evident from the period of the papyri—i.e., the 3rd/4th century AD—and from their character) is strikingly proven by the circumstance that this "Moon Goddess" is in one breath add- <note>The text cuts off here due to the page break; the catchword "ange-" indicates she is being "addressed" or "called upon."</note>

# Hekate-Selene-Artemis in the Magical Papyri

"...she who holds the paths of the mountains" ⁵⁸ <note>original Greek: ἔχουσα ὀρέων δρόμους</note>, yet Hekate is also occasionally ⁵⁹ called "mountain-roaming" <note>original Greek: οὐρεσιφοῖτις</note>. Nevertheless, we would prefer to attribute the <term>cult title</term> <note>original: Epiklese</note> "mountain-wandering" <note>original Greek: ὀρίπλανος</note> (B 562, F 854) to Artemis rather than to Selene or Hekate. As one who traverses the mountains, the title "of the heights" <note>original Greek: ἀκρίη</note> (A 227) might also belong to her, though the papyrus provides the reading *arkie* <note>original Greek: ἀρκιή</note>. However, Artemis was not only a hunter but also a protector of wild game and even of livestock, so we must certainly also assign her the title "she who tends the herds" <note>original Greek: νομαῖος</note> (A 271) ⁶⁰. Likewise, the <term>cult title</term> "mountain-spring-opener" (?) <note>original Greek: ὀρεοπηγανύξ</note> (D 755) likely belongs to her, usually associated with "mountain-grasper" <note>original Greek: ὀρεοβαζάγρα</note>, "earth-shatterer" <note>original Greek: ῥηξíχθων</note> (perhaps "she who tears open the earth"?), and "subterranean" <note>original Greek: ἱππό- (read as ὑπό-)χθων</note>, even though these last two epithets fit the nature of Hekate more closely. 

Artemis was indeed understood as a goddess of springs, rivers, and lakes—and even as a goddess of the sea—and springs, rivers, and lakes frequently play a special role in her myths ⁶¹. Furthermore, springs and even healing waters were often located in or near her sanctuaries, which led to her epithets "of the marsh" <note>original Greek: λιμναία</note> or "of the lake" <note>original Greek: λιμνᾶτις</note>, especially in the Peloponnese <note>The peninsula forming the southern part of mainland Greece</note>, such as in Sicyon, Patras, Tegea, Epidauros Limera, Sparta, Messene, and notably in the famous *Limnaion* on the Laconian-Messenian border ⁶². In Troezen, Artemis was therefore explicitly called "mistress of the lake" ⁶³ <note>original Greek: δέσποινα λίμνης</note> or, with regard to the Kladeos ⁶⁴ and the Alpheios ⁶⁵, "of the river" <note>original Greek: ποταμία</note>. As a sea goddess, she was finally called "overseer of harbors" <note>original Greek: λιμένεσσιν ἐπίσκοπος</note>, "harbor-watcher" <note>original Greek: λιμενοσκόπος</note> ⁶⁶ and, in Laconia and Messenia, "of the harbor" <note>original Greek: λιμενῖτις</note> ⁶⁷. She is also named thus in the *Palatine Anthology* ⁶⁸, and therefore we shall assign the <term>cult title</term> "of the harbor" in B 561, E 853 to Artemis, although Hekate was also called "of the harbor" <note>original Greek: λιμενῖτις</note> with regard to the harbor goddess of Munichia, Artemis Munichia ⁶⁹. Another function of Artemis was her care for the female reproductive life, including menstruation, conception, pregnancy, and childbirth ⁷⁰. ...only Hekate can be meant here; she is, as it were, the dead woman among the dead of Hades and Tartarus. <term>Theocritus</term><note>A Greek bucolic poet (c. 300 BC) known for his "Idylls."</note>⁹² and <term>Hippolytus</term><note>Referring here to a text attributed to Hippolytus of Rome, likely the "Refutation of All Heresies."</note>⁹³ describe her wandering among burial mounds and black blood; she rages like a <term>Bacchante</term><note>A female follower of Dionysus, known for ecstatic, wild behavior.</note> among their souls⁹⁴, and even holds her feast in their graves ("having a feast in the graves" <note>original Greek: τάφοις ἔνι δαῖτα ἔχουσα</note> in B 544, E 856/57). There, she devours the corpses of those who died prematurely ("raw-flesh eater" <note>original Greek: ὠμοβόρος</note>, E 867), but she also commits murder herself ("bringer of death" <note>original Greek: θανατηγός</note>, E 865) in order to drink blood ("blood-drinker" <note>original Greek: αἱμοπότις</note>, E 864), for she takes joy in the blood of the slain⁹⁵ and yearns for it⁹⁶. She also devours the flesh of the dead ("flesh-devourer" <note>original Greek: σαρκοβόρα</note>, B 485; σαρκοφάγος, E 866) and eats their hearts ("heart-devourer" <note>original Greek: καρδιόδαιτος</note>, E 866)—she, the "man-eater"⁹⁷, the "all-consuming" ("all-devouring" <note>original Greek: πανφόρβα</note>, D 748), the "generator of destruction" (E 865: φθορογενής), the "destroyer" (E 860: ὀλέτις), the "bringer of ruin" (A 277: οὐλοή), and (therefore) the "terrible one" (B 544, E 856: δασπλῆτις)⁹⁸.

Also the <term>epithets</term><note>Traditional descriptive titles or "Epiklesen" applied to a deity.</note> "the tormentor" (the goddess of torture, E 860), "fierce-eyed" (the one with the terrible gaze, A 277), "the wicked one" (the sacrilegious, A 286), "the dreadful" (B 611), and "the frightening" (B 547)⁹⁹ can only belong to Hekate, not to the gentle <term>Selene</term><note>The personification of the Moon.</note> or the helpful <term>Artemis</term>. In the <term>Orphic Hymns</term><note>A collection of 87 poems to various deities, likely composed in the late Hellenistic or early Roman era.</note> (LXIX 8), the <term>Erinyes</term><note>The Furies, goddesses of vengeance.</note> are called "the fearful daughters of Hades," and the <term>Eumenides</term><note>Another name for the Furies, meaning "the Kindly Ones" (often used euphemistically).</note> (ibid. LXX 8) are called "the terrible-faced ones," but according to A 339, Hekate is so fearful that she strikes terror even into these terrifying demons ("terrifying the Erinyes, monstrous demons"). However, she is also called here "Savior from Terrors" (A 288), for as the most powerful goddess of ghosts, she can naturally also rescue people from all ghostly distress. After all, she is the "commander" (A 275) of the ghostly host—the "Wild Hunt"—that is, the wandering souls of the dead¹⁰⁰. Since these souls were specifically imagined as dogs, Hekate is given the epithet "Mistress of Puppies" (B 530: "ruling over the whelps") or "Leader of the Pack" (D 721/22)¹⁰¹. ### Hekate-Selene-Artemis in the Magical Papyri

She delights in the barking and yapping of dogs, whether they are ghostly apparitions or real dogs. This is also seen in the works of Nonnos <gloss>103</gloss>, for even household dogs—being animals capable of seeing ghosts—greet Hekate and her spectral army of the dead during the night with their howling <gloss>104</gloss> and tremble before the wild goddess <gloss>105</gloss>. She herself was envisioned as howling like a dog <note>original Greek: <term>κυνυλύγματος</term></note>, and indeed, much like the Erinyes <note>The Furies, ancient goddesses of vengeance and retribution</note> themselves, she was portrayed as a "dog-woman" <note>original: <term>κυνώ</term></note> <gloss>106</gloss> or even a black female dog <gloss>107</gloss>. She preferred to be heard when addressed by her <term>epithet</term> <note>a descriptive title or name</note> "Dog" <note>original: <term>κύων</term></note> <gloss>108</gloss>. In keeping with her savage character, she resembled a rabid dog <note>original: <term>λυσσῶσα</term></note> <gloss>109</gloss>.

Furthermore, because the wolf was also considered a ghostly animal, Hekate is called "wolf-woman" <note>original: <term>λυκώ</term></note> in one text and "she-wolf" <note>original: <term>λύκαιna</term></note> in another, while Porphyry <note>A 3rd-century Neoplatonist philosopher</note> <gloss>110</gloss> applies this name to Artemis—or rather, to the hybrid Artemis-Hekate. On a curse tablet <note>A small lead sheet inscribed with a spell to bind or harm an opponent</note> from Crete <gloss>111</gloss>, "She-wolf" even appears as a specific name for a female demon. Another passage contains the words "an ankle in the form of a wolf for you" <note>original: <term>μορφόλυκον σφυρόν σοι</term></note>, providing a further link to Hekate as a wolf. 

She also appeared in other spectral forms, such as with the head of a horse <note>original: <term>ἱπποπρόσωπος θεά</term>, "horse-faced goddess"</note>, which was another animal believed to possess the ability to see spirits <gloss>112</gloss>. One invocation even addresses her as a "horse-dog" <note>original: <term>ἱπποκύων</term></note>! Porphyry further reports <gloss>113</gloss> that Hekate was pleased to be called "Bull" <note>original: <term>ταῦρος</term></note>. Accordingly, she is named "the bull-shaped one" <note>original: <term>ταυρεόμορφος</term></note> in one papyrus, "the bull-headed one" <note>original: <term>ταυροκάρηνος</term></note> in another, and mention is made of her "bull-like eye" <note>original: <term>ὄμμα σοι ταυρωπόν</term></note>, to which the <term>epithets</term> "bull-faced" <note>original: <term>ταυρῶπις</term></note> refer <gloss>114</gloss>.
