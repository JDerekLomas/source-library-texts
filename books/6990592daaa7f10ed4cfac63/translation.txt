# SYNOPSIS
## OF THE ENTIRE WORK.

### *PART I.*

In the first seven Sections, I show when and on what occasion I discovered my Theory, and where I have treated it up to this point in already published Dissertations; what it has in common with the Leibnizian <note>Refers to Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716), who proposed that the universe is composed of "monads" or simple substances.</note> and what with the Newtonian Theory; in what way it differs from both, and indeed even excels both. I add what I have promised elsewhere pertaining to equilibrium and the center of oscillation, and how—now that these have been discovered and flow entirely of their own accord from a single, most simple, and most elegant theorem—the treatment suddenly grew into a complete work of significant size, even though I had initially approached the task intending only a brief little essay.

Then, up to Section 11, I explain the Theory itself: that matter consists of points that are entirely simple, indivisible, non-extended, and distant from one another. Each of these points possesses a force of inertia <term>force of inertia: the inherent property of matter to resist changes in its state of motion</term> and, in addition, a mutual active force depending on their distances. Thus, at a given distance, both the magnitude and direction of the force are given; however, when the distance is changed, the force itself changes. As the distance is infinitely diminished, the force becomes repulsive and indeed increases to infinity. As the distance is increased, the force diminishes, vanishes, and transforms into an attractive force—first increasing, then decreasing, vanishing, and passing again into a repulsive force. This occurs through many cycles until finally, at greater distances, it passes into an attractive force that decreases, for all practical purposes, in the reciprocal duplicate ratio of the distances <note>original: "ratione reciproca duplicata distantiarum"; this is the "inverse square law" used by Newton to describe gravity.</note>. This connection of forces with distances—and indeed their transition from positive to negative, or from repulsive to attractive and vice versa—I place before the very eyes using the force by which the two points of a spring <note>original: "elastri cuspides"</note> strive to approach one another or recede from one another, depending on whether they are stretched or compressed beyond their natural state.

From there to Section 16, I show how this is not some random collection of forces coalescing by chance, but is represented by a single continuous curve by means of abscissas <term>abscissas: the horizontal or x-coordinates on a graph</term> expressing distances and ordinates <term>ordinates: the vertical or y-coordinates on a graph</term> expressing forces. The Theory of mutual forces, which I first happened upon as early as the year 1745 while I was deriving one consequence after another from well-known principles, and from which I deduced the very constitution of the simple elements of matter, presents a system intermediate between that of Leibniz <note>Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716), a German philosopher and mathematician who proposed that matter is composed of "monads."</note> and that of Newton <note>Sir Isaac Newton (1642–1727), whose physics focused on gravitational attraction between masses.</note>. **III.** But this theory differs most widely from the Leibnizian Theory <note>The philosophy of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716), who proposed that the universe is made of "monads."</note>, firstly because it admits no continuous extension arising from contiguous, non-extended parts touching one another. In my judgment, the "principle of indiscernibles" and the "principle of sufficient reason," so highly extolled by the Leibnizians, can prove nothing at all against this.
