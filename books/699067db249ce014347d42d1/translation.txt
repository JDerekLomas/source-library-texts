# Notes of Experiments

**December 10, 1678 to January 15** — Subliming <term>antimony</term> <note>original: "Sb"</note> with <term>sal ammoniac</term> <note>original: "NH4Cl"; ammonium chloride</note> — alloying antimony with lead and other metals — (no definite result of value).

**January 16, 1679/80** — Subliming antimonial <term>sublimate</term> <note>a substance purified by being turned into vapor and then solidified again</note> with lead antimoniate, etc.

**January 22** — The action of <term>nitric acid</term> <note>original: "HNO3"</note> and sal ammoniac on <term>stibnite</term> <note>original: "Sb2S3"; antimony trisulfide</note>, etc., and further sublimations (most of these experiments are roughly quantitative).

**February 16, 1679/80** — Fusing stibnite with <term>vitriol</term> <note>sulfuric acid or a sulfate mineral</note> and other substances — sublimation of various metals with the help of antimony and sal ammoniac, etc. — the action of <term>sulfuric acid</term> <note>original: "H2SO4"</note> on <term>galena</term> <note>original: "PbS"; lead sulfide</note> — the action of nitric acid on the sublimate of antimony — and other such things — (no results of value).

**August 1682** — Similar experiments — some on lead ore — others on an alloy of tin and bismuth (which he seems to call **Diana** <note>original: "Diana"; in alchemy, Diana usually refers to silver, but here the author applies it to a tin-bismuth alloy</note>).

**July 10 (no year)** — "I saw the [unclear] of the Philosophers" <note>original: "vidi * Philosophorum"; likely a reference to a specific alchemical state or "star" of the philosophers' stone</note>. Sublimations of **white calx** <note>original: "calx albus"; a metal oxide or powder produced by heating</note> with sal ammoniac — what "white calx" is is not clear; it seems to be a carbonate as it effervesces with nitric acid, but it is not <term>calcium carbonate</term> <note>original: "CaCO3"; chalk or limestone</note> as the quantity turned into vapor in experiment 5 — on July 27 — is too much.

**April 28, 1686** — On a volatile salt of Zinc (apparently <term>zinc chloride</term> <note>original: "ZnCl2"</note>) and on an alloy derived from the ores of iron, antimony, tin, lead, and bismuth.

**May 16** — on "ve. vo." <note>This likely stands for "venenum volatile" (volatile poison) or "ventus volatilis" (volatile wind), archaic terms for certain gases or spirits</note>.

**March 5, 1690/91 and March 16** — On some bismuth compound and the action of <term>nitric acid</term> <note>original: "aquafortis"</note> on alloys of tin, bismuth, and zinc.

**Experiments and observations, December 1692 and January 1692/3** — the working of <term>yeast</term> <note>original: "barm"</note> — he says "in distilling new wine before fermentation the <term>phlegm</term> <note>original: "flegm"; the watery, non-alcoholic part of a distillate</note> rises first and then the spirit, but after fermentation the spirit rises before the phlegm."

Contains a comparison of the melting points of alloys of lead, tin, and bismuth. — **April 1693 and June 1693** — further experiments.

**April 1695** — Melting stibnite with ores of iron, copper, and tin; and on April 2, the resulting mass was sublimed with sal ammoniac. Other sublimations.

**February 1695/6** — Sublimations of antimony with iron ores.

**No date** — Action of nitric acid on stibnite, etc. Sublimation of an alloy of antimony and lead with sal ammoniac and similar experiments.

Experiments on lead ore and other things.

**December 10, 1678.** Crude, unwashed, and finely powdered <term>antimony</term> <note>original symbol: ♁; a common mineral used in alchemical processes</note> <insert>240 grains</insert> was mixed with <term>sal ammoniac</term> <note>original symbol: ✱; ammonium chloride, used as a flux or to aid sublimation</note> by <term>sublimation</term> <note>The process of heating a substance until it turns into vapor and then solidifies again</note>. This left 130 grains at the bottom. The <term>sublimate</term> <note>The solid material collected after vaporizing and cooling</note> appeared very red.

**December 11.** 180 grains of this sublimate, mixed with 240 grains of powdered **iron ore**, left 250 grains in the bottom of the vessel.

**December 12.** I took 1 part **iron** <note>original symbol: ♂</note> and 2 parts antimony. <term>Joined together</term> <note>original: conjunctim</note>, I used 360 grains of antimony and 240 grains of sal ammoniac, which left 228 grains.

Also, **tin** (one part) and **bismuth** <note>original: tinglass</note> (one part), <insert>melted together</insert>, produced a more easily melted metal than a mixture of one part tin and two parts bismuth.

**December 13.** I put the 228 grains together with 150 grains of fresh melted iron and 525 grains of sal ammoniac; there remained 176 grains. That is, 510 grains of matter and 775 grains of sal ammoniac (a ratio of 3 to 2) left 176 grains (a ratio of 6/17).

**December 14.** The 176 grains, with more sal ammoniac, left 176 grains of <insert>a solution of</insert> the substance.

**December 15.** After I had precipitated what was sublimed and extracted it in a <term>boiling water bath</term> <note>original: balneo aqua ebullientis</note>, I took what I could extract. The extract was <term>purified</term> <note>original: edulcorated; washed of salts</note> and dried, weighing 116 grains. The residue, from which I could extract no more after it was purified and dried, weighed 242 grains (which is twice as much as the extract). The total sum of 358 grains, added to the 176 grains, makes 534. The extract was of a whitish-grey color and would hardly melt in the flame of a candle. The residue was the color of antimony and melted easily upon a hot iron. They both melted and almost entirely evaporated; <note>original: flew almost all away</note> though the residue melted like antimony, crude antimony would not have evaporated in this way.

Also, **bismuth** (2 parts), **iron** (one part), and **lead** <note>original symbol: ♄</note> "impregnated" (about 2 parts) would not melt together, but only made a soft mixture.

Also, crude antimony (3 parts) and impregnated lead (2 parts) melted together with <term>bubbling</term> <note>original: ebullition</note>. When about 1/3 of the lead was put in, the matter bubbled much and was very liquid. After putting in the rest, it grew almost hard while still in the fire. When cold, it was a hard, brittle powder. Upon a hot iron, they both melted and almost entirely evaporated, which crude antimony would not do.

**December 16.** 127 grains of impregnated lead and the precipitate and extract together (amounting to 254 grains) were well mixed and melted together at a <insert>dark</insert> red heat. This yielded a brittle mixture of about 310 grains. Of this, 120 grains were carried up <note>vaporized</note> with twice its weight of sal ammoniac, leaving 45 grains in the bottom.

January 16, 1679/80: I took 160 grains of sublimated salt <note>original: sal sublimate; likely corrosive sublimate or mercuric chloride</note> (which perhaps, had it been well dried, would not have weighed more than 120 or 140 grains) and 100 grains of antimony <note>original: ♁, the alchemical symbol for antimony or stibium</note> filings <note>original: fial; likely a transcription of "fil." for filings</note> (which, if well dried, might perhaps have weighed 95 grains). These I sublimed <note>Sublimation is the process of heating a substance until it turns into a gas and then solidifies again on a cooler surface, used here for purification.</note>, and after sublimation there remained 75 or 80 grains in the bottom. Some of this residue <insert>caput mortuum</insert> <note>original: caput mort.; literally "dead head," the useless dregs or solid remains left after distillation or sublimation</note> laid on a red-hot iron flowed like wax and evaporated completely. The sublimate was of a dirty color. The residue <note>original: caput mort.</note> tasted sweet.

I added the sublimate to the residue <note>original: caput mort.</note> and sublimed it again, and there remained 20 grains in the bottom. But the glass cracked, and some of the matter, while in fusion <note>liquid, melted state</note>, ran out through the crack and made the sand stick to the glass. Some of this residue (about <insert>20 grains</insert>), <gloss>which flowed like wax and</gloss> also tasted sweet, mostly flew away <note>evaporated/sublimed</note> when laid on a red-hot iron. The sublimate was dirty as before.

Then I sublimed 40 grains of sal ammoniac <note>original: sal Arm.; ammonium chloride</note> and 120 grains of the first simple antimony <note>original: Fial</note> sublimate. There remained 23 ½ grains in the bottom, and the sublimate was now very white. When put in water, it almost all dissolved without leaving <insert>much</insert> precipitate <note>solid matter that settles at the bottom of a liquid</note> in the bottom, as it had done before this sublimation from iron <note>original: ♂, the alchemical symbol for Mars/iron</note>, where I knew this sublimate was much altered by the spirit of iron <note>original: spt of ♂; likely an acidic vapor or essence derived from iron</note>. I dissolved the residue <note>original: caput mort.</note> in water and there remained 5 grains of sediment; the extracted salt, when dried again, weighed 26 grains. From this, I conclude that about 14 or 15 grains of the iron <note>original: ♂</note> was carried up—that is, 3/8 of the whole. Before I dissolved the residue <note>original: caput mort.</note> in water, I put a little of it on a red-hot plate, and it neither flowed nor hardly fumed at all.

Of this sublimate, I sublimed 80 grains from 40 grains of antimoniate, and the sublimate arose white again.

# On Composing and Casting the Metal for Making a Mirror
<note>original Latin: *De metallo ad conficiendum speculum componendo & fundendo*</note>

Take 11 or 12 parts of copper, 4 parts of the best tin, and 1 part of white arsenic. Once the copper has been melted, throw in the arsenic and mix them well by stirring with a wooden stick. Then, throw in the tin (which has been divided into pieces) and stir the mass again quickly; once everything is well mixed, it should be poured into the mold without delay.

**Note 1:** Tin melts very quickly and will later make the mass porous through its fumes if it remains over the fire too long or is heated too intensely.

**Note 2:** Therefore, if the mass is to be melted again, it should not be heated any more than is necessary for liquefaction.

**Note 3:** Before it is mixed with the tin, the copper can be purified by melting it and throwing into 12 ounces of <gloss>melted</gloss> copper, first, one ounce of arsenic and two <gloss>or three ounces</gloss> of crude antimony <gloss>by itself or</gloss> then <gloss>three or</gloss> four <gloss>up to six</gloss> ounces of saltpeter <note>potassium nitrate</note> in stages until all the <gloss>salt</gloss> has burned away. Then, once the mass has cooled, the salty slag should be thrown away, and the metal should be melted again. Add two or three ounces of new saltpeter in stages until it has all burned away as before. It will be sufficiently purified if the salt floating on top comes out white after cooling; if not, it must be melted a third time with salt, if an even higher purity is desired. But meanwhile, one must take the utmost care to avoid the arsenic fumes.

From copper purified in this way, a metal can be composed with arsenic and tin as described above. A sign of the best composition is that the metal appears smooth like glass where it is broken.

**Note 4:** If the metal, after being poured into the mold, shatters into fragments of its own accord while cooling, it indicates that there is too much tin for the quantity of copper.
