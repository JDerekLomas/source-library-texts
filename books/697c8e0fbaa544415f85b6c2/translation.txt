# Preface.

---

Everything passes away; everything here below has an end; God alone is eternal and unchanging. Principalities, kingdoms, and great fortunes—all are subject to change. Yet, commonly, these changes occur only imperceptibly. One sees them in advance from the incidents that announce them and which precede them. Never has one occurred so swiftly and so suddenly as the fall of the Order of the Knights Templar <term>Tempelherren-Orden: A wealthy and powerful medieval Catholic military order suppressed in the early 14th century</term>. In a single day, in the same hour, they were destroyed; and this was a tragic proof of the terrible consequences that the hatred and wrath of kings can bring about. They—these likenesses of the Most High Lord <note>A reference to the "Divine Right of Kings," the belief that monarchs derive their authority directly from God</note>—are sometimes, like Him, creators and destroyers on earth.

Such was the fate of the Order of the Knights Templar, the history of whose dissolution we now undertake to write.

## Preface.

Nothing in the world at the beginning of the fourteenth century was more brilliant than this famous Order, which had spread throughout all Christian kingdoms. It had reached the pinnacle of its greatness at that time, and this was also the very moment in which it vanished.

It had already taken its beginning in the eleventh century. The frequent pilgrimages <term>Wallfahrten: Religious journeys to sacred sites, a central practice of medieval Christian life</term>, which the faithful undertook driven by their piety to visit the Holy Places where the great mystery of our redemption was completed <note>Refers to the locations in Jerusalem associated with the life and death of Jesus Christ</note>, inspired the zeal of a few private individuals. They joined together to make the paths there free and to shield these devout travelers from the dangers to which they were exposed.

Following this, the Crusades <term>Kreuzzüge: A series of religious wars sanctioned by the Church to recover the Holy Land from Islamic rule</term> occurred and were accompanied by glorious conquests. As the number of pilgrims doubled because of these victories, the assistance of these brave defenders became all the more necessary. These were for the most part Frenchmen; initially, there were only eight of them. They knew neither avarice nor ambition, and they renewed the conduct and unity of the first Christians, who had but one heart and one soul <note>original: "ein Herz und eine Seele"; a biblical reference to the communal life of the early church in Acts 4:32</note>.

Hugh of Payens was the first Grand Master of the Order; his successors were just as brave as he, and were much more skillful.

Their fame spread throughout all of Europe. They distinguished themselves in Palestine through the greatest deeds. Nothing was spoken of but their courage, their fearlessness, and their virtues.

The younger sons of the greatest houses soon joined this Order with eagerness. All Christian princes vied with each other to give them gifts. King Baldwin IV <note>The "Leper King" of Jerusalem, who reigned from 1174 to 1185</note> gave them the city of Gaza, which became the headquarters of the Order. They were given estates, fiefs, and even cities with all their revenues in almost all realms.
