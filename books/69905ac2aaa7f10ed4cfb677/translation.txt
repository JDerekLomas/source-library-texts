# ADVERTISEMENT.

Part of the following discourse about light was written at the request of some gentlemen of the Royal Society in the year 1675, and then sent to their Secretary and read at their meetings. The rest was added about twelve years later to complete the first and second books. The third book and the last part of the second were put together from scattered papers some years later. 

To avoid being entangled in disputes about these matters, I have delayed the printing until now, and I would have continued to delay it if the persistent requests <note>original: "importunity"</note> of friends had not prevailed upon me. If any more papers on this subject come to my hand, they are imperfect and were written many years ago. 

Two treatises on the types <note>original: "species"</note> and magnitude of curvilinear figures <note>A mathematical study of shapes with curved lines, such as circles or ellipses.</note> are added so that it may not be thought I used another person's analysis <note>Newton is likely defending his independent invention of calculus here.</note>. In another place, I have endeavored to give an account of the Sun and Moon, but for want of different observations, those matters must be further examined. The subject of the third book is less finished than the rest.

->PART I.<-

**M**y design in this book is not to explain the properties of light by hypotheses <note>original: "Hypotheses"; here meaning unproven theories or speculative assumptions</note>, but to propose and prove them by reason and experiments. In order to achieve this, I shall first set forth the following definitions and axioms.

## DEFINITIONS.

### DEFINITION I.

**B**y the rays of light, I mean its smallest parts, and those *which are both successive in the same lines and simultaneous in different lines*. For it is obvious that light consists of parts that are both successive and simultaneous; because in the same location, you may block the light that arrives at one moment and allow the light that arrives immediately after to pass. Likewise, at the same moment in time, you may block it in one place and let it pass in another. For that part of light which is blocked cannot be the same as that which is allowed to pass. ## DEFINITION II.

***Refrangibility** <note>original: "Refrangibility"; the ability of light to be bent or refracted.</note> of the Rays of Light, is their disposition to be refracted or turned out of their way in passing out of one transparent body or **medium** <note>A substance, such as glass, water, or air, through which light travels.</note> into another. And a greater or less refrangibility of rays, is their disposition to be turned more or less out of their way during similar **incidences** <note>The angle at which a ray of light strikes a surface.</note> on the same medium.* Mathematicians usually consider the rays of light to be lines reaching from the luminous body to the body illuminated, and the refraction of those rays to be the bending or breaking of those lines in their passing out of one medium into another. And rays and refractions may be considered in this way, if light is propagated in an instant. But by an argument taken from the **equations of the times of the eclipses of Jupiter's satellites** <note>Newton refers to Ole Rømer’s 1676 discovery that the timing of Jupiter's moons' eclipses varied depending on Earth's distance from Jupiter, proving light has a finite speed.</note> it seems that light is propagated over time, spending in its passage from the Sun to us about seven minutes of time. And therefore I have chosen to define rays and refractions in such general terms as may agree to light in both cases.

## DEFINITION III.

***Reflexibility** of rays, is their disposition to be turned back into the same medium from any other medium upon whose surface they fall. And rays are more or less reflexible, which are returned back more or less easily.* As if light passes out of glass into air, and by being inclined more and more to the common surface of the glass and air, begins at length to be totally reflected by that surface; those sorts of rays which at similar incidences are reflected most copiously, or by inclining the rays begin soonest to be totally reflected, are most reflexible.

### DEFINITION IV.

*The **Angle of Incidence** is that angle formed between the line described by the incoming <note>original: "incident"</note> ray and the perpendicular line <note>A line at a 90-degree angle to the surface, often called the "normal."</note> to the reflecting or refracting surface at the point where the light hits.*

### DEFINITION V.

*The **Angle of Reflection or Refraction** is the angle formed between the line described by the reflected or refracted ray and the perpendicular line to the reflecting or refracting surface at the point where the light hits.*

### DEFINITION VI.

*The **Sines of Incidence, Reflection, and Refraction** are the sines <note>A trigonometric function used to calculate the ratios of the sides of a right-angled triangle.</note> of the angles of incidence, reflection, and refraction.*

### DEFINITION VII.

*Light whose rays are all equally capable of being refracted <note>original: "Refrangible"</note>, I call **Simple, Homogeneous, and Similar**; and light whose rays are some more capable of being refracted than others, I call **Compound, Heterogeneous, and Dissimilar**. I call the former light "homogeneous" not because I claim it is identical in every single respect, but because the rays which share the same degree of refractability <note>original: "Refrangibility"</note> also share all those other properties which I consider in the following discussion.*

### DEFINITION VIII.

*The colors of homogeneous lights I call **Primary, Homogeneous, and Simple**; and those of heterogeneous lights I call **Heterogeneous and Compound**. For these compound colors are always composed of the colors of homogeneous lights, as will be demonstrated in the following discussion.*

# AXIOMS

## AXIOM I.

The *angles of incidence, reflection, and refraction lie in one and the same plane.* <note>In other words, the incoming ray, the reflected/refracted ray, and the line perpendicular to the surface all exist on the same flat geometric surface.</note>

## AXIOM II.

*The angle of reflection is equal to the angle of incidence.*

## AXIOM III.

*If the refracted ray is returned directly back to the point of incidence, it shall be refracted into the line previously described by the incident ray.* <note>This describes the principle of reversibility: light traveling backward follows the exact same path it took traveling forward.</note>

## AXIOM IV.

*Refraction out of a rarer medium <note>original: "rarer Medium"; a substance with lower density, like air</note> into a denser medium <note>a substance with higher density, like water or glass</note> is made toward the perpendicular; that is, so that the angle of refraction is less than the angle of incidence.*

## AXIOM V.

*The sine of incidence is either accurately or very nearly in a given ratio to the sine of refraction.* <note>This is a statement of Snell's Law, establishing that the ratio between these two trigonometric values remains constant for specific materials.</note>

From this, if that proportion is known for any one inclination of the incident ray, it is known for all inclinations. Thereby, the refraction in all cases of incidence on the same refracting body may be determined.
