# PREFACE

From the standpoint of both religion and culture, Animism <note>The belief that natural objects, places, and creatures possess a distinct spiritual essence.</note> has been described as “the taproot that sinks deepest into human experience and continues its cellular and fibrous structure into the tree trunk of modern conviction.” All the great world religions show traces of animism in their foundations, and none but Christianity (and even that not completely) has uprooted the overgrowth of superstition. In this book, our purpose is to show how Islam sprang up from pagan soil and retained many old Arabian beliefs despite its vigorous monotheism <note>The belief that there is only one God.</note>. Wherever Islam <note>original: "Mohammedanism"</note> went, it either introduced old superstitions or adopted new ones.

The result has been that as a background to the whole ritual, and even within the creed of popular Islam, Animism has triumphed. The religion of the common people from Tangier to Tehran is mixed with hundreds of superstitions, many of which have lost their original significance but still bind the mind and heart with a constant fear of demons, with witchcraft and sorcery, and the call to worship created things. Just as popular Hinduism differs completely <note>original: "in toto"</note> from the religion of the Vedas <note>The oldest and most sacred scriptures of Hinduism.</note>, popular Islam is altogether different from the religion as recorded in its sacred Book [the Quran].

Our purpose in the chapters that follow is to show how this "miry clay" of animism mingles with the "iron" of Semitic theism in the feet of the great image with the head of gold that rests on Asia and Africa <note>This is a reference to the biblical Book of Daniel (Chapter 2), which describes a massive statue made of various metals and clay, representing world empires.</note>. We believe the rapid spread of Islam in Africa and Malaysia <note>original: "Malayia"</note> is largely due to its animistic character. We may connect with this the Muslim belief that bones are the food of *jinn* <term>jinn: supernatural spirits in Islamic mythology that can be good or evil</term> and must not be touched. In Chapter VI of his book (page 157), Mr. Kruijt shows that "soul-substance" <note>original: "soul-stuff"; a term used in anthropology to describe a vital essence or life force believed to reside in people, animals, and objects</note> exists in certain metals, such as iron, gold, silver, and lead. These are therefore considered powerful protectors against evil spirits. For example, iron objects are used to defend infants in the cradle (page 161). The same practice is carried out in Arabia, Egypt, Persia, and Morocco.

The soul, after death, is believed to take flight into the animal kingdom (pages 171–180), especially changing its form to live in butterflies, birds, mice, lizards, and snakes. May we not connect this with the Islamic teaching that the souls of Muslim martyrs enter the bodies <note>original: "the crops"</note> of green birds until the Day of Resurrection? Or closer yet is the common belief in reincarnation <term>metempsychosis: the passage of a soul after death into a new body</term> based upon legends from the Quran <note>original: "Koran"</note>, which were further developed in later commentaries. Does the Quran not teach that certain Jews were changed into apes, and does the Hadith <term>Tradition: the recorded sayings and actions of the Prophet Muhammad</term> tell us that Jews and Christians were changed into hogs?

When we read the pages of Kruijt on the fetish <term>fetish: an object believed to have supernatural powers or to house a spirit</term> (pages 197–232), we are struck in almost every paragraph by the parallels to beliefs current in Islam. Stones are considered sacred because they contain spirits. Trees are sacred for the same reason: "If a man has been successful in fighting, it has not been his natural strength of arm, quickness of eye, or readiness of resource that has won success; he has certainly obtained the *mana* <term>mana: a pervasive supernatural power that can be concentrated in objects or persons</term> of a spirit or of some deceased warrior to empower him. This is conveyed in a stone amulet around his neck, a tuft of leaves in his belt, a tooth hung upon a finger of his hand holding the bow, or in the form of words with which he brings supernatural assistance to his side" (page 201). Word for word, this might be said of many Muslims today.

With regard to stone-worship, Kruijt tells us of sacred stones in the Indonesian islands <note>original: "Indian Archipelago"</note> (pages 204–210) which receive worship because they fell from heaven (compare this to "The Black [Stone of Mecca]...")
