# ANGELO POLIZIANO
## TO NICCOLÒ LEONICENO
### GREETINGS.

<margin>
More danger from the doctor than the disease.
</margin>

<image-desc>Decorative woodcut initial 'D' showing a landscape with a castle tower and a figure in the foreground.</image-desc>
Those lectures of yours, Niccolò, in which you refute the ignorance of Avicenna <note>Avicenna (Ibn Sina) was a Persian physician whose "Canon of Medicine" was the standard medieval textbook, though Renaissance humanists like Leoniceno began to challenge his translations and interpretations</note> and teach the younger physicians in what great darkness of things they are involved, I do not know whether they have brought me more pleasure or pain. For I have rejoiced wonderfully that those matters, upon which the life and health of men depend, have been brought into the light by that man above all whom I have always valued most highly for his talent and learning. And yet, I have grieved in turn for the sake of the human race, because it allows this dismal ignorance to rage against itself for so long with impunity, and sometimes purchases the hope of life at a price from those very sources whence the most certain death proceeds. For who does not see that there is more danger from the doctor than from the disease, since one illness is treated in place of another, and the wrong remedies are applied for the wrong conditions? 

But if that ancient age had produced you, Niccolò—the age in which rewards were rendered according to merit—you would not have lacked even the honors of the gods; unless, perhaps, we believe it is a greater thing to snatch one or two people from death, as Aesculapius <note>The Greek god of medicine and healing</note> did, than to rescue all men alike from the very danger of death, as you yourself are doing. Therefore, I beseech you, continue on the path you have begun: namely, so that by this one labor you may both produce immortal glory for yourself and provide life and health to all those who have truly been "sickly mortals" <note>original: "mortalibus ægris," a Virgilian poetic phrase for humanity</note> until now. 

However, as to the fact that you also refute our Pliny <note>Pliny the Elder, author of the "Natural History"</note>, as if he had mistaken the rock-rose <note>original: "cisthon"</note> for ivy, in this matter—to speak freely and as a friend—I disagree with you entirely. Nor shall I fear to boldly undertake the defense of that man who deserved so excellently of life and letters, especially against you—that is, against an eminent philosopher, to whom nothing ought to be more ancient <note>meaning "more important" or "more venerable"</note> than the truth itself. For those [arguments] are from your scho—

# ANGELO POLIZIANO TO NICCOLÒ LEONICENO

Socrates is a friend, but truth is a greater friend; and while both are dear, more honor must be given to the truth. <note>A variation of the famous Aristotelian maxim "Amicus Plato, sed magis amica veritas."</note> You claim, then, that Pliny—just like Avicenna <note>Ibn Sina (c. 980–1037), the Persian physician whose medical texts were central to Medieval and Renaissance learning.</note>—does not distinguish the *cisthos* <note>The Rock-rose (Cistus).</note> from the *hedera* <note>Ivy (Hedera).</note>; if this were so, it would be a grave error, since the rock-rose differs greatly from ivy in shape, color, and medicinal properties. You add that this is more surprising in Pliny than in Avicenna, because Pliny, being skilled in the Greek language, ought to have known how to separate *cisthos* from *cissos* (which is ivy). You bring forward an argument as to why he does not separate one from the other: because in Book 16, Chapter 34 of his *Natural History*, he divides ivy into male and female, and says the flower of both is similar to the wild rose. Then, in Book 24, he also divides that *cisthos* (under which the *hypocisthis* <note>A parasitic plant that grows on the roots of the rock-rose.</note> grows) into male and female, attributing a rose-colored flower to the male and a white flower to the female. Finally, you note that he even claims to have mentioned this *cisthos* among the ivies. Therefore, you seem to conclude thus: Since Pliny teaches in Book 24 that he made mention of the *cisthos* among the ivies, yet the word itself is nowhere cited previously among the ivies, it is an easy conjecture that the *cisthos* was included by him under the name of "ivy"—especially since he divides ivy into male and female just as he does the *cisthos*, and describes a flower among the ivies. Now that I have set forth your view, I shall explain my opposing opinion. I deny that mention of the *cisthos* was made by Pliny anywhere among the ivies, except in Book 24, where he clearly distinguished it from ivy. For as to what you take as Pliny's testimony—that he spoke elsewhere of the *cisthos* among the ivies—I do not concede this at all. For to me, where he says this (namely in Book 24), he seems to be discussing the *cisthos* only up to that specific clause, which in his text reads:

<margin>,,</margin> Under these especially grows the *hypocisthis*. But immediately after
<margin>,,</margin> that, there is a punctuation mark, and the words begin anew thus: That
<margin>,,</margin> which we mentioned among the ivies, called *cissos erithranos* <note>original: "cissos erithranos" — likely a corruption of the Greek 'cissos erythranos' or red-berried ivy.</note> by those same people...

# THE WORKS OF ANGELO POLIZIANO
<note>Poliziano (1454–1494) was a leading Italian humanist scholar known for his rigorous correction of classical texts.</note>

...is like ivy. Given your own elegance in literature, I do not fear that you will fail to recognize the figure of speech, such as that found in Ovid:
> The letter which you read comes from the abducted Briseis. <note>original: "Quam legis à rapta Briseide litera venit." From Ovid's Heroides 3.1. Poliziano is discussing a grammatical construction where "quam" (which) refers to a noun following it.</note> And again:
> That health which she herself will lack unless you give it, 
> the Cretan girl sends to the Amazonian hero. <note>original: "Quam nisi tu dederis, caritura est ipsa salutem, Mittit amazonio cressa puella viro." From Ovid's Heroides 4.1-2 (Phaedra to Hippolytus).</note>

But if you prefer to refer that "which" to what precedes it, you would be forced to refer it to the *hypocisthis* <term>hypocisthis: a parasitic plant (Cytinus hypocistis) that grows on the roots of the rock-rose</term>, for this stands earlier in the text, rather than to the *cisthos* <term>cisthos: the rock-rose (Cistus)</term>. Yet he [Pliny] is entirely silent regarding the *hypocisthis* earlier—that is, in Book 16 among the ivies—even though he speaks there of the *erithranos* <note>A type of ivy mentioned by Pliny.</note>. Furthermore, he never mentions *cisthos* anywhere else among the ivies, nor does the distinction between male and female seem as specific to *cisthos* as it is to nearly all trees and shrubs—a fact which Pliny himself does not hide. What of the fact that the flower is also described differently? In ivy, the flower of the male and female is the same color, but in *cisthos* they are clearly of different colors. 

As for his comparison of the ivy-like flower to the wild rose, I think he is suggesting not so much the color, but rather the downy fuzz <note>original: "lanuginem"</note> such as is found inside roses; this is why Theophrastus <note>Ancient Greek philosopher and the "father of botany."</note> called the ivy flower *chuoedes* <note>original: "χυοώδη" (chuoōdē), meaning downy or woolly.</note>. Therefore, I believe it is sufficiently clear that there is nothing forcing us to admit that Pliny, in Book 16, included the *cisthos* among the ivies. However, in Book 24, he did link *cisthos* to ivy, just as he did with *chamaecissos* <term>chamaecissos: ground-ivy</term>—not because he believed *cisthos* and ivy were the same thing, but because the Greeks call it by a "neighboring name" (for those are his words). 

Lest anyone be deceived by the similarity of the name, he applied a distinction most carefully in the very place where the occasion for confusion arose. Not content with that, he also added properties of the *cisthos* that are entirely different from ivy. For nowhere previously did he describe ivy as being larger than thyme, or having leaves like basil, or finally, having a white flower. Therefore, who can now doubt that we must rule in favor of Pliny? Not even a suspicion of that error should touch him. I confess I wonder in passing how it came into your mind...

# LETTER TO NICCOLÒ LEONICENO

...it would be a mistake to consider the plant *ledon*—or if you prefer, *lada*, from which *ladanum* <note>A sticky resin obtained from Cistus shrubs, used in ancient medicine and perfumes</note> is named—to be a purely Latin word. It is as if Dioscorides <note>Pedanius Dioscorides, a Greek physician and botanist of the 1st century AD</note> and other ancient Greeks did not use both *leda* and *ladanum* everywhere as native and vernacular terms. 

These are the points I had to raise to you, my dear Niccolò, in defense of Pliny. Whether they are true or false I do not know, but they seem likely enough to me. If these arguments are approved by a man as learned as yourself, I shall truly rejoice that a Latin author is not in the same state of risk as the barbarians <note>A reference to non-classical or medieval scholars whose Latinity was considered inferior by Renaissance humanists</note>. If not, I await your response to these modest thoughts of mine. Indeed, since Pliny’s authority is held in the highest regard among the most educated men,

->his reputation should either not have been challenged at all,<-
->or it should have been shaken somewhat more forcefully<-
->than you certainly seem to have done so far.<-
->Farewell. At Florence, the 3rd day<-
->of January, in the year<-
->1491.<-
