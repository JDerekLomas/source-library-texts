# Preface to Master Hua’s <term>Classic of the Central Treasury<note>original: "華氏中藏經" (Huáshì Zhōngzàng Jīng). This text is traditionally attributed to the Han dynasty physician Hua Tuo, though scholars suggest it was compiled later. The "Central Treasury" refers to the internal organs of the human body.</note></term>

->Written by Deng Chuzhong of Mount Shaoshi, the <term>Perfected One<note>A title for a Daoist adept or immortal who has achieved a high state of spiritual realization.</note></term> of Penetrating Subtleties and Master of Yingling Grotto<-

Master Hua, whose taboo name was Tuo and courtesy name was Yuanhua, was by nature tranquil and detached from worldly ambitions. He delighted in the study of <term>medical recipes<note>original: "方書" (fāngshū). Specifically, books containing medicinal formulas and occult or technical methods.</note></term> and frequently wandered through famous mountains and secluded caves, where he often experienced remarkable encounters.

One day, while resting after drinking wine in front of an ancient grotto on Mount Gongyi, he suddenly heard voices discussing methods for curing diseases. Surprised by this strange occurrence, the Master crept closer to the cave to listen in secret. After a short while, someone inside said, “Master Hua is close by; these arts could be entrusted to him.” 

But another responded, “Tuo is greedy by nature and has no pity for living creatures. How could we possibly grant them to him?” 

Hearing this, the Master was even more shocked. He leaped into the cave and saw two elderly men dressed in bark with crowns made of grass. The Master bowed deeply before them and said, “I happened to hear you wise ones discussing the medical arts, and so I forgot to return home. Moreover, the path of saving others is what I have always cherished; my only regret is that I have not yet encountered <insert>the true way</insert>.”

# Preface to the Classic of the Central Treasury

The words people had spoken previously were thus proven true. I am the Master’s <term>maternal grandson<note>original: "外孫" (wàisūn). While literally meaning maternal grandson, this identifies the narrator as a direct family descendant authorized to handle the Master’s legacy.</note></term>. While I was mourning in the Master’s private chamber, I dreamed that the Master led me to a seat and spoke to me, saying: "The *Classic of the Central Treasury* is a true <term>method for saving lives<note>original: "活人法" (huórén fǎ). In traditional Chinese medicine, this refers to a set of clinical principles or formulas so powerful they are said to restore life to the dying.</note></term>. You may take it, but do not pass it on to those who are unworthy."

I awoke feeling startled and unsettled. I then searched through the Master’s old belongings and discovered a stone casket. Upon opening it, I found a bundle of books; it was indeed the *Classic of the Central Treasury*. Because my own nature is clumsy in practicing these arts, I have passed them on to my successor <note>original: "次予思" (cì yǔ sī). This refers to the next person in the line of transmission; the narrator acknowledges their own limitations and justifies why the book is being shared or published now.</note> and have recorded these facts here to attest to its authenticity.
