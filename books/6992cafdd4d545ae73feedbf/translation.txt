# Methods for Painting Willows

There are four methods for painting the willow tree. The first is <term>outline and fill</term> <note>original: "鉤勒" (goule); a technique where the form is first outlined with fine ink lines and then filled with color</note> using green. The second is to simply wash the forms out with <term>sap green</term> <note>original: "汁綠" (zhilyu); a transparent vegetable-based green pigment</note>; for new shoots, use a tender yellow, and for the lower leaves, use a mature "old" green to distinguish between light and shade. The third method is to add dark green over green dots, then add a few light, small ink dots, and finally cover it with <term>mineral green</term> <note>original: "石綠" (shilyu); an opaque malachite-based pigment</note>, leaving the edges visible. The fourth is to use fine "ink silk" lines and then dot with concentrated green before washing over them. 

Generally speaking, artists of the **Tang Dynasty** mostly used the outline method. Artists of the **Song Dynasty** favored dotting the leaves. Those of the **Yuan Dynasty** preferred the washing and soaking techniques. To master the willow, one must ensure the branching flows with momentum, capturing the elegant spirit of the tree swaying and dancing in the wind. This is the primary goal. 

Furthermore, one must distinguish between the seasons: in the second month of spring, the willow’s branches have not yet begun to droop; in the ninth month of autumn, the tree is already sparse and withered. These two must never be confused. 

The willow among trees is like the famous beauties **Xi Shi** and **Mao Qiang** <note>two of the most legendary beauties of ancient China</note> among people, or like the **Nymph of the Luo River** <note>original: "宓妃" (Mifei); a goddess celebrated in classical poetry for her grace</note> and **Liezi** <note>a Daoist philosopher said to have the power to ride the wind</note> among the immortals. Its posture—gliding over waves and riding the wind, shimmering at the edge of the woods—is an indispensable element of a landscape. Therefore, **Zhao Qianli** <note>Zhao Boju, a Southern Song court painter</note> and **Zhao Songxue** <note>Zhao Mengfu (1254–1322), the influential Yuan Dynasty master</note> painted them often. In his painting *Water Village*, **Songxue** used varying shades of ink to simply "wipe" the forms, creating a sense of infinite, quiet elegance. This represents yet another distinct method.

### Shadow Willow
<note>original: "影柳" (yingliu); a style used to depict the delicate, misty appearance of willows during seasonal transitions.</note>

At the end of autumn or the beginning of spring, one should paint the **shadow willow** amidst bamboo fences and thatched cottages. They appear like the freshly trimmed bangs on a beautiful woman's forehead—a grace and beauty unsurpassed in all the world. When painting the early spring, one may occasionally intersperse the branches with peach blossoms.

The method: Use light ink and a large brush to paint the main trunks. Then, using ink of varying depths, paint the supple branches in a clear green. If painting on silk, use <term>malachite green</term> <note>original: 石綠 (shilü); a bright green mineral pigment</note> to "back-color" the silk <note>Back-coloring (chenbei) involves applying pigment to the reverse side of the silk to create a subtle, luminous depth on the front.</note>. For winter scenes or the end of autumn, simply use <term>ochre</term> <note>original: 赭石 (zheshi); a reddish-brown earth pigment</note> interspersed with green to break up the tones.

***

### Hook-leaf Willow
<note>original: "鈎葉柳" (gouye liu); a more formal technique where each leaf is individually outlined, or "hooked," with a fine brush.</note>

**Wang Wei** <note>699–759; a legendary Tang Dynasty poet and painter often considered the father of literati painting</note> and various other Tang Dynasty artists, as well as **Chen Juzhong** <note>a celebrated Southern Song Dynasty court painter active in the early 13th century</note>, frequently painted in this style. Personally, I find it a bit too rigid; therefore, I have placed it here at the end simply to ensure this specific style is included for the sake of completeness.

### Technique for Dotting Flowers and Tree Trunks
<note>original: 點花樹幹法 (dian hua shugan fa)</note>

There are significant distinctions [between species] that must be observed. The **peach** tree <term>tao</term> must not be rendered in the same manner as the **plum** <term>mei</term>. Likewise, the **apricot** <term>xing</term> and the plum must not be confused with other types of trees. 

Generally, **plum** branches are mostly straight, yet they possess a horizontal vigor <note>referring to 'horizontal strength' (hengjin), a quality of gnarled power in the lateral branches</note>. As for the **apricot**, the ancient masters sometimes only depicted horizontal stumps with dotting. The **peach**, however, is best suited to a profusion of branches <note>meaning the peach tree should appear denser and more complex in its skeletal structure than the sparse, elegant plum</note>.

### Technique for Painting Small Bamboo
<note>original: 畫小竹法 (hua xiao zhu fa)</note>

The master **Ni Zan** <note>original: 雲林 (Yunlin); the art name of Ni Zan (1301–1374), one of the Four Masters of the Yuan Dynasty, famous for his minimalist and "bland" style</note> frequently placed secluded bamboo thickets <term>youhuang</term> <note>original: 幽篁; a poetic term for a dense, quiet grove of bamboo</note> at the base of rocks or beneath trees. There, supple branches <term>rou tiao</term> catch the glow of the late sunset, glimpsed through the flowered windows of a thatched cottage. They rustle with a soughing sound; one looks upon them and recognizes the path of a recluse <note>original: 幽人行徑 (youren xingjing); implying that the presence of such bamboo signals a place where a scholar-hermit lives in harmony with nature</note>.

The artist must capture an atmosphere of "pillowing on the wind and housing the moon"—a refined and effortless elegance <note>original: 枕風簃月清逸之致; "Zhen Feng" (pillowing on wind) and "Yi Yue" (housing the moon) are metaphors for a life of reclusive, poetic leisure</note>. One must avoid a cluttered or messy composition, as this would stifle the **clear spirit** <term>qingqi</term> <note>original: 清氣; the vital, pure energy that gives a painting its spiritual resonance and prevents it from feeling "vulgar" or "heavy"</note>. There are three distinct ways to paint [small bamboo]; the artist should observe the physical structure of the surrounding trees and rocks, and then match the thickness of the brushstrokes to harmonize with the overall composition.

When the Tang Dynasty artists painted trees using the **double-outline** technique <term>shuanggou</term> <note>original: 雙鉤; a method where the form is outlined with two thin lines and then filled with color or washes, rather than being formed by a single calligraphic stroke</note>, their elegant dotting and embellishments often featured **flying white** <term>feibai</term> <note>original: 飛白; a calligraphic brushstroke where the brush is slightly dry, allowing the white of the paper to peek through the hair of the brush, suggesting texture and light</note>. This creates a very charming and spirited effect. Recently, **Qiu Ying** <note>original: 仇十洲 (Qiu Shizhou); c. 1494–1552, a celebrated Ming Dynasty painter known for his refined and meticulous 'blue-and-green' style</note> has also favored this method.

### Technique for Painting Reeds and Marsh Grass
<note>original: 畫葭荻法 (hua jiadi fa); 'Jia' and 'di' refer to different types of reeds and rushes commonly found along riverbanks in Southern China.</note>

Famous masters of the Song Dynasty, such as **Juran**, **Li Cheng**, and **Fan Kuan** <note>original: 巨然, 李成, 范寬; three of the most influential landscape architects of the 10th and 11th centuries</note>, all produced paintings on the theme of "Fishing Pleasures" <term>yule tu</term>. This tradition originated with the "Fisherman of the Misty Waves," **Zhang Zhihe** <note>original: 煙波釣徒張志和; a Tang Dynasty poet-recluse who lived on a boat and wrote famous lyrics about the carefree life of a fisherman</note>. It is said that **Yan Zhenqing** <note>original: 顏魯公 (Yan Lugong); a famous Tang statesman and calligrapher</note> presented a poem to Zhang, who then painted a scene to accompany it. This was considered a celebrated event of the Tang Dynasty.

Later generations followed this example, using the "fishing recluse" <term>yuyin</term> theme to express their own desire for a life away from official duties. This was especially common during the late Yuan Dynasty, as the **Four Masters** <note>original: 元季四大家; referring to Huang Gongwang, Wu Zhen, Ni Zan, and Wang Meng</note> all lived in the **Jiangnan** <note>the region south of the lower reaches of the Yangtze River</note> region. Growing up among the reeds and marsh grass, they were intimately familiar with the true spirit of the fishing life.

In most other types of landscapes, there must be a "primary tree" to anchor the composition. However, in "Fishing Pleasures" scenes, the misty waters are vast and boundless; a tree cannot serve as the primary focus. Instead, the **reeds and marsh grass** become the main subjects. Therefore, I have placed this section here to conclude the chapter on plants and trees.

# Two Methods for Painting Rocks: Interspersing the Large with the Small and the Small with the Large

Just as trees require **interweaving** <term>chuancha</term> <note>original: 穿插; the structural interlocking and overlapping of elements to create depth and a sense of natural growth</note>, rocks also require interweaving. While the interweaving of trees is found in the arrangement of their branches and twigs, the interweaving of rocks is found within their **vital veins** <term>xuemai</term> <note>original: 血脉; literally 'bloodlines,' referring to the internal structural logic and rhythmic continuity that connects various rock forms into a cohesive whole</note>. The alternating arrangement of large and small forms is much like the strategic placement of stones in a game of **Go** <note>original: 置碁 (zhiqi); an ancient strategy board game where the balance of empty space and occupied points is essential</note>.

When painting rocks near water, they should appear like "a thousand infants clutching their mother." When they encircle a mountain, they should look like "an old arm reaching out alone to lead a grandchild." It is in these familial metaphors that the vital veins of the landscape are preserved.

> **Wang Sishan** <note>original: 王思善 (Wang Yi, active c. 1360s); a Yuan Dynasty painter and theorist famous for his work on the 'Secrets of Writing Portraits' and landscape composition</note> says: "The method for painting rocks should always begin with light ink; this allows the artist to correct or save the forms. Gradually building up to concentrated, dark ink is the superior approach." He also notes: "A secret to painting rocks is to allow a bit of **gamboge** <note>original: 滕黃 (tenghuang); a transparent yellow pigment derived from the resin of Garcinia trees</note> to soak into the ink brush; this naturally makes the ink look moist and lush. Do not use too much, however, or the brush will become sluggish and the ink will lose its clarity. Occasionally mixing **shell-blue** <note>original: 螺青 (luoqing); a deep mineral blue-green pigment</note> into the ink is also a wonderful technique."

<note>Jing Hao (active c. 870–930) and his student Guan Tong (active c. 907–960) were the primary architects of the "Northern School" of landscape painting. Their work focused on the rugged, towering granite peaks of North China, emphasizing the "skeletal" structure of the earth.</note>

## Texture Stroke Method of Xu Xi
<note>Xu Xi (died before 975) was a master of the Southern Tang dynasty. Although he is most celebrated for his "boneless" style in flower-and-bird painting—which prioritized ink washes over rigid outlines—his approach to landscape texture brought a similar organic, fluid quality to the depiction of stones and riverbanks.</note>

### The "Hook and Sickle" Method for Opening Mountains
<term>Hook and Sickle Method</term> <note>original: Kai zhang gou lian fa; "Opening the mountain" refers to defining the main skeletal structure and ridges of a landscape, while "Hook and Sickle" describes the specific interlocking brushwork used to carve out the peaks.</note>

Before the hundred bones of the human body are fully formed, the "bridge of the nose" must be established first. The very first brushstroke one makes is what we call the "bridge of the nose" of a front-facing mountain. One must carefully observe the entire form from every angle, then give renewed weight and attention to the crowning stroke at the summit; this is known as the "backbone" <note>original: gugu; literally "occipital bone" or "looking-bone," referring to the structural support of the peak</note> that governs the mountain's crest.

This point of rising and falling serves as the "Master" of the entire mountain. The **veins and energy pathways** <note>original: qimai; the rhythmic flow of vital energy and visual lines that connect different parts of a landscape</note> link together to form the composition of the entire scroll. Even a single rock must honor this Master. ### The "Host and Guest" Method of Formal Salutation
<term>Host and Guest Salutation</term> <note>original: Bin zhu chao yi fa; a compositional principle where a dominant "Master" or "Host" peak is surrounded by smaller "Guest" peaks that appear to bow or lean toward it in a gesture of respect, creating a sense of organic unity.</note>

**Mo Jie** <note>The courtesy name of Wang Wei (699–759 AD), a legendary Tang dynasty poet and painter often credited as the founder of the Southern School of landscape painting.</note> said: "When painting mountains, first examine the **atmosphere** <note>original: qixiang; the overall mood, scale, and vital energy of the landscape</note>. Next, distinguish between the clear and the turbid. Establish the formal salutation between the 'Host' and 'Guest' peaks, and arrange the various mountain clusters with proper dignity. If there are too many peaks, the result is chaos; if there are too few, the result is a lack of spirit."

Mountains have both high and low forms. In **high mountains**, the "blood vessels" <note>original: xuemai; the internal structural lines and flow of energy (qi) within the landform</note> are located at the base. The "shoulders and thighs" <note>the slopes and ridges</note> spread wide, and the foundation is sturdy and thick. Smaller peaks and mountain caves <term>Luanxiu</term> <note>original: luan xiu; 'luan' refers to pointed peaks and 'xiu' refers to mountain clefts or caves</note> surround the main body, connecting and reflecting each other without interruption. This is what constitutes a high mountain. It must be drawn this way to be considered "not solitary" and "not cluttered."

In **low mountains**, the "blood vessels" are at the top. Their summits fall away flatly, and the "brows and foreheads" of the hills seem to cling to one another. The foundation is immense, and the mounds appear "swollen" or full. Their deep roots are unfathomable. This is what constitutes a shallow mountain. It must be drawn this way to be considered "not thin" and "not dispersed."

Because the goal here is to make the outlines distinct and the "veins" of the mountain orderly, no **texture strokes** <term>Cunfa</term> <note>original: cunfa; the various brush techniques used to depict the texture and shadow of rocks and soil</note> have been added to this illustration, so that the student may clearly observe the structural method. Texture stroke methods are diverse; they have already been shown in the sections covering the "mountain peaks" <term>Luantou</term> and rock methods of the various Great Masters.

# Discussion of the "Three Distances" Method for Mountains

In the painting of mountains, there are three types of distance:
Looking up from the foot of the mountain toward its summit is called **High Distance** <term>Gao yuan</term>. 
Looking from the front of the mountain toward what lies behind it is called **Deep Distance** <term>Shen yuan</term>.
Looking from a nearby mountain toward those in the far distance is called **Level Distance** <term>Ping yuan</term>.

The power of **High Distance** lies in its towering loftiness. 
The intent of **Deep Distance** lies in its sense of dense layering. 
The charm of **Level Distance** lies in its peaceful expansiveness. 

These three perspectives serve as the grand resolution for an entire composition. If a mountain is meant to be "deep" but lacks "distance," it will appear shallow. If it is meant to be "level" but lacks "distance," it will appear too close. If it is meant to be "high" but lacks "distance," it will appear low. 

In landscape painting, falling into these errors is like encountering a shallow person or a common laborer <note>original: "yu chuan zao kang," referring to grooms and low-status workers who lack refinement</note>; it betrays a vulgar and common "bone structure" in the work. A person of quality <note>original: "zhong ren," a person of refined character</note> would have no choice but to abandon their home, leave their family behind, hold their nose, and run away as fast as possible!

However, if you wish to achieve the height of **High Distance**, you should use **soaring waterfalls** to emphasize it—for what are the thousand-fathom heights of the Yandang Mountains or the "Three Folds" of Mount Lu if not the very definition of High Distance? 

If you wish to achieve the depth of **Deep Distance**, you should use **dense clouds** to obscure it—for what are the Jade Maiden's Peak shrouded in green mystery or the emerald locks of the Morning Star if not the very definition of Deep Distance? 

If you wish to achieve the flatness of **Level Distance**, you should use **low-lying mist** to level it—for what are the clear ridges of Mount Gang, the valleys below Mount Hua, or the cold reaches of the "Foolish Old Man" <note>likely referring to a specific valley or legendary site associated with the myth of the Foolish Old Man who moved the mountains</note> if not the very definition of Level Distance?

### Dong Yuan

The peaks and hills of the **Master of the Northern Park** <note>original: "北苑" (Beiyuan), an official title by which Dong Yuan is frequently identified</note> are clear and deep, and his creative spirit is loftily ancient. ### Mi Fu

**Mi Fu** <note>Also known as Mi Xiangyang (1051–1107); a celebrated calligrapher and painter who pioneered the "Mi Dot" style to depict misty landscapes</note> adopted the **splashed ink** <term>pomo</term> <note>A technique involving bold, free-flowing applications of ink</note> technique of **Wang Qia** <note>An 8th-century Tang dynasty painter known for eccentric ink-tossing methods</note>, and integrated it with **broken ink** <term>pomo</term> <note>Layering wet ink over dry, or vice versa, to create texture and depth</note>, **layered ink** <term>jimo</term> <note>Repeatedly building up washes of ink to create density</note>, and **scorched ink** <term>jiaomo</term> <note>The use of very thick, dry, black ink for emphasis</note>. Because of this, his work is rich, thick, and full of flavor.

People say that Master Mi was skilled in the use of ink, but I alone say he was skilled in the use of the brush. When Mi’s brushwork is applied to his calligraphy, it is sometimes overly strained <note>original: 奴張 (nuzhang), suggesting a tense or forceful quality</note>; yet when applied to his painting, one senses only a rounded thickness and a "mellowed maturity."

While "rounded maturity" can be achieved through persistent practice, "horizontal thickness" <note>Referring to the iconic horizontal "Mi Dots"</note> must come directly from one's natural talent. Those with meager natural talent who try to learn this are like the harsh legalist **Lord Shang** attempting to match the refined virtue of **Shu Du** or **Yan Hui** <note>Paragons of Confucian virtue; the author is saying a person of "low" artistic spirit cannot imitate a "high" master's essence</note>—they will never succeed.

Although the elder Mi <note>Mi Fu, as opposed to his son Mi Youren</note> studied Wang Qia, his style actually has its source in the **Master of the Northern Park** <term>Beiyuan</term> <note>The honorific for Dong Yuan</note>. Modern students of the Mi style fail in two ways: their work is either too blurry or too clearly exposed. Where Mi's work is "exposed," it is like a light cloud in the Milky Way where the stars shine brightly; but in the hands of imitators, it becomes like "iron cotton threading through fermented beans" <note>A metaphor for stiff, clumpy, and unappealing ink dots</note>. Where Mi's work is "blurry," it is like a divine dragon, spirited and majestic, appearing and disappearing unpredictably; but modern people produce something like a "pile of dung and straw infested with ants" <note>A harsh critique of messy, muddy, and lifeless ink washes</note>.

How then should one study Mi? It is said: use the brush like steel, and use the ink as if it were flying. It is also said: "Cherish your ink as if it were gold, but wield your brush as if playing with a marble." Only when the traces of brush and ink are completely fused together do you have the true Mi style.

# Ni Zan’s Level-Distance Mountains

Ni Zan <note>also known by his art name Yunlin</note> began by studying the works of various masters. By the time he reached a state of divine transformation, he had taken the **texture stroke** methods <term>cun-fa; the brush techniques used to depict the surface and spiritual essence of rocks and mountains</term> of those masters and refined them through "a thousand firings and a hundred smeltings." He sought to reach that state where the painting exists even where **Yunlin’s** brush does not touch the paper. Today, whenever people encounter simple or shallow arrangements of hills and valleys, they immediately claim, "This is the Yunlin style." I am particularly serious about explaining this in detail. I divide his compositions into two forms: one is **high-distance** <note>gaoyuan; a vertical perspective looking up at peaks</note> and the other is **level-distance** <note>pingyuan; a horizontal perspective looking across a wide expanse</note>. This shows that even within his high-distance works, there is a clear "opening and closing" of form, while his level-distance works never lose the essence of the natural world.

# Huang Gongwang

<insert>Huang’s Method for Capped Stones and "Inserted and Spreading" Strokes</insert>

**Zijiu’s** <note>Huang Gongwang's courtesy name</note> mountains resemble those of **Dong Yuan**, yet he was able to transform those methods to become a great master in his own right. His peaks are often filled with crags and rocks, yet they possess a certain wind-swept elegance. In all painting, there must be a sense of three-dimensional volume—the **concave and convex**. One should study the specific terrain of the landscape before deciding which technique to use.

### Wang Meng

**Shuming** <note>the courtesy name of Wang Meng (c. 1308–1385), the youngest of the Four Masters of the Yuan Dynasty and a grandson of Zhao Mengfu</note> frequently employed the calligraphic methods of ancient **seal and clerical scripts** <note>Zhuanshu and Lishu; these are early, formal styles of Chinese writing that require a centered, firm brush tip</note>, blending them into his **texture strokes** <term>cun; the brush techniques used to define the surface and mass of rocks and mountains</term>. His brushwork is like a gold drill engraving stone, or saw-teeth cutting through a great swan <note>a metaphor for the sharp, rhythmic, and vigorous quality of his lines</note>. Although his strokes are dense and numerous, their vitality is self-sustaining and ever-changing. His style is natural and unconstrained by rules; it is powerful but never stiff. His forms are rounded without becoming fuzzy or unstructured, and square without showing sharp, protruding corners. 

In his synthesis of the various masters of the Tang and Song dynasties, there was not a single style he could not replicate with startling accuracy. At the end of the Yuan Dynasty, he was esteemed as the foremost artist of his time. When a student studies a master, they must not allow themselves to be "killed" <note>trapped or creatively stifled</note> by remaining within the confines of a single person’s style. An artist like Shuming, however, mastered all the great schools so thoroughly that his work truly leaves nothing to be desired, even by a hair's breadth.

### Disordered Hemp Texture
->(Luanma Cun)<-

If a young girl shakes a tangled ball of hemp and, for a moment, becomes flustered and confused, unable to find a place to start or a way to find the thread—can such a thing truly be called a **texture method** <term>cunfa; the technical brushwork used to describe the physical surface, mass, and "wrinkles" of rocks and mountains</term>? 

I say: No, definitely not. 

It must be like a net attached to its head-rope: organized and without disorder. When learning the texture methods of the ancients, the most important thing is to be able to initiate the movement and "shake" the strokes into fragments. However, even within this fragmented disorder, one must still see a rigorous sense of structural order.

### Lotus Leaf Texture
->(Heye Cun)<-

This method is named because its veins and filaments are interconnected, resembling the skeletal patterns of a lotus leaf. This is an example of what is called a **pictogram** <note>original: "xiangxing"; one of the six principles of Chinese character formation where the character resembles the object it represents</note> within the **Six Scripts** <term>liushu; the traditional classification system for Chinese characters</term>. 

**Beiyuan** <note>original: "Beiyuan"; the official title of Dong Yuan (c. 934–962), a founding master of the Southern School of landscape painting</note> frequently used this technique. In recent years, **Lan Tianshu** <note>original: "Lan Tianshu"; the courtesy name of Lan Ying (1585–1664), a famous professional painter of the late Ming Dynasty</note> has also enjoyed creating works in this style.

# Mustard Seed Garden Painting Manual, Volume 3

### Disordered Firewood Texture
->(Luanchai Cun)<-

Previously, I listed each artist's name and associated their specific **texture method** <term>cunfa; the technical brushwork used to describe the physical surface, mass, and "wrinkles" of rocks and mountains</term> with them. In this instance, however, I have written the name of the texture directly, without linking it to a specific individual.

Furthermore, placing the name of the texture in the position usually reserved for an author—as if the texture itself were a person—is a variation in my editorial method. I have done this because "Disordered Firewood" and "Disordered Hemp," within the hierarchy of **texture methods** <gloss>cunfa</gloss>, are considered **degraded or relegated styles** <note>original: "lingzhe"; a term implying these methods are seen as less refined, more chaotic, or "punished" versions of classical techniques</note>. Therefore, I felt it necessary to treat them as exceptions. 

Moreover, because many different masters have employed these techniques on occasion, it is difficult to attribute them exclusively to any one person.
