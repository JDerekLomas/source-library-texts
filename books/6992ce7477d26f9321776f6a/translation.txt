# Liu Jinu
<note>original: 劉寄奴, Liu Jinu; scientific name: *Artemisia anomala*. The plant is named after the childhood nickname of Emperor Wu of the Liu Song Dynasty (Liu Yu), who is said to have discovered its healing properties while hunting.</note>

The **Liu Jinu** herb grows in **Jiangnan** <note>A traditional region encompassing the lands south of the lower reaches of the Yangtze River</note>. Today, it is also found in **Hezhong Prefecture**, **Mengzhou**, and **Hanzhong**. 

In spring, the sprouts and stems emerge, looking much like **mugwort** <note>original: 艾蒿, Aihao; *Artemisia argyi*</note>. The stems have **four distinct ridges** and grow to a height of about two or three feet. The leaves are green and resemble those of a **willow**. 

In the fourth month of the lunar calendar, it produces tiny, fragmented flowers of a yellowish-white hue, similar in shape to the **rock pine** <note>original: 瓦松, Wasong; *Orostachys fimbriata*</note>. In the seventh month, it bears fruit that is fine and resembles **millet**. Several spikes grow alternately along a single stem. The roots are pale purple and resemble those of common **wormwood**. 

The sprouts, flowers, and seeds are harvested together in the sixth and seventh months for medicinal use. Its flavor is **bitter** and its nature is **warm**. It is primarily used to <term>break blood</term> <note>A traditional Chinese medical action referring to the dispersal of severe blood stasis or internal bruising</note> and reduce abdominal swelling. It is also exceptionally effective for treating <term>scalds and burns</term> <note>original: 湯火瘡, Tanghuochuang; literally "soup and fire sores"</note>.

<note>The following list represents key terms found in the text:</note>

**Vocabulary**
*   **Liu Jinu**: The herb *Artemisia anomala*.
*   **Jiangnan**: "South of the River," a fertile region in East China.
*   **Hezhong Prefecture, Mengzhou, Hanzhong**: Specific administrative regions in historical China (modern-day Shanxi, Henan, and Shaanxi provinces).
*   **Mugwort**: A common aromatic herb used in moxibustion and medicine.
*   **Rock Pine**: A succulent plant often found growing on the roofs of traditional tiled houses.
*   **Scalds and Burns**: Injuries caused by hot liquids or fire.
*   **Break Blood**: To invigorate blood circulation and remove stagnation.

# Bone-Mender (Gusuibu)
<note>original: 骨碎補, Gusuibu; scientific name: *Drynaria fortunei*. The name is highly descriptive: *gu* (骨) means bone, *sui* (碎) means shattered or broken, and *bu* (補) means to mend or supplement. It is a staple of traditional traumatology.</note>

(A woodcut illustration occupies the center of the page, depicting the **Bone-Mender** plant. The image shows the plant's dual nature: the tall, feathery green fronds reaching upward and the thick, scale-covered <term>rhizome</term> <note>a horizontal, creeping stem that grows along the surface of rocks or trees</note> at the base.)

The **Bone-Mender** is a perennial fern often found clinging to rocks or tree trunks in shaded mountain valleys. The illustration captures the distinct morphology of its leaves and its thick, fleshy rootstock, which is the part used in medicine.

In traditional practice, the **rhizome** is harvested and prepared by scraping or singeing off the fine, brownish-yellow hairs that cover it. Its properties and uses include:

*   **Healing Fractures:** True to its name, it is a primary herb for treating **bone fractures**, falls, and strike injuries. It is believed to promote the mending of bone tissue and the healing of tendons.
*   **Kidney Health:** It is used to "tonify the Kidneys" <note>In traditional Chinese medicine, the Kidney system is thought to govern bone development and marrow production</note>.
*   **Pain Relief:** It is effective for chronic lower back pain, weakness in the legs, and traditional ailments like <term>tinnitus</term> <note>ringing in the ears</note> or loose teeth, which were historically linked to Kidney vitality.

The flavor of the herb is characterized as **bitter** and its nature as **warm**. This visual guide in the *Sancai Tuhui* served as a vital reference for identifying the correct species in the wild, ensuring that the "hairy" rhizome shown here was not confused with other less effective ferns.

# Bone-Mender (Gusuibu)

**Bone-Mender** grows in the regions of Jiangnan, Huai, Zhe, Shaanxi, and the Kui Circuit; it is also found in various other prefectures and commanderies. The roots grow upon large trees or rocks, primarily in shaded areas. It extends its roots in long strands covered with fine yellow hairs and small attached leaves. It also possesses larger leaves that form branches; the upper surface is a lush green, while the underside is marked with yellow spots. <note>These "yellow spots" refer to the sori, or spore clusters, typical of ferns.</note> Other varieties may have a bluish-white underside with reddish-purple spots. The leaves sprout in the spring and turn dry and yellow by winter. It produces neither flowers nor fruit. 

Only the root is used for medicinal purposes. It may be harvested at any time of year. Before use, the fine hairs must be scraped or singed off. Its original name was **Macaque Ginger** <note>original: "Husunjiang" (胡孫薑). "Husun" is an old term for macaque or monkey, and "jiang" means ginger, likely referring to the appearance of the rhizome.</note>. However, because the Tang Emperor Xuanzong <note>original: "Tang Minghuang" (唐明皇), reigned 712–756 AD</note> found it to be extraordinarily effective for treating fractures and traumatic injuries, he bestowed upon it its current name, "Bone-Mender." It is also known by the name **Stone Anlu** <note>original: "Shianlü" (石菴䕡).</note>.

Its flavor is bitter, its nature is warm, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to break up stagnant blood, stop bleeding, and repair fractures or injuries. It also treats **gnashing-breath** <term>齘氣 (xieqi): an archaic term for a condition involving the involuntary gnashing of teeth or dental disorders caused by "wind" or "dampness"</term>, toothaches, bleeding gums, loosening of the teeth, and localized itching or pain.

# Forsythia (Lianqiao)

**Forsythia** grows in the mountain valleys of Mount Tai <note>A sacred mountain in Shandong province, historically significant for gathering medicinal herbs.</note>, as well as in Hezhong, Jiangning, Runzhou, Zizhou, Yanzhou, Yazhou, Lizhou, and Nankang. It is found in all these regions.

There are two varieties: the **Great Forsythia** and the **Small Forsythia**. They grow in low-lying damp lands or upon mountain ridges. The leaves emerge as green sprouts that are narrow and long, resembling the leaves of an elm tree. The stems are reddish in color and reach a height of approximately three or four feet. The yellow flowers are quite lovely. In autumn, the plant bears fruit that resembles a lotus seedpod. Because the fruit stalks "rise up" (*qiao*) above the surrounding grasses, the plant earned its name **Lianqiao** <note>The name Lianqiao (連翹) literally suggests "connected rising."</note>. The roots are yellow, similar to the roots of artemisia. The seedpods are harvested in the eighth lunar month and dried in the shade.

The **Small Forsythia** grows on high plains and plateaus; its leaves, flowers, and fruit are all similar to the Great Forsythia but more delicate. It is also known as "Dry Lian Herb."

Today, physicians in the South say there are two distinct types of Forsythia. One type resembles the unopened fruit of the **Chinese Toona** (*Toona sinensis*); its shell is small and hard, and the exterior is complete without an attached calyx. When sliced open, the interior is fresh and the aroma is very fragrant. The other type is like a **lotus bud** (*handan*); the shell is soft and has an attached calyx clutching it. It lacks veins and has no fragrance.

It is also called "Strange Qiao," "Orchid Flower," or "Broken Root."

<gloss>It is also named "Zhi" or "Sanzhe." The flavor is bitter, its nature is balanced, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat chills and fevers, scrofula <note>Historically called "King's Evil," referring to swollen lymph nodes in the neck.</note>, chronic sores, carbuncles, swelling, malignant ulcers, goiters, tumors, heat stagnation, and poisoning from parasites, and it eliminates white intestinal worms.</gloss>

# Caper Spurge (Xusuizi)

Caper Spurge grows in the Shu Commandery <note>original: 蜀郡 (Shu jun); modern-day Sichuan province</note> and is found everywhere. The seedlings resemble Peking Spurge <note>original: 大戟 (Daji); Euphorbia pekinensis</note>. It initially grows a single stem, with leaves emerging at the tip. From within these leaves, several more stems emerge in succession, which is how it earned its name <note>The name 續隨子 (Xusuizi) literally means "successively following seeds/stems."</note>. 

The flowers also resemble those of the Peking Spurge. Stalks sprout from among the leaves to produce fruit, which are green and have a hull. Many people plant it in their gardens and courtyards to use as an ornament. It is sown in autumn, grows through the winter, flourishes in the spring, and bears fruit in the summer; for this reason, it is also named "Winter-Resister" <note>original: 拒冬 (Judong)</note>. 

The fruit is also known as <term>Thousand Gold Seed</term> <note>original: 千金子 (Qianjinzi). This name reflects the high value placed on its potent medicinal effects.</note>. Its flavor is acrid, its nature is warm, and it is toxic. 

It is primarily used to treat "blood knots" and the cessation of menstruation in women, as well as <term>Abdominal Masses</term> <note>original: 癥瘕 (Zhengjia); refers to various types of hard or movable tumors and growths in the abdomen.</note>, <term>Chronic Stagnation</term> <note>original: 痃癖 (Xuanpi); a traditional diagnosis for localized pain or masses caused by chronic indigestion or "cold" energy.</note>, blood stasis, and "Gu" poisoning <note>original: 蠱毒 (Gudu); a legendary form of venom or parasitic infection associated with dark magic or environmental toxins.</note>. It also treats "childhood infusion" <note>original: 児疰 (erzhu); a chronic wasting disease or infectious condition in children.</note>, pain in the heart and abdomen, and bloating or fullness caused by "cold qi." 

It benefits the function of the large and small intestines <note>acting as a powerful purgative</note>, removes <term>Phlegm-Fluid</term> <note>original: 痰飲 (Tanyin); a TCM concept referring to the accumulation of fluids that have thickened due to poor metabolism.</note> and internal accumulations, and expels foul or stagnant matter from the body. The white sap found within the stem can be used to remove moles and dark skin spots.

# Lanru

<note>original: 蘭茹 (Lanru); historically identified as a species of Figwort (Scrophularia) or potentially a variety of Euphorbia, though the description of the sap suggests a specific medicinal variety used for topical treatments.</note>

**Lanru** grows in the mountain valleys of Dai Prefecture. Today, it can also be found in Heyang, Zi, and Qi Prefectures. In the second lunar month, the sprouts emerge. The leaves resemble the <term>Daji (Peking Spurge)|Euphorbia pekinensis, a potent and somewhat toxic herb used in traditional medicine</term>, but the flowers are yellow. The root is shaped like a radish; its skin is a reddish-yellow, while the flesh inside is white. When the root is first cut, the sap flows out and congeals, becoming as black as lacquer. 

In the third month, it blooms with light red flowers, which may also appear pale yellow; notably, this plant does not bear seeds. <term>Tao Yinju|Tao Hongjing (456–536 CE), a renowned physician and polymath who organized the first systematic pharmacopoeia in China</term> stated that the specimens originating from <term>Goryeo|An ancient kingdom on the Korean Peninsula</term> are very similar to this variety. 

The roots should be harvested in the fourth and fifth months and dried in the shade. Those that possess the "lacquer head" <note>The dark, resinous cap formed by the dried sap at the cut end of the root</note> are considered to be of the highest quality. There is another variety known as "Grass Lanru" which is white in color. Unscrupulous harvesters sometimes use a red-hot iron to sear the tips of these white roots to turn them black, attempting to pass them off as the genuine "lacquer head" variety; these are not the true medicine.

This herb is also known by the names **Quju** or **Liwei**.

Its flavor is pungent and sour; its nature is slightly cold and contains a small amount of toxin. It is primarily used to erode necrotic flesh, treat festering sores, and remove dead muscle tissue. It is effective at killing scabies mites, draining pus, and eliminating "bad blood" <note>Refers to stagnant or infected blood according to traditional pathology</note>. 

Furthermore, it expels "Great Wind" <note>A historical term for severe skin diseases or neurological conditions like leprosy</note> and heat energy. It is used to treat forgetfulness and melancholy. It also relieves "heat obstruction" <note>Pain or numbness caused by heat and dampness</note>, breaks down <term>Zhengjia|Hard abdominal masses or tumors believed to be caused by stagnant energy and blood</term>, and removes polyps.

# Gold Star Grass

Gold Star Grass <note>original: 金星草 (Jinxingcao)</note> grows in the regions of Guanshan, Sichuan, Shu, and the prefectures of Tan and Wu; it is found in all these places. It is also called "Gold Bracelet Grass." Its taste is bitter, its nature is cold, and it is non-toxic.

The leaves are green. It grows mostly in clean, shady spots on rocks, or within bamboo thickets where there is little sunlight. It may also grow beneath large trees or on the shady sides of old tiled roofs. When it first emerges, it is deep green, and the leaves grow to one or two feet in length. In late winter, yellow star-like dots appear on the underside of the leaves. These dots are arranged in pairs and have a color like gold, which is how the plant earned its name. It has no flowers or fruit. The leaves do not wither even in the depths of winter.

Its roots are twisted and coiled like those of bamboo, but thinner. When broken, they contain fibers resembling pig bristles. In the fifth month of the lunar calendar, the plant is harvested along with its roots and then dried in the wind.

It is primarily used to treat <term>carbuncles and ulcers|癰疽 (yongju); severe skin infections and abscesses</term>, as well as various sores and toxins. It is highly effective at neutralizing poisoning from sulfur and <term>mineral elixirs|丹石 (danshi); refers to the toxic side effects of alchemical minerals like cinnabar or arsenic often used in ancient medicines</term>. For the treatment of carbuncles on the back, swellings, or <term>hard nodules|結核 (jiehe); referring to subcutaneous lumps or swollen lymph nodes</term>, the leaves and roots should be decocted in wine and taken orally.

## Chinese Elder
<note>original: 陸英 (*Luying*); botanical name: *Sambucus chinensis*</note>

The **Chinese Elder** grows in the mountain valleys of **Mount Xionger** <note>a mountain range in Henan Province</note>. It is also known by the name **Shuozhou** <note>original: 蒴藋</note>, though ancient records do not specify exactly which provinces or territories it originates from. Today, it can be found growing almost everywhere. In the spring, it sprouts stems with distinct nodes; from between these nodes, branches and leaves grow that closely resemble **Water Celery** <note>original: 水芹 (*shuiqin*)</note> or the **Bone-knit** plant <note>original: 接骨 (*jiegu*), likely referring to *Sambucus javanica*</note>. The leaves should be harvested in the spring and summer, while the roots and stems are collected during the autumn and winter.

Some identify this plant specifically as the *Luying*. **Master Su** <note>Su Gong, a famous Tang Dynasty pharmacologist and editor of the *Tang Materia Medica*</note> states that in the *Treatise on Medicinal Pairings* <note>original: 藥對 (*Yaodui*)</note> and other ancient formulas, the name *Shuozhou* is never used—only *Luying* is mentioned. This clearly suggests they are one and the same. However, current commentaries note that because the medicinal "nature" and "flavor" of descriptions vary, some suspect they might actually be two different species.

**Medicinal Properties:**
The flavor is **bitter** and it is **non-toxic**. It is primarily used to treat various forms of **blockage and numbness** <term>Bi-syndrome: a condition in traditional medicine where external evils like cold or dampness "block" the body's circulation, causing pain</term> between the bones, as well as cramping, aching, and pain in the four limbs. It is also effective for "cold pain" in the knees, **impotence** <note>original: 陰痿 (*yinwei*)</note>, shortness of breath, and swelling of the feet.

# Pipewort

<note>original: 穀精草 (*Gujingcao*); botanical name: *Eriocaulon buergerianum*</note>

**Pipewort** <note>also known as "Grain Essence Herb"</note> is found throughout the rice-growing regions. It is so named because it typically flourishes in the paddy fields after the grain has been harvested, seemingly emerging from the "residual essence" of the cereal crops left in the soil.

The plant grows in dense, grassy tufts. In the late autumn, it sends up numerous slender, leafless stalks. At the tip of each stalk sits a small, white, globular flower head. These blossoms are quite distinctive, resembling tiny white stars or pearls scattered across the field. 

**Medicinal Properties:**
The flavor is **sweet** and **spicy**, and its nature is **neutral**. It is a primary herb used in the treatment of **eye disorders**. It is particularly effective for clearing **wind-heat** <term>Wind-heat: a diagnostic pattern in traditional medicine associated with external pathogens causing inflammation, fever, and redness</term> that affects the vision.

It is frequently prescribed to treat:
*   **Redness and swelling** of the eyes.
*   **Photophobia** <note>sensitivity to light</note>.
*   The formation of **superficial opacities** <term>Ocular film or "nebula": a cloudy layer that forms over the cornea or pupil, often following an infection</term>.

The flowers and stalks are the parts used for medicine, harvested when the flower heads are fully formed and then dried in the sun.

# Pre-knowledge Seed

<note>original: 預知子 (*Yuzhizi*); botanical name: Akebia quinata, also known as the Chocolate Vine</note>

The **Pre-knowledge Seed** is a plant of the climbing vine variety. In the records of the **Materia Medica** <note>original: *Bencao*; refers to the traditional Chinese pharmacological compendia</note>, it is noted for its ability to clear the pathways of the body and sharpen the senses. 

Its physical form is that of a vigorous vine that twines around supports, such as bamboo poles or trees. The leaves are compound, typically appearing in clusters of five. The fruit, which contains the medicinal seeds, is oblong and splits open when it reaches maturity. Because the fruit bursts open during the harvest season, it is colloquially known in some regions as the **"August Fried Melon."**

The flavor of the seed is **acrid** and **bitter**, its nature is **neutral**, and it is **non-toxic**. It is primarily used to treat **stagnation of Qi** <term>Qi: the vital energy or life force. Stagnation occurs when this energy is blocked, leading to pain or discomfort</term>. 

According to traditional practitioners, it is highly effective for:
*   Clearing **heat** and promoting urination.
*   Resolving **scrofula** <term>Scrofula (luoli): a traditional term for chronic swelling or lumps in the neck, often associated with the lymph nodes</term>.

<note>original: 預知子 (*Yuzhizi*); botanical name: Akebia quinata</note>

In older records, the native provinces and lands where the **Pre-knowledge Seed** originated were not documented. ## Horsetail

**Horsetail** <note>original: 木賊 (*Mùzéi*); literally "Wood Thief"</note> grows in damp ground near water and in the shaded valleys of mountains. It resembles a slender arrow shaft and is characterized by distinct joints or segments. The appearance of its stalk is much like the "Horse-broom" plant <note>referring to other species in the *Equisetum* genus</note>. When the stalk is pulled apart, the segments detach cleanly from one another at the joints.

The plant is typically harvested after the autumn and dried in the shade. It earned the name "Wood Thief" because its surface is exceptionally sharp and abrasive; it is capable of scouring and polishing wood or bone to a smooth finish, "stealing" the roughness away <note>The plant contains high levels of silica, making it a natural sandpaper used by early woodworkers and artisans</note>.

In terms of traditional pharmacology, its flavor is sweet and bitter, and its energetic nature is neutral.

### Primary Medicinal Uses

*   **Eye Disorders:** It is primarily used to clear "clouds" or films that grow over the eyes <term>Yì: a historical term for cataracts, pterygiums, or corneal opacities that obscure vision</term>.
*   **Internal Congestion:** It helps to dissipate "accumulations and blocks" within the body <note>referring to physical masses or stagnant energy in the abdomen</note>.
*   **Women's Health:** It is used to treat "flooding and spotting" <term>Bēnglòu: a condition of abnormal or heavy uterine bleeding</term> as well as various types of vaginal discharge.
*   **Digestive Health:** It is effective at stopping "intestinal wind," a condition marked by the passing of bright red blood in the stool <note>often associated with bleeding hemorrhoids in modern contexts</note>.

The **Niubian** <note>original: 牛扁 (*Niúbiǎn*); *Aconitum barbatum* var. *puberulum*</note> is a plant that typically grows in mountain valleys and moist shaded areas. 

As seen in the accompanying illustration, the plant features large, **palmate leaves** <note>leaves that spread out like the fingers of a hand</note> that are deeply lobed and marked with a distinct, dotted texture. These leaves grow from slender stems that rise from a thick, fibrous rhizome. 

In traditional medicine, the Niubian is categorized as having a bitter flavor and a "cold" nature <term>cold: *hán* (寒), a property used to clear heat and toxins, often associated with anti-inflammatory effects</term>. It is primarily used for external applications. Its most notable use is in the treatment of **scabies and skin sores** <note>original: 疥癬 (*Jièxuǎn*)</note>. 

Furthermore, its name—which literally translates to "Ox Flat"—refers to its historical use in veterinary medicine. It is highly effective at killing **lice and parasites** <note>original: 虱 (*Shī*)</note> that infest cattle and horses. By boiling the plant to create a wash, farmers could rid their livestock of pests and soothe their skin.

## Wood Sorrel

**Wood Sorrel** <note>original: 酢漿草 (*cùjiāngcǎo*)</note> is commonly referred to in the vernacular as **Sour Sauce** <note>original: 酸漿 (*suānjiāng*)</note>. Older records did not specify the particular provinces or territories where it originated, noting only that it grows by the roadsides. 

Today, it is found in great abundance throughout the low-lying, damp lands of the south, as well as within the gardens and orchards of private homes. It can also occasionally be found growing in northern regions. The leaves resemble duckweed and grow in dense clusters; at the tip of each stem, there are three leaflets. Small yellow flowers bloom from between these leaves, and the fruit is black. 

The leaves are harvested for use during the summer months. When the plant is first sprouting and tender, children often eat it for its tart flavor. People in the south also use the plant to polish **brass** <term>brass: *tōushí* (鍮石), a high-quality copper alloy often resembling gold; the acidic juice of the sorrel cleans the oxidation</term> vessels, making them as bright and white as silver.

In terms of medicinal properties, its flavor is sour, its nature is cold, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat **malignant sores** <note>original: 惡瘡 (*èchuāng*)</note>, swellings, and **fistulas** <term>fistula: *lòu* (瘻), a persistent, abnormal passage or ulcer in the body</term>. For treatment, the herb should be **pounded into a paste and applied** <note>original: 搗傅 (*dǎofù*)</note> to the affected area. It is effective at killing various small parasites and is also used to treat **chronic headaches** <term>chronic headaches: *tóufēng* (頭風), literally "head-wind," a term used for recurring or neurological head pain</term>.

## Selfheal

->[Illustration: A traditional Chinese woodcut of the medicinal herb **Selfheal** (*Prunella vulgaris*), showing its distinct square stem, opposite leaves, and the spiked flowering head from which it is identified.]<-

**Selfheal** <note>original: 夏枯草 (*xiàkūcǎo*)</note> is recorded as growing in **Maozhou** <note>a historical region in what is now Mao County, Sichuan Province</note>. It is a unique plant that begins to sprout after the **Winter Solstice** <note>original: 冬至 (*dōngzhì*)</note>, unlike many other herbs that wait for the spring thaw.

The leaves of this plant resemble those of the **Big Thistle** <note>original: 大薊 (*dàjì*); *Cirsium japonicum*</note>, though they are smooth and lack the thistle's sharp thorns. A defining characteristic of its growth is the stem, which is **four-angled** <note>original: 四稜 (*sìléng*); a square stem typical of the mint family</note>. During the third and fourth lunar months, it produces purple flowers that grow in a dense **spike** <note>original: 穗 (*suì*)</note> at the top of the stem. By the fifth month, as the heat of summer intensifies, the plant suddenly withers and dries up <note>this phenomenon gives the plant its Chinese name, which literally translates to "Summer-Withered Grass"</note>. It is at this moment of withering that the herb is harvested for its medicinal properties.

In terms of its pharmaceutical nature, the flavor is bitter and pungent, its energy is slightly cold, and it is entirely non-toxic. It is a vital herb in the **materia medica** <term>materia medica: *běncǎo* (本草), the traditional body of knowledge regarding therapeutic substances</term> for "venting" heat from the Liver channel. It is primarily used to treat **scrofula** <term>scrofula: *luǒlì* (瘰癧), chronic swelling of the lymph nodes in the neck</term> and **rat-fistulas** <note>original: 鼠瘺 (*shǔlòu*), referring to ulcerated sores</note>, as well as to alleviate persistent dizziness and swelling of the eyes.

# Self-heal

**Self-heal** <note>original: 夏枯草 (*xiàkūcǎo*)</note> grows within the river valleys of the **Shu Commandery** <note>a historical region in modern-day Sichuan Province</note>. Nowadays, it is also commonly found throughout the prefectures of **Hedong**, **Huai**, and **Zhe** <note>regions spanning modern Shanxi, the Huai River basin, and Zhejiang</note>.

The plant displays a unique growth cycle: its leaves begin to sprout after the **Winter Solstice** <note>original: 冬至 (*dōngzhì*)</note>, appearing similar in shape to those of the **Elecampane** <note>original: 旋復 (*xuánfù*); *Inula* species</note>. During the third and fourth lunar months, it blooms with purple-white flowers that cluster together to form a **spike** <note>original: 穗 (*suì*)</note>, bearing a resemblance to the flowers of **Red Sage** <note>original: 丹參 (*dānshēn*); *Salvia miltiorrhiza*</note>. Its seeds also develop within these spiked heads.

By the fifth lunar month, the herb withers away—a characteristic that gives the plant its primary Chinese name, which literally translates to "Summer-Withered Grass." It is harvested for medicinal use during the fourth month. This herb is also known by the alternative names **Xiju** <note>original: 夕句</note> and **Naidong** <note>original: 乃東; literally "Enduring Winter," referring to its early sprouting</note>.

In terms of its medicinal profile, its flavor is both bitter and pungent, its energy is cold, and it is considered entirely non-toxic. It is primarily prescribed for alternating **chills and fever**, and for the treatment of **scrofula** <term>scrofula: *luǒlì* (瘰癧), a swelling of the lymph nodes in the neck, often tubercular in nature</term> and **rat-fistula** <term>rat-fistula: *shǔlòu* (鼠瘻), a traditional term for chronic, suppurating sores or ulcerative tracks</term>. It is further used to heal **sores on the head**, to break up **internal abdominal masses** <term>internal masses: *zhèng* (癥), hard obstructions or concretions in the abdomen</term>, and to dissipate **goiters** <note>original: 癭 (*yǐng*)</note> and **stagnated qi**. Additionally, it is effective against **swollen legs** and **damp numbness** <note>conditions involving joint pain or heavy sensations in the limbs attributed to environmental moisture</note>. It is said that use of this herb can **lighten the body** <note>a common phrase in historical herbals suggesting the promotion of agility, health, and longevity</note>.

# Purple-backed Dragon Tooth
<note>original: 紫背龍牙 (*zǐ bèi lóng yá*); this name literally translates to "Purple-backed Dragon Tooth," likely referring to the leaf color and serrated edges of a plant like *Potentilla discolor*.</note>

It is also used to treat pain in the throat; the medicinal effect is achieved by holding the herb in the mouth and swallowing the juices.
