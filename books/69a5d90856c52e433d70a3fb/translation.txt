# The Method of Measuring Lengths.

## Plate 26.

The distances that one wishes to measure are either entirely accessible (such as those measured mechanically by the rod <note>original: "verge". A historical unit of length, often translated as a rod or pole, used in surveying.</note>), or accessible only in parts, or else entirely inaccessible.

### Figures A, B, & C.

Let there be a distance AB which we wish to measure, and it is accessible at point A; having placed the instrument at A, let the angle BAE be made by placing a staff at E, then measuring AC with a chain by a certain number of rods, such as 16. Let angle ECD be equal to the first angle, placing a staff at D on the straight line EB. After having measured DC and CE, one will say: if the distance EC of 4 gives me a CD of 6, how much will an EA of 20 give? The result will be 30 rods for the distance AB. <note>This is a classic application of similar triangles, where the ratio of the sides of the smaller triangle CDE is used to calculate the unknown side of the larger triangle ABE.</note>

Regarding the length EC: if it is an aliquot part <term>aliquot part</term> <note>A part of a number that divides it exactly without leaving a remainder; for example, 5 is an aliquot part of 20.</note> of EA, the calculation will be all the easier; for if EC is a fifth part of AE, then CD will also be 1/5 of AB.

As for angle A or C, it matters little what the angle is, provided they are equal; however, if the angle is too oblique, the practice of it is not so certain.

### D.

One could also arrange it so that AD is perpendicular to BAC; then, using the instrument at D, let angle ADC be made equal to ADB, then place a staff at C, and once AC is measured, it will be equal to AB. <note>By creating two congruent right-angled triangles, the surveyor can simply measure the accessible baseline on the ground to find the inaccessible distance.</note>

Then, place the <term>square</term> <note>original: "eſquierre". A surveyor's tool used to sight and set right angles.</note> along the line DF, as at E and G, so that AE and BG are perpendicular to it; then GE will be equal to BA.

### Plate 1, after 42.

A **Body** or **Solid** is that which has length, width, and depth; its extremities are surfaces.
A **Solid angle** is the meeting of several planes at a single point, where, when they are extended, they all intersect at that point; <note>original: "item"</note> likewise, more than two plane angles are required to create a solid angle—see the tip of Figure 3—and it must be noted that it is impossible to describe bodies on a flat surface, except through the use of **perspective**.

Euclid defined "Bodies" in his work on **Stereometry** <term>Stereometry|the branch of geometry that deals with the measurement of solid, three-dimensional figures</term>, but since the figures provided here illustrate quite well what he intends in his definitions, we shall now explain the same concepts, or their equivalents. The first is, or signifies, a **cube**, which is composed of 6 squares on its sides. Figures 4 and 14 represent a **parallelepiped**, made of 6 parallelograms of which the opposite sides are identical. Figure 2 is a **sphere** or globe; it is created by the rotation of a semicircle when the diameter remains stationary (Figure 23). Figures 5, 6, 7, and 19 are **right pyramids**, occurring when the perpendicular line from the apex falls exactly in the center of the base. Figures 11, 12, and 13 are **oblique pyramids**; they are named according to their bases—those with a triangle for a base are called triangular pyramids, others are quadrangular, pentagonal, etc., or even round, which are properly called **Cones**. Figure 8 is a regular **Octahedron**, composed of 8 equal equilateral triangles. Figure 9 is a regular **Dodecahedron**, composed of 12 equal regular pentagons. Figure 10 is a regular **Icosahedron** of 20 equal equilateral triangles.

A **Column** <note>In Marolois’s terminology, a "column" refers to what we would now call a prism: a solid with two identical, parallel bases and a uniform cross-section.</note> is a body contained between two identical and parallel surfaces, of equal thickness from one surface directly to the other (Figures 1, 4, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18).

A **Cylinder** is a round column, such as Figure 18, which is scalene or oblique.

A **Conic Rhombus** <note>A solid formed by two cones joined at their bases.</note> is shown in Figure 22.

A **Sector of a Sphere** is a body of two surfaces, one of which is conical (the apex of which is the center of the other spherical surface), as seen in 24 and 25; however, 24 is a "Great Sector" and 25 is a "Small Sector." <note>original: "item"</note> Likewise, half of the body of the Sphere is called a **Hemisphere** (28, 29).

A **Section of a Sphere** <term>Section of a Sphere|today more commonly called a spherical segment; a solid cut from a sphere by a plane</term> is a body of two surfaces: one flat, the other spherical (26, 27), where 26 is a "Great Section"; the same can be said for the **Spheroid** (34, 35, and 36).

## How to measure Columns.

**M**ultiply the surface area of the base by the height; the product will be the volume of the Column. 
For in Figure 44, multiplying the base of 36 square feet by the height of 4, the product will be 144 cubic feet: and likewise in the following figures of the same plate, as in Figure 49, if one multiplies the base of 148 by the height CE, one will have the capacity of the Cylinder: in Figure 50, if one multiplies the surface area of the base ABCD by the height BE or AG, one will have its solid volume. ## How to measure pyramids.

### Plate 4.

**M**ultiply the base and the height by one another; one-third of the product will be the volume of the pyramid.
In Figure 52, if one multiplies the base ABC by the height DO, one-third of the product will be its capacity: and likewise to measure the truncated pyramid <term>truncated pyramid|also known as a frustum; a pyramid whose top has been cut off by a plane parallel to the base</term> ACL KGF, one will measure the [full] pyramid ALB and [the smaller top portion] GFB; their difference will be the required amount.

## A. GIRARD to the Readers. Greetings.

*<term>**T**he</term> reason that moved me to provide an explanation of the geometric figures that Marolois had created was none other than that it would have been tedious for me to see a book reprinted so full of errors—both from the author himself and from the printing—since it was possible for me (with the help of God) to recorrect it and make it more brief, or to simply explain the figures. Furthermore, my desire tended more toward increasing science than the number of books, of which our century finds itself quite abundant, notably by those who bring to light by day what they dreamed up at night, and who think that forging books is work enough. It is this plurality of books that deterred me from creating one of my own on practical geometry using a different method than the present author held, and in a somewhat easier fashion. Nevertheless, I shall try to present one to the public, since one must not fear these obstacles, wherein I intend to prove that the number of <term>Mathematicians</term> <note>In the 17th century, "Mathematician" was a broad term covering astronomers, engineers, and teachers of arithmetic, not just theoretical scholars.</note> is not in such great abundance as Honorat du Meynier thinks in his poor, recently published paradoxes. In these, he shows clearly enough that he has not yet even seen one <note>Meaning he hasn't met a true mathematician.</note>, since he wishes to maintain that science brings no more advantage than what experience produces without it. For he believes that in all things experience precedes science, but that can only be true in very specific instances in <term>mathematics</term>, such as for forming axioms. This general principle, which he calls a paradox in the plural, being of too low a condition, could only give birth to little paradoxes similar to himself, ultimately bringing back the opinion of the ridiculous sect of the <term>Pyrrhonians</term> <note>Followers of an ancient Greek school of skepticism who argued that certain knowledge is impossible.</note>. But this not being the place to refute and silence these makers of great uproars, I will leave him to fume against those he wrongly and haphazardly names mathematicians, as if he had ever known what Mathematics is, and as if one could even find eight in the entire universe who might claim to possess even a small portion of it, so to speak. What I touch upon here is only to warn those who have seen his work not to take such close heed of the sense of the title of said paradox. For the author, wishing to signify to some poor schoolmasters who earn their bread teaching reading (who, beyond their duties, sometimes teach their students to perform a <term>rule of three</term> <note>A mathematical method to find a fourth value from three known values using proportions.</note>, or the first six books of <term>Euclid</term> <note>Euclid’s *Elements*, the foundational textbook for geometry for over 2,000 years.</note> concerning the A, B, C's of Geometry) that he had conceived some reason why it would be easier for them to learn to run before walking, goes and pitches his voice eighteen tones higher, calls them Mathematicians, and—were it not for that—one would say he wants to make this divine science odious. For without thinking, he says that Mathematicians deceive the youth; yet if one searches the book, he speaks of them no more. Instead, here and there, he insults those of the reformed religion, calling them heretics, jumping with a single bound "from the cock’s comb to the ass’s ears" <note>A French idiom for jumping nonsensically from one subject to another.</note> to curry favor with a Cardinal to whom he dedicates his work in the form of a rhapsody, in order to make a "crossed blessing" <note>A pun on the sign of the cross and the "cross" as a name for a coin.</note> fall into his purse—all of this, no doubt, to make some composition of <term>Alchemy</term> multiply within it. Finally, leaving this discourse aside, let the Reader take this small work, such as it is, in good part. If it please him.*
