## LECTURE I.

### NEW MATERIALS FOR THE EARLY HISTORY OF INSTITUTIONS.

The sources of information concerning the early history of institutions which have been opened to us during the last few years are numerous and valuable. On one subject in particular—which may be confidently said to have been almost exclusively investigated until lately by writers who had followed a false path—the additions to our knowledge are of special interest and importance. We at length know something concerning the beginnings of the great institution of property in land. The collective ownership of the soil by groups of men who are either actually united by blood-relationship, or who believe and assume that they are so united, is now entitled to take rank as an established primitive phenomenon. This was once a universal characteristic of those human communities whose civilization has a distinct connection to our own.

<summary>This introductory page establishes that recent discoveries have revealed the origins of property law, specifically highlighting that collective land ownership by kinship groups was a universal starting point for Western civilization.</summary>

The evidence has been found on all sides of us, dimly seen and verifiable with difficulty in countries which have undergone the enormous pressure of the Roman Empire, or which have been strongly affected by its indirect influence. However, it is perfectly plain and unmistakable in the parts of the world peopled by the Aryan race where the Empire made itself felt very slightly or not at all. 

As regards the Slavonic <note>original: "Sclavonic"</note> communities, the emancipation <note>original: "enfranchisement"</note> of the peasantry in the Russian dominions in Europe has stimulated inquiries that formerly attracted only a few curious observers; consequently, the amount of information collected has been very large. We now know much more clearly than we did before that the soil of the older provinces of the Russian Empire has been, from time immemorial, almost exclusively distributed among groups of self-styled kinsmen. These people are collected in cultivating village communities which are self-organized and self-governing. 

Since the great reform of the current reign <note>Referring to the Emancipation Reform of 1861 by Tsar Alexander II, which abolished serfdom.</note>, the collective rights of these communities—and the rights and duties of their members toward one another—are no longer entangled with or limited by the manorial privileges of a chief landowner. There is also fresh evidence that the more underdeveloped outlying Slavonic societies are organized according to essentially the same model. # LECTURE I. VESTIGES OF THE VILLAGE COMMUNITY

The ideas of such a large portion of the human race—including its ideas regarding property—are inextricably bound up with the notions of family interdependency, collective ownership, and a natural subjection to patriarchal power. The traces of this ancient social order in Germanic and Scandinavian countries are, I hardly need to say, considerably fainter and tend to become increasingly obscured over time. However, the re-examination of written evidence regarding ancient Teutonic <note>Teutonic: Relating to the ancient Germanic peoples.</note> life and custom proceeds without interruption. Incidentally, much light has been thrown on the early history of property by the remarkable work of Sohm, *The Constitutional and Judicial System of the Frankish Empire* <note>original: "Fränkische Reichs-und Gerichtsverfassung"</note>.

The results obtained by the specialized methods of G. L. Von Maurer have, in the meantime, been verified by comparison with phenomena discovered in the most unexpected places. The researches of M. de Laveleye, in particular, have been conducted over a very wide field. Although I disagree with some of the economic conclusions he has reached, I cannot speak too highly of the value of the materials he has collected and described in his recently published volume entitled *Property and its Primitive Forms* <note>original: "La Propriété et ses Formes Primitives"</note>.
