# THE PROPORTIONS OF THE FACE AND HEAD

->Observations on the measurements of the human figure<-

The distance from the chin to the base of the nose is exactly one-third of the face. <note>Leonardo frequently used "thirds" as a modular system for facial harmony.</note> The nose itself, from its base to the meeting of the eyebrows, forms the second third. Finally, the forehead, from the eyebrows to the hairline, completes the third part.

<margin>The ear is as long as the nose.</margin>

If you divide the whole head into degrees of height, you will find that the space between the eyes is equal to the width of one eye. <insert>This rule applies to the ideal form, though nature often varies.</insert>

### TABLE OF MEASUREMENTS
| PART OF THE HEAD | PROPORTIONAL VALUE |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Total height of the head** | One-eighth of the total body height |
| **Width across the temples** | Equal to the length of the face |
| **From the mouth to the chin** | One-quarter of the face's lower third |

<column-break/>

The movement of the eyelid is governed by the muscles of the brow. When the eye closes, the upper lid descends further than the lower lid rises. <note>Leonardo's anatomical studies were often precursors to his paintings, helping him capture realistic expressions.</note>

<gloss>The center of the pupil aligns with the corner of the mouth when the face is at rest.</gloss>

->The beauty of a face resides in the perfect balance of these parts.<-

<term>Proportionality: The mathematical relationship between different parts of a whole. Leonardo believed that "Man is the model of the world" and that these ratios reflected a divine order.</term>

# THE ANATOMY OF THE ARM AND HAND

The <term>nerves<note>original: "nerbi"; in Leonardo's time, this term often conflated nerves, tendons, and muscles into a single functional system of movement.</note></term> that move the fingers of the right hand originate in the arm, and the <term>tendons<note>original: "corde"; literally "cords," referring to the tough, fibrous connective tissues.</note></term> originate in the nerves of the arm and fingers.

Regarding the fingers on the inside, at the palm of the hand, you will see that they originate from the bone of the arm. <note>Leonardo is observing how the flexor muscles and their tendons are anchored to the humerus or forearm bones.</note>

Observe how one bone of the arm moves over the other <note>A reference to the radius rotating over the ulna, allowing the hand to flip (pronation and supination).</note> from the arm outward.

You must first represent the bones, then the muscles that are attached to them, and finally the <term>veins<note>original: "vene"; Leonardo often used this term to describe both veins and arteries collectively as "vessels."</note></term> and nerves that provide life and movement.

->**The Distribution of the Vessels**<-

# On the Proportions of the Human Head

->**The Measurement of the Face**<-

From the chin to the top of the head is the sixteenth part of the height of a man.
From the outer corner of one eye to the other is as great a distance as the length of the eye itself.
The space between the eyes is equal to the width of one eye.
The ear is exactly as long as the nose.
The mouth is as wide as the distance from the bottom of the nose to the bottom of the chin.

<column-break/>

<margin>
Proportions of the ear
</margin>

The ear should be placed between the line of the eyebrows and the line of the base of the nose. 
<note>Leonardo frequently used these horizontal "landmarks" to ensure symmetry in his portraits.</note>
The width of the nose at the nostrils is the same as the width of one eye.
If you divide the face into three equal parts, the first part is from the bottom of the chin to the bottom of the nose; the second is from the bottom of the nose to the eyebrows; the third is from the eyebrows to the start of the hair.

<insert>Note: In the case of a bald man, use the highest wrinkle of the forehead as the boundary.</insert>

---

### Observations on the Profile

The distance from the tip of the nose to the ear is the same as the length of the ear itself. 
<gloss>This remains true regardless of the age of the subject.</gloss>
The depth of the head, from the nose to the back of the skull, is equal to the height of the head from the chin to the crown.

<note>original: "testa" (head) and "volto" (face). Leonardo distinguishes between the entire cranium and the features of the face.</note>

<term>Cranium: The skull, especially the part enclosing the brain.</term>
<term>Symmetry: The quality of being made up of exactly similar parts facing each other or around an axis.</term>

<summary>This page details the specific mathematical ratios of facial features, such as the eyes, nose, and ears, relative to the whole head. Leonardo establishes a tripartite division of the face that became a standard rule in Renaissance art theory.</summary>

### On the Motion of the Toes

The tendons <note>original: "corde"</note> that move the toes from the top side are those which, starting from the muscle <term>a b</term> <note>referring to the tibialis anterior or similar extensor muscle group</note>, pass through the ring of the ankle <note>the extensor retinaculum, a band of tissue that holds tendons in place like a bracelet</note> and go to the ends of the toes.

<margin>Note how the tension of the heel affects the arch.</margin>

When the foot is extended, the tendons are hidden; but when the foot is drawn upward <note>dorsiflexion</note>, these tendons become visible and hard like wood. 
<insert>This occurs because the muscle contracts and pulls the cords tight.</insert>

->The power of the foot is born in the calf.<-

It is necessary to represent the leg in four different aspects: from the front, from behind, and from the two sides. This is because the muscles of the leg are many, and if you do not show them from all sides, you will not be able to demonstrate their true origin and their true purpose <note>original: "offitio"</note>.

The muscle <term>m n</term> <note>likely the gastrocnemius or calf muscle</note> is of great strength because it must support the entire weight of the man when he stands on his toes. <gloss>This muscle is thicker in those who carry heavy burdens.</gloss>

<note>Leonardo is comparing the human leg to a mechanical crane, where the tendons act as ropes and the bones as rigid beams.</note>

If you pull the tendon <term>p</term>, you will see that all the toes are drawn together; but if you pull the tendon <term>q</term>, only the great toe will move. This proves that every part of the body has its own specific "motor" or source of movement.

### On the Intersection of Visual Rays

The air is filled with infinite pyramids, composed of radiating straight lines, which originate from the edges of the bodies placed within that air. The further they are from their source, the more these pyramids intersect and interweave, yet they never blend into one another <note>Leonardo argues that light rays from different objects can pass through the same point in space without interfering with each other.</note>. They travel through the air, spreading out independently, each representing the specific form of its origin.

---

#### The Geometry of the Pupil

If the object is larger than the pupil, the pyramid of its images will be truncated as it enters the eye. <margin>The intersection occurs at the center of the pupil.</margin> But if the object is smaller than the pupil, the pyramid will be complete. 

The <term>species: the visual essence or "likeness" of an object</term> of any large object, having passed through the narrowness of the pupil, will appear to flip. <note>Leonardo is grappling with the "camera obscura" effect, where images appear upside down after passing through a small aperture.</note> <insert>This is proven by the way shadows fall in a darkened room.</insert>

---

->**The Law of Perspective**<-

The size of the image of the object in the eye is determined by the angle of the rays. Every part of the object is in every part of the air, and the whole object is in every part of the air. <gloss>The whole in the whole.</gloss> Therefore, the eye, wherever it is positioned, can receive the form of the object.

<column-break/>

As the distance increases, the <term>pyramid of vision</term> narrows, which is why distant objects appear smaller to the sense. <margin>Distance diminishes the power of the species.</margin> 

If you place a screen with a small hole between the eye and the object, you will see that the images of the objects beyond the hole cross one another. The object on the right will appear on the left side of the screen, and the object on the left will appear on the right. <note>original: "il destro si fia sinistro". This observation is fundamental to Leonardo's understanding of the eye as an optical instrument.</note>

->On the nature of the air and the blue color of the sky.<-

The blue of the sky is not its own color, but is caused by heated moisture evaporated into minute and imperceptible particles, which draws to itself the rays of the sun and becomes luminous against the deep, intense darkness of the region of fire that lies beyond it. <note>Leonardo believed, following Aristotelian cosmology, that a "region of fire" existed above the atmosphere; he correctly identifies that the blue sky is a result of light scattering against a dark background.</note>

### Of the density of the air

The air is more dense the closer it is to the earth, and the more it rises, the thinner and more transparent it becomes. <margin>The air near the horizon is thicker.</margin> Therefore, when the sun is in the east, the mountains in the west appear of a beautiful blue color; this is because of the great quantity of air that lies between your eye and those mountains.

<column-break/>

If you see a mountain that appears very blue, it is a sign that there is a great distance between you and it. <gloss>Distance makes the blue deeper.</gloss> This happens because the air, being a thick medium, interposes itself and hides the true color of the object. <insert>The more air, the more blue.</insert>

->How to represent distance in painting<-

1.  The first thing is the reduction in the size of objects.
2.  The second is the fading of their colors.
3.  The third is the loss of the definition of their shapes and boundaries. <note>These three points form the basis of Leonardo's "three perspectives": linear perspective, color perspective, and the perspective of disappearance (sfumato).</note>

# The Principles of Mechanical Motion

### Introduction to the First Book

It is necessary, before we proceed to the construction of the engines themselves, to understand the primary forces that govern all movement. As the ancient masters observed, nothing moves of its own accord unless acted upon by an external weight or a hidden spring. <note>The author refers to the Aristotelian view of motion, which required a constant force to keep an object in motion.</note>

->**Of the Lever and the Balance**<-

The first principle we must consider is the <term>equilibrium</term> <note>a state of physical balance</note> of the lever. If a weight is placed at a distance from the center, it exerts a force proportional to that distance. <margin>See the diagrams on the following leaf for the ratios of weight to distance.</margin>

<column-break/>

**Table of Proportions**

| Distance from Center | Weight Required | Resulting Force |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 Part | 10 Pounds | Equal Balance |
| 2 Parts | 5 Pounds | Equal Balance |
| 5 Parts | 2 Pounds | Equal Balance |

<column-break/>

In this manner, a small child may lift a great stone, provided the beam is of sufficient length and the <term>fulcrum</term> <note>the pivot point on which a lever rests</note> is placed with precision. <insert>This was famously noted by Archimedes of Syracuse.</insert> <note>original: "Dos pou sto kai tan gan kinaso" — Give me a place to stand and I will move the earth.</note>

We shall now examine how these linear forces are converted into circular motion through the use of the <term>toothed wheel</term> <note>a gear</note>. <gloss>Note: the teeth must be carved from seasoned pearwood to prevent shrinking.</gloss>

# On the Construction of the Great Dome

It is necessary to consider the **weight of the masonry** and the outward thrust exerted upon the supporting pillars. If the base is not sufficiently reinforced with iron chains <note>A common Renaissance technique to prevent the dome from spreading outward</note>, the entire structure will succumb to the force of its own gravity.

->**The Proportion of the Pillars**<-

The pillars must be of such a thickness that they can sustain not only the vertical load but also the lateral pressure. <margin>See the calculations for the Cathedral of Florence</margin> As we have observed in the ancient ruins of Rome <note>Likely referring to the Pantheon or the Basilica of Maxentius</note>, the strength of the vault depends entirely upon the stability of the foundation.

<column-break/>

When the elevation is viewed from the ground, the *optical distortion* must be corrected by increasing the height of the upper windows. <insert>This was also the opinion of the Master of Works</insert> Without this adjustment, the dome will appear squat and unpleasing to the eye of the spectator.

The use of a double shell <gloss>a structure with two layers</gloss> allows for a lighter construction while providing a walkway for the workmen. We must ensure that the ribs <note>original: "coste"</note> are aligned perfectly with the primary axes of the building to distribute the weight evenly.

# On the Perfection of the Lantern and the Curvature

The **Lantern** <note>The small structure atop a dome that lets in light and provides a decorative finish</note> must be proportioned to the mass of the dome so that it does not appear as a heavy burden, yet it must be heavy enough to compress the ribs and stabilize the crown. If the weight at the summit is insufficient, the ribs may spring outward, causing cracks in the masonry.

->**The Geometry of the Arch**<-

To achieve the most graceful and strong profile, the architect should employ the *pointed arch* <note>original: "quinto acuto"</note> rather than the semi-circle. <margin>Refer to the proportions of the ancients</margin> As the Greeks demonstrated in their temples <note>A reference to the mathematical harmony found in classical architecture</note>, the beauty of a structure is found in the ratio of its parts to the whole.

<column-break/>

The curvature must be checked daily with a wooden template <insert>The master mason must oversee this personally</insert> to ensure that no deviation occurs during the drying of the mortar. <gloss>The binding agent of lime and sand</gloss> Even a small error at the base will result in a great deformity at the top, rendering the closure of the eye <note>The circular opening at the top of the dome, or oculus</note> impossible.

### Table of Materials for the Upper Works

| Material Type | Intended Use | Quality Required |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Travertine Stone | Base of the Lantern | Fine-grained and without veins |
| Lead Sheets | External Covering | Thick and well-beaten |
| Oak Timber | Internal Scaffolding | Seasoned for three years |

The placement of the copper ball <note>A traditional finishing element for the very top of a cathedral dome</note> shall be the final act. It must be gilded with the finest gold so that it reflects the light of the sun, acting as a beacon for the city. We must pray that the foundations remain steadfast under this immense glory.
