**E**ver since that Herculean and stupendous labor of the Masoretes <note>the Jewish scribes and scholars between the 6th and 10th centuries who standardized the text of the Hebrew Bible</note> became known to Christians, it has not met with the same reception nor the same judgments. It has been praised by some and blamed by others. Among the Roman Catholics, the judgment of Gilbert Genebrard in his *Preface to the Commentary on the Psalms* is as follows: *The Hebrews are holy, they are theologians; they wrote without vowel points or marks, expressing their thoughts in pure and simple letters. The Masoretes are their profane interpreters, mere Grammarians; through their distinctions, accents, marks, and points, they have partly illuminated the text and partly obscured it; as interpreters are sometimes wont to do, being more devoted to their own imaginations and feelings, or more eager for glory, so that they might seem to say something not yet said, and to acquire fame for themselves by disparaging the ancients, even if those ancients shone with the illumination of the Holy Spirit.* Indeed, in his *Commentary on Psalm 78:47*, he charges them with manifest impiety. Nor were the censors Delrio, Marin, and others any milder. Among the Reformed, Louis Cappel, in his *Defense of Sacred Criticism*, deems that the Masoretes could have omitted that entire study without any loss; and in his *The Secret of Punctuation Revealed, Book 1, Chapter 17*, and elsewhere, he attributes to them carelessness, thoughtlessness, and <term>ablepsia <note>original Greek: ἀβλεψίαν; a lack of sight or a failure to notice errors</note></term>. # CHAPTER I.
## ON THE NAME AND DEFINITION OF THE MASORA.

§. 1.

Since names are the "vehicles" <note>Greek: *ochemata*</note> of things, it does not seem foreign to our present discussion if, before we approach the matter itself and the "citadel" <note>Greek: *akropolis*, meaning the central stronghold or core argument</note> of the cause, we examine the name itself. Therefore, *Masora* [מסורה] or *Masoret* [מסורת]—for although others use different forms, namely *Masoret*, *Masoreta*, and *Masoret* <note>original: מסורת / מסורתא & מסורת</note>, these are not as common, as Buxtorf <note>Johannes Buxtorf the Elder (1564–1629), author of the foundational work *Tiberias* on the Masora</note> notes in *Tiberias, book 1, chapter 1*—comes from the root *masar* [מסר] "to hand down" or "to deliver." It signifies any tradition, whether it be the physical handing over of a material object from hand to hand, or the communication of a concept conceived in the mind through the teacher's voice. Thus, its counterpart is *Kabbalah* [קבלה], "acceptance," from the root *kibel* [קבל], "he received" from others. In Greek, this would be called *paradosis* [παράδοσις, meaning tradition].

§. 2. But since this very name is broader than the thing being signified—for any tradition of the elders concerning any subject can be called "Masora" or "the tradition of the sages" [מסורת חכמים]—it is clear that in our present work the word *Masora* is used as a synecdoche <note>A figure of speech where a part represents the whole or a general term is used for a specific one</note>. Specifically, it signifies for us the critical doctrine regarding the sacred text handed down by the Hebrew sages.

Regarding this sense, Aben-Esra <note>Abraham ibn Ezra (c. 1089–1167), a distinguished Spanish Jewish scholar, philosopher, and biblical commentator</note> says in *The Foundation of Awe*: <note>original Hebrew: האמרו כי יש שכר לפעולות בעלי המסורת שהם כשומרי חומות העיר כי בעבורם עמדה תורת יי׳ וספרי הקדש על מתכנתם בלי תוספת ומגרעת</note> that is: *“Certainly, a great reward is due to the authors of the Masora, who are like the guardians of the city walls; for because of them, the Law of the Lord and the Holy Books remain in their proper form, without any addition or subtraction.”* Compare also Elias Levita <note>Elias Levita (1469–1549), a renowned Hebrew grammarian who taught many Christian scholars during the Renaissance</note> in the *Tradition of the Masora, Preface 3, page 27.*

§. 4. So much for the "What's in a Name"; now follows the "What is the Thing itself." We approve of the definition of the Masora provided by Buxtorf <note>Johannes Buxtorf the Elder, whose work "Tiberias" became the standard reference for the Masora in the 17th century</note> in *book 1, chapter 2*: *“The Masora is a Critical doctrine, ingeniously invented by the ancient Hebrew sages regarding the Hebrew text of <term>Holy Scripture</term> <note>original Latin: SSæ, an abbreviation for Sacræ Scripturæ</note>, by which its verses, words, and letters are numbered, and every variation among them is noted and indicated in their proper places along with the recitation of individual verses; thus, its constant and genuine reading is preserved, and it is eternally protected and fortified against all corruption or change.”*

§. 5. In this present definition, we propose to consider its *category*—namely, a *doctrine*—and not just any kind, but a *critical* one, since the Masora does not concern itself with the explanation of the theological matters of <term>Holy Scripture</term> itself, but only with the characteristics and accidental features of the words. Then, the *distinction* is noted, which is drawn from its causes. The *efficient* causes (the creators) of the Masora are, of course, the ancient Hebrew sages.
