<meta>This page continues the dedication to Cardinal Gabriele Paleotti. It develops the argument that human beings are divinely intended to serve the common good, specifically praising those who write books for the benefit of future generations.</meta>

...each person might be able to benefit not only himself, but also others as much as possible. For this reason, it has come to pass that those who were concerned only with their own well-being <note>original: "salute," which can mean safety, health, or spiritual salvation.</note> and not that of others, have always been rebuked by everyone and held worthy of being justly banished from human society. 

On the other hand, those who have worked zealously to deserve well of others, that learned Antiquity <note>referring to the classical Greek and Roman world</note> judged worthy of all praise; indeed, it even placed them among the number of the Gods. And truly, who would not gladly praise and admire that class of men who have directed all the powers of their intellect toward the goal of providing not only for the people of the present through their teaching, but also for posterity through carefully written books? 

Among their number, without any controversy, must be placed first those supreme Theologians <note>scholars who study the nature of God and religious truth</note> who have written so many and such great works, no less learnedly than diligently, so that men might [be moved] both to worship God piously and to uniquely love their neighbor—

<meta>catchword: dum (part of "diligendum," meaning "to be loved")</meta>

<summary>Menghi argues that humans are social beings meant to help one another, praising authors and theologians who record their wisdom in books to guide future generations toward piety and charity.</summary>
