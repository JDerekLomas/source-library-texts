## The Huan Ritual Stand <note>original: "Huan Zu"; a ceremonial stand used for holding meat during sacrifices, characterized by its plain, unbraced legs</note>

The *Classic of Rites* <note>original: "Li Ji"</note> records that the Youyu clan <note>the lineage of the legendary sage-king Shun, roughly 2200 BCE</note> used the *Huan* stand. 
Its four legs are shaped like those of a side table. It measures two feet and four inches in length, and stands one foot high. The two ends are finished in red lacquer, while the center is black.

---

## The Jue Ritual Stand <note>original: "Jue Zu"; a ritual stand distinguished by a horizontal support beam</note>

The *Classic of Rites* records that the Xia Dynasty <note>original: "Xiahou Shi"; China's first traditional dynasty, founded by Yu the Great</note> used the *Jue* stand. 
It resembles the *Huan* stand in overall form but includes an additional horizontal beam of wood. This piece serves as a brace <term>ju; a structural crossbar or 'spur' used to reinforce the stability of the legs</term> at the base of the legs.

### The Qi Ritual Stand <note>original: "Qi"; a ceremonial stand used for holding meat during the Shang Dynasty</note>

The *Book of Rites* <note>original: "Li Ji"</note> states that the ritual meat stand <term>zu; a low table or pedestal used to hold sacrificial meats during offerings</term> of the Shang Dynasty <note>approx. 1600–1046 BCE</note> is called the *Qi*. The name is pronounced the same as the word for a carpenter's square <note>original: "ju"</note>. 

The legs of this stand are curved and bent. This form is derived from the *Zhiqi* tree <note>the Japanese Raisin Tree, *Hovenia dulcis*</note>, the branches of which are known for being particularly crooked and winding. The legs of the Shang ritual stand are shaped to resemble these natural curves.

---

### The Altar Stand <note>original: "Jin"; literally "prohibition," a ritual tray or low altar designed to hold wine vessels, intended to serve as a reminder against excessive drinking</note>

The *Book of Rites* records that for the ritual offerings of Great Officers and Scholars <note>original: "Dafu" and "Shi"; the middle and lower tiers of the ancient Chinese aristocracy</note>, the *Jin* stand is used. 

It measures four feet in length and two feet four inches in width. Including the overall frame and the legs, it stands three inches high. The center of the stand is finished in red lacquer.

# The Ritual Fork and the Earthenware Steamer

### The Earthenware Steamer <note>original: "Wa Yan"; a ceramic vessel used for steaming grains or meat during sacred rites</note>

The *Book of Rites* <note>original: "Li Ji"</note> specifies that for the sacrifices made to Heaven, an **earthenware steamer** must be used. 

Its capacity is three *qi* <note>a ritual measurement of volume</note>, which is equivalent to five *dou* <note>a unit of measure; approximately 10 liters in modern terms</note>. The mouth of the vessel has a diameter of **one foot**, and the belly of the vessel stands **two inches** high.

---

### The Ritual Fork <note>original: "Bi"; a long-handled, fork-like tool used to stabilize or lift large pieces of meat during ceremonies</note>

The chapter "The Special Victim for the Suburban Sacrifice" <note>original: "Jiao Te Sheng"</note> in the *Book of Rites* states: 

The **Ritual Master** <term>Zongren; a high-ranking official responsible for overseeing ancestral temple protocols and religious etiquette</term> enters first, holding the **ritual fork**. At the moment the Host <note>the primary celebrant or ruler performing the sacrifice</note> lifts the sacrificial meat, the Ritual Master raises the fork to assist the Host in lifting the offering.

# The Ritual Serving Stand <note>original: "Si"; while this term often refers to a small ritual spoon, the dimensions provided here describe a large ceremonial tray or table used for presenting offerings</note>

The "Jade Beads" <note>original: "Yu Zao"</note> chapter of the *Book of Rites* states:
When a **Great Officer** <term>Dafu; a high-ranking member of the aristocracy or a senior administrative official in ancient China</term> uses a side-vessel, a **serving stand** is employed. 

It is **four feet** long, **two feet four inches** wide, and **five inches** deep. It is used for **setting out the food offerings** <note>original: "Chen Zhuan"; the formal arrangement of sacrificial meats and grains for the spirits</note>.

# The Cauldron Covers <note>original: "Mao" or "𦵩"; specifically referring to covers made from aquatic plants like water-shields or reeds</note>

The *Book of Rites* notes that **vessel covers** <note>original: "Mi"; protective lids or mats for ritual bronze vessels</note> are made either by bundling or by weaving. 

For the **covering of the ritual tripod cauldrons**, one uses **water-shield plants**. If the stalks are long, the **base is bundled**; if they are short, they are **woven through the middle**.

# The Tripod and the Cauldron
<note>original: "Qi" (錡) and "Fu" (釜)</note>

->[Vessel illustrations]<-
<note>The illustration depicts two sturdy vessels used for boiling. On the left is the 'Qi', a cauldron supported by three legs. On the right is the 'Fu', a round-bottomed pot without legs. Both are shown with long-handled stirring implements or strainers resting inside them.</note>

The **cauldron** and the **tripod** are both vessels used for boiling. A vessel without legs is called a **cauldron** (*fu*); one with legs is called a **tripod** (*qi*). 

The ***Examination of Ancient History*** <note>original: "Gushi Kao" (古史考), a 3rd-century historical text by Qiao Zhou</note> states: "The **Yellow Emperor** <note>the legendary ancestor of the Chinese people</note> first created the **cauldron** and the **steaming pot** (*zeng*). From this, the practice of **cooking food with fire** was established."

The ***Commentary on the Trigrams*** of the ***I Ching*** <note>original: "Yi Shuogua" (易説卦)</note> says: "**Kun** <term>the trigram representing Earth and the receptive principle</term> **is the cauldron**." <note>This symbolic link suggests the cauldron, like the Earth, is a vessel that contains, sustains, and transforms life-giving substances.</note>

The ***Guangya*** <note>an early 3rd-century dictionary</note> lists several related vessels: 
- The **Shuo** <gloss>pronounced like the combination of *di* and *dian*</gloss>
- The **Bing** <gloss>pronounced *bing*</gloss>
- The **Li** <gloss>pronounced like *hu* or *fu*</gloss> <note>a specific tripod with hollow legs for better heat distribution</note>
- The **Xuan**, **Lu**, **Xuan**, and **Ao** <gloss>pronounced *man* and *deng*</gloss> <note>various regional or specialized names for shallow pans and kettles</note>
- The **Yan** <gloss>pronounced *ci* and *gui* combined</gloss>

The ***Shuowen*** <note>original: "Shuowen Jiezi" (説文解字), the first dictionary of Chinese characters from the 2nd century</note> records the names of the **cauldron** and **tripod** using the variant characters *fu* and *qiao*, noting they belong to the same category of metalwares.

<note>original: "Yan" (筵)</note>

<note>original: *Tong hufu zhi* (銅虎符制). The tiger tally was a physical authorization device used from the Warring States period through the Han dynasty. Cast in the shape of a tiger and split vertically, one half was held by the emperor and the other by a local commander.</note>

->**Interior of the Tiger Tally**<-
<note>original: *Hufu yin* (虎符陰). This refers to the inner, flat side of the tally half where the two pieces would join. <term>Bronze Tiger Tally</term>

<term>Military Tally</term>
<note>original: *Bingfu* (兵符). A general category of command tokens, which could be made of wood, bamboo, or metal, and shaped like tigers, dragons, or other auspicious beasts.</note>

# The Han Dynasty Knife-Pen <note>original: *Hàn dāobǐ* (漢刀筆)</note>

The form of this object is made of metal, resembling a small knife or dagger <note>original: *dāobǐ* (刀匕)</note>. Between the handle sections, there is a space where a cord may be threaded and knotted, as this was an implement intended to be worn as a pendant on a ceremonial sash <note>original: *pánpèi* (鞶佩)</note>. 

In ancient times, when bamboo slips and wooden tablets <term>Jiandie (簡牒): the primary writing surfaces in China before the widespread use of paper</term> were used, everyone carried a knife-pen with them so they could scrape away characters to correct mistakes. The *Classic of Poetry* <note>The *Shijing*</note> says: "How should I not long to return home? But I fear the records on these bamboo slips." This suggests that "scraping writing" <note>using a knife to shave the surface of wood or bamboo to erase ink</note> was already a practice as far back as the Three Dynasties <note>The Xia, Shang, and Zhou periods</note>.

The "Appraisal" section of the *History of the Western Han* mentions that the great statesmen Xiao He and Cao Shen both began their careers as "knife-pen officials" <note>clerical scribes</note> under the Qin dynasty. Thus, these tools were used continuously from the Qin through the Han dynasty. 

However, during the Qin period, General Meng Tian had already invented the writing brush. The reason the term "knife-pen" was still used during the Han dynasty is likely because the new technology had not yet completely replaced the old <gloss>some of the old tools were still in use</gloss>.

It notes that while the hair brush was invented by the Qin dynasty, the knife-pen remained a standard symbol of the clerical office well into the Han period.</summary>
