### Emerald Cloud Grass

Emerald Cloud Grass grows among mountain rocks. Its stems are as fine as silk, and its tiny leaves are a fresh, verdant green, appearing quite lovely. The *Assembly of Fragrant Plants* <note>original: "Qunfang Pu"; a comprehensive botanical encyclopedia compiled during the Ming Dynasty</note> includes a record of it. Many people cultivate it in stone displays <note>original: "shigong"; these are miniature rockeries or scholars' stones used for indoor decoration</note> and in shaded, damp locations for their aesthetic enjoyment. 

In Jiangxi, local traditional doctors refer to it as **Dragon’s Whisker** <note>original: "Longxu"</note>. In southern Yunnan <note>original: "Diannan"</note>, it is known as **Sword Cypress** <note>original: "Jianbai"</note>. These practitioners all state that it has the medicinal property of being able to relax the sinews and meridians <term>Relax the Sinews and Meridians</term> <note>original: "Shu jin luo"; a traditional therapeutic concept referring to the relief of muscle tension, joint stiffness, and the promotion of "qi" and blood flow through the body's pathways.</note>.

<summary>This entry describes the Emerald Cloud Grass (Selaginella uncinata), noting its delicate beauty, its popularity in scholars' rock gardens, and its various regional names and medicinal uses for relieving tension.</summary>

### Adder’s-tongue Fern

The Adder’s-tongue fern grows among the mountain rocks of Yunnan. # Clubmoss

<term>Clubmoss (石松): Known in Chinese as *Shi Song* (literally "Stone Pine"), this is not a true pine but a lycophyte—a primitive vascular plant that reproduces via spores. In traditional medicine, it is often called "Stretching Tendon Herb" (*Shujincao*).</term>

**Clubmoss** grows upon the rocks of deep mountains. Its creeping stems trail across the stone surfaces, sending out small, branching stalks that resemble the needles of a **pine tree**, though they are much finer and softer to the touch. It remains a vibrant green throughout the four seasons, never withering even in the cold of winter.

<note>The author, Wu Qijun, likely observed these in the humid, mountainous regions of southern China where *Lycopodium japonicum* is prevalent. Its evergreen nature led to various folk names suggesting longevity and resilience.</note>

The plant is frequently harvested by mountain dwellers. When dried, the entire herb is utilized for its medicinal properties, particularly for "dispelling wind and dampness" <note>A traditional medical concept referring to the relief of joint pain and stiffness caused by environmental factors</note>. 

<gloss>Stone Pine, Ten Thousand Year Green, Stretching Tendon Herb, Mountain Pine</gloss>

### Clubmoss

<term>Clubmoss (石松): Literally "Stone Pine" (Shi Song). A primitive vascular plant (lycophyte) that resembles a miniature pine tree. It is valued in traditional medicine for its ability to "relax the sinews" and improve circulation.</term>

**Clubmoss** grows among the mountain rocks of **Yunnan** <note>A province in Southwest China known for its immense biodiversity</note>. It is a low-growing herb with a large, substantial root system. Its long leaves are densely clustered together, bearing a striking resemblance to the needles of the **Buddhist Pine** <note>original: *Luohansong* (Podocarpus macrophyllus), a tree often used in classical Chinese gardens</note>. As the leaves shed or peel away, the thick stem is left with a rough, textured surface; these scars are as coarse as the teeth of a **file** <note>original: *cuò* (錯), a metalworking tool used for rasping or grinding</note>.

## Golden Silk Dwarf Ta-Ta

<term>Golden Silk Dwarf Ta-Ta (金絲矮它它): A regional name, likely from Yunnan, for a species of epiphytic fern. The "Golden Silk" refers to the lustrous, hair-like scales on its rhizome, while "Dwarf Ta-Ta" is a colloquialism for its low, spreading growth habit. It is identified by modern botanists as a member of the *Davallia* genus, often called the "Rabbit's Foot Fern" in the West.</term>

**Golden Silk Dwarf Ta-Ta** grows upon the stone cliffs of **Yunnan** <note>A province in Southwest China known for its diverse mountainous terrain</note>. Its root <gloss>rhizome</gloss> is about as thick as a finger and is completely covered in a dense, soft coating of yellow hairs that shimmer with a metallic luster like gold. 

The leaves sprout directly from this creeping root; they grow to be four or five inches long. The foliage is finely divided and intricate, bearing a close resemblance to a miniature **Drynaria fern** <note>original: *Gushibu* (骨碎補), literally "Mender of Broken Bones," a common medicinal fern</note>, though it is significantly more delicate and tender in appearance. This plant is evergreen and does not wither even during the cold winter months. 

<note>The shimmering "golden" roots described here are a hallmark of the Davalliaceae family, where the rhizomes are protected by chaffy scales that can appear silver or gold depending on the species.</note>

### Golden Silk Dwarf Tuotuo

<term>**Golden Silk Dwarf Tuotuo** (金絲矮陀陀): A regional name for a species of small fern, likely a member of the *Davallia* family. ## Peperomia
<note>original: *doubanlü* (豆瓣綠), literally "Bean-Petal Green." In modern botany, this name often refers to *Peperomia blanda* or similar succulent herbs found in humid, rocky environments.</note>

Peperomia grows among the mountain rocks of Yunnan. It is a small herb reaching only a few inches in height, with stems and leaves that are green and brittle. Every four leaves cluster together to form a layer; each leaf is about the size of a split bean <note>original: *douban* (豆瓣)</note>, thick and succulent, resembling the "Buddha’s Fingernail" stonecrop <note>original: *fozhijia* (佛指甲), referring to species of *Sedum*.</note>. At the tips of the stems, it produces small spikes a few fractions of an inch long, which are also brittle.

Local physicians say its nature is "cold" and it is used to treat injuries from falls and strikes. In Shunning <note>modern-day Fengqing County, Yunnan</note>, a medicinal paste is prepared from it, which sometimes proves effective. However, in Southern Yunnan, any herb with nourishing or restorative properties is labeled a "Deer-Lick" herb <note>original: *luxian* (鹿銜), literally "held in the deer's mouth," based on the legend that deer find these herbs to heal their own wounds.</note>. Such claims are often exaggerated and not entirely trustworthy; nevertheless, I have preserved the recipe here for reference.

### Six-Ingredient Deer-Lick Herb Paste

The ingredients for the Six-Ingredient Deer-Lick herb were formerly gathered in Shunning County from shaded caves, forest crags, and rocky ledges. One must collect:
1. Bean-Petal Deer-Lick
2. Purple-Back Deer-Lick
3. Rock-Back Deer-Lick
4. Dendrobium-style Deer-Lick <note>original: *shihu* (石斛)</note>
5. Bamboo-Leaf Deer-Lick
6. Roe-Deer-Back Deer-Lick

Take these six varieties and add Poria <term>Poria cocos (*fuling*), a medicinal fungus used to drain dampness and support the digestive system</term>. Boil the mixture over a fire of mulberry wood. After removing the dregs, add other specific medicines and simmer for one full day and night. Finally, stir in rock sugar to thicken it into a paste.

The nature of this paste is balanced and mild; it can be overcome and taken by men, women, the elderly, and the young alike. While using it, one must avoid sour or cold foods. 

- To treat "phlegm-fire" <note>a traditional medical category involving heat and respiratory or emotional congestion</note>, take with taro-root wine. 
- For elderly weakness, dizziness, or blurred vision, take with a decoction of dried longan and red dates. 
- For children with congenital deficiencies or the "Five Exhaustions and Seven Injuries" <term>a traditional collective term for various chronic internal injuries and wasting conditions</term>, take mixed with grain spirit. 
- For those who have been bedridden with long-term illness and find it difficult to rise, take with a broth of longan, red dates, Poria, and ginger. 

If this paste is taken over a long period, it promotes longevity and turns graying hair and beards back to black.

# Herbal Blood-Clotter

**Herbal Blood-Clotter** <note>original: *Cao Xue Jie* (草血竭), *Polygonum paleaceum*</note>, also known as **Turning-Back Herb** <note>original: *Hui Tou Cao* (回頭草)</note>, grows among the mountain rocks of Yunnan. Its tangled roots are as fine as a bundle of hair, black in color, and grow horizontally. It has long petioles <note>leaf stalks</note> and long leaves that slightly resemble the **Stone-Leather Fern** <note>original: *Shi Wei* (石韋), *Pyrrosia*</note>, though they are softer. The surface of the leaf is green, while the underside is pale, and the stalk is tinged with a slight purple. In the spring, it sends up a flowering stem which blooms into spikes, looking much like the flowers of the **Small White Knotweed** <note>original: *Xiao Bai Liao* (小白蓼)</note>.

According to the **Materia Medica of Yunnan** <note>original: *Dian Bencao* (滇本草), a 15th-century regional herbal by Lan Mao</note>, its flavor is pungent and bitter, with a slightly astringent quality. Its nature is "warm." It is used to relax the chest and abdomen, aid digestion, and dissolve "masses" <note>original: *pi* (痞), refers to a feeling of fullness or a physical lump in the abdomen</note>. It treats stomach pain, edema caused by "cold-dampness," abdominal masses <note>original: *zheng jia* (癥瘕), traditional terms for fixed and movable internal tumors or growths</note>, and blood stagnation.

<gloss>For masses and abdominal accumulations in both men and women: take one *qian* <note>a traditional unit of weight, approx. 3.75 grams</note> of Herbal Blood-Clotter, roast it and grind it into a powder. Take this with granulated sugar and warm wine. For patients with "excessive Qi" <note>indicating a robust or overactive constitution</note>, add **Betel Nut** <note>original: *Bin Lang* (檳榔)</note> and **Lindera Root** <note>original: *Tai Wu* (台烏)</note>.</gloss>

For edema caused by cold-dampness: take Herbal Blood-Clotter, **Fennel Root** <note>original: *Hui Xiang Gen* (茴香根)</note>, and **Black Cardamom** <note>original: *Cao Guo Zi* (草果子)</note>. Grind these together into a fine powder. Cook this mixture with **Ricefish** <note>original: *Jiang Yu* (鱂魚), a small freshwater fish</note> and eat it. Taking this three or four times will prove effective.
