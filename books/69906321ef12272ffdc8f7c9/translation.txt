Regarding what is observed by sight, we say this can be proved in no other way than by those who are privy to it, combined with the positive proofs and presumptions mentioned previously.

We do not claim that people who are completely lacking in knowledge or a natural conscience should be admitted as witnesses in any case; such as infants, the insane, or the mentally impaired <note>original: "Foolish Persons"</note>, etc. Nor do we argue that Thomas Lindsay or Christian Shaw <note>Christian Shaw (1685–1737) was the central accuser in the Paisley witch trials; Thomas Lindsay was a young "confessant" or witness.</note>, who are minors, should be required to swear an oath. Instead, they should only be examined separately before the court using specific questions. Through this, it may be determined whether or not their accounts agree with the four other confessing witnesses who are to testify <note>original: "depone"</note> before them. This is to the prisoner’s advantage in the event that their stories do not match. However, we insist that any person above the age of legal majority <note>original: "Nonage"</note> who demonstrates significant knowledge and a natural conscience should be accepted as a witness. This provides a sufficient basis for the credibility we require in this case, which is already nearly fully proven.

As for the second point, we shall make this as clear as the noonday sun: first, from reason and the nature of the matter; second, from the unanimous judgment of lawyers in all nations and ages; third, from our own customs and legal decisions; and fourth, from the unique circumstances of this specific case.

Regarding the first: traveling to and from meetings (especially on foot), falling down and worshipping the Devil appearing there in a physical <note>original: "Corporal"</note> shape (the same shape he took when he tempted our Savior to do so), the frequent murdering of children with a cord and napkin, and the tormenting of others with pins and similar items, are all plain objects of the senses. Therefore, the senses are to be believed concerning them. [Witnesses] are to be trusted until it is proved that the occurrence is impossible or that the witness is an impostor. It is part of the bargain <note>original: "Purchase"</note> witches make with the Devil that they cannot be seen on certain occasions. Consequently, the abominations committed then would remain unpunished if such witnesses were not admitted. It is unlikely that witches (who of all criminals are most reluctant to confess) would risk losing their own lives by testifying <note>original: "deponing"</note> against others for whom they have no personal grudge, or even for whom they have particular affection—as several of the prisoners are relatives of the witnesses.

Nor does the Devil have any specific reason to incite them to do this; for several of the prisoners have confessed to other horrible <note>original: "execrable"</note> crimes, and it cannot be assumed that Satan would work against himself <note>A reference to Mark 3:23–26: "If Satan rises up against himself and is divided, he cannot stand."</note>. In His usual care for the world <note>original: "ordinary Providence"</note>, God ensures that public trials are protected, so that the special power of the enemy of justice [Satan] ceases in those matters. This is evident because witches are unable to do further harm or escape after God’s ministers [the magistrates] begin to act against Satan’s agents through imprisonment.

Finally, the strangeness of the crimes, the combination of circumstantial evidence <note>original: "Presumptions"</note>, and the facts of the case—where these confessing witnesses (even if they didn't know the facts otherwise) agree with other undeniable evidence—sufficiently add to their credibility. Just as falsehood is never assumed (since it is a crime), a person found to be telling the truth in many things is presumed to continue doing so until the opposite is proven.

As for the second point, an accomplice in a crime <note>original: *Socius Criminis*</note> is admitted as a witness if the crime is heinous <note>original: *nefandum*</note> (Giacomo Menochius, *On the Arbitrary Questions of Judges*, book 2, case 474, number 27 and following); or if the crime is hidden <note>original: *occultum*</note> and the truth cannot be found elsewhere (Giuseppe Mascardus, Volume 1, Conclusion 466, Number 6); or if it is difficult to prove (Prospero Farinacci, Book 2, *Criminal Works*).

# WITCHCRAFT, &c.

*Titles 6, Question 63, Number 18; Mascardus, Volume 3, Conclusion 1360, Number 4; Menochius, Book 2, Case 116, Book 1, Question 58: Regarding crimes committed during the night, which are said to be difficult to prove <note>original: "nocturno tempore commissa quæ difficilis dicuntur Probationis"</note>. Boerius, Decision 68, Number 6; Menochius, Case 116, Number 14: And therefore not only are presumptions and conjectural evidence sufficient, but even unqualified witnesses are admitted <note>original: "non solum præsumptio & conjecturata probatio sufficit, verum inhabiles admittuntur"</note>. Farinacci, Book 2, Title 6, Question 55, Number 40; Mascardus, Book 2, Conclusion 1124, Number 13: The same applies to crimes committed in the wilderness, woods, mountains, or other secret places <note>original: "Idem in delictis commissis in Eremo, Nemore, Monte aliove loco secreto"</note>. Gomez, Various Resolutions, Volume 3, Book 12, Number 21; Farinacci, Title 6, Question 62, Number 55: But a crime is not called "hidden" simply because no one was actually present, but because the nature of the crime or the circumstances of the place and time mean that other qualified witnesses could not have been present <note>original: "Sed occultum non dicitur, quod actu non Intervenerint, at quod de Natura delicti vel Ratione loci & temporis alii testes habiles intervenire non poterint"</note>—such as witchcraft <note>original: "Maleficium"</note>, in which an accomplice <note>original: "socius Criminis"</note> is admitted to testify. Menochius, Book 2, Chapter 5, Case 474, Number 33; Campegio, Regarding Witnesses, Rule 86; Fallew. 6; Crotus, Regarding Witnesses, Part 4, Number 97; Oldendorp, Regarding Witnesses, Title: On the Categories of Witnesses, Number 21.*

In a word, all lawyers who have written specialized treatises on witchcraft in Germany, Italy, Louvain, France, and Spain, etc., conclude that unfit <term>Archaic legal term "inhabil" referring to witnesses disqualified by age, character, or involvement in the crime.</term> witnesses, and particularly accomplices, are to be admitted in witchcraft trials. Only the strictest of them believe that this admission should be "with caution" <note>original: "cum Nota"</note>, or as Delrio expresses it in the passage cited for the prisoners: *Upon this evidence alone <note>original: "ex his solis"</note>, the judge is not to condemn, nor do we require it.*

Regarding the third point: We have the testimony of our famous King James VI in his *Daemonologie* (Book 3, final chapter). He tells us it is our law that boys, girls, and disreputable persons <note>original: "Infamous Persons"</note> are not to be rejected in witchcraft cases any more than they would be in cases of treason <note>original: "human lese Majesty"</note>. This is true even if they claim others were present at imaginary meetings, because this assumes they have already entered into a prior contract <note>a pact with the Devil</note>. He says that the Devil’s Mark <note>a spot on the body thought to be insensitive to pain</note> and the inability to cry are compelling aids to discovery. He provides the example of a girl who, after naming several witches while in fits <note>seizures or trances</note>, saw them all condemned based on other supporting circumstantial evidence <note>original: "concurring Presumptions"</note>.
