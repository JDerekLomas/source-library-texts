<margin>27</margin> They pass to and fro: these they call by the Greek name **daemons** <note>original: "δαίμονας" (daimonas). Through these same beings, as Plato asserts in his *Symposium*, all prophecies and the various miracles of magicians and all types of omens are <margin>134</margin> governed. <margin>41 XIII</margin>
Who among the gods has ever repented of former things? For a changed plan cannot follow without a weakening of preceding ones. Therefore, God ought to undergo no temporal exercise of either hatred or love, and for that reason should be touched neither by indignation nor by mercy, nor be contracted by any anguish, nor leap with any joy; rather, he should be free from all passions of the mind <note>The term "passions" here refers to the Stoic concept of *pathe*—disturbances or emotions that affect the soul's steady state.</note>, never grieving nor ever rejoicing, nor willing or nilling anything suddenly. But all these things and others of that kind rightly suit the **middle-nature** of daemons. For they are situated between us and the gods, as much in the location of their region as in the nature of their minds, having in common with those above immortality, and with those below, passion. For they can just as we do undergo all the soothing or inciting <margin>148</margin> movements of the soul: they may be incited by anger, moved by mercy, invited by gifts, softened by prayers, exasperated by insults, and soothed by honors, and in all other things vary in a manner similar to <margin>10</margin> <margin>42</margin> us. Indeed, to summarize the definition, daemons are: **living beings by genus, rational by intellect, subject to emotion by soul, aerial by body, and eternal by time.** <note>This is the most famous definition of daemons in Western philosophy, later cited extensively by St. Augustine.</note> Of these five attributes which I have mentioned, they share the first three with us from the beginning, the fourth is their own, and the last they hold in common with the immortal gods; yet they differ from the gods by their "passion" <note>Here "passion" (*passio*) means the capacity to feel emotion or be affected by external things.</note>. Therefore, I have not absurdly, I think, called them **subject to emotion**, because they are liable to the same disturbances of the <margin>15</margin> mind that we are.

## Critical Notes.

Since I have recently demonstrated at length—in the *Transactions of the Philological Society of Leipzig* edited by Ritschl <note>Friedrich Wilhelm Ritschl (1806–1876), a giant of German classical philology.</note>, Vol. III, pp. 445 ff.—what method I believe a critic should follow regarding the major works of Apuleius, I have taken this opportunity to present his dissertation *On the God of Socrates*. This text has been corrected as much as possible with the help of friends, so that I might reveal my opinion on the critical condition of the "minor books" <note>This refers to Apuleius's shorter works, like *De Deo Socratis* and *De Mundo*, as opposed to his famous novel *The Golden Ass*.</note>. To explain this even more fully, I shall briefly discuss the manuscripts of these works and their various corruptions, using examples drawn from this book.

I. Regarding the manuscripts (*codices*), Goldbacher <note>Alois Goldbacher, a 19th-century scholar known for his work on Apuleius and St. Augustine.</note>—a man who has served the study of Apuleius most excellently—rightly established that they all descended from a single archetype <note>The original "parent" manuscript from which all surviving copies were made.</note>. They agree so closely in their exchanges of letters, false repetitions of words, gaps, and later additions (*interpolations*). When he established two classes of these manuscripts, he argued that the "better" class included Munich 621, Vatican 3385, Gudian 168, and Paris 8624. The "inferior" class included Paris 6634, Laurentian shelf 76, 36 (the Florentine manuscript, which was formerly kept in the monastery of San Marco numbered 284 but has now been moved to the Laurentian library). However, it seemed sufficient to me, out of this great hodgepodge of handwritten books, to select the Munich and Florentine manuscripts to represent those classes, both having been written in the twelfth century. Where the Munich manuscript is missing text—specifically in the final part of the book *On the World*—Vatican 3385 must be substituted. Aside from corruptions caused by a slip of the pen, the Vatican manuscript is so much a twin of the Munich one that there can be no doubt it was copied from the same original. For if anyone examines the record of the other manuscripts using Goldbacher’s critical apparatus, they cannot help but admit that they agree with these in all major matters. Whatever unique readings they might show are either the result of skilled men making clever guesses (*conjectures*), or simply the result of the laziness of the scribes while writing. Therefore, I have discarded these others entirely, except for a few conjectures which I preferred to take mainly from Paris 8624, though I could have found them in the commentaries of other learned men. I have provided the full variant readings of the Munich and Florentine manuscripts, except for minor differences in spelling, based on my own comparisons (*collations*). Through the kindness of Halm <note>Karl Felix von Halm (1809–1882), a noted German critic and librarian.</note>, the Munich manuscript was recently sent to me at Flensburg; the Florentine one I collated as diligently as possible while searching through the treasures of the Laurentian library in Florence\*). Furthermore, I have collected testimonies from ancient authors just as Goldbacher did, but I chose not to note every single difference in the text found in the excerpts of Vincent of Beauvais and John of Salisbury <note>Medieval scholars who quoted Apuleius in their own encyclopedic works.</note>: for it is clear that they used manuscripts that had already been altered by later hands.

And although these manuscripts <note>The Latin word used is *codices*, referring to handwritten books.</note> originate from a single archetype, they cannot have been derived from it in the same way; for they are not without points of disagreement. If you ask which of the two is more trustworthy: the model from which the **Munich manuscript** was copied is closer to the archetype both in its quality and in time. The parent of the **Florentine manuscript**, however, is further removed in time and has been handled by a "tampering hand" <note>original: *manu interpolatrice*; this refers to a scribe who tried to "fix" or change the text rather than copying it exactly.</note>. First, if any parts were lost to gaps <note>original: *lacunis*; missing sections in the source text.</note>, the Munich scribe indicated this—especially in the preface—by leaving a blank space of several letters. The Florentine scribe, by contrast, either failed to recognize the gap or filled it with false additions. Furthermore, the Munich manuscript alone preserves the true reading in several places:

*   **§ 14** *probing* [M] vs. *wondering* [F] <note>original: *rimabundi* vs. *mirabundi*</note>
*   **§ 17** *indescribable* [M] and *indescribable* [F]: He <note>The editor refers here to the 16th-century scholar Bonaventura Vulcanius.</note> did not want the word *indescribable* to be paired with an "incredible excess of majesty" without a connecting word; Vulcanius removed this perceived flaw by writing *incredible*.
*   **§ 21** *near* [M] vs. *ready* [F] <note>original: *propte* vs. *prompto*</note>; Apuleius is intending to demonstrate that "no god mingles with men." ## II. 

Let us begin with that category of error which arises from a few letters being swapped with one another, or wrongly added or removed. In this category, however, I will not mention anything except those things which I myself was the first to innovate at my own risk, or those found by older scholars but brought back to light after being generally rejected by more recent ones. The first passage for consideration comes from section 4, which is the most severely deformed of all; it is right to transcribe the whole passage according to the record of the manuscripts:

> "The thought is taken up by a sudden word, because it has arisen from a sudden event, just as it is necessary for stones to be placed in a garden wall by a haphazard throwing-together, neither by weight on the inside nor by a aligned placement on the front, nor with lines converging <note>original: "conniventibus"</note> according to the ruler: for I, the builder of this speech, do not bring a stone cut straight from my own mountain, perfectly smoothed on all sides, balanced to the fingernail <note>original: "ad unguem"; an idiom for perfect precision</note> from the **outermost edges** <gloss>from the best edges</gloss>, but I must adapt it to each task either with rough inequality or slippery smoothness."

But other things remain that await a corrector. He who calls himself the *builder of this speech* does not adapt the material to *each work* <note>original: "cuique operi"</note> but *only to one*; furthermore, there is missing in this part of the sentence something to contrast with what is written in the other part, *from my own mountain*. The second type of these interpretations is that by which things, rather than just words, are explained. I shall set these forth, beginning with the more certain examples.

And so, in **section 50**, in the passage *nothing for a man [in the presence of those guards] . . . should be secret, so that he [the spirit] participates in all things*, that which I have marked in brackets should have been excluded. — In **section 29**, the following is commonly found in the editions: *who in the aerial regions . . . are just as much at home as in any other part of nature its own animals [flying in the ether, walking on the earth].* <note>original: "in aethere volantia, in terra gradientia"</note> Apuleius did not write those words I have enclosed in brackets. For, even setting aside that the proper animals of the waters are not mentioned, Apuleius certainly would not have used "ether" to name the entire element of fire; since he calls ether the "most liquid glow of fire," he surely considers it to be only a specific part of it. Furthermore, birds are described there as *flying in the ether*: yet Apuleius believes not only that the stars appear in the ether (section 30), but also that birds are actually terrestrial animals (section 32).

— In **section 30**, the following is supported by the protection of the manuscripts: *why would nature allow this alone of the four elements of the air . . . to be deserted by its own inhabitants, without also producing animals in that air.* <note>original: "cur hoc solum quattuor elementa aëris . . . desertum a cultoribus suis natura pateretur, quin in eo quoque aëre animalia gigneret"</note> First, in this passage, since the discussion is about the air alone, it is impossible to say *that air*, as if it were to be distinguished from some other air. Therefore, let us omit the word **air** <note>original: "aëre"</note>, which was clearly added to explain the phrase **in it** <note>original: "in eo"</note>. Or would you prefer *in the air also* or *in it also, in the air?* If you approve of the former, you overstep the bounds of reasonable daring; if the latter, you add a troublesome explanation to words that are already clear enough. But if you condemn that word **air**, you will similarly reform the preceding words as *why this alone || among || the four elements [of air]* rather than restoring *how great the element of air* from Vulcanius’s conjecture <note>Bonaventura Vulcanius was a 16th-century humanist scholar who edited Apuleius.</note>, which is neither simpler nor less suspicious due to its faulty structure.

— In **section 2**, the words commonly read cannot stand: *you shall hear these, our [impromptu] works, with the same labor with which we write them, and with a more leaning favor than we read them.* <note>original: "quae i. e. subitaria nostra scilicet audietis pari labore quo scribimus, venia propensiore quam legimus"</note> There is a need for relative pronouns so that the meaning required by the context emerges: *you shall hear our impromptu works with a labor equal to that **with which [we write]** those things we write, and with a favor more indulgent than **that with which [we read]** those things we read.* But even if you add these, other difficulties remain: the same thing—"that which has been written"—is said twice with different words, and instead of **we write**, if I am not mistaken, **we have written** should have been said, and **you read** instead of **we read**. Therefore, I have preferred to cast both aside as an invention not quite fit for illustrating the meaning.

— Likewise, in **section 15**, in the phrase *the profane [of philosophy] crowd of the unskillful*, that word **of philosophy** <note>original: "philosophiae"</note> was added so that it might be understood who the "unskillful" are. If Apuleius himself had wished to add it, he would have placed it after the others in the order of explanations—such as *empty of holiness* and so on—rather than destroying the harmony of the sentence.

— Shortly after, in **section 16**, from the phrase *whom Plato considers gods, [incorporeal] living natures*, Wilamowitz <note>Ulrich von Wilamowitz-Moellendorff (1848–1931), a titan of classical philology.</note> has persuaded me that **incorporeal** <note>original: "incorporalis"</note> must be ejected. That phrase **living natures**, which forms a single concept, ought not to be pulled apart by its insertion, nor should the same idea be expressed again by what follows: *by their own nature removed from the contagion of the body.*

— Finally, in **section 9**, I wonder if I should remove the word **of matter**, so that this unusual arrangement of words—*let us pursue [the things] of matter*—might be avoided, as it is in the other Latin manuscripts (noted as *latinar* in the Florentine and *latina* in the Munich manuscript). ## B. Chronicle of the Institution.

At the conclusion of the 1876/77 school year, the regular teacher Mr. Ernst Stark left his official position here to transfer as a regular teacher to the Royal Cadet Corps — the Berlin Cadet House. Faithful in the performance of his duties, a skillful and gifted teacher, and as an educator as humane as he was firm, he worked at our institution with most gratifying success and secured a lasting memory among his students. His colleagues, who also knew how to value his amiable courtesy and obliging manner, maintain a loyal affection for him. Dr. Hugo Müller, who had belonged to our institution since December 1876 as a <term>probationary candidate (original: cand. prob.)</term>, was entrusted with the provisional handling of the position he vacated.

To the pre-celebration of the Most High Birthday of His Majesty the Emperor and King, a *public* school ceremony will be held on Thursday, March 21, at 6:00 PM in the Aula <note>The "Aula" is the ceremonial great hall of the school.</note>, at which the undersigned will deliver the commemorative address.
Immediately following this, the dismissal of the current graduates <note>original: "Abiturienten," students who have passed their final secondary school exams</note> from the school community will take place.

The summer semester begins Thursday, April 25, at 8:00 AM. — Newly entering students who have already attended another public educational institution and are provided with a proper leaving certificate <note>original: "Abgangs-Zeugnisse"</note>, as well as those who otherwise wish to be admitted to the Gymnasium or the Realschule, are to report to the Gymnasium building for admission or testing on Wednesday, April 24, at 9:00 AM. — For admission to the preparatory school, the undersigned will be available in his office on Tuesday, April 23, at 10:00 AM.

All newly admitted students must present a medical certificate regarding their vaccination and, if they have completed their 12th year, a certificate of revaccination <note>In the German Empire, the Vaccination Act of 1874 made smallpox vaccination and revaccination mandatory for all citizens.</note>. Students from out of town may only choose or change their place of residence with the prior permission of the Director.
