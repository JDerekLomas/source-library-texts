# TO THE MOST ILLUSTRIOUS
## AND MOST REVEREND
->my Lord, and most singular Patron;<-
# THE LORD CARDINAL CAETANO.

<image-desc>A large, square decorative woodcut initial 'E' containing swirling acanthus leaf motifs and floral patterns.</image-desc>

*IT IS INCREDIBLE how much the ease of Printing has complicated the business of printing; because this [invention], from death to life, has not only resurrected the writings of the most famous ancient Scholars <note>original: "Letterati"</note>—which were already consumed by old age and torn by time—but it has filled the World with such a great quantity of new ones that lovers of good letters have been able to provide themselves with a very numerous collection of books for very little money. This circumstance has caused the number of Virtuosi <note>In the 17th century, "Virtuosi" referred to dedicated scholars, scientists, or men of letters who pursued knowledge and the arts with passion.</note> to increase infinitely. Because the pen has been so successfully handled by them, they have occupied every possible subject of writing, even the most specialized; and because the perpetual thirst for knowledge among Scholars is fueled rather than extinguished by the multitude of subject matters, the infinite volumes of others' learned labors have caused the taste of these intellectuals to reach not only an excessive refinement—*

<summary>This page features the formal dedication to Cardinal Caetano, where the author reflects on how the invention of the printing press has revolutionized the intellectual world by making both ancient and modern books widely accessible.</summary>

In such a way that in these present times, those who wish to immortalize themselves in the memory of men through their writings must navigate as far as the Indies if they wish to bring any fruit to the table of these so-disaffected modern Scholars <term>Letterati; men of letters and intellectuals</term> that might be received by them with applause and tasted with pleasure. Many fine Italian wits have written seriously—and well—about political <note>original: "Politiche"</note> and moral <note>original: "Morali"</note> matters; but with jests <term>scherzi; playful or humorous writings</term> and pleasantries <term>piaceuolezze; charming or witty diversions</term>, no one has, as far as I know. I have forced myself to occupy this empty square, as it were, and to treat this new subject matter with whatever success the world shall judge. 

It is quite true that the undertaking has proven as difficult for me as the wisest Scholars <note>original: "Letterati"</note> have always found such a business—if not impossible, at least very arduous—to delight the reader with witty jests <term>facetie; clever humor or "bon mots"</term> and not nauseate him with buffoonery <note>original: "buffonerie," implying crude or low-class clowning</note>; to treat high matters while using low concepts <note>original: "concetti," the intellectual metaphors or "conceits" popular in 17th-century writing</note>; to speak of one person and mean another; to reveal oneself and yet not wish to be seen; to offer witty "salts" <note>original: "sali," a classical reference to "Attic salt," or sharp, sparkling wit</note> and not stumble into insipidity; to sting with satire <note>original: "satira"</note> and not bite with slander <note>original: "maladicenza"</note>; to joke and yet speak in earnest; to treat political matters and not offend those who rule; and, in the persons of dead men, to rebuke the vices of the liv— <note>The text cuts off here, continuing on the next page with the word "living."</note>
