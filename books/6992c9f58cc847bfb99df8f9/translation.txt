## Knife Designs

The blade of the **Lance Knife** <note>original: *Pidao* (鈹刀), an ancient surgical scalpel used primarily for lancing abscesses and draining pus.</note> is one <term>inch<note>The measurements used here are traditional Chinese units. It is half a tenth of an inch thick, with a handle measuring two inches long.

The **Iron Needle** has a thin, long tip measuring one and a half inches. The point and the tail sections are each one and a half inches long; the exterior of the needle is thick and rounded.

# Methods of the Needle

When using the knife, the hand must hold it with a firm and steady grip, the eyes must see the site clearly, and the mind must be focused and determined. Make one horizontal stroke followed by one vertical stroke <note>This creates a cross-shaped incision, a common historical technique to allow for better drainage of pus and to prevent the wound from closing too quickly.</note>. The incision must not be too deep—aim for approximately half an <term>inch<note>About 1.6 centimeters. In traditional surgery, this depth was considered sufficient to reach the pocket of an abscess without damaging deeper structures.</note></term>. 

The patient will inevitably experience a momentary loss of consciousness <note>original: *fajue* (發厥). In a pre-anesthesia era, the shock of the incision often caused vasovagal syncope or fainting.</note>; however, they will recover after a short while, so there is no need for worry, alarm, or fear. Once the pus and blood have been discharged, immediately apply a **medicated plaster** to the opening of the sore and administer internal **herbal decoctions** to regulate and harmonize the body's internal state. If you are using a needle rather than a knife, simply pierce the site; the horizontal and vertical incisions are not required. In all cases, one must treat both the internal and external aspects of the condition simultaneously.

## Needle-Nourishing Powder

Master Qixuan <note>likely referring to the Tang dynasty commentator Wang Bing, who used the pseudonym Qixuanzi, or a specific lineage of "Subtle Opening" teachings</note> provided this method for treating **carbuncles and ulcers** that have formed a "head" but have not yet broken open, as well as **boils**, swelling, and seasonal toxins that may appear on the limbs. If the progression of the ailment is somewhat slow and the patient has a **fear of needles**, it is best to first mix the medicine with vinegar and apply it to the head of the sore. Meanwhile, the patient should take internal medications designed to **support the interior** <term>support the interior<note>*Tuoli* (托裏) is a classic surgical strategy in Chinese medicine using tonifying herbs to "push" the toxins outward, ensuring the body has enough energy to expel pus and heal the wound.</note></term>.

# Powder to Substitute for the Needle
From the *Standards for Diagnosis and Treatment* <note>original: *Zhun Sheng* (準繩), referring to the 1602 medical compendium *Zhengzhi Zhunsheng* by Wang Kentang.</note> for treating carbuncles and abscesses that have formed pus but not yet ruptured, or cases where the sore has broken but the discharge of pus is sluggish.

**Sparrow Droppings** <note>original: *Bai Ding Xiang* (白丁香). Valued in surgery for its "boring" properties.</note>, **Sal Ammoniac** <term>Nao Sha<note>Ammonium chloride; a mineral salt used for its corrosive and "softening" effect on hard swellings.</note></term>, **Myrrh**, **Frankincense** <gloss>equal parts of each</gloss>, and **Lime Cake**.

Inside this, "plant" fourteen grains of glutinous rice. The method is as follows: take five <term>sheng<note>A traditional unit of volume, roughly equivalent to one liter.</note></term> of wood ash and three *sheng* of furnace charcoal. Drench these with five *sheng* of water to collect the clear liquid. Boil this liquid in a large pot until it reduces to two *sheng*, then store it in a ceramic vessel. When needed, take a small green cup and fill it halfway with the concentrated liquid. Place a piece of parchment paper onto the surface of the thick liquid to stabilize it, then take fourteen grains of glutinous rice and "plant" them on top. Let them sit overnight and they will be ready. Grind the ingredients listed above into a fine powder. Form the [medicated] glutinous rice into pills the size of wheat grains. For each application, use one grain. If the sore has not yet broken, apply the medicine to the thinnest part of the sore's head, and it will immediately rupture. If the flow of pus is stagnant and sluggish, insert one grain into the opening.

### [Topical Formula for Sores]
<note>This untitled recipe is a potent topical treatment for stubborn skin ulcers or lesions.</note>

Take three *fen* <note>approx. 1.1 grams</note> of **Calomel** <term>Qingfen</term> <note>A mercurous chloride compound. In historical medicine, it was a standard treatment for skin diseases and syphilis due to its powerful antimicrobial properties, despite its high toxicity.</note>, one *qian* <note>approx. 3.75 grams</note> of **Radish Seed** <note>The seeds of *Raphanus sativus*, used here to help resolve stagnation and "disperse" the hardness of a lesion.</note>, and fourteen **Peach Kernels** <note>The inner seed of the peach pit, used in Chinese medicine to invigorate blood flow and break up localized swelling.</note>. 

Grind these ingredients into a fine powder. Rub the powder onto the **sore** <note>original: "chuang" (瘡), a term encompassing boils, ulcers, and inflammatory skin conditions</note>.

It will be cured immediately.
