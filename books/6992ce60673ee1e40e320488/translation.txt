# The Dragon

The dragon possesses eighty-one scales, a quantity derived from the product of nine times nine. As nine is the maximum number associated with <term>Yang<note>The masculine, active, and celestial principle in traditional Chinese philosophy.</note></term>, this represents the dragon's supreme spiritual power. Those with scales are called <term>Jiaolong<note>Often translated as the Scaled Dragon or Flood Dragon, associated with water and rain.</note></term>; those with wings are called <term>Yinglong<note>The Winged Dragon, a powerful deity in ancient mythology.</note></term>; and those with horns are called <term>Qiulong<note>The Horned Dragon.</note></term>. The *Huainanzi* <note>A classic 2nd-century BCE text that blends various philosophical schools.</note> states that all creatures—whether feathered, furred, scaled, or shelled—are descended from or related to the dragon.

Upon the dragon’s head is an accumulation of bone resembling a <term>Boshan<note>Refers to the jagged, mystical mountain shapes seen on Han-dynasty incense burners.</note></term>; it is named the <term>Chimu<note>Literally "Foot-long Wood."</note></term>. Without this "foot-wood," a dragon is unable to ascend to the heavens. Its nature is spiritual and fierce, yet it harbors a fear of iron. It has a great love for pearls and <term>Kongqing<note>A variety of malachite or azurite used in traditional medicine and art.</note></term>, and it possesses a particular craving for roasted swallows.

When rain is imminent, the dragon bellows with a sound like the clashing of copper basins. Its saliva can be used to create many kinds of incense, and its exhaled breath transforms into clouds. From the fourth month of summer onward, dragons divide the sky and earth into specific territories, each governing its own region. Consequently, even within a tiny space of two <term>Mu<note>A traditional unit of area; two mu is roughly 0.3 acres. This describes "sun-shower" or highly localized rain patterns.</note></term>, the weather can differ entirely, with rain and sun occurring side-by-side. Dragons are also responsible for many violent storms; as the commentators say: fine, soaking rain is the rain of Heaven, but sudden, violent outbursts are the "rain of dragons."

### The Winged Dragon (Yinglong)

On Mount Gongqiu <note>original: "Gongqiu shan"</note>, there exists the **Winged Dragon**, which is a dragon that possesses wings. 

In ancient times, when <term>Chiyou<note>A legendary tribal leader and war deity often depicted as a bronze-headed, four-eyed rebel who fought against the Yellow Emperor.</note></term> rose up against the <term>Yellow Emperor<note>Huangdi, the foundational ancestor of Chinese civilization and a symbol of sovereign order.</note></term>, the Emperor commanded the Winged Dragon to launch an attack in the wilderness of Ji <note>original: "Jizhou," a central region in ancient Chinese geography.</note>. 

During the era of <term>Nüwa<note>The mother goddess of Chinese mythology, famous for creating humanity and mending the sky with five-colored stones.</note></term>, she traveled in a carriage drawn by mythical beasts and utilized the Winged Dragon in her service. 

When <term>Yu the Great<note>A legendary ruler renowned for his dedication to flood control, which allowed for the birth of agriculture and civilization in China.</note></term> was managing the primordial floods, a Winged Dragon assisted him by using its tail to trace lines upon the earth. These lines became the channels through which the floodwaters were guided and contained.

# The Flood Dragon <term>Jiao<note>Also known as the Jiaolong (蛟龍), this creature is a staple of Chinese mythology, often depicted as a dragon in a developmental stage or a master of freshwater floods.</note></term>

The Flood Dragon belongs to the category of scaled dragons. Its appearance is similar to that of a snake, but it possesses four legs and a slender neck. Upon its neck, it has a white swelling <note>original: "baiying" (白癭), referring to a goiter-like protrusion or a distinct fleshy ring</note>. The largest among them measure several spans in circumference. They are born from eggs. Because their eyebrows cross over one another <note>original: "meijiao" (眉交); the character for this dragon, Jiao (蛟), contains the component for "crossing" (交), suggesting its name is derived from this physical trait</note>, they are called the "Jiao." They do not possess the power to bring down rain, but they are capable of splitting open mountains.

# The Hornless Dragon <note>original: 螭 (chī). A mythological creature of the dragon lineage, often associated with water or mountains and frequently used as a decorative motif in jade carvings and architecture.</note>

The Hornless Dragon is likewise a member of the dragon family, though it is distinguished by having no horns. Emperor Wu of the Han Dynasty <note>Reigned 141–87 BCE. Dong He <note>A figure identified in historical lore as an attendant or scholar during the Han period.</note> remarked: "In the distant past, King Jie of the Xia Dynasty <note>The legendary last ruler of the Xia, often depicted in history as a decadent tyrant whose excesses led to the dynasty's collapse.</note> doted upon his consort Mo Xi <note>A woman of great beauty often blamed for distracting the King from his duties.</note> while she sat upon his lap. Seeing as this creature's belly still shows the scar of a golden hairpin's puncture, how can we know for certain that it is not that very same one?" <note>This anecdote suggests the creature had transformed from a jade object into a living being, or that it was an immortal spirit capable of surviving across millennia.</note>

## The Dipper-Ox <note>original: 斗牛 (douniu). A mythical creature named after two Chinese constellations. It was a prominent symbol in the Ming Dynasty, often appearing on high-ranking "bestowal robes" (feiyufu) granted by the Emperor.</note>

The **Dipper-Ox** has a forehead and scales like those of a dragon, but its horns are curved <note>resembling the horns of a water buffalo</note> and it possesses only three claws.

During the **Jiajing** reign <note>1522–1566</note>, within the **Lakes of the Western Precinct** <note>the imperial gardens of the Forbidden City</note>, there was a creature in the form of a Dipper-Ox, appearing much like a winding hornless dragon. Whenever it encountered overcast skies or rain, it had the power to summon clouds and mist. It would frequently slither and coil along the roadsides and atop the **Bridge of the Golden Turtle and Jade Rainbow** <note>original: Jin'ao Yudong (金鰲玉蝀). A famous marble bridge located between the Beihai and Zhonghai lakes in Beijing</note>.

At that time, the **Emperor Shizong** <note>original: Shihuang (世皇). This refers to the Jiajing Emperor, who famously favored the Western Precinct for his Daoist rituals and purification</note> was residing in the Western Precinct for ritual fasting. When the palace maids passed by this area during their shift changes and saw the beast, they would inevitably collapse in terror; some were even so frightened they ran and fell into the water. The palace eunuchs reported these incidents to the throne.

**His Majesty** remarked: "This beast belongs only behind the mountains. Why is it here frightening people?"

That very night, only the sounds of crashing waves and leaping splashes were heard. At dawn, it was observed that the lake water had been split open, leaving a long, winding trail; the creature had evidently moved away. From that day forward, its appearances were never sighted beyond the area behind **Longevity Mountain** <note>original: Wansui Shan (萬歲山). Now known as Jingshan, the hill directly north of the Forbidden City</note>.

## The Shen <note>original: 蜃 (shèn). A mythical creature often depicted as either a dragon-like monster or a giant clam. It is most famous for exhaling "Shen-breath," which was believed to create mirages of cities over the sea.</note>

The Shen is shaped like a snake but is much larger. From its waist down, all its scales grow in reverse <note>In Chinese dragon lore, "reverse scales" are often a sign of power or ferocity</note>. It exhales breath that forms the likeness of towers and pavilions; these appear when rain is about to fall. If its fat is mixed with wax to make candles, their fragrance can be detected from a hundred paces, and the smoke they emit forms the shapes of towers and palaces.

They also produce pearls that can be worn as earrings; thus, they belong to the family of **clams and oysters** <note>original: 蚌 (bàng)</note>.

# The Yuan <note>original: 黿 (yuán). The Asian giant softshell turtle (*Pelochelys cantorii*). In Chinese mythology and natural history, it is the king of all turtles.</note>

# The Soft-shelled Turtle

The soft-shelled turtle is hatched from eggs. Its shape is round with a domed spine, and it features a <gloss>fleshy skirt</gloss> <note>original: 裙 (qún); this refers to the soft, leathery edge of the shell characteristic of this species</note> around its four sides. It is an armored creature that "hears" with its eyes. It lives in the water but reproduces on land.

Duan Chengshi <note>a famous Tang Dynasty scholar (c. 803–863) known for recording wonders and natural oddities</note> said: "Armored creatures incubate by their shadows, while feathered creatures incubate with their bodies." Currently, we see that the soft-shelled turtle remains hidden in the deep abyss, yet its eggs hatch on the hillsides; this is a transformation brought about by the power of thought <note>Ancient Chinese natural history often suggested that certain animals could influence their offspring's development through mental focus or 'spiritual' connection rather than direct physical contact during incubation</note>.

The *Classic of Fish Raising* <note>traditionally attributed to Fan Li (5th century BCE), this is one of the world's oldest treatises on aquaculture</note> states: "When the number of fish in a pond reaches three hundred and sixty, a dragon will become their leader and lead them to fly away out of the water. However, if a soft-shelled turtle is placed among them, the fish will no longer leave." For this reason, another name for the turtle is the <term>Spirit Guardian</term> <note>original: 守神 (shǒu shén); a name reflecting its perceived ability to 'guard' the livestock of a pond</note>.

According to the nature of Heaven and Earth, creatures with thin waists are entirely male, while those with broad waists are entirely female. Broad-waisted creatures such as <gloss>turtles and giant soft-shelled turtles</gloss> <note>original: 龜黿 (guī yuán)</note> take snakes as their mates. Wherever a soft-shelled turtle is located, there will surely be floating foam on the surface of the water above it; this is called "turtle spit." Those who hunt turtles use this sign to predict their location.

# The Turtle

The turtle has its bones on the outside and its flesh on the inside. Its intestines are attached to its head. It has broad shoulders and lacks males, mating instead with snakes <note>In ancient Chinese cosmology, the turtle was often depicted entwined with a snake, forming the "Black Tortoise" (Xuanwu), one of the Four Auspicious Beasts</note>. A common saying goes: "the turtle thinks, and the soft-shelled turtle gazes." Nowadays, turtles and soft-shelled turtles are both said to gaze at the moon across the water's expanse. 

The turtle is skilled at concealing itself for long periods and is capable of "circulating its breath" <term>daoyin: ancient Chinese physical and breathing exercises, a precursor to modern qigong, which turtles were thought to perform naturally to achieve longevity</term>. Its back is slightly arched; the philosopher Han Fei calls it the "Vaulted Turtle" <note>original: 穹龜 (qióngguī)</note>. There are many varieties, such as those with green hair or golden threads. A turtle of a thousand years swims atop the leaves of the water-lily; one of three thousand years swims atop the leaves of the cocklebur. Because they are produced in the state of Cai, they are also known as "Cai" <note>In the Zhou Dynasty, the state of Cai was famous for producing large turtles used in divination</note>. Those that reach a length of one foot and two inches are considered precious treasures.

There is also a small turtle called the "Sheltering Turtle" <note>original: 攝龜 (shèguī); likely referring to the yellow-margined box turtle, known for its hinged shell</note>. It is small and narrow with a long tail, and its lower belly shell has a horizontal fold. When it encounters a snake, it snaps it up and eats it. It lives mostly on land and is even capable of climbing trees. It has a very fishy and foul odor. In the Jiangdong region, it is called the "Mound Turtle," or alternatively the "Mandarin Duck Turtle" or the "Hawk Turtle."

# The Hawksbill Turtle

# The Snake <note>original: 蛇 (Shé)</note>

The ancient form of the character for **Snake** was simply written as **它** <note>original: tā; an ancient pictograph of a serpent</note>, which represented a creature that is "long" and belongs to the class of "creeping things." Snakes dwell among the grasses. Whenever they have eaten and are sufficiently full, they will **shed their skins** <note>original: 脫殼 (tuōqiào)</note>. During the winter months, they take soil into their mouths and enter a state of **hibernation** <note>original: 蟄 (zhé)</note>; upon emerging from their winter sleep, they spit this soil out.

# The **Cuttlefish**

The cuttlefish is shaped like a **counting-rod bag** <term>suannang: a traditional drawstring pouch used for carrying wooden or ivory rods for mathematical calculations</term> with two tentacles that are remarkably long. This fish carries ink within its belly; upon spotting a human or a large predatory fish, it habitually ejects this ink to fill an area of several square feet, using the dark cloud to hide its body. Paradoxically, humans use this very ink cloud as a guide to find and capture the creature. On its back, there is a single internal bone about three or four *fen* <note>approximately 1 to 1.3 centimeters</note> thick. This bone is light and brittle, much like the **pith-paper plant** <term>tongcao: the soft, spongy inner tissue of the rice-paper tree, used in both crafts and medicine</term>. It is known by the name **cuttlebone** <term>haipiaoxiao: literally "sea-mantis-egg-case," a substance used in traditional pharmacy to treat various ailments</term>.

# The **Yellow Eel**

The **Yellow Eel** <note>original: 鱓 *shan*; a variant of the modern character 鱔, referring to the ricefield eel</note> resembles a snake but lacks scales. It has a yellow body with black patterns, and its skin is covered in a slimy mucus. # Fish: The **Sturgeon**

The **Sturgeon** <note>original: 鱏 *xun*</note>, which is also known by the name *zhan* <note>original: 鱣; historically referring to the Chinese Sturgeon or similar large species</note>, is a creature of immense size. ## The **Crocodile**

The **Crocodile** <note>original: 鰐魚 *eyu*</note> is found in the South Seas <note>refers to the South China Sea and coastal Southeast Asia</note>. It has four feet similar to those of a soft-shelled turtle <note>original: 鼈 *bie*</note>. Its body reaches a length of over ten feet <note>original: 丈 *zhang*; a traditional unit of length roughly equal to 3.3 meters</note>, with a snout three feet long, a lengthy tail, and sharp teeth.

When tigers or even dragons cross the water, the crocodile strikes them with its tail, snapping them in two—much like the way an elephant utilizes its trunk. By nature, they frequently seize and devour the sheep and pigs <note>original: 豕 *shi*; an archaic term for swine</note> raised by local households. In areas where they are numerous, they cause great harm to the populace; they are also capable of eating humans. Once they have eaten their fill, they float upon the surface of the water in a state resembling a drunken stupor. The local people <note>original: 土人 *turen*</note> watch for this state of intoxication to kill them.

## The **Shark** <note>original: 魦魚 *shayu*; also known as "sand-fish" due to its rough, abrasive skin</note>

According to the records, the varieties of **sharks** are not uniform. While their skin and flesh are generally similar, their head shapes differ slightly. The largest among them is the **Yellow Shark** <note>original: 黄魦 *huangsha*</note> of the open sea, which voraciously preys upon all other fish. The largest of these can weigh five or six hundred catties <note>a *jin* or catty is approximately 600 grams; thus, these weigh roughly 300-360kg</note>.

The **Hu Shark** <note>original: 胡魦 *husha*</note> is blue-green in color with a sandy texture on its back. The larger specimens reach over ten feet in length, while the smaller ones are three to five feet long. One variety has a **nose like a saw**; its skin can be sliced into thin strips for sashimi <note>original: 膾 *kuai*; refers to thinly sliced raw meat or fish, a delicacy in ancient China</note>. 

There is also the **Black-head Flat-tail** <note>original: 烏頭頼尾 *wutou lawei*</note>, which has a black back and can weigh over a hundred catties. It buries itself in the ocean sand and does not flee from humans; people can even cut its flesh while it is stationary, and it only departs once the tide comes in. 

To prepare the skin, it must be soaked in boiling water to clean it; it can then be sliced into thin strips for sashimi. The **fin-filaments** <note>original: 鬛鬚 *liexu*; refers to the fibrous "needles" within shark fins</note>, when soaked to remove the outer skin, reveal "silk" that is also used for sashimi. Its color and quality are crystal clear, resembling **silver threads**. 

The **Jiao-sand Shark** <note>original: 鮫沙 *jiaosha*</note> resembles a *jiao* <note>a mythical aquatic dragon or a specific large shark</note> but has a longer nose. Its skin is durable enough to be used for decorating sword hilts. 

There are furthermore several other varieties, including:
*   The **Tiger Shark** <note>original: 虎沙 *husha*</note>
*   The **Saw Shark** <note>original: 鋸沙 *jusha*</note>
*   The **Dog Shark** <note>original: 狗沙 *gousha*</note>
*   The **Sword Shark** <note>original: 劍沙 *jiansha*</note>
*   The **Black-fin Shark** <note>original: 烏鬛 *wulie*</note>
*   The **In-and-Out Shark** <note>original: 出入魦 *churusha*</note>
*   The **Seasonal Shark** <note>original: 時魦 *shisha*</note>
*   The **Hat Shark** <note>original: 帽魦 *maosha*</note>

Additionally, there is a species called the **Blowing Shark** <note>original: 吹魦 *chuisha*</note>. The large ones weigh over a catty, while the small ones are as thin as a finger—narrow, round, and long.

# The Reeves's Shad
<note>original: 鰣魚 *shiyu*; the Reeves's shad (*Tenualosa reevesii*), one of the most famous delicacies in Chinese history, known for its seasonal appearance.</note>

The Rib Fish resembles the Shad but is smaller; its body is thin and its bones are fine. Those caught in the winter are called **"Snow-Reflecting Fish"** <note>original: 雪映魚 *xueyingyu*</note>, and their flavor is excellent; however, by the time summer arrives, their taste diminishes. Large vessels are used to head out to sea to capture them, with the harvest season usually centered around the **Summer Solstice**.

The Shad is also known as the **"Arrow Fish"** <note>original: 箭魚 *jianyu*</note> because the fine bones beneath its belly resemble the heads of arrows. Its delicious flavor is concentrated at the point where the skin and scales meet; for this reason, the scales are not removed when the fish is cooked. Those caught in **Dangyang** <note>original: 當陽; likely referring to the sunny, southern banks of the Yangtze River or specific fishing grounds in that region</note> are considered especially fine. It is because of this fish that the great poet **Su Dongpo** <note>Su Dongpo (1037–1101): A famous Song dynasty statesman and gourmet who famously listed "Shad having too many bones" as one of the great spiritual regrets of his life.</note> lamented the Shad's abundance of bones.

There is also another variety called the **Ji** <note>original: 鰶 *ji*; the Gizzard Shad or *Konosirus punctatus*</note>. It resembles the Reeves's Shad but is smaller with blue-green scales. It is colloquially called the **"Green Crucian"** <note>original: 青鯽 *qingji*</note> and is also known as the **"Green Scale."**

The **Fine Fish** possesses the physical quality of a **carp** but the scales of a **trout** <note>original: 鱒 *zun*; here referring to fish with fine, delicate scales</note>. Its flesh is exceptionally delicious. It is said to feed on "milky springs" <note>mineral-rich waters found in limestone caves</note>. These fish emerge from the **Bing Caves** <note>original: 丙穴 *bingxue*</note>; thus, the *Rhapsody of Nanjun* <note>a poetic composition describing the southern commandery</note> states: "The Fine Fish emerge from the Bing Caves."

Earlier scholars have noted that in the north of Mian County, Hanzhong <note>located in modern-day Shaanxi province</note>, there are three "Milky Caves." The fish are traditionally harvested in the third lunar month. Because the mouths of these caves face the *Bing* direction <note>in the traditional Chinese compass (the Ten Heavenly Stems), *Bing* represents the South</note>, they are called the "Bing Caves."

<term>Fine Fish (嘉魚)</term>: Literally "Excellence Fish." A name applied to various prized freshwater fish in ancient China, often associated with clear, cold mountain water and esteemed in classical poetry and cuisine.
<term>Carp (鯉)</term>: The common carp (*Cyprinus carpio*), used here as a standard for comparing the fish's body shape and texture.
<term>Bing Caves (丙穴)</term>: Famous limestone caves in the Qinling Mountains. The term became a literary shorthand for the source of high-quality fish and pure waters.
<term>Mian County (沔縣)</term>: A historic location in the Han River basin, famous in the Three Kingdoms period and noted for its unique geography and springs.
