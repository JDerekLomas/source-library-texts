## Here begins the life of Aesop. His whole life was unfortunate, although he was subtle and wise. He was a native of Greece, near Great Troy, from a town called Amoneo. Among all men, he was deformed; for he had a large head, a great face, long cheeks, sharp eyes, a short and hunched neck, a large belly, and he had thick legs and wide feet. And what is worse, he was so tongue-tied <note>original: "bꝛet" (bret); meaning he had a speech impediment or was mute</note> that he could not speak. Nevertheless, he possessed great nobility of mind and was highly ingenious and subtle in clever arguments and joyful words.

On that day, it happened that Aesop, coming from his labor, asked for food as he was accustomed to do. And Agatopus, who was guarding the figs, ate two of them and said to one of his companions: "If I did not fear my master, I would eat all the figs." And his companion said to him: "If you want me to eat with you, I will find a clever trick so that we shall suffer no harm." And Agatopus said, "How can this be done?" And his companion told him: "When my lord has returned, we will say that Aesop ate them. And because he does not know how to speak <note>Aesop is depicted as mute at the start of the legend</note>, he will not know how to defend himself and will be very well beaten, and we shall fulfill our desire." 

And having agreed upon these things between them, they went to eat all the figs, saying to one another: "This villain shall be well thrashed." And when the lord returned from the bath, he asked that his figs be brought to him. And Agatopus said to him: "My lord, when Aesop came from his labor, he found the cellar open and entered therein without reason, and then ate all the figs." The lord was greatly angered and said: "Go fetch Aesop." 

And when he had come, he said to him: "Deformed servant, had you no fear of eating my figs?" When Aesop heard these things, he was afraid because he could not speak, looking at those who had accused him. The lord commanded that he be stripped. Then Aesop went to throw himself at the feet of his lord and, as best he could, prayed him to give him time to excuse himself. And the lord did so. 

Afterward, Aesop took a vessel full of hot water which was on the fire, and taking the water, he put it in a basin and drank it; immediately after, he put his fingers in his mouth and vomited only water, for that day he had taken nothing but that water. And he prayed <note>through gestures</note> that his accusers should likewise drink of that water as he had. And when they had drunk, they held their hands before their mouths so that they could in no way vomit. And because their stomachs were unsettled by that water, they went and vomited the water and the figs together. 

Then the lord, looking at them, said: "Why have you lied to me against this man who cannot speak?" and then commanded them to be stripped and publicly beaten, saying that whoever did such things to another would be punished with such a penalty. And these things having passed, Aesop returned to his labor.

And as he was working in the fields, he saw a priest <note>original: "prestre"; likely a priest of Isis or a traveler in the original Greek context</note> named Yfidis who was going to the city and had lost his way; and seeing Aesop, he prayed him to show him the path to go straight to the city. And Aesop took him by the hand and made him sit under a fig tree, and set before him bread and herbs, praying him to eat. And he tempered water from the well and gave him to drink. And when he had eaten and drunk, he took him again by the hand and put him back on the right path to go to the city. Which thing being done, the priest lifted his hands toward heaven, praying to God for Aesop.
