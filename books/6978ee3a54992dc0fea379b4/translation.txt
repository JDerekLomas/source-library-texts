## What prayer is.

<margin>Prayer is the ascent of the mind to God. <note>original Latin: "ORATIO est ascensus mentis in Deum." This definition was highly influential in the Middle Ages and Reformation, originally attributed to the 8th-century theologian John of Damascus.</note></margin>
But before I list those same twelve elements, I wish first to explain something about prayer—what it means to pray—and its different forms. Namely, prayer is an uplifting of the faithful soul, or an ascending of the mind into God in heaven, either with thanksgiving for His blessings, or with earnest persistence to obtain something from God. It is called holding a spiritual conversation with God, crying out to God for help, sighing, petitioning, pouring out the heart before God, and lamenting and presenting our needs (of which there are many) to Him through prayer. It is also called calling upon the name of the Lord, singing to the Lord from the heart with psalms, spiritual songs, and hymns of praise in <term>grace</term> <note>The author refers to the state of being under God's favor and the influence of the Holy Spirit.</note>, and offering Him praise and thanks for the kindnesses received from Him; all of this is understood and encompassed by the word "Prayer."

At this point, one should know right at the start that no person can pray correctly unless they are a believer; for without faith it is impossible to please God. For whoever wishes to come to God must believe, says St. Paul, that He exists and that He is a rewarder of those who seek Him. Hebrews 11.

## II. Petition.

<margin>The first is a plea against evil, whether future or present; the second, however, is a desire and request for what is good.</margin>
The second type of prayer or praying that Paul writes about is called petitioning for something good. Just as the first part <note>referring to "Deprecation" or "Abbitten" discussed previously</note> involves pleading for the removal of transgression and punishment, and asking that we be delivered or protected from evil, misfortune, and all the malice of Satan—so this second part is a request that every good thing may happen to us. In this, we pray for what we lack spiritually or physically, while reminding God the Lord of His goodness, love, faithfulness, truth, and promised mercy. We persist and urge Him that, for the sake of these attributes and His own promises, He would not refuse our request.

The fourth kind of prayer or praying of which St. Paul writes is Thanksgiving for all of God's benefits.
