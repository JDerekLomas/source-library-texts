# Preface to the Profound Meaning of the Five Elements

## Compiled by Xiao Ji, Grand Master for Splendid Happiness, Equal in Rank to the Three Services, and Founding Duke of Chengyang County

Now, the <term>Five Elements (Wuxing)</term> are indeed the root and source of creation and the foundation of human relations. All varieties of life receive their capacity for change from them, and all spirits achieve resonance through their connection. They are rooted in <term>Yin and Yang</term> and are dispersed as subtle essences and visible forms. They encompass the entirety of Heaven and Earth and extend to the limits of both the hidden realm of spirits and the manifest world of the living.

The cardinal points—North, South, East, and West <note>original: "Zi, Wu, Mao, and You," the four Earthly Branches representing the primary directions and hours</note>—serve as the warp and woof of the world, while the "eight winds" and the "six pitch-pipes" <note>representing the winds of the eight directions and the musical system used to harmonize the calendar</note> serve as its guiding framework.

Thus, Heaven has the five celestial measurements <note>the five visible planets: Jupiter, Mars, Saturn, Venus, and Mercury</note> to display its signs; Earth has the <term>Five Materials</term> <note>Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water</note> to provide for practical use; and humanity has the <term>Five Constants</term> <note>the core Confucian virtues of Benevolence, Righteousness, Propriety, Wisdom, and Trustworthiness</note> to manifest inner virtue. All things in existence are organized by the measure of five; once a count exceeds five, the numerical cycle transforms.

Truly, it is by the harmonization of the "five breaths" (<term>qi</term>) that the four seasons are balanced, all species are nurtured, and the myriad things are molded and forged. When good prevails, the five virtues follow their proper course and the "three luminaries" <note>the sun, moon, and stars</note> shine brilliantly. When evil prevails, the "nine accomplishments" <note>the successful management of the elements and social welfare</note> are not cultivated, and the "six calamities" <note>natural or atmospheric disturbances</note> arise one after another.

Tracing things from their origins to their ends, it is impossible to fully exhaust the mystery of their first omens. Therefore, the Sages embodied these principles before they had even fully emerged; they established words to "trap" the meaning of the signs <note>original: "quán," a metaphor for a fish-trap used to capture meaning</note> and established signs to make worldly affairs visible. Since these affairs...

The celestial signs hanging above can be understood. Once these signs are known, they begin to proliferate; through this proliferation, **Numbers** are generated, and once there are numbers, things can be recorded and ordered. **Images**, on the other hand, can be given physical form. Because they possess form and can be recorded, the underlying principles of the universe can be grasped through intermediary means. These means are none other than <term>tortoise shell and yarrow stalk divination</term> <note>These were the two primary methods of ancient Chinese divination: "Gui" (tortoise shell) provided visual signs or "images" through heat-induced cracks, while "Shi" (yarrow stalks) produced numerical hexagrams</note>.

The tortoise shell represents the **Images**; therefore, it uses the [solar] days as the primary origin of the Five Elements. The yarrow stalks represent the **Numbers**; therefore, they use the [lunar and zodiacal] chronograms <note>original: "Chen," referring to the twelve divisions of the Chinese zodiac and the positions of the moon</note> as the master of the Five Elements.

As for the rising and setting of the constellations Orion and Scorpius <note>original: "Can" and "Chen," two prominent star groups that are never seen in the sky at the same time</note>, the waxing and waning of the sun and moon, the rumbling of thunder and the appearance of rainbows, the drifting of clouds and the falling of rain—these are the **Images of Heaven**.

The <term>Twenty-Eight Lunar Mansions</term> <note>the divisions of the sky used to track the moon's progress</note>, the various inner and outer celestial palaces, the <term>Seven Luminaries</term> <note>the sun, moon, and five visible planets</note> and the <term>Three Lights</term> <note>sun, moon, and stars</note>, the stellar divisions and the stations of the year—these are the **Numbers of Heaven**.

The mountains, rivers, waters, and lands; the heights, depths, plains, and marshes; the presiding peaks and the flowing waterways; the wind, mist, dew, and vapor—these are the **Images of Earth**.

The eight extremities and the four seas, the three rivers and the five lakes, the nine provinces and the hundred commanderies, the thousands of miles and the tens of thousands of acres—these are the **Numbers of Earth**.

<term>Ritual</term> used to regulate affairs, <term>Music</term> used to harmonize the heart, the ranks, emblems, seals, and banners that denote status, and the punishments used to suppress villainy—these are the **Images of Humanity**.

The hundred officials who govern, the ten thousand people who are established in their lives, the "Four Teachings" <note>traditionally: culture, conduct, loyalty, and faith</note> to cultivate civil refinement, and the "Seven Virtues" <note>martial virtues such as repressing cruelty and calling for peace</note> to review military prowess—these are the **Numbers of Humanity**.

# Preface to the General Meaning of the Five Elements

The complete progression of the **Five Elements** depends upon <term>tortoise shell and yarrow stalk divination</term> <note>original: "gui shi," referring to the two most ancient methods of Chinese divination used to consult the cosmic order</note> to distinguish the auspicious and inauspicious aspects of Yin and Yang. Thus, events are understood through **Images**, and things are established through **Numbers**. 

Whenever I have studied the ancient archives <note>original: "fen suo," referring to the *Three Graves* and *Five Cords*, legendary lost texts predating the Confucian Classics</note> and exhaustively researched the classical canon, I find that from the era of <term>Fuxi and Shennong</term> <note>the legendary progenitors of Chinese civilization</note> down through the Zhou and Han dynasties, there was not a single ruler who did not treat the **Five Elements** as the foundation of governance, nor failed to prioritize the divination of yarrow and tortoise to determine the outcome of good and evil. 

Therefore, the *Commentary* <note>original: "zhuan," referring to the *Zuo Zhuan*, a historical commentary on the Spring and Autumn Annals</note> states: "Heaven produces the **Five Materials**; to discard even one is impossible." The *Classic of History* <note>original: "Shangshu"</note> records that King Zhou of Shang, though he had received the Mandate, "treated the **Five Constants** with contempt and utterly abandoned the **Three Administrations**." From this, we know that those who grasp these principles will flourish, while those who lose them will perish.

During the Jin dynasty, when the Central Plains descended into chaos and the Jin court migrated to the south, the fundamental texts of our tradition became scarce, while "superficial scholarship" <note>original: "zhiti zhi xue," literally "the learning of branches and twigs," as opposed to the "root"</note> began to flourish. Empty rhetoric and clever writing vied for temporary glory, while the profound scholarship required to govern a nation was discarded for ten thousand generations. 

Subsequent generations followed these trends until they became the established norm. Although the arts of <term>prognostication</term> <note>original: "zhan hou," the practice of observing celestial and meteorological omens to predict the future</note> were still practiced, they all followed "unorthodox paths" <note>original: "zuo dao," literally "left-hand paths," referring to heterodox, superstitious, or corrupted methods</note>. While the methods of divination remained in use, the underlying principles of the **Lines and Images** <note>original: "yao xiang," the visual and numerical components of the hexagrams in the *I Ching*</note> were no longer understood. The <term>Monthly Ordinances</term> <note>the *Yueling*, an ancient ritual calendar that coordinated human activity with the seasons</note> were no longer followed, and seasonal regulations were inevitably flawed. 

As the saying goes, "a mistake of a hair's breadth leads to an error of a thousand miles." Droughts and floods arose, yet their causes were not discerned; strange portents and omens appeared, but...

<summary>Xiao Ji traces the importance of the Five Elements from legendary rulers to the Han dynasty, arguing that the decline of this "root" knowledge after the Jin dynasty led to the rise of superficial and unorthodox divination practices.</summary>

...yet they do not know its essential purpose. It is not because the **Images** and **Forms** are rarely proven; rather, it is that those who investigate to the end observe the errors and delusions [of others] and sigh at their learning.

People all believe in the "branches" <note>the superficial or secondary aspects</note> and forget the "root" <note>the fundamental principles</note>; they take up the coarse aspects and overlook the subtle details. As the ancients said: "Only when one climbs a mountain does one first see the height of Heaven; only when one approaches a ravine does one then realize the depth of the Earth." If one has not heard the Way of the former Sages, there is no way to know the greatness of learning. 

How much more so is this true for the **Five Elements**, which are so profound and deep—how could one hope to write of them elegantly without effort? 

Now, therefore, I have widely collected from the <term>Classics and their Commentaries</term> <note>original: "jing wei," literally the 'warp and woof,' referring to the primary Confucian classics and the esoteric apocryphal texts that expanded upon them</note> and searched exhaustively through the ancient records to discuss the general meaning briefly. 

The work consists of **twenty-four sections** in total, which are further divided into **forty sub-sections**. 

*   The number **twenty-four** corresponds to the <term>seasonal breaths</term> <note>original: "jie shu zhi qi," referring to the 24 solar terms of the traditional calendar which regulate the agricultural and ritual year</note>. 
*   The total of **forty** represents the <term>Completion Numbers</term> of the **Five Elements** <note>In ancient numerology, each element has a "birth number" and a "completion number." The completion numbers are 6, 7, 8, 9, and 10, which together sum to 40</note>. 

Beginning with the **"Explanation of Names"** and ending with **"Insects and Birds,"** everything categorized under the **Five Elements** is contained within these principles. I hope that this Way will not fall into ruin and that its origins will be known. 

If one can govern the heart, quiet the will, and study its subtleties, it will not merely delight the spirit, nourish one's nature, preserve virtue, and keep the body whole; it can also assist in harmonizing the various affairs of government and bring benefit and peace to all existence. This is what the <term>Perfected Person</term> <note>original: "Zhi Ren," a term for a sage who has attained the highest state of wisdom and alignment with the cosmos</note> attains. 

In the past, people were moved by things to create the classics; today, I, Xiao Ji, <note>The author refers to himself here by his given name, Ji</note> describe these meanings based on the affairs of the world. Although written at different times, they share...

# The Great Meaning of the Five Elements, Volume One

## Written by Xiao Ji, High Official of the Three Ranks and Founding Duke of Chengyang Commandery <note>Xiao Ji (c. 548–614 AD) was a renowned scholar and cosmologist of the Sui Dynasty who synthesized centuries of Five Elements theory into this work.</note>

### Section One: Explaining the Names—This section is divided into two parts

One: Explaining the names of the <term>Five Elements</term> <note>original: "Wuxing" (五行). These are Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water—the fundamental forces of Chinese cosmology.</note>.
Two: Discussing the names of the <term>Stems and Branches</term> <note>original: "Zhigan" (支干). This refers to the Ten Heavenly Stems and Twelve Earthly Branches used in the traditional calendar and divination.</note>.

### Part One: Explaining the Names of the Five Elements

All things naturally possess a physical substance and essence. The Sages observed their categories and established names for them. Thus it is said: "Names are used to determine the substance." The "nameless" is the beginning of Heaven and Earth; the "named" is the mother of all things <note>This is a direct reference to the first chapter of the *Dao De Jing*, the foundational text of Taoism</note>. Because these names arise from their merits and practical applications, specific titles were established.

The <term>Book of Rites</term> <note>original: "Li" (禮). One of the Five Classics of the Confucian canon.</note> says that when a child is three months old and begins to gurgle, they are given a name. Before they are born, there is originally no name. Since the Five Elements precede the "myriad things" <note>A traditional term for everything that exists in the universe</note>, and their forms and functions are provided by the creative transformations of nature, how could their names not be established first to clarify their substance and application?

The <term>Spring and Autumn: Shell of the Original Mandate</term> <note>original: "Chunqiu Yuanmingbao" (春秋元命苞). An influential Han dynasty esoteric commentary on the Spring and Autumn Annals.</note> says: "Wood" (*mu*) means "to touch" (*chu*); it touches the earth and...

Its position is within the Center. "Inside" <note>original: *nei* (內)</note> implies penetration and connection. Water moistens, flows, and exerts its influence by traveling in secret underground. Therefore, the character for "Water" <note>original: *shui* (水)</note> is formed by two people intersecting with the number "one" emerging from the center. This "one" is the beginning of all numbers. The two people represent the union of male and female, or Yin and Yang, whose intersection gives rise to the "one." 

Water is the beginning of the Five Elements; it is the gathered fluid of the <term>Primordial Energy</term> <note>original: *yuanqi* (元氣); the fundamental life force or substance of the universe in Chinese philosophy.</note>. <term>Guanzi</term> <note>original: *Guanzi* (管子); an ancient Chinese text named after the philosopher-statesman Guan Zhong.</note> says: "Water is the blood and energy of the Earth, the circulation through its veins and pathways. Therefore, it is called Water." 

<term>Xu Shen</term> <note>original: *Xu Shen* (許愼); the Han dynasty scholar who compiled the *Shuowen Jiezi*, the first dictionary to analyze the structure of Chinese characters.</note> says: "The character depicts a spring with several streams flowing together, containing a subtle essence of Yang energy within it." 

Its season is Winter. <term>Shizi</term> <note>original: *Shizi* (尸子); a syncretic philosophical text from the Warring States period.</note> says: "Winter <note>original: *dong* (冬)</note> means 'the end' <note>original: *zhong* (終)</note>. At this point, the myriad things reach their conclusion and are stored away." The <term>Book of Rites</term> says: "The word for Winter implies 'the interior.' To be in the interior is to be hidden or stored." 

Its position is the North. <term>Shizi</term> says: "This is the direction of total prostration. When the myriad things reach winter, they all go into hiding; the noble and the lowly are treated as one." 

Because the Five Elements each have their specific seasons and cardinal directions, I have analyzed and explained them individually.

### The General Names of the Branches and Stems

Regarding the word for **Stem** <note>original: *Gan*</note>, there are three different ways it is written: 
1. The first is written as **Management** (幹). 
2. The second is written as **Trunk** (榦). 
3. The third is written as **Stem** (干).

To explain the character for **Trunk** (榦): since these Branches and Stems are paired together for use, they are like a tree having smaller branches and a main trunk <note>original: *jingan* (莖榦)</note> which together form the body of the tree. Therefore, they are called "Trunks." 

As for the version written as **Management** (幹), it carries the meaning of "to manage" or "to accomplish." The word **Branch** (支) carries the meaning of "to support" or "to sustain." Because these days and time-periods sustain and manage the myriad affairs of the world, they are called "Branches and Stems."

Regarding the character written simply as **Stem** (干), it also shares the meaning of "management." Just as an object placed upon a pole <note>original: *gan* (竿)</note> stands firm and is clearly visible to all, it is also referred to as a "pole" or "stem." Most common books follow a simplified style and therefore mostly use this character (干).
