<meta>This page serves as a celebratory introduction to the geography and history of Beijing, justifying its status as the supreme capital of the Ming Dynasty. It bridges the gap between the "foreign" rule of the previous dynasties and the legitimacy of the Yongle Emperor’s reign.</meta>

The Capital is the ancient land of You and Ji <note>Ancient administrative regions in northern China</note>. To its left, it is encircled by the vast blue sea <note>original: "滄海" (Canghai), referring to the Bohai Sea</note>; to its right, it is embraced by the Taihang Mountains. To the north, it rests against the Juyong Pass <note>A vital strategic mountain pass in the Great Wall</note>; to the south, it is bordered by the Yellow and Ji Rivers. Its strategic advantages <note>original: "形勝" (Xingsheng), referring to the defensive and auspicious nature of the terrain</note> are the finest in the world; it is truly what is called a "land of heavenly abundance."

Although the Liao, Jin, and Yuan dynasties previously established their capitals here, they were all foreign rulers governing China <note>original: "夷狄" (Yidi), a historical term for non-Han ethnic groups</note>. They were not worthy of the excellence of such a magnificent strategic location. 

When it came to our Emperor Chengzu Wen <note>The Yongle Emperor (reigned 1402–1424), who moved the capital from Nanjing to Beijing</note>, he resided here as a "hidden dragon" <note>original: "龍潜" (Longqian), a term for a prince living in his fiefdom before ascending the throne</note>. Upon succeeding to the Great Lineage, he established this place as Beijing and moved the capital here. From this seat, he could govern the myriad states and pacify the tribes of the four directions. It truly matches the excellence of the landscape and serves as a grand foundation, unshakeable for ten thousand generations. Since the eras of the legendary Emperors Yao and Shun <note>original: "唐虞" (Tang Yu)</note> and the Three Dynasties <note>The Xia, Shang, and Zhou, considered the golden age of Chinese civilization</note>, there has never been a capital of such magnificence.

<summary>This text describes the geographic and spiritual superiority of Beijing, arguing that its strategic landscape was only truly fulfilled when the Ming Emperor Chengzu (Yongle) established it as the capital.</summary>

<meta>This page continues the historical and geographical survey of Beijing, moving from the Ming Dynasty’s relocation of the capital back to its ancient origins in the Zhou and Qin dynasties, specifically focusing on the natural water defenses of the region.</meta>

The Capital of Yan <note>Beijing</note> occupies the ancient territories of Jizhou and Bingzhou. According to the "Records of the Bureau of Maps" <note>original: "職方氏" (Zhifangshi), an ancient office responsible for geography and maps</note>, the region north of Yan is called Bingzhou, and its principal river is the Hutuo.

The Ouyi <note>An ancient river or irrigation system</note> waters this land, along with the Lai and Yi rivers. The "Tribute of Yu" <note>original: "禹貢" (Yu Gong), a classic geographical text</note>, in its description of Jizhou, notes that once the Heng and Wei rivers were brought into their channels and the Great Plain <note>original: "大陸" (Dalu)</note> was cultivated, the Lai 
and Yi rivers followed. These rivers—the Yi, Heng, and Wei—constitute the very substance of the Yan region. Long ago, the Duke of Zhao <note>original: "召公" (Shao Gong), a statesman who helped found the Zhou Dynasty</note> was first granted this land as a fiefdom. Later, as the state declined, the Qin Dynasty sought to annex it. 

The Qin still feared the Dukang region <note>A fertile and strategic area in the state of Yan, modern-day Baoding</note>, as its dense network of waterways made it difficult for an army to penetrate. It was only when Jing Ke <note>original: "荆卿" (Jing Qing), the famous assassin-envoy</note> presented the map of this territory that the King of Qin was greatly pleased. Cao 
Yinru <note>A Ming dynasty scholar</note> observed that the lands south of Yan are fortified by water. Amidst the Gemo <note>A historical marshland</note> and various other wetlands, the narrow embankments and paths are linked together like delicate threads.

It is exactly what Chen Guan <note>A historical commentator</note> described as a place "created by Heaven and established by Earth."

The <term>Western Park<note>Xi Yuan; a vast imperial garden complex located west of the Forbidden City in Beijing, comprising what are now Beihai and Zhongnanhai parks.</note></term> is located within the Imperial City. Inside, there is the <term>Taiye Lake<note>Taiye Chi, literally "Great Liquid Pool."</note></term> and <term>Qionghua Island<note>Qionghua Dao, or "Exquisite Jade Flower Island," the central feature of Beihai Park.</note></term>. The lake's circumference is both deep and wide; its ripples are clear and shimmering. Green lotuses and fragrant water-weeds exhale their scent and display their elegance, while swimming fish and floating birds compete in play, gathering in great flocks. The island is composed entirely of strange rocks, jagged and <gloss>piled high</gloss>, looking down upon the lake waters. Atop the island sits the <term>Guanghan Palace<note>Guanghan Dian, the "Palace of Vast Cold," named after the mythical palace of the Moon Goddess.</note></term>, its beams and eaves <gloss>soaring like birds in flight</gloss>. Gold and jade-green colors reflect upon one another, with multi-storied galleries and high pavilions arching toward each other from left and right. Ancient pines and weathered cypresses are shrouded in mist and clouds, making it seem hidden away like the <term>Penglai Immortal Abode<note>A legendary mountain in Chinese mythology said to be the home of the Eight Immortals.</note></term>. Among the <term>Eight Views of the Capital<note>Jingshi Bajing; a traditional list of the most beautiful scenic spots in Beijing.</note></term>, the ones called "Taiye Sparkling Waves" and "Qionghua Spring Clouds" refer to this place. Furthermore, to the northeast of the park lies <term>Mount Wansui<note>Wansui Shan, the "Mount of Ten Thousand Years," likely referring to the artificial hill known today as Jingshan.</note></term>; it is lofty, bright, and elegant, winding and majestic. Its peak pierces the high heavens, casting its shadow over the palaces. All of these are the finest sights within the <gloss>Forbidden City</gloss>.

The purity and clarity of the water are lovely to behold. Following the <gloss>path</gloss> to the northeast of the pond, willows, apricots, and peaches grow alongside grass that blankets the banks like a carpet, while the fragrance of flowers greets the traveler. Upon reaching the **Pepper Garden**<note>Jiao Yuan; a garden area likely named for its fragrant trees or historical associations with the warmth and fragrance of imperial quarters.</note>, the pines and junipers are a lush, deep green, standing distinct from the surrounding trees. In the center is a circular hall where gold and jade-green colors reflect one another; it is open and spacious on all four sides and is called **Chongzhi**<note>Chongzhi Dian, the Hall of Honoring Wisdom.</note>. To its south is a small pond where goldfish move in formation, playing in the water. To the west is a small pavilion overlooking the water, surrounded by fragrant trees, called **Wanfang**<note>Wanfang Ting, the Pavilion of Enjoying Fragrance.</note>.

Proceeding north again, one arrives at the **Round City**<note>Yuancheng; a high, circular terrace-fortress located at the southern entrance of Beijing's Beihai Park. It remains one of the oldest preserved parts of the imperial gardens.</note>. Ascending via the gate-arches on either side, there are three ancient pines with jagged branches and proud, gnarled trunks like dragons stretching their claws into the air, standing tall against the sky. In front are several varieties of flowering trees with an exceptionally pure fragrance. In the center, a circular hall rises majestically and high, called **Chengguang**<note>Chengguang Dian, the Hall of Receiving Radiance, which houses a famous white jade Buddha statue.</note>.

Looking north from here, the mountain peaks are craggy and the summits a brilliant green. Looking down, the lake ripples are swaying and crystal clear. Between the mountains and the water, a thousand postures and ten thousand forms present their wonders and beauty before the windows and tables of the hall. To the west is a long pond; crossing over the pond and passing a stone bridge to the north is a mountain called **Wansui**<note>Wansui Shan, or Longevity Mountain. This refers to the artificial hill known today as Jingshan (Coal Hill), located directly north of the Forbidden City.</note>. Its jagged, strange rocks form three gates, through which one may enter from either the east or the west.

There is a hall leaning against the mountain, where stones are set to its left and right to serve as peaks. These peaks stand opposite one another in sequence, and on all four sides, the rocks resemble the jagged teeth of the **Bixi**<note>A mythical creature, one of the nine sons of the dragon, typically depicted as a giant tortoise capable of carrying heavy loads, often used as the base for stone steles.</note>. They are sealed with moss and entwined with creeping vines, while fine trees and exotic grasses droop from above or cling to the sides. Winding wisteria and tangled greenery provide dense cover. On both flanks, stones are stacked to form steep paths, which twist and turn ruggedly as they ascend.

The mountain side contains more than one cave or grotto. Along the slopes, three halls stand in a row: the center is called **Renzhi**<note>Renzhi Dian, the Hall of Benevolence and Wisdom.</note>, the left is **Jiefu**<note>Jiefu Dian, the Hall of Great Blessing.</note>, and the right is **Yanhe**<note>Yanhe Dian, the Hall of Extending Harmony.</note>. Reaching the very summit, there is a hall called **Qingxu**<note>Qingxu Dian, the Hall of Pure Void.</note>. Here, the cold air is so biting that even at high noon in the height of summer, the heat cannot reach it. It feels exceptionally vast, cool, and refreshing, as if completely isolated from the human realm; for this reason, it is called **Guanghan**<note>Guanghan Gong, or the "Palace of Vast Coldness," the legendary home of the Moon Goddess Chang'e. This refers to the main hall on the island's peak.</note>. 

To the left and right are four pavilions situated on the tops of various peaks, named **Fanghu**, **Yingzhou**<note>Fanghu and Yingzhou are two of the three legendary islands of the immortals in Chinese mythology.</note>, **Yuhong**<gloss>Jade Rainbow</gloss>, and **Jinlu**<gloss>Golden Dew</gloss>. Within these, one may lean back and rest. At the front cliff, there is a wall through which a path passes. Four holes have been cut into this wall to allow for expansive viewing. The lofty towers and palaces rise up majestically, and the beautiful scenery is exactly like a painting.

Descending past the **East Bridge** and turning north around the peak, there is a hall overlooking the pond called **Ninghe**<note>Ninghe Dian, the Hall of Condensed Harmony.</note>. Two pavilions overlook the water: **Yongcui**<gloss>Embracing Emerald</gloss> and **Feixiang**<gloss>Flying Fragrance</gloss>. Proceeding north to the **northeast corner**<note>The original uses the term "Gen" (艮), the trigram for the northeast direction in the I-Ching.</note>, one finds the source of the pond. It is said that this water comes from the **Jade Spring** of the **Western Hills**.

Looking east from here, the mountain peaks appear reflected upside down within the shimmering waves of the **Taiye Pool**<note>The 'Great Liquid Pool,' a name for the expansive imperial lake (now part of Beihai and Zhongnanhai).</note>. The deep green of the slopes and the mountain mist are so vivid they seem as if they could be gathered up by the handful. Further to the southwest, there is a small hill. From a distance, I observed it looking lush and dense; the sunlight shone horizontally across it, revealing layers of purple and emerald. Upon reaching it, one finds a hall leaning against the hill. Beneath the hill is a cave, and above the cave, rocky crags are arranged horizontally. Spring water gushes out from many small openings, cascading down in a torrent called the **Water Curtain**<note>Shuilian (水簾).</note>. Its gurgling, splashing, and the way the fine mist sprays and scatters is most delightful to behold. The sound of the water is cool and tinkling as it seeps into a stone basin, where a stone dragon raises its head.

### The Definition of the Three Wu Regions

The *Classic of Waterways* <note>original: "Shuijing" (水經), an early work on Chinese hydrography</note> states that the **Three Wu** <note>A historical and cultural region in the Yangtze River Delta</note> consists of **Wuxing**, **Wujun**, and **Huiji**. However, the *Comprehensive History of Institutions* <note>original: "Tongdian" (通典), an institutional history of China written by Du You in the Tang Dynasty</note> identifies them as **Wuxing**, **Wujun**, and **Danyang**. There appears to be a discrepancy between these sources.

This discrepancy arises because one might not realize that during the **Spring and Autumn period** <note>c. 770–476 BCE</note>, the capital of the **Wu State** was situated between the modern cities of **Suzhou** and **Changzhou**. Consequently, Suzhou contains **Wu County**, and Changzhou contains **Jingxi**. 

During the **Han Dynasty**, when the capital of Wu was enfeoffed <note>granted as a feudal estate</note> at **Huiji**, this "Huiji" did not refer to the modern **Shaoxing** <note>a city in Zhejiang province currently associated with the name Huiji</note>, but rather encompassed the regions of **Changzhou**, **Suzhou**, **Jiaxing**, and **Hangzhou**. 

As for the **Kingdom of Wu** during the **Three Kingdoms period** <note>220–280 CE</note>, its ruler was initially the Governor of Huiji and was enfeoffed as the **Marquis of Wu**. Although the lands where the state was eventually established were not exclusively "Wu," the administration governed **Suzhou**, **Songjiang**, **Changzhou**, **Zhenjiang**, **Hangzhou**, **Jiaxing**, and **Huzhou** for the longest duration. 

During the **Qin Dynasty**, the **Huiji Commandery** <note>term: "Jun" (郡), an administrative subdivision similar to a province or prefecture</note> was established. Later, **Emperor Wu of the Han** separated **Huzhou** to create the **Danyang Commandery**. Under **Emperor Shun of Han**, these were unified again as **Wu Commandery**. In the Three Kingdoms era, Huzhou alone was designated as **Wuxing**. Throughout these changes, the region never truly lost the name "Wu."

The reason why **Suzhou**, **Hangzhou**, **Jiaxing**, and **Huzhou** are so prominently known by the name "Wu" today is that they have held that title for a very long time, stretching back to antiquity. In contrast, the **Wu State** of the Three Kingdoms period was relatively short-lived. Furthermore, the names of areas like **Songjiang**, **Changzhou**, and **Zhenjiang** underwent frequent changes during the **Jin** and **Tang** dynasties, being renamed to **Jinling**, **Jiangjun**, **Xu**, and **Yan**. These differing historical timelines and administrative renamings account for the confusion in the names we use today.

Jinling <note>The historic name for modern-day Nanjing</note> possesses many fine mountains; **Ox Head Mountain** is the most eminent of them all. This mountain sits to the south of the city. **Wang Dao** <note>276–339 CE; a prominent Chancellor of the Eastern Jin Dynasty</note> once pointed toward it and remarked, "This is the Gate of Heaven!" Consequently, it also became known as **Heaven-Gate Mountain**.

The *Records of the Grand Historian* <note>original: "Shiji" (史記)</note> states: "I first rose up in Xiapi and met the Sovereign <note>The founding Han emperor, Liu Bang</note> at Liu. A poem by Liu Changqing <note>A famous poet of the Tang Dynasty (709–785 CE)</note> says:

*Visiting the ancient ruins beneath these city walls,*
*Where could Zifang be found today?*
*The white clouds have drifted away, never to return,*
*Leaving the high battlements empty and imposing.*
*Long ago, during the struggle between the kingdoms of Chu and Han,*
*His great talent for <term>statecraft|the term "jingji" (經濟) here refers to the classical meaning of "governing the world and providing for the people," the root of the modern word for economics</term> was widely known.*
*Devising strategies amid the dust and turmoil of war,*
*He possessed the power to reshape heaven and earth.*
*Now, creeping weeds accumulate with every passing day,*
*And the ancient pines have long since withered.*
*While his fame and merit fill the <term>green annals|history books; traditionally written on green bamboo slips</term>,*
*His memorial shrine is covered only in dark green moss.*

### The Platform of the Singing Wind

The **Platform of the Singing Wind** is located in Pei County. <note>Pei County, in modern-day Jiangsu Province, was the ancestral home of the Han imperial family.</note> After **Emperor Gaozu of the Han** <note>Born Liu Bang, r. 202–195 BCE</note> suppressed the rebellion of **Ying Bu**, he passed through Pei on his return journey. He prepared a feast at the Palace of Pei and summoned all his old friends, the town elders, the local matrons, and the youth of the area to join him. They served the wine and reminisced about old times, sharing laughter and joy.

When the wine had flowed freely and the Emperor was in high spirits, he struck the **zhu** <term>an ancient stringed instrument played by striking the strings with a bamboo stick</term> and sang a song he had composed himself:

->**A great wind rises, and the clouds fly high.**<-
->**My power reaches all within the four seas; I return to my old home.**<-
->**Where shall I find brave warriors to guard the four directions?**<-
<note>original: "大風起兮雲飛揚，威加海內兮歸故鄉，安得猛士兮守四方。" This is the famous "Song of the Great Wind," reflecting the Emperor's triumph and his underlying anxiety about maintaining his empire.</note>

Upon finishing, he rose to dance, filled with deep emotion and a heavy heart. Tears fell down his face in several lines as he spoke to the elders: "The traveler always grieves for his homeland. Even after a thousand autumns and ten thousand years, my soul and spirit will still long for Pei."

**Wenzhongzi** <note>The philosopher Wang Tong (584–617 CE), known for his commentaries on governance and history.</note> remarked: "In the 'Song of the Great Wind,' the Emperor did not forget the potential for danger even while in a state of safety; is this not the persistent heart of a **hegemon**?" <term>霸心 (baxin): the strategic ambition and vigilance required of a supreme ruler to maintain power.</term>

### The Horse-Leaping Terrace

The **Horse-Leaping Terrace** <note>original: *Ximatai* (戲馬臺)</note> was the location where **Xiang Yu** <note>The "Hegemon-King of Western Chu" (232–202 BCE), a legendary general and tragic rival to the founder of the Han Dynasty</note> held equestrian displays and reviewed his troops during the time he established his capital at **Pengcheng** <note>modern-day Xuzhou</note>. It is situated at the southern foot of **Cloud Dragon Mountain** <note>original: *Yunlong Shan* (雲龍山)</note> in present-day Xuzhou.

The hero has long since passed away, and the scenery is no longer what it once was. The noble steeds are silent, and the beautiful ladies <note>likely a reference to Consort Yu and the palace dancers</note> have ceased their dance. Amidst the desolate mounds and broken ridges, the reeds and rushes rustle mournfully in the wind. Now, there is only the sight of wild grain growing in lush abundance <note>original: *he shu li li* (禾黍離離), a classical literary allusion used to express sorrow over seeing a former capital or palace turned into farmland</note>, swaying to and fro in the autumn breeze.

### End of Volume Six of the *Goblet Words of the Hermit of Mount Yanzhou*
<note>original: 弇州山人巵言 (*Yanzhou shanren zhiyan*). This is a celebrated collection of literary and historical criticism by the Ming scholar-official Wang Shizhen (1526–1590), who used the pseudonym "Hermit of Mount Yanzhou."</note>

The **Jiji Pavilion** <note>literally "Pavilion of Lodging upon Lodging," implying the temporary nature of earthly life and official posts.</note> is located within the compound of the Branch Bureau of the <term>Ministry of Revenue<note>The *Hubu*, responsible for census, taxes, and the distribution of grain and funds.</note></term> at <term>Qingjiangpu<note>An essential port and administrative hub on the Grand Canal in modern-day Huai'an, Jiangsu.</note></term>. The design of the pavilion is very small; pond water surrounds it on all four sides, and one must cross wooden frames to reach it. It was constructed when <term>Shao Wenjing<note>Shao Bao (1460–1527), a scholar and official known for his integrity.</note></term> was overseeing military provisions. The commemorative essay for the pavilion was written by Master <term>Cheng Huangdun<note>Cheng Minzheng (1445–1499), a high-ranking scholar-official.</note></term>.

Master <term>Li Xiya<note>Li Dongyang (1447–1516), a lead poet of the Ming Dynasty.</note></term> wrote in a poem:
*Inside the Lodge of Lodging, I lodge this body,*
*This body truly becomes a lodger within a lodging.*

A later official in charge of military provisions also built the **First Spring Thatched Pavilion** to its south. To the north of the original pavilion, there was the **Hall for a Pleasant Evening**; this is the same building that Master <term>Yangming<note>Wang Yangming (1472–1529), one of the most influential Neo-Confucian philosophers in Chinese history.</note></term> inscribed with the name "Thatch Hall of Qingjiang." The calligraphy on the pavilion's nameplate is of excellent quality; it is the brushwork of Master <term>Bao’an<note>Wu Kuan (1435–1504), a Grand Secretary and renowned calligrapher.</note></term>.
