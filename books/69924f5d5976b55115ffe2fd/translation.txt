**4. 1. a sanctuary of Athena surnamed Warlike.** <note>original Greek: *Areia*</note> According to the historian Plutarch (*Life of Aristides*, 20), eighty talents <note>A talent was a large unit of currency; eighty talents represented a massive sum of money at the time.</note> were assigned to the people of Plataea by the Greeks as their share in the spoils taken at the battle of Plataea (not the battle of Marathon, as the writer Pausanias mistakenly says). With these eighty talents, the Plataeans built a sanctuary for the goddess Athena, installed a statue, and decorated the temple with paintings that remained in excellent condition until Plutarch's time. The level of detail in Plutarch's account regarding the dispute over the money makes it likely that he had better information than Pausanias regarding the temple's origins.

**4. 1. the bronze image on the Acropolis.** See book 1, chapter 28, section 2 with the accompanying note in volume 2, page 348 and following.

**4. 2. the other, by Onasias, depicts the former expedition of the Argives etc.** Compare book 9, chapter 5, section 11. The painter Onasias is not mentioned in any other historical records. Compare the Critical Note on this passage in volume 1, page 601.

**4. 2. Arimnestus, who commanded the Plataeans.** Compare the historian Herodotus, book 9, chapter 72; and Plutarch, *Life of Aristides*, 11.

**4. 3. a sanctuary of Eleusinian Demeter.** <note>Demeter of Eleusis was a primary deity of the harvest and mystery cults.</note> This is unlikely to be the same sanctuary where the Spartans fought fiercely against the Persians during the Battle of Plataea. See above, pages 5 and following.

**4. 3. Leitus.** He is named as one of the leaders from Boeotia <note>A region in central Greece where Plataea was located.</note> by Homer in the *Iliad*, book 2, line 494.

**4. 3. The Gargaphian fountain.** See above, page 13. Herodotus notes (book 9, chapter 49) that this spring was filled with earth and ruined by the Persian cavalry.

**4. 4. a river Oeroe.** This is a small, slow-moving stream, now called the Livadostro River <note>original: *Potami-Livadostro*</note>. It is formed near Plataea by several brooks coming down from Mount Cithaeron. It flows west along the northern base of the mountain and empties into the bay of Creusis. See Bursian, *Geography* <note>original: *Geogr.*</note>, volume 1, page 244; Vischer, *Memories* <note>original: *Erinnerungen*</note>, page 550; Baedeker, 3rd edition, pages 176, 179; *Guide-Joanne*, volume 2, page 26.

**4. 4. Scolus.** This location is mentioned in Homer’s *Iliad* (book 2, line 497). It was situated to the east of Plataea, as the Persian army under General Mardonius looted the lands of Scolus while marching from Tanagra to Plataea (Herodotus, book 9, chapter 15). The geographer Strabo tells us that Scolus was a village in the Parasopia (the valley of the Asopus river) at the foot of Mount Cithaeron, located in a rough, rocky area. There was even a local proverb: "Go not to Scolus yourself, nor follow another there" (Strabo, book 9, page 408). On a small, rocky plateau at the base of a ridge of Mount Cithaeron, overlooking the Asopus valley, sits a branch of the monastery of Saint Meletius. Scholars Leake and H. N. Ulrichs believed this site perfectly matches the descriptions given by Strabo and Pausanias. It is located a few minutes west of the large Albanian village of Darimari. Near this plateau is a spring that feeds a brook flowing north to meet the Asopus about half a mile away. The Asopus river usually dries up in the summer, leaving only a few deep pools where fish are plentiful. Its riverbed is thick with vegetation.

The notion that water, especially the water of a spring, is guarded by a dragon or serpent has been held by many peoples. We have seen that the water of the Styx <note>the legendary river of the underworld in Greek myth</note> was supposed to be guarded by dragons or serpents and is still called the "Dragon Water" (see the note on Book VIII, chapter 17, section 6). In Bechuanaland <note>modern-day Botswana</note> there is a species of large water-snake which the Bechuana people regard as sacred; they believe that if one of these snakes is killed, the fountain will dry up (J. Philip, *Researches in South Africa* (London, 1828), vol. 2, p. 118). The Zulu people believe in a fiery serpent which comes out of the water; it runs very fast. Some men once waylaid a serpent as it came out of its pool and killed it; consequently, the pool dried up. See Callaway, *Nursery Tales, Traditions, and Histories of the Zulus*, p. 290. The Namaqua people <note>an ethnic group in Southern Africa</note> think that in every fountain there lives a snake, and that if the snake leaves the fountain or is killed, the fountain will dry up (Th. Hahn, *Tsuni-Goam, the Supreme Being of the Khoikhoi* <note>original: "Tsuni-Goam, the supreme being of the Khoi-Khoi"</note>, pp. 53, 77). The Budduma people, who inhabit some islands in Lake Chad <note>original: "Lake Tchad"</note>, have the greatest veneration for a fabulous being who haunts the lake in the shape of a gigantic serpent; in great emergencies they implore his advice and help (G. Nachtigal, *Sahara and Sudan* <note>original: "Sahǎrâ und Sûdân"</note>, vol. 2, p. 369). In a lagoon near Gimbo-amburi in West Africa, a prodigious serpent is believed to reside, which has the power of curing those suffering from madness. A madman is bound hand and foot by his friends and flung into the lake; the serpent carries him off to the bottom of the lake, and after twenty-four hours releases him alive and in his right mind (Labat, *Historical Account of Western Ethiopia* <note>original: "Relation historique de l’Éthiopie occidentale"</note>, vol. 1, p. 348). In a frightful ravine in Algeria there is a spring which was believed to have been once guarded by a serpent; the people fancied that the serpent was animated by the soul of one Sidi Mohammed and had the power of healing sore eyes (Certeux and Carnoy, *Traditional Algeria* <note>original: "L’Algérie traditionnelle"</note>, p. 129 and following <note>original: "sq."</note>). # CHAPTER XIV CERESSUS

When the troops were drawn up in array and the enemy was in sight, the king sacrificed a she-goat to **the Huntress** <note>original: *Agrotera*, an epithet for the goddess Artemis</note>. Then he ordered the flutes to strike up **Castor’s tune**, every man put a garland on his head, and the whole army advanced singing a marching song and stepping to the music of the flutes (Xenophon, *in the work cited* <note>Latin: *op. cit.*</note> xiii. 8 ; *the same author* <note>Latin: *id.*</note>, *History of Greece* <note>Latin: *Hellenica*</note>, iv. 2. 20 ; Plutarch, *Life of Lycurgus*, 22). But the first sacrifice offered by the king on the field of battle was to the **Muses** (Plutarch, *Life of Lycurgus*, 21). The meaning of this sacrifice seems obvious. The stirring effects of martial music were probably explained by the early Greeks as due to the direct inspiration of the goddesses of music, the Muses. Hence sacrifices were offered to them and their favor entreated just before the flutes struck up the charge. As to the military music of the Greeks, see Plutarch, *On Music* <note>Latin: *De musica*</note>, 26. Athenaeus says (book xiii. page 561) that the **Lacedaemonians** <note>The Spartans and their regional allies</note> sacrificed to **Love** <note>Greek: *Eros*</note> before an engagement, and that in Crete also sacrifices were regularly offered before a battle to Love by the handsomest men in the army dressed in gay attire.

**13. 6. Xenocrates.** Compare iv. 32. 6 note.

**13. 11. The victory —— was the most famous etc.** The same estimate of the **Theban victory at Leuctra** <note>A decisive battle in 371 BCE where Thebes ended Spartan military supremacy</note> is expressed by Plutarch (*Life of Agesilaus*, 29).

**13. 12. The Thebans —— lost forty-seven men etc.** Xenophon says that the Lacedaemonians had nearly 1000 slain, of whom 400 were Spartans ; he does not mention the loss on the Theban side (*History of Greece*, vi. 4. 15). Diodorus says (xv. 56) that the Lacedaemonians lost not less than 4000, and the **Boeotians** <note>The regional group led by the Thebans</note> about 300. Plutarch (*Life of Agesilaus*, 28) puts the loss of the Lacedaemonians at 1000. According to Dionysius of Halicarnassus (*Roman Antiquities* <note>Latin: *Antiquitates Romanae*</note> ii. 17) the Spartans lost 1700. Pausanias’s estimate of the loss on the Boeotian side is, as Grote remarks (*History of Greece*, vol. 9. p. 401), preposterously low. The Spartans, trained soldiers as they were, must have sold their lives more dearly.

**14. 1. Epaminondas ordered the rest of the Peloponnesians to depart etc.** Pausanias’s statement that **Epaminondas** <note>The famous Theban general and statesman</note> dismissed the allies of the Lacedaemonians but kept the Lacedaemonians themselves prisoners, seems inconsistent with the narrative of Xenophon, who says that the beaten army, including both the Lacedaemonians and their allies, retreated by a difficult road and met the fresh levies, which Archidamus was leading to their assistance, at Aegosthena in the territory of Megara (Xenophon, *History of Greece*, vi. 4. 26).

**14. 2. Ceressus.** The site of this place is uncertain. H. N. Ulrichs inclined to place it at *Eremokastro* <note>Meaning 'Deserted Castle'</note>, a modern village on an eminence close to Thespiae. On the slope towards Thespiae there are a few large stones, which Ulrichs thought might be remains of the walls of Ceressus. But the eminence on which *Eremokastro* stands can hardly be described as a ‘stronghold.’ It is low and nowhere, I think, in the least precipitous. Wheler thought that Ceressus may have been near *Palaeo-Panagia* <note>Meaning 'Old Virgin Mary'</note>, a village about 2 miles to the north-west.

## HYRIA OR MYCALESSUS? BOOK IX. BOEOTIA

The <term>Hermaeum<note>A sanctuary or monument dedicated to the god Hermes, who was the protector of travelers and boundaries.</note></term> was located 2 miles from Mycalessus. They attacked the town at daybreak; word reached Thebes (about 12 miles distant as the crow flies) a few hours later. The Theban cavalry came galloping to the scene of the massacre and overtook the retreating Thracians a mile or two along the road to Chalcis. They followed them all the way to the shore, but without making much impression on them, as the Thracians kept in close order. It was only when the latter at last reached the shore and attempted to get on board their ships that they met the fate they so richly merited.

The site of the Hermaeum mentioned by Thucydides may have been at a point where the road to Chalcis leads through a natural pass (now called the pass of *Anephorites*) between two peaked heights. Here, on either side of the road, may be seen the remains of a thick wall of loose stones running at right angles to the road and ascending the heights both to the left and to the right of it. On the right (east) the wall may be traced as far as the ruined town (probably Hyria) on the *Megalo Vouno* <note>Greek for "Big Mountain."</note>. This wall, part of which was hastily repaired in the Greek War of Independence, seems to have been intended to defend the frontier of the Theban territory towards Chalcis; and as Hermes is associated with boundaries <note>original Latin citations: ii. 38. 7, viii. 34. 6, viii. 35. 2</note> he might very well have a sanctuary (Hermaeum) at the point on the road where the wall marked the Theban boundary. The distance of this point from the ruins which, along with H. N. Ulrichs and Bursian, I take to be those of Mycalessus, appears to correspond tolerably with the distance (16 furlongs) of the Hermaeum from Mycalessus. The Hermaeum mentioned by Livy <note>original: xxv. 50</note> seems to have been a different place; it was on the shore of the Euripus <note>The narrow channel of water separating the island of Euboea from the Greek mainland.</note> and there was a regular ferry across from it to Euboea. Leake appears to have involved himself in topographical difficulties from supposing that the Hermaeum of Thucydides and the Hermaeum of Livy were the same place.

Lieutenant (afterwards Admiral) Spratt was of opinion that the ancient road from Chalcis to Thebes did not, like the present road, go direct by the pass of *Anephorites*, but kept close to the coast, and made a circuit round the bay of *Vourko* to Aulis; for he found the ruts of chariot-wheels very distinct all round the bay of *Vourko*. But this only proves that there was, as we should expect, a coast road from Chalcis to Aulis, Delium, and Oropus. It does not show that the high-road from Chalcis to Thebes did not follow the direct and natural route by the pass of *Anephorites*.
