# The Rising Tone

---

**Chief**
<gloss>Chief</gloss> <note>Original: 酋 (Qiú). This character originally depicted a vessel of aged wine, later coming to mean a tribal leader or headman.</note>

**Silk**
<gloss>Silk</gloss> <note>Original: 絲 (Sī). A pictograph showing two bundles of silk thread, representing the importance of textile production.</note>

**To Collapse**
<gloss>To Collapse</gloss> <note>Original: 圮 (Pǐ). This character describes the destruction or ruin of an object, particularly an earthen structure or wall.</note>

**Heart**
<gloss>Heart</gloss> <note>Original: 心 (Xīn). Representing the physical organ, the heart was traditionally viewed as the seat of both emotion and intellect.</note>

---

**Ninth Stem**
<gloss>The Ninth Heavenly Stem</gloss> <note>Original: 壬 (Rén). Part of the Ten Heavenly Stems used in the sexagenary cycle for counting days and years; it is associated with the element of Water.</note>

**First**
<gloss>First</gloss> <note>Original: 先 (Xiān). Composed of a foot above a person, signifying someone walking ahead of others in time or space.</note>

**Forest**
<gloss>Forest</gloss> <note>Original: 林 (Lín). A simple yet effective ideograph doubling the 'tree' character to represent a dense collection of trees.</note>

**Sound**
<gloss>Sound</gloss> <note>Original: 音 (Yīn). Often used to describe musical notes or the specific pronunciation of a word.</note>

---

**Claw**
<gloss>Claw</gloss> <note>Original: 爪 (Zhuǎ). A pictograph of a hand reaching down, resembling the talons of a bird or the claws of a beast.</note>

**Gold**
<gloss>Gold</gloss> <note>Original: 金 (Jīn). While specifically meaning gold, it is the general term for all metals in traditional Chinese philosophy.</note>

**Straight**
<gloss>To stand tall</gloss> <note>Original: 挺 (Tǐng). Conveys the idea of being upright, prominent, or physically enduring.</note>

**Male**
<gloss>Male</gloss> <note>Original: 男 (Nán). A combination of the characters for 'field' and 'strength,' reflecting the historical role of men in agricultural labor.</note>

---

**Three**
<gloss>Three</gloss> <note>Original: 三 (Sān). Three horizontal lines representing the number three, often symbolizing Heaven, Earth, and Humanity.</note>

**Sweet**
<gloss>Sweet</gloss> <note>Original: 甘 (Gān). Depicts a small mark inside a mouth, signifying the pleasant taste of food.</note>

**Salt**
<gloss>Salt</gloss> <note>Original: 鹽 (Yán). A complex character referring to the vital commodity harvested from brine or mines.</note>

**Blazing**
<gloss>Blazing</gloss> <note>Original: 炎 (Yán). Formed by stacking two 'fire' characters to represent intense heat or a great conflagration.</note>

---

**Ornate Strokes**
<gloss>Ornate strokes</gloss> <note>Original: 彡 (Shān). A radical representing long hair, feathers, or the shimmering rays of light.</note>

**Long Hair**
<gloss>Shaggy hair</gloss> <note>Original: 髟 (Biāo). A radical found in characters related to hair, depicting long, flowing locks.</note>

# Discourse on the Methods of Seal Script

In the use of the brush for Seal Script, it is most appropriate to use the <term>single-wrap grip</term> <note>original: 單苞 (*dānbāo*); a specific method of holding the brush with the index finger alone wrapping the handle, allowing for greater fluidity in the rounded strokes of Seal Script</note>. With this grip, squareness, roundness, levelness, and straightness can all be achieved exactly as one desires. Because many today have lost the traditional teachings of the masters, their characters are often slanted and their momentum lacks vitality. When beginning one's studies, if one can keep the palm hollow, extend the middle finger, and join it with the index finger to draw in the air upon the desk—without being restricted to the left—then one may truly begin to hold the brush.

In Seal Script, characters that are horizontally broad are called <term>Chibian</term> <note>original: 螭匾 (*chībiǎn*); literally "hornless-dragon flatness," referring to a horizontally compressed, powerful style of script</note>. The scholar Xu Xuan referred to this style as the work of a "mature hand." The <term>Stone Drum Inscriptions</term> are of this style. 
<note>Xu Xuan (916–991 AD) was a renowned Song Dynasty philologist. The Stone Drum Inscriptions are the oldest known large-scale stone inscriptions in China, dating back to the late Zhou Dynasty; they are considered the "ancestor" of all Seal Script.</note>

For **Small Seal Script**, it is common for people to prefer elongated forms. However, if they are too long, they lose their technical discipline. One should use a ratio of one-and-a-half standard square characters as a measure: one character’s height for the "main body" and a half-character’s height for the **"hanging legs"** <gloss>the vertical strokes that extend below the main frame of the character</gloss>. These legs should not exceed a proportion of three to nine. When a character has three legs, the central leg should be treated as the primary one, while the others are slightly shortened, resembling a flowing banner.

In characters such as **"To Grow"** or **"Ear,"** the strokes that extend upward actually serve as the "legs." 
<note>Originals: 生 (*shēng*) and 耳 (*ěr*). The author is explaining that "legs" (protruding strokes) can go up or down depending on the character's logic.</note> 
This follows the principle of nature: just as with plants and trees, when they grow upright, the branches emerge upwards; when they hang down, the branches emerge downwards. This is simply the nature of the thing.

<meta>Continuing from the previous page's discussion on the elongation of Small Seal Script, this section provides specific instructions on managing negative space and the proportions of internal enclosures within characters to ensure they remain balanced and "渾厚" (vigorous and profound).</meta>

In the internal openings of Seal Script characters, the space within the circles should not be completely filled. It is like placing a character within a <term>well-frame</term> <note>original: 井斗 (*jǐngdǒu*); refers to the structural grid or the central square of a character’s layout, much like a well-curb</note>; one must allow the space below to remain empty so that <term>hanging strokes</term> <note>original: 垂筆 (*chuíbǐ*); vertical strokes that descend straight down, often tapering like a needle</note> can be placed. Only then will the surrounding enclosure not feel excessively large. 

When compared to other characters, these enclosures must also have their "mouths" slightly constricted. The shape should be neither perfectly round nor perfectly square; one should simply use the standard of <term>carving a mold with charcoal</term> <note>original: 炭斲範子 (*tànzhuó fànzi*); a metaphor for the precise, deliberate shaping used by craftsmen when creating molds for casting metal</note> to achieve a natural beauty. 

Characters such as "Sun" (日) or "Eye" (目) must be made even smaller in scale. However, within <term>seal inscriptions</term> <note>original: 印文 (*yìnwén*); script specifically designed for carving into personal or official seals</note>, for <term>flattened openings</term> <note>original: 匾口 (*biǎnkǒu*); horizontally compressed internal spaces</note> as well as the "mouth" radical and the upper portion of the character for "Child" (子), the openings must instead be slightly wider. By allowing more visible empty space within these openings, the character finally achieves a quality that is <term>vigorous and profound</term> <note>original: 渾厚 (*húnhòu*); a central aesthetic in Chinese calligraphy denoting a sense of being solid, simple, and full of internal strength</note>.

### Formula for Dividing the Five Sounds

*Jian*, *Xi*, *Qun*, and *Yi* are **Molar sounds**. <term>Molar sounds (牙音): Velar consonants produced at the back of the mouth, similar to "k," "g," or "ng."</term>
*Duan*, *Tou*, *Ding*, and *Ni* are **Tongue-tip sounds**. <term>Tongue-tip sounds (舌頭音): Alveolar consonants like "t," "d," and "n."</term>
*Zhi*, *Che*, *Cheng*, and *Niang* are **Tongue-top sounds**. <term>Tongue-top sounds (舌上音): Retroflex sounds made with the tongue curled toward the roof of the mouth.</term>

*Bang*, *Pang*, *Bing*, and *Ming* are **Heavy-lip sounds**. <term>Heavy-lip sounds (重唇音): Bilabial sounds made with both lips, like "p," "b," and "m."</term>
*Fei*, *Fu*, *Feng*, and *Wei* are **Light-lip sounds**. <term>Light-lip sounds (輕唇音): Labiodental sounds made with the teeth and lower lip, like "f" and "v."</term>
*Jing*, *Qing*, *Cong*, *Xin*, and *Xie* are **Tooth-tip sounds**. <term>Tooth-tip sounds (齒頭音): Dental sibilants like "ts" or "s."</term>

*Zhao*, *Chuan*, *Chuang*, *Shen*, and *Chan* are **True-tooth sounds**. <term>True-tooth sounds (正齒音): Palatal or retroflex sibilants like "sh" or "zh."</term>
*Xiao*, *Xia*, *Ying*, and *Yu* are **Throat sounds**. <term>Throat sounds (喉音): Guttural sounds or glottal stops produced in the throat.</term>

*Lai* and *Ri* are **Half-tongue and Half-tooth sounds**. <note>These are "semivowels" or liquids, like "l" and "r," that don't fit perfectly into the other categories.</note>

### Technical Definitions

**Movable type board** <term>huózì bǎn (活字板): A printing system where individual characters are carved into small wood blocks and arranged in a frame. This was much more efficient for large-scale printing than carving an entire wooden block for every single page.</term>

## Nine-Parts View
<note>original: 九分像 (jiufen xiang). In this orientation, the head is turned only slightly away from the viewer. This angle—showing ninety percent of the face—was often considered more aesthetically pleasing than a perfectly flat frontal view because it provides a better sense of the depth of the nose and the contour of the far cheek.</note>

## Rear View
<note>original: 背像 (beixiang). Literally "back likeness." This view focuses on the structure of the back of the head, the neck, and the positioning of headwear. In narrative painting, this perspective was essential for depicting figures walking away or engaged in scenes where their faces were hidden from the viewer's vantage point.</note>

## Ten-Parts View
<note>original: 十分像 (shifen xiang). A full, symmetrical frontal view (one hundred percent). <term>Painting Manual (Huapu)</term>: <note>original: 畫譜</note>. A category of instructional books that used woodblock illustrations to teach aspiring artists the standardized "vocabulary" of brushwork, anatomy, and composition.

<term>Likeness (Xiaoxiang)</term>: <note>original: 肖像</note>. A general term for a portrait. The character "Xiao" (肖) implies resemblance or "to be like" one's parents or ancestors, highlighting the importance of lineage in Chinese portraiture.

# Illustration of Washing One's Feet
<note>original: 濯足圖 (Zhuózú tú)</note>

<note>
This woodcut illustration depicts a man, likely a scholar or a hermit, seated at the edge of a stream or on the side of a small boat, dipping his bare feet into the flowing water. His robes are tucked up, and his expression is one of tranquil detachment. This image is a hallmark of "literati" art, where simple physical acts are imbued with deep philosophical meaning regarding a person's relationship with society and the state.
</note>

In the cultural vocabulary of the Ming Dynasty, the act of "washing one's feet" (濯足) serves as a powerful metaphor for <term>reclusion (yǐnyì)</term>. This concept is rooted in the ancient "Song of the Fisherman" from the *Songs of Chu*, which states: "When the water of the Canglang is clear, I wash my hat-strings; when the water of the Canglang is muddy, I wash my feet." 

To "wash one's hat-strings" means to prepare for government service when the world is just. However, to "wash one's feet" in the muddy water signifies a withdrawal from the "dusty" or corrupt world of politics and officialdom. By washing away the literal and metaphorical dust of the road, the individual seeks to maintain their moral purity and find peace within the natural world. For the readers of this encyclopedia, this illustration provided a visual archetype for the life of a retired official or a sage who has chosen a path of spiritual independence over worldly ambition.

**Key Terms:**

*   <term>Washing Feet (Zhuózú)</term>: <note>original: 濯足</note> A symbolic gesture of retreating from the corruption of public life and the "dust" of the mundane world to embrace a life of purity.
*   <term>Reclusion (Yǐnyì)</term>: <note>original: 隱逸</note> The act of living in withdrawal from society and official duties, often to cultivate one's character or escape political turmoil.
*   <term>Human Affairs (Rénshì)</term>: <note>original: 人事</note> The section of the *Sancai Tuhui* encyclopedia that categorizes human behaviors, social roles, and physical postures.

## Illustration of Sitting Cross-Legged
<note>original: 趺坐圖 (Fūzuò tú)</note>

<note>
This woodblock illustration depicts a bearded practitioner—likely a scholar or a Daoist adept—sitting in the "lotus position" on a woven mat. His hands are placed together in his lap, and his gaze is directed forward in a state of focused calm. This posture is a cornerstone of traditional Chinese meditative and health-preserving practices.
</note>

The practice of <term>sitting cross-legged (fūzuò)</term> is an essential method for both spiritual cultivation and physical longevity. In this posture, the practitioner folds their legs so that the soles of the feet are turned upward, resting upon the opposite thighs. This "locked" position is intended to stabilize the body, allowing the mind to remain undistracted by physical discomfort or shifting weight.

In the Ming Dynasty context of this encyclopedia, this pose is closely associated with <term>sitting meditation (zuòchán)</term>, a practice shared by Buddhists, Daoists, and Neo-Confucian scholars alike. While the specific intent might vary—Buddhists seeking enlightenment, Daoists practicing <term>internal alchemy (nèidān)</term>, or scholars practicing "quiet sitting"—the physical form remains the same. By grounding the lower body, the practitioner encourages the <term>vital energy (qi)</term> to descend from the head and circulate through the core, fostering a state of "stillness in motion" where the internal spirit is gathered and the external world is forgotten.

# Illustration of Strolling
<note>original: 散步圖 (Sànbù tú)</note>

<note>
This woodblock print depicts a scholar in traditional robes and a cap, walking with a steady, leisurely gait. He carries a long staff over his shoulder, a common symbol in Chinese art for the wandering sage or the retired official who finds peace in nature. The illustration emphasizes a sense of "unfettered ease," where movement is not a chore but a refinement of the spirit.
</note>

The practice of <term>strolling (sànbù)</term> is considered an essential counterpart to the "quiet sitting" described previously. In the traditional Chinese view of health, balance is found in the alternation between movement and stillness. If one sits for too long, the <term>vital energy (qi)</term> and blood may become stagnant; conversely, if one overexerts themselves, the spirit becomes scattered. 

Strolling serves as a gentle "moving meditation." It is particularly recommended by physicians and Daoist practitioners after a meal to assist the "splenic fire" in digestion, or after a long period of study to refresh the eyes and mind. The goal of this practice is not to reach a destination quickly, but to move with a relaxed body and a wandering mind, allowing the breath to remain deep and natural.

By carrying a staff and walking through a garden or mountain path, the practitioner harmonizes their internal rhythm with the external world. This simple act of walking is seen as a way to "dispel the old and take in the new," ensuring that the body remains supple and the mind remains clear.

<note>

The practice of **drunken chanting** is more than a simple act of leisure; it is a refined method of spiritual and emotional release. In the Chinese tradition, to <term>chant (yín)</term> is to recite verse with a melodic, rhythmic cadence, a form of expression that sits between speech and song. When this is performed in a state of <term>intoxication (zuì)</term>, it is believed that the scholar's rational mind—bound by social rules and official duties—steps aside, allowing his true, spontaneous nature to emerge.

For the historical figure Bai Juyi, who styled himself the <note>original: 醉吟先生 (Zuìyín Xiānshēng)</note> "Master of Drunken Chanting," this state was a sanctuary. After years of service to the state, he found that wine and poetry together could "dispel a thousand cares." In this state, the poet is not merely "drunk" in the modern sense, but is intoxicated by the beauty of the world and the rhythm of his own words.

Within this manual of human affairs, the illustration serves to remind the reader that health is not merely a matter of physical exercise or quiet meditation. The health of the "heart-mind" also requires moments of unfettered joy and the vocal expression of one's inner spirit. By chanting aloud in a state of relaxed ease, the practitioner clears "stagnant clouds" from the chest and allows the <term>vital breath (qi)</term> to flow freely through the voice and the body.

It was less about the literal consumption of fungi and more about the cultivation of a "mountain-and-forest" temperament. The presence of the attendant in the illustration suggests a hierarchy of learning—the youth performs the labor of the search, while the master possesses the wisdom to recognize the spiritual value of the find.

This pursuit represents the "reclusive" side of the scholar's life. If "Passing the Cup" represented the scholar’s social obligations and friendships, **Gathering the Spirit Fungus** represents his private devotion to health, longevity, and the mystical secrets of the natural world.

<note>original: 携琴圖 (Xiéqín tú)</note>

<note>
This woodblock illustration depicts a quintessential scene of the Chinese scholarly ideal. A gentleman, dressed in the voluminous, flowing robes of a Ming-era literatus, wanders through an unseen landscape. The scholar’s upward gaze suggests he is attuned to the mountains, clouds, or the wind in the trees, searching for the perfect moment of inspiration.

The **Illustration of Carrying the Zither** captures the core of the "recluse" aesthetic. In the tradition of the Chinese scholar-official, the <term>Guqin (gǔqín)</term> was the most prestigious of instruments. It was not intended for public performance or rowdy entertainment; rather, it was a vehicle for self-cultivation and a way to communicate with nature. 

To **carry the zither** into the mountains was a symbolic act. It represented leaving behind the noise of the city and the burdens of bureaucracy to find "wordless communication" with the Dao. Often, a scholar might not even play the instrument, finding that the mere presence of the zither in a beautiful landscape provided enough "music" for the soul. This concept is famously associated with the poet Tao Yuanming, who was said to keep a "stringless zither," claiming that as long as one understands the inner meaning of the music, there is no need for the sound of strings.

In this composition, the relationship between the master and the servant is central to the narrative. The attendant performs the physical labor of transporting the instrument, allowing the master to remain in a state of pure contemplation. This imagery reinforces the Ming Dynasty's social and cultural values, where the pursuit of art and nature was the highest calling for a refined man.

# Illustration of Writing on a Wall
<note>original: 題壁圖 (Tíbì tú)</note>

<note>
In this woodblock illustration, we see a scholar standing before a rugged rock face or a plaster wall, a common sight at mountain temples, roadside inns, or scenic overlooks. While the previous page showed the scholar seeking harmony through the sound of the zither, here he seeks to immortalize a moment of inspiration through the written word. His young attendant stands by, likely holding the brushes and ink stone required for the task.
</note>

The **Illustration of Writing on a Wall** depicts the ancient tradition of <term>Tibi (tíbì)</term>, which literally means "inscribing a wall." For the Chinese literati, walls were not merely structural; they were public canvases. When a poet was moved by a beautiful vista, a sense of melancholy at a parting, or the historical weight of a specific site, they would brush a poem directly onto the architecture or the stone.

To **inscribe a wall** was to engage in a conversation across time. A scholar might arrive at a remote pavilion and find poems written there by famous predecessors decades or even centuries earlier. By adding his own verse, he joined a "literary lineage," connecting his personal experience to the broader flow of history.

This practice served several purposes:
*   **Commemoration:** Marking a visit to a famous mountain or temple.
*   **Social Networking:** Leaving a message for friends who might travel the same path later.
*   **Expression:** Releasing emotions that were too powerful to be kept internal.

The composition of this image, with its jagged lines and the scholar's focused stance, highlights the interaction between the human mind and the permanence of nature. Just as the zither player on the previous page sought to echo the sounds of the wind, the poet here seeks to give the silent mountains a voice through his calligraphy.
