Having suffered shipwreck, they have plunged themselves into the horrific tides and whirlpools of the highest impiety. Others, by contrast, have been ruined by a slowness and stupidity of mind; this state is especially exposed to gullibility when stirred up—as if by a fan—by anger, greed, and other passions that easily drive one forward. From this sort of bird-hunting <note>The author uses "aucupio" (fowling/bird-catching) as a metaphor for the Devil's methods of trapping souls.</note>, the Devil is accustomed to prepare his prey. For he attacks those who are moved by these "stings," as it were, and who are prompt and ready for any plan. Once they are led on by magnificent and plausible promises, he claims them for himself and enslaves them.

Those of the former kind—the <term>atheists</term> <note>original: ἄθεους (atheous). Literally "those without God."</note>—are mostly produced, nourished, and protected by that "liberty" which has erupted in our times from the variety and confusion of different regions. There is a rumor—though one not easily believed—that their number has already grown immensely. But they go unnoticed and undetected, either because they digest their impiety in silence and cover themselves with the veil of whatever religion is at hand, or because they stir up no riots out of zeal for their own opinions. Thus they are neglected, and (by a most unworthy softening of the name for such great impiety of the human heart) they are merely called "licentious," and no investigations are made against them, nor are examples made of them.

As for this second group, infamous for the stain of <term>sorcery</term> <note>original: sortilegii. -># TO THE READER.<-

I do not deny that I have immersed myself in this *Demonolatry* <note>original: "Dæmonolatreia"; the worship or service of demons</note> which I have now published. For during the nearly fifteen consecutive years in which I conducted investigations and gathered evidence against the guilty <note>original: "sontes"; referring here to those accused of capital crimes</note> whom Lorraine <note>original: "Lotharingia"</note> held during that time, I filled my head, as it were, with the prodigious assemblies, banquets, dances, enchantments, and poisons of the witches who frequently appeared among them. I dealt with their rushings through the air, their unspeakable custom of debauchery with the Demon, their frequent—so it seems—transformations into foreign shapes, and those kinds of outrages and impieties with which their lives are commonly defiled and contaminated.

And if ever it happened that I turned aside to the "gentler Muses" <note>a classical metaphor for poetry and literature</note> to meditate on something for the delight of the soul (as those who are practiced in literary study are accustomed to do; for sometimes, withdrawn from the courthouse, the page would yield a verse), those same images nevertheless offered themselves and forced their way in with a certain persistence of recent memory. I did not ungratefully reject them, because they seemed likely to supply no unhappy material for poems (the pastime I then happened to choose for my leisure).

Therefore, while I took up now this, now that, just as the mind plucks each thing for itself as the occasion arises, it happened to me as it does to those who, from every garment they fashion, first throw the scraps <note>original: "segmenta"</note> into a chest in a heap and without care. Later, when they think the scraps have grown to a certain number, they handle each one more diligently and gather from the most suitable pieces a "patchwork garment" <note>original: "centonem"; a poem or prose work made up of various excerpts</note>. If this work has nothing else, certainly the very variety—by which it is distinguished like an inlaid pattern <note>original: "emblema"</note> of mosaic tiles <note>original: "vermiculatis crustis"</note>—brings it some recommendation.

Truly, since the narrations of deeds (with which this patchwork is mostly filled) receive their chief increase of grace from the very light of truth—because true things are read more attentively than those that are feigned or devised, and because men spend their time more easily on those matters which have the expectation of utility joined with pleasure—I thought I would not be employing my labor badly if I rescued these accounts from an undeserved reputation of falsehood. Their credibility might have wavered, both from their novelty and strangeness, and from the style of speech in which they are written; therefore, I have applied those things which commonly have the most power to assert the truth.

...with arguments specific and distinct to the *matters, persons, places, and times* involved. Since I had not initially prepared a full supply of these details, and had only touched upon them playfully (as I mentioned) as they presented themselves to my mind in a general and universal way from a scattered collection of individual cases, I began five years ago to observe and transcribe from the <term>interrogations of the accused</term> <note>original: "reorum quæstionibus" — the formal legal questioning of suspected witches</note> those things which seemed capable of being specifically referred and adapted to those points. Furthermore, to fill the work more abundantly, I began to unroll and examine those records which I had neglected in previous years. Finally, while I was seeking some way to charm away the solitude of the countryside (where I had withdrawn because of the <term>plague</term> <note>original: "pestilentiam"</note> spreading in the city), I fortunately came across the bundles of papers in which I had recorded these commentaries. I then leisurely reduced them into certain chapters, which corresponded in their own order to those bare and simple notes, and joined them like parts to their whole.

These things I now present for your inspection, reader, modestly and faithfully recounting what I have discovered through long use and observation. I believe you have no reason to reject them, unless you hold (as they say) that those who abuse their leisure by terrifying men's minds with affected narratives of vain and futile things—which could scarcely have defended themselves even in those unrefined ages—should not be tolerated. Although the authority of the circumstances I just mentioned (witnessed and confirmed by the public monuments of every place where these deeds are recited) sufficiently blunts such criticism, I nevertheless wished to pluck this scruple and doubt from the minds of those who might approach these subjects as strangers and outsiders. Therefore, I did not grudge briefly adding certain consistent and, as it were, <term>parallel examples</term> <note>original: "παράλληλα" (parallēla) — Greek for things set side by side for comparison</note> from wealthy and excellent authors; for the commemoration of similar deeds is said to bring no small light and authority to the truth.

Indeed, there are many things which Demons <note>original: "Dæmones"; in this context, supernatural entities or spirits, not necessarily purely evil in the modern sense</note>, by their great power, perform that are entirely foreign to every mode and order which nature is accustomed to hold. 

No one judges such things unworthy of being handed down to posterity, provided that those things which contain nothing absurd are not false. For I know for certain that there are many who, using these spirits as a pretext, devise many ridiculous and silly tales: such as those who claim that witches <note>original: "sagas"</note> can strip men of their human form with their potions <note>original: "pharmacis"</note> and turn them into wild beasts; that souls migrate from their bodies for a time and return to them as if by a right of recovery <note>original: "postliminio"; a legal term for the restoration of rights to one returning from exile or captivity</note>; that women become pregnant by intercourse with Demons; and many other such things which they relate to us in all seriousness and try to persuade us to believe.

For an equal error is committed on both sides: both by rejecting those things which reason and long experience prove, and by admitting and confirming those things which the faith of every wisest man has always rejected. The reader will therefore estimate and weigh everything according to his own judgment.
