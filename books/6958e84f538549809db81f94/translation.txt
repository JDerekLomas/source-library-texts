->**O**mnipotent and eternal God<-

I have frequently revolved in my mind, with repeated consideration, the wonderful and admirable works of the Almighty and eternal God, the creator of all nature. I considered how, from the beginning of the world when the heavens were created, He adorned them with most bright stars. To these stars He gave the power and virtue to influence all uncreated things beneath the heavens. I also considered how He produced from nothing the four elements, differing among themselves in a marvelous way, in a most beautiful order: namely Fire, which is hot and dry; Air, hot and moist; Water, cold and moist; and Earth, dry and cold—bestowing upon each its own specific nature. Furthermore, I considered how the Supreme Craftsman and Master, having produced herbs, stones, and all living creatures, finally made and formed Man, more noble than all other creatures. And thus He disposed all things, so that all things under heaven having life and motion should, through the mediation of the stars, receive their nature and the permanence of their existence. 

In all things—whether herbs, stones, or even animals—there would be the four primary qualities of the four elements breaking forth: heat, cold, moisture, and dryness. These exist also in human bodies in a measure and a most excellent temperament <note>The "temperament" refers to the balance of the four humors: blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile.</note> suited to the life and nature of man. As long as that temperament remains balanced, man enjoys perfect health. But when it is dissolved through the failure of proper governance—when heat exists as a victor over the other natures and labors to expel cold, or when cold wishes to extinguish heat, or when a man is filled with disordered humors, or if he should fall away too much from his natural moisture through a deficiency—he immediately falls into great infirmities, drawing near to death. 

The reason for this dissolution of the natures in which the life and health of man consist is not single, but manifold. For at one time there are the hidden, poisonous influences of the heavens contrary to the nature of man; at another time, 

---

the impurity of the air, or unsuitable food, or disordered labor, and many other things that lead a man away from perfect health. If I wished to enumerate all of these, I believe it would be easier for me to count the sands of the sea. Indeed, since more than a thousand dangers surround a man, and the accidents that dissolve human health are almost innumerable according to the various influences of the stars, there is scarcely any moment to be found in which a man can be secure in his health or life. 

While I was silently revolving these things within myself, I remembered on the other hand the wisdom of the Creator, who from the beginning provided for the human race—set amidst such great dangers—a healthy and suitable remedy. This He provided in herbs, animals, and other creatures, into which He instilled such virtue that they might lead dissolved natures back to their proper temperament, harmony, and proportion. For one herb heats, another induces cold, each according to the degree of its own complexion <note>term: complexion (the specific combination of qualities—hot, cold, moist, dry—inherent in a substance)</note> and nature. So too other creatures living in the waters or on the earth contribute to the preservation of human life, the Creator of nature so disposing it. Thus, by the virtue of these herbs and other creatures, a man destitute of strength through the imbalance of the primary qualities might return to full health. 

And because in this mortal and transitory life it is impossible for a man to possess a treasure more dear or desirable than true health of the body—as the Wise Man says in **Ecclesiasticus** chapter thirty: "Better," he says, "is the health of the soul and a strong body than all gold and silver, and an immense fortune. For there is no wealth above the wealth of the body's health" <note>original: "Melior est... salus animae et validum corpus quam census immensus." A reference to the Book of Sirach 30:15-16.</note>—it seemed to me to be the best course to set forth a book containing a multitude of figures of herbs, stones, animals, and other creatures, depicted in their certain proper colors, along with a description of their virtues. 

I could not perform a more laudable work than this, since it brings great solace to those who are well and confers no small utility upon those who are suffering. Moreover, charity urged me most principally to complete this task—the charity which made me prepare for the poverty of those who lack temporal means and money to provide for physicians and apothecaries <note>term: apothecary (a historical medical professional who formulated and dispensed materia medica to physicians and patients)</note> in their time of need. For these people...

->Tractatus<-

The teachings of this book have been gathered with very little expense. They will be able to provide themselves with preventative remedies and perfect medicines. To undertake this task, I was moved no less—but indeed most greatly—by a certain noble lord <note>Likely referring to Bernhard von Breydenbach, who traveled to the Holy Land and sponsored such works</note> who, by traveling through various kingdoms and lands—namely Germany, Italy, Istria, Slavonia, Croatia, Dalmatia, Greece, Saxony, Morea, Candia, Rhodes, Cyprus, and the Holy Land with its city Jerusalem; from there going into Little Arabia toward Mount Sinai; from Mount Sinai toward the Red Sea, Cairo, and Babylon <note>Old Cairo</note>; through Alexandria even into Egypt and elsewhere—gained great experience regarding the often-mentioned herbs, animals, stones, and other things necessary for the composition of medicines, which are unknown because of their rarity. Describing their virtues, he took care to portray their likenesses with appropriate outlines and certain colors. All and each of these you will find depicted in the present work under their proper figure, form, color, and in exquisite order. Moved therefore by these two reasons, I began this laudable work, relying on the help of God. I compiled it from the most proven masters of medicine: namely Avicenna, Hippocrates, Galen, Vincent, Serapion, Platearius, Pliny, Dioscorides, the *Pandects* <note>A reference to Matthaeus Silvaticus</note>, Johannes Mesue, Palladius, Constantinus, Almansor, and many others no less expert. It pleased me that it should be called the *Hortus Sanitatis* <note>The Garden of Health</note>. This entire book is divided into eight treatises, each of which contains a unique subject. The first, therefore, treats herbs and other things that occur for medical use, in which you will also find suitable figures of the herbs. The second is on the nature and complexion of many animals that lead their lives on land. The third is on birds and their natures. The fourth is on fish and their properties and natures. The fifth is on precious stones and their virtues. The sixth is on the disposition of urine, its color, and significance. The seventh contains a register for the easy finding of illnesses and remedies. The eighth and last contains a table according to alphabetical order, demonstrating everything contained in this book. May this most pleasant, fragrant garden of ours go forth into the whole world and increase itself for all who desire it. To you, therefore, O Creator of the heavens and the earth, who has infused curative virtue into all herbs, animals, stones, and other things contained in this work, I return immeasurable thanks, since you have given me this hitherto hidden treasure to bring into the light. To you be honor and glory for ever and ever. Amen.

## Operations <note>The medicinal uses and effects of the herb</note>

**A** **Pandectarius**, chapter 1: If ashes are made from southernwood and the head is washed with a strong lye made from them, it cures hair loss <term>allopicia: mange or baldness</term>. The herb itself performs this same work when boiled in lye. **B** Its seed, boiled in water, cures asthma and tightness of the chest. **C** A fumigation <note>burning the herb to produce medicinal smoke</note> of southernwood drives away serpents. Its juice, when drunk, is effective against the venom of reptiles. **D** A warm compress <term>cucarisma: a medicinal poultice or vapor bath</term> made from the water it was boiled in provokes menstruation. The herb itself, crushed and applied with old oil, cures the shivering fits of fevers. **E** **Galen**, in the book cited above: The leaves and flowers of southernwood appear to be cleansing for ulcers, yet they can be irritating. Furthermore, however many [fevers] occupy the body according to cycles, if you rub the herb on before the onset, they will shiver less. That it kills intestinal worms <term>lumbricos: parasitic roundworms</term> is fitting, because it is so bitter. **F** Also, boiled with a quince or with bread and applied, it heals inflammations of the eyes; the herb itself, when boiled, brings raw pustules to a head. **G** Burnt southernwood is hot and dry and has more power than burnt dry gourd. Likewise, when applied to moist ulcers, it leads to healing without inflammation, especially for those seen to arise on the prepuce of the private parts. And thus it is preferred for hair loss when mixed with old oil, or oil of dill or sesame; for it is composed of refined parts, is stinging, and is hot. **H** **Avicenna**: Burnt southernwood is preferred for hair loss, especially with castor oil <note>original: "alkerua"</note> or radish oil. And southernwood is preferred for this so that hair may grow, which is slow to be born, when it is cooked with certain oils because of its opening quality. **I** It also binds the blood of the gums and dissolves phlegmatic swellings. **K** **Dioscorides**: The seed of southernwood, boiled in water or infused raw in water, heals orthopnea <note>difficulty breathing while lying down</note>, bruises, pleurisy, and paralysis. Mixed with betony and <unclear>p̃dito vã</unclear>, it is a singular remedy for sciatica and difficulty urinating. **L** **Serapion**: It loosens the belly, expels intestinal worms and pinworms, and dissolves phlegmatic superfluities from the intestines. ## Chapter Three.

Wormwood: in Latin, *Absinthium*; in Greek, *absinthion*; in Arabic, *saricon*. *Absinthium* is a Greek name, and it most likely grows in the region of Pontus <note>An ancient region on the southern coast of the Black Sea</note>. Hence it is also called "Pontic." **Platearius**, in the book on simple medicines: There are two kinds of wormwood. One is found on the island of Pontus. Because it is Pontic, it has a [specific] flavor; it has a green color and a bitter taste, and then its effect is lesser. The other is Roman wormwood, which is better because it is far from the smell of the sea and grows in free lands; it has white, smooth, and flat leaves. It is gathered at the end of spring, dried in the shade, and preserved for a year. Wormwood has two contrary virtues: it is astringent due to the thickness of its substance and its harshness, and laxative due to its heat and bitterness. It is stated in the letter that Galen sent to the glutton: "Wormwood," he says, "has three virtues: one laxative, and another..."
