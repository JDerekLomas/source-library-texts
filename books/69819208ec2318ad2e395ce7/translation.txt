# Preface

---

After the fantasies of many centuries, the truth about alchemy—namely, that it is nothing but deception and fraud—has finally been brought into the light of day.

Chemistry was still in its infancy until the time of <term>Constantine the Great<note>Constantine I (reigned 306–337 AD), the Roman Emperor who legalized Christianity. The author suggests scientific progress was minimal until late antiquity.</note></term> *). For what one reads regarding <term>Hermes Trismegistus<note>A legendary figure, supposedly an ancient Egyptian priest and philosopher, to whom many foundational (and often mystical) alchemical texts were attributed.</note></term> is nothing but fairy tales.

# Preface

<meta>catchword: Thereby</meta>

The actual growth of chemical knowledge proceeds from <term>Paracelsus<note>Philippus Aureolus Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim (1493–1541), a Swiss physician and alchemist who pioneered the use of chemicals and minerals in medicine.</note></term> *) up to <term>Becher<note>Johann Joachim Becher (1635–1682), a German chemist and physician known for his development of the theory that would become the phlogiston theory.</note></term> and <term>Stahl<note>Georg Ernst Stahl (1659–1734), the chemist who formalized the phlogiston theory, which suggested a fire-like element was contained within combustible bodies.</note></term>, and from them once again to the great discoverers of <term>artificial types of air<note>original: "erkünstelter Luftarten." In the 18th century, "airs" was the term used for what we now call gases (e.g., oxygen was "dephlogisticated air").</note></term> and the expanders of <term>salt chemistry<note>The branch of chemistry concerned with the composition and reactions of acids, bases, and salts.</note></term>: Macbride, Priestley, Ulrici, Marggraf, Macquer, Lavoisier, and many others.

Chemistry was first taught publicly only at the end of the 16th century. The first textbook was written by <term>Libavius<note>Andreas Libavius (c. 1555–1616), whose work "Alchymia" is considered the first systematic textbook of chemistry.</note></term>, Frankfurt 1597, in <term>quarto format<note>original: "4.", a book size where sheets are folded twice to form four leaves.</note></term>, and expanded in 1606 in <term>folio format<note>A large book size where sheets are folded only once.</note></term>. In 1780, I possessed all of his chemical writings in my library. Being ignorant in the various branches of medicine, many chemists administered doses without proper judgment, and thereby frequently caused tragic consequences for the sick.

# Preface

...they made chemical preparations so notorious that in the year 1566 in Paris, by a decree of Parliament, no preparation of <term>antimony<note>original: "Antimonium oder Spießglanze." Antimony was a controversial remedy in the 16th century, often used as a powerful emetic (to induce vomiting).</note></term> was permitted to be used for the sick. Although many physicians soon after came to the defense of chemistry, it was not until 1673 that Johann Matte was appointed the first demonstrator of chemistry in Montpellier *).

When <term>Stahl<note>Georg Ernst Stahl (1659–1734), a central figure in the transition from alchemy to chemistry.</note></term> transformed metals into <term>calx<note>original: "Kalk." In historical chemistry, "calx" refers to the powdery substance left after a metal has been roasted; we now know this as a metal oxide.</note></term>, and turned this back into metals again, chemists could finally see how much their art depended on not inventing things about nature, but rather seeking through experience that which reason places within nature. Such men, who have worked with their own hands, are to be preferred over all those pharmaceutical chemists who merely theorize.

If only all lovers of alchemy would proceed as did the one who, in the year 1785 in <insert>Silesia</insert> <note>original: "Schle="; likely "Schlesien" (Silesia), truncated at the page break.</note>
