# Preface

The present work has—to confess it unreservedly—cost me a full decade of my life, during which I devoted myself to it exclusively, almost entirely setting aside all other obligations. The profound interest of the subject matter carried me away, captivated. The more I immersed myself in it, the more clearly I recognized its infinite branches in every direction, through all sciences, into every detail of practical life, both the sensory and the spiritual. Permeated by the feeling of the wide-ranging significance of the material I had grasped, it was not difficult for me to dedicate myself to it with all my energy. If I have succeeded in illuminating many previously dark and confused phenomena and bringing them into a scientific connection under common theoretical viewpoints, I owe this to the charm that the overcoming of every difficulty and the gain of every new clarification holds for the <term>natural scientist</term> <note>original: "Naturforscher"; in the 19th century, this term encompassed both physical scientists and natural philosophers</note>, a charm that transforms effort and perseverance into pleasure and spiritual elevation.

The task of this writing is the presentation of a widely branched investigation, but not the delivery of a textbook, much less a handbook. I would ask the reader to keep this somewhat in mind while reading. As much as feasible, I have indeed endeavored to organize the subject according to its various branches and to bring together similar elements in experiments and conclusions, so that a tolerably systematic arrangement could be achieved. Some may think that I could have been more concise in my arguments here and there, and generally speaking, I am of the mind that they may be right. And from where does Mr. von Liebig derive such absolute certainty that "sensitives" are "nerve-weak" people, a label he immediately applies to all my assistants? There, he might run the risk of being rebuffed for one of his usual premature judgments. Sensitives are more nerve-irritable than non-sensitives, but not weaker in their nerves. He fails to make this distinction and therefore confuses the two. Nowhere in physiology is it written that irritability goes hand in hand with weakness. Quite the contrary: as a rule, irritability decreases where weakness increases; consequently, conversely, irritability usually increases where power and strength grow. Sensitive phenomena are not based on weak nerve activity, but rather on heightened, elevated, and stronger activity. Sensitives are in no way weak, but rather nerve-strong, at least in the area of their <term>odic receptivity</term> <note>The author's term for the ability to perceive "Odic force," a hypothetical vital energy he claimed to have discovered</note>. They are not sensitive because they are sick—for if they were, every sick person would soon have to be sensitive—but because their nerve-irritability is heightened, a state for which one does not necessarily have to be ill, as is shown by a large number of my perfectly healthy sensitives. From pathology, we know that in many cases where vegetative life <note>Basic biological functions like digestion and growth</note> appears depressed, nerve activity appears heightened; in such cases, the patient suffers locally in the vegetative sphere, but not essentially in the general nervous system. The latter is in a state of excitation, called to increased activity, and thus affected, but not necessarily diseased; on the contrary, the strengthening and health of the diseased vegetative area must be drawn from the health and power of the general nervous system. Sensitives feel and see more, not because they are weak, but because their perceptive ability is heightened and the power of their <term>receptivity</term> <note>The capacity to receive external stimuli or impressions</note> is strengthened. I have before me male and female <term>sensitives</term> <note>Individuals allegedly capable of perceiving subtle energies</note> of striking nerve-strength, and Mr. von Liebig’s assertion here, made without any testing or experience, is arbitrarily stated and completely mistaken.

Anyone who racks their brain over the internal qualities of substance beyond mere appearance—yet refuses to listen to, or even allow a word from, the person who comes to provide a tangible grasp of the most important phenomena of feeling and light upon which those hidden appearances depend—for such a person, nothing remains but pity for their unworthy blindness. I must accuse the present before the future and can only expect satisfaction from the latter.

I regret that I must increase the difficult and complex doctrine of the <term>Dynamides</term> <note>Reichenbach uses this term to describe imponderable forces or "powers" like heat, light, and electricity</note> by one more link, and indeed expand it by one that now directly and very intimately intertwines physiology—which until now has kept itself rather aloof—with physics. Through this, natural science is considerably enriched on what has been its most meager spot, but it also admittedly complicates the study and thorough penetration into its higher realms, where life joins with <term>crystallization</term> and where material vegetation begins to grasp spiritual life. Herein probably lies a main reason why I encounter adversaries almost everywhere on my path; it is not difficult to take a few individual experiments from journals and insert them into one's store of knowledge in their proper place; but it is very laborious, very time-consuming, and very difficult to enter into an entirely new field of knowledge, to follow it through its far-reaching complexities, to take it into memory, and to weigh its comprehensive significance. Only rarely will one of the older, heavy, scientific authorities condescend to do this. I cannot help it, however. "What is true is a power." And the facts that exist, the truths that have been uncovered, demand their due. A man respected in the world of natural research wrote to me: "If it were as you write, it would be too beautiful, and such a thing does not occur in nature." So, because the discoveries revealed through the <term>Od</term> <note>The "Odic force," a hypothetical vital energy or life force coined by the author</note> are too beautiful, they are groundless, and they cannot and must not exist! Because of the greatness of their significance, their existence is not permitted! ... If some guild-certified physicists believe that the circle of <term>Dynamides</term> in nature is closed with the established <term>imponderables</term> <note>Weightless substances or forces, a common 19th-century scientific concept</note> of heat, light, electricity, magnetism, etc., they might be no less mistaken than those who once thought—
