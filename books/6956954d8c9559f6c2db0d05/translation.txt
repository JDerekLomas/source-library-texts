# PREFACE.

The text adopted for this edition is that of Bekker, as reprinted at the Oxford University Press. Here and there I have ventured on changes, the reasons for which I have given in each case. These changes do not claim to rest on the authority of manuscripts, but rather on my own view of their advisability, or on the authority of others, whether they be editors or commentators. 

In editing any Greek or Latin writer, the question of the authority of various manuscripts is one which many editors must leave unapproached. The sound judgment required in such matters must be the result of extensive experience and study of that particular branch of editorial work. It seems to me a complete specialty; one who has not been led, either by inclination or professional pursuits, to give it special attention could not hope that any weight would be attached to his decisions. Such an editor had better, therefore, accept the conclusions of those who are recognized as authorities on the subject. 

Unfortunately, in the specific case of Aristotle, the editors of the great Berlin edition <note>The Berlin edition (1831) by Immanuel Bekker is the standard reference for Aristotle's works.</note>—which is acknowledged as the basis for any subsequent editions—have remained silent regarding the value of the manuscripts whose various readings they include. They are also silent regarding the grounds for their preference of the readings they adopt. This leaves us in ignorance, so far as their judgment is concerned, of how much assistance might be derived from the manuscripts. 

However, it would seem from the shared testimony of those who have focused their attention on the *Politics* of Aristotle that the amount of help from manuscripts is not large. The greatest opportunity for such alteration appears to be presented by the punctuation. Careful attention to this in the *Politics*, as in other works of Aristotle, will often give the clue to a difficult passage; and this is a point on which, I presume, all editors are equally free to act. I have therefore in many passages altered the punctuation, but I have done so without increasing the number of stops <note>original: "stopping" or "stops" refers to commas, periods, and other punctuation marks.</note>; the sparing use of punctuation is, I conceive, a great improvement in recent editions of the classics.

As a general rule, I have introduced no parenthesis where one did not already exist in the text I adopted. In certain cases, I have enclosed paragraphs of various and at times considerable length in brackets. In doing so, I have not intended to suggest that such paragraphs are not Aristotle’s—a point I feel quite incompetent to decide—but merely to show that, in my view of the context, they interfere with the connection of the thought, acting either as repetitions or as poorly timed digressions. And as it is in this connection of thought that the great difficulty often lies, I have, so far as I was able, endeavored to clear away any obstacle to its perception.

Such convenience no longer exists, and a subsequent editor need have little scruple in selecting that method which seems to his judgment absolutely the best.

And I cannot but think that an attentive study of the work will lead most readers to agree with the order adopted as most in keeping with the real order of thought, however much they may wish that the old order had remained undisturbed for the sake of convenience. For the new arrangement brings into close proximity two books, VI and VII (formerly IV and VI), whose separation is clearly the result of some accident. The rearrangement of these is advocated, as I have elsewhere remarked, even by the firmest opponents of the change in general. Again, by placing IV and V (formerly VII and VIII) immediately after III, the new order makes Aristotle’s aristocracy or ideal state—the second of the two correct forms of government—follow directly after his treatment of monarchy (the first form), and precede his treatment of *Politeia* <note>original: *Politeia*; here referring to "Constitutional Government" or the "Polity," Aristotle's third correct form of government</note> (the third form). 

In the existing arrangement, however, this third form is placed between the first and second. Lastly, after the analysis of the first two ideal forms has been completed, he proceeds, by a separate treatment of the elements of the third form, to prepare the way for a treatment of those elements in combination—in other words, for the treatment of that third form. Once its discussion is over, he goes through the problems connected with existing governments, their formation, and their organization; he elaborates, that is, the statics of Greek society. Then, in the absence of any theory or expectation of change, there is nothing left for him but to treat the diseases to which that society was liable: its chronic state of dissension and its acute state of revolution. This more logical order is entirely obscured by the traditional arrangement, and I feel convinced that its restoration is a real gain in studying the work. I need not add more on this point, as I have touched on it occasionally in the body of the work.

# PREFACE.

I have prefixed a short summary to each book. In this, I have aimed to provide a simple outline of the logical connection and an easy means of finding the general purpose of each chapter. I have also added marginal headings to enable the reader to more easily grasp the main subject of the page or to refer back to what they have previously read. For the purposes of reference, I have placed the page numbers of the Berlin Quarto Edition <note>The standard 1831 edition of Aristotle’s works edited by August Immanuel Bekker, which established the universal "Bekker numbering" system still used by scholars today.</note> alongside the text. In any quotations from other works by Aristotle, I have provided a double reference: to that edition and to the Oxford reprint of it.

In the notes, when offering my own translations, I have marked them with single quotation marks; when borrowing the words of others, I have used double quotation marks.

Regarding the content of the notes, my object has been to make them as helpful as possible for understanding the text, rather than using them as a vehicle for learned discussion. In a work like the *Politics*, more than in some others, it seems the main duty of an editor is to facilitate the study of the work itself—to place the student in a position to master it with as little difficulty as possible, and so to immerse themselves in its spirit and thought. The student should not have their attention diverted at every turn by the suggestion of difficulties regarding questions of outside interest. I have endeavored to carry out this view of an editor's role. If at times I have wandered in my notes from the strict interpretation of Aristotle, or from the necessary clarification of his statements, and instead offered my own opinions, I trust I shall be seen to have been sparing in such deviations and to have exercised fair self-command in not interfering with the appreciation of the work itself. The greatness of the author and the importance of the subject both acted as a check. It is in furtherance of this view that I have ventured to provide a translation of most of the difficult passages.

I have provided an index of the proper names that occur in the work, as well as an index of words and expressions. I trust this latter index will be found—if far from complete—sufficiently thorough to be a great advance over any existing index.
