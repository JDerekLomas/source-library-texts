From England, four hundred and seventy-one members are elected. In total, there are six hundred and fifty-eight members <note>While the text provides a total of 658, the specific figures listed for England, Wales, and Ireland add up to 629; the remaining 29 members likely represent Scotland, which is mentioned elsewhere in the manuscript but omitted from this specific count.</note>. Whenever there is a matter of national importance, a summons is sent out to gather the people of the various districts to the national capital, Parliament <note>original: "Baliman," a phonetic rendering of Parliament</note>, for a meeting. Originally, because it was impossible for all the people from every district to arrive at once, it was decided that each district would instead elect one or two members of the gentry <note>original: "shenqi," referring to the local elite or elders</note> to go to the national assembly. Once the business is concluded, they each return home. Later, it was formally established that these representatives should be chosen through a system of public election.

## The Court of Common Pleas
The Court of Common Pleas <note>original: "Gānwénbùliè yámén," a phonetic rendering of "Common Pleas"</note> specifically adjudicates cases involving disputes and accusations among government officials. The established positions include a Chief Justice <note>original: "zhìzhì," likely a phonetic approximation of the word "Justice"</note> to oversee the proceedings.

## The Court of the Exchequer
The Court of the Exchequer <note>original: "Yìshìzhījiā yámén," a phonetic rendering of "Exchequer"</note> specifically adjudicates cases regarding land disputes and marriage. The established positions include one Chief Baron <note>original: "mǎlún," phonetically rendering the title "Baron"</note> and three Puisne Barons <note>original: "bùyīshì mǎlún," where "puisne" is an old legal term for a junior judge</note>.

## The Courts of Assize and Nisi Prius
The Courts of Assize and Nisi Prius <note>original: "Āxīshì ānní xī bùlái’āshì yámén," phonetically rendering the names of these traveling courts</note> are organized into six Circuits <note>original: "sǎjiǔ," phonetically rendering "Circuit"</note>. Each circuit is assigned two Justices, totaling twelve judges. They are specifically responsible for the trial of English criminal offenders, traveling to conduct these sessions twice every year.

## The General Quarter Sessions
The Court of the General Quarter Sessions of the Peace <note>original: "Yīnílā’ěr gēdá xiē sūn ā fù lí bǐshì yámén," a phonetic rendering of the English court title</note> holds trials for offenders from various districts <note>original: "bùluò," literally "tribes," used by the author to denote counties or regional jurisdictions</note> four times a year.

## The Sheriff's Office
The Sheriff's Office <note>original: "Shèlā dáwén yámén," phonetically rendering "Sheriff"</note> <gloss>The specific duties of this official were missing from the original text</gloss>.

## The Constabulary
The Constabulary <note>original: "Xiàn yámén," likely referring to the office of the High Constable or local law enforcement</note> dispatches one hundred marshals <note>original: "mǎluò," likely a phonetic rendering of "marshals" or "patrolmen"</note> every year to inspect various districts and verify that the local regions are peaceful and orderly.

# Additional Notes on the Islands of Scotland, a Dependency of Great Britain

The land of **Scotland** <note>original: "Sī-gé-lán"</note> is formed of three connected regions with a river flowing through the center. The southeast is flat and open, while the northwest is predominantly mountainous. It was originally settled by the people of **Ireland** <note>original: "Ài-lún" (Eirin)</note>. 

The central region was once divided between the **Scots** <note>original: "Sī-gé-sī"</note> and the **Picts** <note>original: "Bì-sī"</note>; eventually, the southeastern region took its name from the Scots. There was also the kingdom of **Strathclyde** <note>original: "Shì-tè-nà-là-lǐ-lǜ"</note> which occupied the northwest. Around the year 500 of the Christian Era <gloss>the second year of the Yongyuan era of the Southern Qi Dynasty</gloss>, the Scots were annexed. A tribal capital was established on the hills of **Edinburgh** <note>original: "Ā-dǐng-lǜ" (Dunedin)</note>. After more than two hundred and fifty years, they were defeated by **Kenneth** <note>original: "Gēn-ní-cì" (Kenneth MacAlpin)</note> of the **Saxons** <note>original: "Sāi-xún"</note>, and the land's name was changed to Scotland.

In later years, **Robert the Bruce** <note>original: "Bù-lǔ-shì"</note> and **John Baliol** <note>original: "Bā-lì-gé"</note> struggled against one another for the throne. **King Edward** <note>original: "Yī-hè Wáng" (Edward I)</note> of England intervened with his army to support Baliol, establishing him as King. The throne eventually passed to the **House of Stuart** <note>original: "Shì-dū-gá"</note>, but they ruled without principles <term>wú dào: a classical Chinese term for a ruler who has lost the moral right to govern</term>, and the various tribes grew resentful. In the year 1603 <gloss>the 31st year of the Wanli era of the Ming Dynasty</gloss>, Great Britain took advantage of these internal divisions to conquer them, led by **Elizabeth** <note>original: "Yī-lín-má-shè" (Elizabeth I). While Elizabeth died in 1603, the Union of the Crowns occurred as James VI of Scotland became James I of England; the author frames this as an English acquisition.</note>.

## Appendix on Ireland, a Dependency of Britain

Ireland <note>original: "Ài-lún"</note> is located to the west and slightly north of Britain, standing alone as a single island. It is said that France first began to cultivate the land there, and a leader was publicly elected to manage its affairs. It is heard that roughly from the year 900 of the Era of Jesus <note>the Christian Era or Anno Domini</note>, after more than two hundred years of rule centered in Dublin <note>original: "Qīng-mò" (傾墨), a phonetic rendering of the Irish capital</note>, it was seized and conquered by Britain.

Establishing Dublin <note>original: "Là-mò-qīng" (臘墨傾)</note> as the primary settlement, the British appointed officials to restrain the population. The laws and decrees were severe: they permitted goods to be transported and sold only to London <note>original: "Lán-dùn"</note> and strictly forbade trade with any other nation. The people of the local tribes felt this grievance deeply in their hearts, and none were willing to submit.

In the year 1640 <gloss>the 13th year of the Chongzhen reign of the Ming Dynasty</gloss>, the masses gathered and slaughtered forty thousand British people, driving all the remaining survivors out of the territory. <note>This refers to the Irish Rebellion of 1641, a major uprising against British and Protestant rule.</note> Shortly thereafter, they were suppressed and pacified by troops from London.

Later, taking advantage of the years of continuous warfare between Britain and America <note>referring to the American Revolutionary War</note>, the Irish people finally began to gradually establish trade with other nations.
