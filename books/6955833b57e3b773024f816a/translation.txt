# To His Royal Highness
## George Augustus Frederick, Prince of Wales.

Sir,

These ancient remains <note>original: "Remains" – referring here to the surviving fragments of songs and poems</note> of the Welsh Bards, which I humbly present to your Royal Highness, are, I hope, not unworthy of such distinguished patronage.

In the country from which you derive your majestic title, music has always been considered one of its favorite forms of entertainment; and, when joined with poetry, it provided a type of luxury that was both innocent and educational.

There was a time when the Princes of Wales claimed it as their right to preside over the Congress of the Bards <note>The *Eisteddfod*, a traditional Welsh gathering of poets and musicians dating back centuries</note>, and thought it not beneath their rank to personally award those prizes decreed to merit in that famous ceremony. The name of the Bard was respected by royalty itself, and the number and skill of the poets gave dignity to the Prince's throne and lasting fame to his reputation.

Many of the following compositions often echoed during festival days through the halls of your illustrious ancestors. I am certain that your Royal Highness will feel some interest in bringing back to public attention what has received such honorable approval, and will deign to confirm with your favor these venerable remains of music and poetry, which descend to you as your inherited right.

The graciousness with which your Royal Highness has agreed to become the patron of this work is a noble proof of an early dedication to the interests of literature and the arts, and a promising sign for their future and permanent well-being.

Whatever the success of this attempt to save the surviving traces of the bards from being forgotten, it will serve as a tribute to the deep respect I shall always hold for your Royal Highness's person and your noble protection of the arts, while I have the honor to be,

->Your Royal Highness's<-
->Most Dutiful and<-
->Most Devoted Servant,<-
->Edward Jones.<-

## Necessary DIRECTIONS to the READER who is a Stranger to the WELSH LANGUAGE; showing the right Pronunciation of all the Letters that differ from the English Orthography <note>original: "Orthography"; refers to the conventional spelling system of a language.</note>.

To read Welsh, a correct knowledge of the Alphabet is all that is necessary; for, generally speaking <note>original: "not going to a nicety"</note>, all the Letters retain one consistent sound, which must be distinctly pronounced, as there are no silent letters <note>original: "Mutes"</note>. Letters that have a circumflex accent <note>the symbol ^ used over vowels</note> must be pronounced long, such as *Bôn* pronounced like the English word "bone"; *Bŵn* like "boon"; *Bîn* like "been"; and so on.

*C*, as the English 'C' in *Can*; but never soft as in *City*.

*Ch*, as the Greek letter Chi <note>original: "χ"</note> properly pronounced. If instead of touching the roof of the mouth <note>original: "Palate"</note> with the tip of the tongue to pronounce 'K', you touch it with the back of the tongue <note>original: "Root"</note>, it will produce this sound.

*Dd*, as the English 'TH' in *Them*; that is, very soft and voiced; not hard as in *Thought*.

*F*, as the English 'V'.

*Ff*, as the English 'F' and 'ff'.

*G*, as the English 'G' in *God*, but never soft as in *Genius*.

*I*, as the English 'I' in *King*, and 'ee' in *Been*; but never as the 'I' in *Fine* \*.

*Ll*, is an aspirated 'L' <note>produced with a breathy sound; a voiceless alveolar lateral fricative</note>; and can be represented in English only by 'Lh' or 'Llh'.

*Th*, as the English 'TH' in *Thought*; but never soft, as in *Them*.

*U*, as the English 'I' in *Bliss*, *This*, *It*, etc.

*W*, as the English 'oo' in *Good*.

*Y*, as the English 'U' in *Burn*, though in the last syllable of a word, and all one-syllable words <note>original: "monosyllables"</note>, except for *Y*, *Ydd*, *Ym*, *Yn*, *Yr*, *Ys*, *Fy*, *Dy*, and *Myn*, it is like the 'I' in *Sin*, *It*, etc. Both its sounds are nearly shown in the word *Sundry*.

# AN HISTORICAL ACCOUNT OF
## THE WELSH BARDS;
### AND
## THEIR MUSIC AND POETRY.

Due to the Roman invasion and the even more brutal raids of the Saxons, the Danes, and the Normans, as well as the migration of the Britons to Brittany <note>original: "Armorica"</note><sup>a</sup>; and through the frequent destruction of manuscripts<sup>b</sup> and the massacres of the clergy<sup>c</sup> and the Bards<sup>d</sup>, the poetry and music of Wales have suffered a loss that has cast a dark cloud over the history of these native arts. For a long time, this threatened their total extinction. Yet, from the records still in existence and the poetical and musical compositions that time has spared, we are often able to produce unquestionable evidence—and always to form a probable theory—concerning their rise and progress among us. There is no living nation that can produce works of such great antiquity and, at the same time, of such undisputed authority as the Welsh.

Our historians, always desiring to trace their subject back to the furthest point of ancient times, have derived the name and profession of the *Bards* from *Bardus*, the fifth king of Britain, who began his reign in the year of the world 2082 <note>This refers to the *Anno Mundi* calendar, which dates years from the biblical creation of the world.</note>. Berosus says he reigned over the Celts and was famous for inventing poetry and music. Perizonius, as Vitus asserts, called the music of Bardus "not just any music, but that which is poetic" <note>original Latin: "non omnem musicam, sed eam quae poetica est"</note><sup>e</sup>. Bardus, however, if other accounts are to be believed, was not the first to cultivate these sister arts on this island. Blegored, a king of Britain who died in the year of the world 2069, was called "the god of harmony" for his extraordinary skill in vocal and instrumental music<sup>f</sup>.

The Bards were originally a constitutional part of the Druidic hierarchy, which was divided into three classes: priests, philosophers, and poets<sup>g</sup>. At *Llanidan* in Anglesey—formerly inhabited by Druidic communal societies—we still find remains of *Tre'r Dryw* (the Arch-Druid’s mansion) and, near it, *Tre'r Beirdd* (the hamlet of the Bards)<sup>h</sup>. In his work *Caractacus*, Mr. Mason adopted the ancient distinction of the three orders of Druids. Having spoken of the Arch-Druid, he continues:

> ->His brotherhood<-
> ->Possess the neighboring cliffs:<-
> ->On the left<-
> ->Reside the wise Euvates: yonder grottos<-
> ->Are tenanted by Bards, who nightly from there,<-
> ->Robed in their flowing vests of innocent white,<-
> ->Descend, with harps that glitter to the moon,<-
> ->Singing immortal strains.<-

<sup>a</sup> Around the year 383, one hundred thousand Britons, in addition to a large army of soldiers, followed the Emperor Maximus to Brittany <note>original: "Armorica"</note> (now *Bretagne* in *France*), which he conquered. He placed Conan Meriadoc, a British lord and general, on the throne. See *Geoffrey of Monmouth*, book 5, chapters 12, 13, and 14. Also see *A Mirror of the First Ages* <note>original: "Drych y Prif Oesoedd"</note> by Theophilus Evans; Wynne’s *History of Wales*, page 8; and further details in Owen’s *History of the Ancient Britons*, page 100, volume 1.

<sup>b</sup> The Welsh nobles who were held captive in the Tower of London (formerly called the White Tower, a name part of it still carries) were allowed to have the contents of their libraries sent from Wales to entertain them during their isolation and imprisonment. This was a common practice, so over time the Tower became the primary storehouse of Welsh literature. Unfortunately for our history and poetry, all the manuscripts collected there were burned through the malice of a man named Scolan, about whom nothing else is known. *Gutto’r Glyn*, a famous Bard of the 15th century, wrote the following in one of his poems:
> The books of Wales and their villainous destroyer
> Were hidden in the White Tower.
> Wicked was the deed of Scolan,
> Who threw the heap of books into the fire.

Of the Bards, however, and of their poetry and music from those remote periods, little more than a faint tradition is preserved. What little we know is derived either from the poetic and legendary remains of the British annals or gathered from where it is scattered across the broader field of Roman history. There is no account of Britain in any writer preceding Caesar; however, it is incredible to suggest that its ancient arts first appeared under the oppression of the Roman yoke. Since it has never been claimed that any part of these arts was borrowed from the conquerors, whatever mention of them is found in the Greek and Roman authors who followed the first invasion may fairly be presented as being, in some measure, descriptive of their state before that time.

Those nations could not surely be unrefined in the composition of their poetry and music, among whom—as Caesar declares$^i$—the supremacy and omnipotence of the gods was acknowledged, the immortality and transmigration of the soul was believed$^k$, opinions were formed concerning the motion of the planets and the dimensions of the world, and whose youth were instructed in the nature and philosophy of things.

In all the Celtic nations, we discover a remarkable uniformity of manners and institutions. It was the custom of the ancient Germans, when they marched to battle, to inspire themselves by singing verses prophetic of their success, which they called *Barditus*$^l$ <note>original Latin: "Barditus"</note>. It was the honorable duty of the Bards of Britain to sing to the harp at weddings and funerals, at their games and other ceremonies, and at the head of their armies, singing the praises of those who had distinguished themselves by virtuous and heroic actions$^m$. This entertainment made a deep impression on the young warriors, lifting some to heroism and encouraging virtue in every heart. Among the Celts, says Diodorus Siculus$^n$, there are composers of melodies called Bards, who sing to instruments resembling lyres, performing songs of praise or criticism. They are held in such high reverence that when two armies, prepared for battle, have already thrown their spears and drawn their swords, they immediately stop upon the arrival and intervention of the Bards. Thus, even among the fierce barbarians, wrath gives place to wisdom, and Mars <note>The Roman god of war</note> yields to the Muses <note>The goddesses of the arts and music</note>.$^o$

A fragment of *Posidonius*, preserved in the works of Athenaeus$^p$, allows us to show the only example of the Bards' genius that can be attributed with certainty to a date earlier than the sixth century. Describing the wealth and magnificence of *Luernius*, *Posidonius* relates that, eager for popular favor, he was frequently carried over the plains in a chariot, scattering gold and silver among the thousands of *Celts* who followed him. On a day of banqueting and celebration, when he hosted his countless attendants with an abundance of choice food and a wealth of costly drinks, a barbarian poet arrived long after the others. He greeted Luernius by singing the praise of his unmatched generosity and noble virtues, but lamented his own bad luck in arriving so late. *Luernius*, delighted with his song, called for a purse of gold and threw it to the Bard, who, filled with gratitude, renewed his praise and proclaimed: *that the tracks of his chariot wheels upon the earth brought wealth and blessings to mankind.*

The students of the Druidical Bards, during an apprenticeship of twenty years, learned an immense number of verses$^q$, in which they preserved the principles of their religious and civil laws by unbroken tradition for many centuries. Although they were familiar with the use of letters, they never committed their verses to writing, both to strengthen their mental faculties and to keep their mysterious knowledge from the common people. The meter in which these poetic doctrines were shared was called *Englyn Milwr*, or the Warrior’s Song. As the reader will see in the attached example, this is a stanza of three lines, each with seven syllables; the first and second lines contain the general subject of the poem, and the third provides some divine or moral teaching or a practical proverb$^r$.

In the first three verses, the Druids seem to invoke their sacred groves and set forth their priestly <term>sacerdotal</term> privileges and exemptions. In the other three, they address the mountain *Eryri* (Snowdon), the Parnassus of Wales. We learn from Gildas <note>Gildas was a 6th-century British monk and chronicler.</note> that the ancient Britons had an extraordinary veneration for mountains, groves, and rivers.

When the Roman legions—after the invasion of Britain and the conquest of the Gallic provinces—were recalled to oppose the power of Pompey in Italy, the Bards’ exultation at recovering the secure possession and exercise of their ancient poetic function is described in a very animated manner by the Roman poet Lucan:

> You too, you Bards! whom sacred raptures fire
> To chant your heroes to your country’s lyre;
> Who consecrate in your immortal strain
> Brave patriot souls in righteous battle slain;
> Securely now the tuneful task renew
> And noblest themes in eternal songs pursue $^z$!

Such was the new but imperfectly discovered scene which the great Caesar’s ambition opened in Britain. Nor are these accounts only imperfect; they are also biased, as some bold spirits even among the Romans have suggested $^a$.

The Druids, expelled from Britain by the legions, took refuge in Ireland and the Isle of Man—places which the Roman sword could not reach at that time. The theory of British music moved with them and settled in Ireland, which from that period was for many ages the seat of learning and philosophy, until wars and dissensions buried almost every trace of them in oblivion $^b$.

The Bards, having now lost their sacred Druidical status, began to appear in an honorable, though less dignified, capacity at the courts of the British kings. The Oak Mistletoe $^c$ <note>The mistletoe was a plant sacred to the Druids, often harvested from oaks in religious ceremonies.</note> was deprived of its ancient authority, and the sword prevailed in its place. The music as well as the poetry of Britain, no doubt, received a flavor <note>original: "tincture"</note> from the martial spirit of the times. The Bards, who once had dedicated their profession to the worship of the gods in their forest temples, the celebration of public ceremonies, and the praise of the arts of peace—and who had once held back the fury of armies preparing to rush upon each other's spears—now:

In the sixth century—the golden age of Welsh Poetry—the Bards resumed the harp with unusual boldness to inspire their country’s last successful struggle against the Saxons.

# THE WELSH BARDS.

Taliesin—who in one of his poems gives an honorable testimony to the fame of Aneurin<sup>f</sup>—was, like him, called *Penbeirdd*, or Chief of Bards <note>original: "king of Bards"</note>. He lived during the reign and enjoyed the favor of Maelgwn Gwynedd, King of Britain. As an infant, he was found abandoned in a weir <term>a trap or fence across a river designed to catch fish</term>, which Gwyddno Garanir, the minor king of *Cantre'r Gwaelod* <note>the "Lowland Hundred," a legendary sunken kingdom</note>, had granted as a source of income to his son, Prince Elphin. 

Elphin, though possessing many likable qualities, was wasteful with his money. Having little success with the fish trap, he became discouraged and depressed. At this moment, Taliesin was found by the prince's fishermen, and by the prince’s command, the infant was carefully raised and given a thorough education. When he reached the proper age, the accomplished Bard was introduced by his royal patron to the court of his father, Gwyddno. On that occasion, Taliesin presented a poem called *Hanes Taliesin*, or *Taliesin’s History*. At the same time, he presented another to the prince called *Dybuddiant Elphin*<sup>g</sup>, or *The Consolation of Elphin*, which the Bard addresses to him from the perspective of an abandoned infant. Taliesin lived to repay the kindness of his benefactor: through the magic of his poetry, he rescued Elphin from the castle of Deganwy (where he was imprisoned for some misconduct by his uncle Maelgwn), and afterward granted him immortal fame.

Taliesin was the teacher or poetic mentor of Myrddin ap Morfryn <note>the historical figure often identified with Merlin</note>. He enriched British poetic structure with five new meters and has passed down in his poems such traces as provide new light on the history, knowledge, and customs of the ancient Britons and their Druids, much of whose mystical learning he had studied.

The poem I have chosen as an example of Taliesin’s style is his description of the battle of Argoed Llwyfain, fought about the year 548. It was contested by Goddeu, a king of North Britain, and Urien Rheged, King of Cumbria, against Fflamddwyn, a Saxon general (supposed to be Ida, King of Northumberland). *Llywarch Hên*, or *Llywarch* the Aged, a Cumbrian prince, is the third great Bard of the British annals. He spent his younger days at the court of King Arthur, with the honorable distinction of being a free guest. When the British power was weakened by the death of Arthur, *Llywarch* was called to the aid of his kinsman *Urien Rheged*, King of Cumbria, and to the defense of his own principality against the invasions <note>original: irruptions</note> of the Saxons.

This princely Bard had twenty-four sons, all invested with the golden torc <note>original: torques; a stiff neck-ring worn by ancient nobility</note>, which appears to have been the ancient badge of British nobility <margin>n</margin>. Many of them were slain in the Cumbrian wars, and the Saxons at length prevailed. The unfortunate *Llywarch*, with his few surviving sons, fled into *Powys* to revive the unequal and unsuccessful contest under the leadership <note>original: auspices</note> of the prince of *Powys*, *Cynddylan*. Having lost all his sons and friends by the end of these wars, he retired to a hut at *Aber Ciog* <margin>o</margin> in North Wales to soothe the memory of misfortune with his harp and express the sorrows of old age through elegiac poetry <note>original: numbers</note>.

These were the poetical luminaries of the sixth century. Their works are full of feeling, imagination, and enthusiasm; they do honor to the nation that produced them. Foreigners who read them will be forced to soften some of those dark colors in which they have usually painted our ancestors. The rays of genius that shone forth in the Britons, amidst the gloom of the Dark Ages, are more valuable in the eye of reason and contribute more to their glory than all the bloody trophies they ever erected. But how can their poetry produce this effect if their language remains unintelligible—if no one will translate it into the other languages of Europe? <note>original: footnote u, referencing M. Mallet's *Introduction à l'Histoire de Dannemarc* (Introduction to the History of Denmark).</note>

The writings of these ancient Bards deserve to be explored and published, not merely as sources of poetical and philosophical pleasure, but as stores of historical information. Their origin is not doubtful like that of some venerable works which we have reason to fear were drawn together from fictitious records or vague tradition; these were composed based on recent exploits, copied immediately from their subjects, and sent abroad among nations that had acted in or seen them. From a diligent investigation and accurate editions of them by learned Welshmen, many important advantages may be promised to British history. If supplied and improved from these rich fountains, history would no longer offend with incredible fables of giants and magicians, but engage the reader with a description of real events and true heroes. For early poetry has always been known in every country to give the fullest and most exact picture of life and manners.

The Druids, in their migration to Ireland, had not left Britain entirely destitute of its music, which—though no longer communicated by the formal teachings of that learned order—was preserved by practice. It languished indeed for a time, but afterward grew and flourished in Wales along with the other surviving arts of Britain.

When the chief Bard appeared at the court of the Welsh princes, he sat next to the judge of the palace. None but himself and the Bard of the palace were allowed to perform in the presence of the prince. When the prince desired to hear music, the chief Bard sang to his harp two poems: one in praise of the Almighty, the other concerning kings and their heroic exploits. After this, a third poem was sung by the Bard of the palace. He obtained his pre-eminence through a poetical contest, which was decided by the judge of the palace. On this occasion, the judge received from the successful candidate, as an honorary fee, a bugle-horn, a gold ring, and a cushion for his chair of dignity. His poetical rights and authority were not subject to the control of the prince, and his privilege of legal protection lasted from the beginning of the first song in the hall of the palace to the conclusion of the last<sup>z</sup>. But what remains to be said of the manner of his election and the nature of his office, I must defer until the institutes of *Gruffudd ap Cynan* <note>Prince of Gwynedd (c. 1055–1137) who reorganized the rules for bards and musicians.</note> enable me to speak more extensively, and with greater certainty, of this dignified person.

The Bard of the palace, who was in rank the eighth officer of the prince's household, received at his appointment a harp and an ivory chess-board from the prince, and a gold ring from the princess. On the same occasion, he presented a gold ring to the judge of the palace. At the prince's table on the three great festivals of *Christmas, Easter*, and *Whitsuntide*, he sat next to the master of the palace and publicly received from the hands of that officer the harp on which he performed. When he went with other Bards upon his *Clera* <term>itinerant circuit of minstrels</term> or musical journey, he was entitled to a double fee. He was obliged, at the queen's desire, to sing to his harp three pieces of poetry, but in a low voice, so that the court might not be distracted from their duties. Towards the close of the eleventh century, the great prince **Gruffudd ap Cynan** <note>Prince of Gwynedd (r. 1081–1137), who spent much of his early life in exile in Ireland.</note> invited some of the best musicians of Ireland to Wales. Being partial to the music of that island, where he was born, and observing with displeasure the disorders and abuses among the Welsh Bards, he created a body of statutes <note>original: "institutes"</note> for the improvement of their manners and the correction of their art and practice. Accordingly, I find in an old manuscript of Welsh Music <note>Located in the library of the Welsh School.</note> a curious account of this remarkable revolution, beginning with these words:

> "Here follow the twenty-four measures of instrumental music, all conforming to the laws of harmony, as they were settled in a congress by many doctors skillful in that science, both Welsh and Irish, in the reign of Gruffudd ap Cynan. These were written in books by the order of both parties—princely and principally—and thence copied, etc."

This grand reformation of the **Bards** was achieved by dividing them into classes and assigning to each class a distinct profession and employment. Previously, we have seen them acting in a very broad and varied capacity. It was their office to applaud the living and record the dead; they were required to possess learning and genius, a skill in pedigrees <note>genealogy</note>, an acquaintance with the laws and meters of poetry, a knowledge of harmony, a fine voice, and the command of an instrument. This diversity of character is well expressed by Michael Drayton in the sixth song of his *Polyolbion*:

> "Musician, Herald, Bard, thrice may you be renowned,
> And with three several wreaths immortally be crowned!"

Such a variety of excellence was unattainable by human capacity alone. The Bards were now, therefore, distributed into three grand orders: **Poets**, **Heralds**, and **Musicians**. Each of these was further divided into subordinate distinctions.

None of these orders or distinctions was any longer compatible with the others, nor with any other profession. According to a more detailed arrangement, there were six classes of regular Bards who could proceed to degrees in the **Eisteddfod**: three of Poets and three of Musicians.

The first class of Poets consisted of historical or antiquarian Bards <note>original: *Prydydd*, or *Prifardd*</note>, who sometimes mixed prophecy with their inspiration. They were also critics and teachers, and to them belonged the duty of praising virtue and censuring vice. It was their responsibility to celebrate the gifts of imagination and poetry. They were required to address married women without an air of gallantry, and to speak to the clergy in a serious tone suitable to their function. They were to satirize without indecency, and to answer and overthrow the lampoons of the inferior Bards without resorting to crude insults themselves.

The second class was formed of domestic or instructional Bards <note>original: *Teuluwr*, or *Posfardd*</note>, who lived in the houses of the nobility to celebrate their exploits and amiable qualities. They sang the praises of generosity, contentment, domestic happiness, and all the social virtues; in this way, they significantly contributed to enlivening the leisure time of their patrons. It was also their role to request presents in a familiar, easy style, without being bothersome.

The third class, though listed last, was probably not the least esteemed, for it consisted of **Herald Bards** <note>original: *Clerwr* or *Arwyddfardd*</note>. These were the national chroniclers and were also well-versed in pedigrees, the heraldry of coats of arms, and the works of the ancient bards, such as *Taliesin* and the two *Merlins*. According to the account given by *Giraldus Cambrensis* <note>original: *Cambria Descriptio*, or *Description of Wales*, chapter 3</note> in the following century, they were admirably qualified for poetry—if invention is one of its principal requirements. He claims that they could trace the ancestry of their princes and nobles not only back to *Roderic*, but to *Beli*, *Sylvius*, and *Æneas*, and even to *Adam* himself. However, their poetry was generally of a humbler sort, usually confined to subjects of joking, mimicry, insults, and reproach.

Regarding the musical **Bards**, the first class was reserved for performers on the **Harp**. The reader may find more information about them in the remainder of this short history and in the section on Welsh musical instruments later in this volume.

The second class contained performers on the six-stringed **Crŵth** <note>a traditional Welsh bowed lyre</note>; for more information on them, I also refer the reader to those same sections.

The third class consisted of singers <note>original OCR: "fingers"</note>, whose job was to sing the compositions of the poetical Bards to the accompaniment of others' harps. A variety of other qualifications was expected of them. "A singer," according to the Laws, "should know how to tune a Harp or *Crŵth* and how to play several exercises and ornaments: two preludes, a *cwlwm* <note>a "knot" or melodic composition</note>, a *caniad* <note>a song or tune</note>, and the 13 principal tunes, including all their flats and sharps <note>original: $^p$</note>. He should also understand the 13 principal styles of expression and how to accent them with his voice to various tunes. He should know the 24 metres of Poetry and the 24 measures of Music, and be capable of composing in two of the *Englyn* <note>short stanza</note> metres and one of the *Cywydd* <note>rhyming couplet</note> metres. He should read Welsh properly, write it with exactness, and be skilled in correcting and restoring any old poem or song that has been corrupted by transcribers."

At the weddings of the prince or any of the royal blood, the singer <note>original OCR: "finger"</note> attended to the illustrious bride, and at those entertainments was expected to skillfully carve every kind of fowl that might be served to him.

Such, and so varied, were the regular Bards. Through a novitiate and probation of an appointed term of years, and by performing poetical and musical exercises, they earned degrees in the *Eisteddfod* <note>a traditional Welsh festival of literature, music, and performance</note>. As that venerable assembly existed long before the period I am describing, a description of it perhaps should have already been presented; however, I chose to wait until I could display it in its most perfect form, under the patronage of a prince to whom our poetry and music are forever indebted.

The *Eisteddfod* was a triennial assembly of the *Bards*. It was usually held at *Aberffraw* (formerly the royal seat of the princes of North Wales, situated in Anglesey), *Dinefawr* (the royal castle of the princes of South Wales, in Carmarthenshire), or *Mathrafael* (the royal palace of the princes of Powis, in Montgomeryshire). Its purpose was the regulation of poetry and music, the conferring of degrees, and the advancement of rival candidates to the "chair" of the *Eisteddfod* through poetical and musical contests—or confirming the *Chief Bard* who already held that honorable seat.

Wishing to convey a clear idea of this important subject to my readers, I have included an extract, faithfully translated, from the *statute of Prince Gruffudd ap Cynan* concerning the manner of holding an *Eisteddfod*:

> "When the congress has assembled at the appointed place, according to the notice and summons previously issued, they shall choose twelve umpires skilled in the Welsh language, poetry, music, and heraldry. These umpires shall give the *Bards* a subject to sing upon in any of the twenty-four metres <note>the twenty-four codified meters of Welsh specialized poetry</note>, but not in alternating songs <note>original: "amæbean carols"; songs where two singers answer each other</note> or any such frivolous compositions. The umpires shall ensure that the candidates do not descend into satire or personal insults, and shall allow each person sufficient time to compose his *Englyn* or *Cywydd* <note>specific forms of Welsh poetry</note>, or whatever other task they assign. Furthermore, they shall record the names of the various *Bards* present who intend to sing, so that everyone may be called by name in order to the chair to perform his composition. The unsuccessful candidates shall acknowledge in writing that they are overcome and shall deliver their acknowledgment to the Chief Bard (that is, to him who wins the chair). Everyone shall drink to the health of the Chief Bard and pay him fees; he shall govern them until he is overcome in a future *Eisteddfod*."

From this injunction, it appears that the duties which belonged to the "judge of the palace" during the reign of *Hywel Dda* were later held by a commission of umpires.

What served greatly to increase the competition among the *Bards*—if they needed any additional incentive—was the presence of the prince, who usually presided over these contests. The compositions delivered on these occasions are frequently based on historical subjects and are valuable for their authenticity; for it was the business of the *Eisteddfod* not only to set the rules for poetry and music, but to eliminate falsehoods and establish certainty in the reporting of events.

> "A custom so good," says Michael Drayton <note>an English poet, 1563–1631</note>, "that had it been judiciously observed, the truth of history would not be so uncertain. For there was, we suppose, a correction of what was faulty in form or matter, or at least a judgment by the hearers upon what was recited. Some have wished for a revival of this practice, so that either the correction of opinions or a change of purpose in publishing might prevent the spread of errors."

Before anyone could be enrolled in the *Eisteddfod*, the permission of the prince or lord within whose jurisdiction he lived was necessary. If he desired to pursue degrees in poetry, he was obliged at his presentation to explain the five *Englyn* metres and sing them in such a way that one of the principal *Bards* would declare, upon his conscience, that the candidate was competent to be admitted. He then became the pupil of one of the principal Bards, whom he was required to attend annually during Lent. Without his teacher's approval, he could make no composition public. For three years (that is, until the next *Eisteddfod*), he remained a non-graduate and was called a *Difgybl Yspas* <margin>John Rhydderch’s *Welsh Grammar*, pages 188, 189.</margin> *cerdd dafod* <note>original: "cerdd dafawd"; literally "art of the tongue," referring to vocal or literary craft</note>, or a probationary student of poetry.

At the next *Eisteddfod*, after three years had expired, the *Difgybl Yspas* was examined for the degree of *Difgybl Disgyblaidd*, or Bachelor of the Art of Poetry. To the *Pencerd* <note>original: "Pencerd," a Doctor of Poetry or Chief Singer</note>, or Doctor of Poetry, who obtained his degree at the end of the same period, belonged the entire mystery of the art. He knew how to sing in parts and harmony and was well-versed in complex transposed alliteration. Among his qualifications were creativity in choosing poetic subjects, a wealth of knowledge and invention, the authority to make artistic decisions, and a talent for composing praises for the nobility that would be delightful to hear or read and long remembered.

If a *Disgybl* <note>a disciple or student</note> of any degree was caught in taverns or secret places gambling for money with dice or any other game, anyone was authorized to take whatever money was found in his purse. For mockery, insults, or the invention and spreading of lies, the *Disgyblion* <note>the plural of Disgybl</note> were also punished with fines and imprisonment. For, says the law, the Bards shall be well-behaved and peaceful in their manners, friendly in their disposition, and humble in their service to the prince and his followers.

Only those Bards who had earned the degree of *Pencerd* were authorized to teach, and no more than a single pupil was allowed for each *Pencerd*. The pupils were expressly ordered to refrain from mocking their teachers for the "absent-mindedness" or inattention that is natural to a contemplative mind. But the most valued privilege of the *Eisteddfod* <note>a traditional Welsh festival and competition for bards and musicians</note> was the exclusive right to the "chair." All those who aspired to the honor of presiding over the Bards came forward (as the statute prescribes) at the triennial assembly to compete with each other and with the Chief Bard who already held the position. The successful candidate was seated in a magnificent chair and was thereafter called the *Bardd Cadeiriog*, or "Chair-Bard." At the same time, he was presented with a small silver or gold chair, which he wore on his chest as the badge of his office. As his rank was high, his earnings <note>original: "emoluments"</note> were considerable. These came from the *Disgyblion* when they finished using the horsehair-strung harp and were admitted to professional practice; from brides at their weddings; and from the marriage fines of the daughters of all the Bards within his jurisdiction. Additionally, his own daughter received a marriage portion from the prince.

Whoever desired to advance to degrees in music was presented to the *Eisteddfod* by a musical *Pencerd* who vouched for his ability. During his three-year apprenticeship <note>original: "noviciate"</note>, he was called a *Disgybl Yspás hebrádd*, a probationary student of music without a degree. If he learned to play the harp, he was only allowed to use an instrument strung with horsehair—likely so that his clumsy attempts at harmony would not torment the ears of the masters, and to motivate him to study harder in hopes of eventually playing a harp with more resonant and pleasing strings.

His next step was the degree of *Disgybl Yspás graddol*, a graduated probationary student of music. For this, he was required to know ten *cwlums* <note>musical pieces or themes</note>, one *colofn* <note>a major musical composition, literally a "column"</note>, five *cwlums* of *cydgerdd* <note>ensemble or accompanied music</note>, one *cadair* <note>a masterwork piece, literally a "chair"</note>, and eight *caniads* <note>songs or tunes</note>.

He then became a *Disgyblaidd*, or Bachelor of Music, but was first required to master twenty *cwlums*, two *colofns*, ten *cwlums* of *cydgerdd*, two *cadaïrs*, sixteen *caniads*, and the twenty-four measures of music, and to play them with ease and accuracy.

He next became a *Pencerdiaidd*, or Master of Music, a degree which required a preparatory knowledge of thirty *cwlums*, three *colofns*, fifteen *cwlums* of *cydgerdd*, three *cadaïrs*, twenty-four *caniads*, and four *goftlegs* <note>a specific type of complex musical composition</note>, as well as the skill to define them properly and distinctly.

Lastly, he was admitted as a *Pencerd*, or Doctor of Music. He was obliged to know forty *cwlums*, four *colofns*, twenty *cwlums* of *cydgerdd*, four *cadaïrs*, thirty-two *caniads*, and four *goftlegs*. He had to understand all the laws and variations of harmony, especially the twenty-four *Measures of Music*, and explain them as they were written in the book of musical theory. He had to compose a *caniad* judged flawless by the expert Bards and demonstrate all its properties: its divisions, subdivisions, rests, natural notes, flats, sharps, and every change of movement through the various keys. If the *Pencerd* was a harper, he was required to know the three "excellent *Meichiau*" <note>advanced musical pieces or technical standards</note>, which were equal to the four *cadaïrs*, and the three "new *Meichiau*." He had to know and perform all of this in a masterly way, so that the professors would declare him competent to be a composer <note>original: "author"</note> and a teacher of his art.

The *Eisteddfod* was a strict school. A poetic or musical student who, at the end of his three-year term, could not earn a higher degree, was condemned to lose the degree he already held.

We know that before the time of *Gruffudd ap Cynan*, the musical Bards were secondary to the Chief Bard of the Poets.

The revenues of the Bards came from presents at princely and other weddings <note>original: "nuptials"</note>, and from fees during their annual circuits at *Christmas, Easter*, and *Whitsuntide* <note>The week of Pentecost.</note>, and in their triennial *clera*, or grand circuit. Their fees and presents were regulated in proportion to their degrees; and the number of visitors was matched to the social status of the person who received them. Likewise, in order to encourage the <term>clerwr</term> <note>A traveling minstrel or circuiting bard.</note> to keep up the language and the memory of the exploits and pedigrees of the Britons, they were allowed a certain sum out of every "plough-land" <note>An old unit of land measurement representing what one yoke of oxen could plow in a year.</note>, and a proportional amount out of every half plough-land in their district. A month before each festival, the pupils asked their teachers what routes they should take in their upcoming circuit, so that too many would not resort to the same part of the country. A <term>Pencerdd</term> <note>A Chief Singer or Master Bard.</note> was not licensed to visit the common people unless he chose to accept a fee beneath his station and dignity; nor could any Bard of an inferior degree appear before the gentry and nobles. The Bards were not permitted to request presents beyond a certain value, under penalty of being deprived of their musical instruments and practice for three years. When this happened, the illegally requested present was forfeited to the prince.

The <term>Eisteddfod</term> was followed by the grand triennial <term>Clera</term>, which was not limited (as the festival circuits were) to <term>commots</term> and <term>cantrefs</term> <note>Medieval Welsh land divisions; a cantref was roughly equivalent to a "hundred."</note>, but extended through all Wales. Such was the benevolence of the Welsh institutions that Bards afflicted with blindness, or any other natural disability, were granted the privilege of <term>Clera</term>, as were the four poetical and five musical graduates. At a wake or festival, a circuiting Bard was not permitted, during its duration, to leave the house he first visited without the consent of the master of the house, or an invitation given to him by another. If he wandered from house to house, or became intoxicated, he was deprived of his <term>Clera</term> fees, which were then given to the church. If he committed any indecency toward a mistress or maid, he was fined and imprisoned, and lost his <term>Clera</term> privileges for seven years.

Every art has its subordinate professors. Besides the four classes of regular or graduated Bards I have described, there were four other classes of inferior and unlicensed Bards (if that name may be given to them without profanation): these were Pipers, Players on the three-stringed <term>Crwth</term> <note>An ancient bowed lyre.</note>, Taborers <note>Players of a small drum called a tabor.</note>, and Buffoons. Regarding the pipe, the three-stringed <term>Crwth</term>, and the tabor, the reader will find some mention near the illustration of the musical instruments of the Welsh. The performers who used them were looked upon among Bards as weeds among flowers; they had no connection with the <term>Eisteddfod</term>, and their reputation and profits were equally insignificant. One of their number, the <term>Datceiniad Pen Pasgwr</term> <note>A vocal performer or declaimer who recited poems without an instrument.</note>, was a minstrel who only rehearsed and played no instrument. On festive occasions, he stood in the middle of the hall where the company was assembled, and beating time with his staff, sang a poem to the band. When any of the regular Bards were present, he attended them as a servant and did not presume to sing unless they signaled their approval.

The only connection that existed between the higher and lower orders of the Bards is found in the appointment of the <term>Cyff Clêr</term> <note>Literally the "butt of the bards" or "stock of the minstrels."</note> at the marriage of a prince, or any person of princely descent. A year and a day before the celebration of the wedding, notice was given to a <term>Pencerdd</term> to prepare himself to take on that role. When the time came, he appeared in the hall, and a humorous subject being proposed, the inferior Bards surrounded him and attacked him with their ridicule. In these mocking, satirical outbursts, they were restrained from any personal insults or real offense. The <term>Cyff Clêr</term> sat in a chair in the middle of them and silently allowed them to say whatever they chose that might serve to entertain the assembly. For this unpleasant service, he received a considerable fee. The next day he appeared again in the hall, answered his revilers, and provoked the laughter and gained the applause of everyone present by exposing them in their turn, throwing all their ridicule back upon them.

The period which intervened between the reign of **Gruffudd ap Cynan** and that of the last prince, **Llewelyn**, is the brightest in our annals. It will be sufficient for me to mention a few illustrious names who, with veneration derived from their great predecessors, the arts of poetry and music, and transmitted them with increased honors to their posterity. Since writing and practical music have become separate professions, the celebrity of the poor musician has died with the vibration of his strings. The voice of acclaim and the thunder of applause pass away like vapors; and those hands that were most active in showing temporary approval allow the fame of those who charmed away their cares and sorrows in the glowing hour of innocent delight to remain unrecorded. Some of the musical productions of this period are to be found in the present collection, and some are far more ancient. I decline the task of pointing them out by any decisive opinion because the original titles are lost, and they are now known by other names substituted by later Bards in compliment to later patrons. This remark is minor but necessary; for without it, the age of some of the best remains of Welsh music might inadvertently be mistaken.

Early in the twelfth century, harmony and verse had approached their highest degree of perfection in Wales. Nor, by the common fate of the arts in other countries, did they suddenly fall from the excellence they had attained. If, in the progress of the succeeding age, they showed any symptoms of decay, a remedy was so diligently applied by the skill of the **Eisteddfod** <note>A traditional Welsh festival and competition of poetry and music</note> to the declining parts that they preserved their former vigor and perhaps acquired new graces. And had not the fatal accident which overwhelmed, in the hour of its prosperity, the hereditary princedom of Wales, involved in the same ruin its poetry and music, our country might have retained to this day its ancient government and its native arts in the heart of those mountains which protected them for ages. 

The poets of these memorable times added energy to a vigorous <term>original: "nervous"</term> language, and the musicians called forth from the harp its loudest and grandest tones to re-animate the ancient struggle of their brave countrymen for freedom and the possession of their native soil. What the success of their virtuous and noble purpose was, the history of the eras when they flourished can best explain. <margin><sup>r</sup> *Michael Drayton*, through the information provided by his friend Mr. *John Williams*, was extremely well-informed about the Bards and their institutions; his accurate knowledge is conveyed in his poem *Poly-Olbion* in the most elegant and spirited poetry.</margin>
Without bestowing such extravagant praise upon it, I can boldly state that *Drayton’s* *Poly-Olbion*<sup>r</sup> and *Gray’s* *The Bard* have enhanced their authors' reputations as much as they have celebrated the glory of Wales. They are the only modern works worthy of making up for the immense loss of literary treasures and the irreparable destruction of genius we have suffered.

After the dissolution of the princely government in Wales, the tyranny exercised by the English over the conquered nation was so severe that the Bards born "since Wales’s fatal day" <note>The conquest of Wales by Edward I in 1282.</note> might be said to have been born under a cursed and unlucky star. They were forced to practice their sacred art in obscurity and sorrow, and were compelled to suppress the indignation that would otherwise have burst forth in the most spirited verses against their ungenerous and cruel oppressors. Yet they were not silent or inactive. So that their poetry might express their patriotism without fear of punishment, they became cryptic, prophetic, and oracular. Just as the monks of the Welsh church, in their conflict with Rome, had written several religious poems to support their doctrines—which they falsely claimed were the work of *Taliesin*—the Bards now attributed many of their political writings to that same respected author. They also produced many other works as if they were the prophecies of the elder *Merlin*. 

Consequently, much uncertainty surrounds the authentic remains of the sixth century, a great part of which has come down to us damaged and corrupted. This also accounts for the mysterious air that permeates all the poetry of the later periods I am now describing. The forgery of those poems that are entirely fake—even though they may have passed unchallenged by critics such as Dr. *Davies* and Dr. *John David Rhys*—can, I think, be easily detected. They were written to serve a popular and temporary purpose and were not crafted with enough care to hide their historical mistakes, modern language, and other marks of fraud from the eye of a wise and enlightened scholar.

While the Bards were restricted in their poetic work, they had more time and opportunity to study heraldry and perform their other domestic duties. Every great man had under his roof and patronage some eminent Bard who, at his death, composed a funeral poem about his family history, his titles, and the actions of his life. This poem was solemnly recited by a <term>Datceiniad</term> <note>A singer or reciter who performed the Bards' compositions.</note> in the presence of his surviving relatives<sup>s</sup>. It is because of this tradition that family trees are so well-preserved in Wales.

Although heroic poetry was no longer attempted in Wales after this time, a long series of Bards followed who have made their names memorable through the ages with their elegies and odes. Among these, *Dafydd ap Gwilym*$^z$, often called the "Welsh Ovid," holds a deservedly high position. He often combines the sublime with the beautiful, as seen in his *Cywydd y Daran* (Ode of the Thunder), which is a noble example of his skill. It depicts a well-chosen scene with admirable variety: it opens with peaceful ideas and rural images—a lovely maiden and a delightful landscape—and then follows a sudden and terrifying change in the weather. The beauties of nature are overshadowed and hidden, animals are terrified, and the young woman shrieks. A thousand similar examples of excellence could be found in the writings of this elegant Bard and his contemporaries. Let those who complain that the current scarcity of genius forces them to read *Horace*, *Pindar*, and *Gray* for the tenth time instead explore the buried treasures of Welsh poetry. Their search will be rewarded with new sources of pleasure and new beauties of language and imagination.

In the reign of George II, Powel, a Welsh Harper who used to play before that *Monarch*, drew such tones from his instrument that the great Handel was delighted with his performance. Handel composed for him several pieces of music, some of which are in the first set of *Handel’s Concertos*. Having now conducted the short history I intended nearly to our own times, I will pause briefly before bringing it to its conclusion to examine more minutely the causes that conferred such peculiarity and excellence on the poetry and music of Wales. The laws, manners, and fortunes of nations have a principal influence in giving an original character to national arts. The first care of the Welsh laws was the freedom of the people. They were free, and their manners accordingly were at once generous and impetuous; gentle, hospitable, and social among their friends, and full of resentment and revenge against their enemies. They inhabited a country where they found in the works of nature what they afterwards copied into their own: the beautiful and the sublime. They were equally addicted to love and war. When they forsook the camp, they did not return to agriculture, commerce, or the mechanical arts, but passed their leisure in hunting and other manly sports and games, in conversation with women <margin>Lord Lyttelton, from *Giraldus Cambrensis, History of Henry II*, vol. II. p. 69.</margin>, and in recounting their exploits amidst drinks of mead at the tables of lords and princes. From this, they learned to write verse and play the harp.

To an English reader, this may seem like a laborious way of wasting time, but every language has its own unique laws of harmony. The ancient languages of Greece and Rome were not burdened with an overabundance of consonants; they were primarily composed of multi-syllable words and vowel endings. Their poetry, therefore, based its rhythm on "quantity"—the artistic arrangement of long and short syllables. In contrast, the old British language was rich in consonants and made up of single-syllable words, which are incompatible with "quantity." The Bards could only bring the language into harmony by placing its harsher consonants at specific intervals, mixing them with vowels, and repeating or dividing sounds to create a pleasing effect from their structure.

As a result, the rules of poetic composition in this language are so strict and rigorous that they would severely limit a Bard’s genius, if not for the language's natural aptitude for this type of alliterative melody. This melody is as essential to Welsh poetry as harmony is to music, and it constitutes its greatest beauty. To the ears of native speakers, Welsh meter is extremely pleasing and does not restrict the Bard any more than the various rhythmic "feet" restricted Greek and Roman poets <margin>ʳ *Northern Antiquities*, 8vo. London, vol. I. p. 401, etc.</margin>. Traces of <term>Cynghanedd</term> <note>original: *Cynghanedd*; a complex system of internal rhyme and alliteration unique to Welsh poetry</note> or alliteration appear in the poetic remains of the Druids. From the Norman Conquest until the death of <term>Llywelyn</term> the Last, the Bards became more strict. From the time of Llywelyn until the reign of Elizabeth I, the laws of alliteration were prescribed and followed with scrupulous exactness. A line that was not perfectly alliterative was condemned by Welsh grammarians as severely as a mistake in syllable length would be by the Greeks or Romans <margin>ˢ *Northern Antiquities*, vol. ### THE WELSH BARDS.

And where could the Muses have chosen a happier residence? At one moment you are delighted with valleys that are both wild and beautiful; in other parts, you are astonished by a continuous stretch of dreary, cloud-capped country—"hills whose heads touch heaven"—dark, fearsome precipices, swift rivers roaring over broken rocks, black caverns, and gushing waterfalls. Did *Salvator Rosa's* <note>original: "Salvata Rosa"; a 17th-century Italian painter known for "wild" and "savage" landscapes</note> extravagant imagination ever indulge itself in such grand and savage scenery? Or has *Claude Lorrain's* <note>original: "Claude Lorraine"; a French Baroque painter famous for his idealized landscape paintings</note> incomparable brush ever surpassed the beauty of the Vale of *Clwyd*?

It is not surprising that the venerable Welsh songs held such influence over the minds of our ancestors, when we consider their beautiful and varied changes of style and tempo; transitions as abrupt as the rocky landscapes of the country, and as sudden as the passions of its people.

The most ancient style of Welsh music is the *grave* and *solemn*, which was dedicated to religious purposes and occasions <gloss>x</gloss>. The next, distinct from the former, is fiercely *martial* and *magnificent* <gloss>y</gloss>. Another is mournful and expressive of sorrow, being reserved for elegies and the commemoration of the dead <gloss>z</gloss>. Another is of the *pastoral* kind, and perhaps the most agreeable of all; it is closest to nature, possessing a pleasing simplicity and a soothing tranquility suitable for tender love <gloss>*</gloss>.

I have recovered some authentic remains of these ancient melodies, and their effects are not entirely lost or forgotten. A new era of Welsh harmony has arisen in our times, and the wonderful things reported of it in former ages have already been realized.

### CONCERNING THE
# WELSH PENNILLION,
## OR,
# EPIGRAMMATIC STANZAS*, and PASTORALS.

->You shall sing in turns; the Muses love alternating song. <note>original: "Alternis dicetis. Amant alterna Camœnæ: VIRGIL."</note><-

**T**hese have been passed down to us by oral tradition from time immemorial and are still the domestic and conversational poetry of the people of Wales—a people uncommonly sensitive to all the impressions of sorrow, love, and joy.

The verses of remembrance, which in the time of Caesar<gloss>b</gloss> were never committed to writing and which the Druidical disciples spent so many years learning, were *Pennillion* <term>a traditional form of Welsh poetry, typically sung to the harp</term>, delivered in that most ancient meter called *Englyn Milwr* <term>the Soldier’s Stanza</term>.

When the Bards had developed their numerous and favorite meters into a highly sophisticated system, those meters which they rejected<gloss>c</gloss> were left to dress the "Rustic Muse"—the *Awen* <term>the Welsh word for poetic inspiration</term> of the common people. When Wales became an English province, poetry had already spread widely among the lower classes. From that period on, they forgot their former favorite subjects of war and terror and were limited to love and its closely related passions of pity and grief. Thus, these types of *Pennillion* were naturally kept and admired for the tender beauties they contained.

Eventually, toward the reign of Queen Elizabeth, the official constitutional system of the Bards became entirely extinct in Wales. The only poetry that survived was poured forth in spontaneous *Pennillion* around the hearths of farmers and in the cottages of shepherds. What helped keep the poetical talent of the Welsh peasantry alive under every discouragement of foreign oppression was their ancient spirit of hospitality<gloss>d</gloss> and social cheer. This spirit brought them together to drink mead, sing, and dance to the harmony of the Harp, *Crwth* <term>a bowed lyre</term>, Pipe, and Drum. What has preserved many of these little sonnets from very distant times is their singular merit and the affection with which they are remembered. Some of the old English songs, which have been repeated a thousand times, still continue to please, while the popular song of the day is echoed for a while and then consigned to everlasting oblivion. The meters of these stanzas are varied: a stanza may contain from three to nine lines, and a line consists of a specific number of syllables, from two to eight. One of these meters is the *Triban*, or Triplet; another is the *Awdl Gywydd*, or *Hên ganiad* (The Ancient Song of the old strain); and another is what would be called "Anapestic" in English poetry. There are several kinds of *Pennill* meters that can be adapted and sung to most of the following tunes, with some part of a tune occasionally being used as a symphony <note>In this context, an instrumental interlude</note>. Unlike an English song, a single set of words is not restricted to one tune but is commonly sung to several.

The skill of the *pennill*-singers in this is admirable. Depending on the meters of their *pennillion*, they join the melody at the proper place and follow it with wonderful exactness to the instrumental interlude or the conclusion. While the Harp to which they sing might be wandering through small variations and embellishments, their singing is not disrupted but stays true to the fundamental tune. This account explains the state of our music and poetry as described by *Giraldus* <note>Giraldus Cambrensis, or Gerald of Wales, a 12th-century chronicler</note> in his own time, when the Welsh were a nation of musicians and poets; when musical bands were common among them; and when their children learned to sing in concert from their infancy<gloss>e</gloss>.

# PENNILLION.

In his time, it was common for groups of young men, who knew no profession but that of arms, to enter every house they came to without distinction. There they enjoyed the free conversation of the young women, joined their voices to the melody of the Harp, and spent the day in the most lively festivity <note>f</note>. "Even today, a trace of the ancient minstrelsy survives among our mountains. A number of people of both sexes assemble and sit around the harp, singing alternately *Pennillion* <note>Welsh: short stanzas of poetry, usually sung to the harp</note> or verses of ancient or modern composition."

> "With charming symphony they introduce
> "Their pleasing song, and waken high raptures;
> "No voice is exempt, no voice but can well join
> "The melodious part."

"The young people usually begin the night with dancing, and when they are tired, they take up this form of relaxation. They alternately sing, dance, and drink—not for hours, but for days and weeks—and measure time only by the continuation of their mirth and pleasure. Often, like the modern *Improvvisatore* <note>Italian: performers who improvise verse</note> of Italy, they sing improvised verses; and a person skilled in this art readily produces a *Pennill* in response to the last one that was sung." Many have their memories stored with several hundreds, perhaps thousands, of *Pennillion*, some of which they always have ready as answers to every subject that can be proposed; or if their memory should ever fail them, they have the creativity to compose something relevant and appropriate for the occasion. The subjects provide a great deal of amusement: some are humorous, others satirical, but most are romantic, which, by the nature of the subject, are the best preserved. They continue singing without interruption, never repeating the same stanza (for that would lose the honor of being considered the lead singer), and, like nightingales, sustain the contest throughout the night. The audience usually calls for the tune: sometimes only a few sing to it, and sometimes the whole company. But when a group of expert singers assembles, they rarely call for the tune, as it does not matter to them what tune the harper plays. Parishes are often pitted against parishes; even counties contend with counties; and every hill echoes with the chorus <note>g</note>."

In these rural customs, which are best preserved in the mountainous counties of Merioneth and Caernarvon, we have a distant, pleasing glimpse of ancient innocence and the manners of a golden age.

## The MUSICAL INSTRUMENTS of the WELSH.

<margin>
^a "Let the Roman applaud you with the Lyre, the Barbarian with the Harp, the Greek with the instrument of Achilles, and let the British <term>Crowd</term> sing." <note>original: "Romanusque Lyra plaudat tibi, Barbarus Harpâ, Graecus Achilliaca, Crotta Britanna canat." — Venantius Fortunatus, Book 7, Poem 8. Venantius was a 6th-century Latin poet.</note>
</margin>

The musical instruments used in ancient Wales are as distinct from those of other nations as Welsh music and poetry themselves are. ^a

These instruments are five in number: the <term>Telyn</term> (Harp), the <term>Crwth</term> (Crowd <note>An ancient bowed instrument, often considered a precursor to the violin</note>), the <term>Pibgorn</term> (Pipe <note>A traditional hornpipe</note>), the <term>Tabwrdd</term> (Tabret <note>A small drum or tambourine</note>), and the <term>Corn Buelin</term> (Cornet or Bugle Horn). An accurate representation of these is attempted in the "Trophy" (the decorative engraving) at the beginning of this section.

<margin>
^b Genesis, chapter 4.
</margin>

The Harp, the most important of those I have listed and indeed the queen of all instruments, finds its origins in very ancient times. Jubal, among the Hebrews, is called the father of all those who handle the harp and organ. ^b King David introduced it into his choir and praised the Lord on an instrument of ten strings.

Among the Greeks, the harp is also very ancient and is described as having the triangular shape of the Greek letter **Δ** (<term>Delta</term>). The honor of its invention is attributed to Mercury, who, finding a dead shellfish on the shore of the Nile, fashioned its shell into a lyre, fitted it with strings, and added a crossbar <note>original: "jugum"</note> to tighten or loosen them. This lyre, according to the historian Diodorus Siculus, had only three strings, corresponding to the three seasons of the year—Spring, Summer, and Winter—which were the only seasons the Greeks recognized. Some ascribe four strings to it, in imitation of the cosmic music of the four elements. *Homer*, *Pindar*, *Horace*, *Virgil*, *Nicomachus*, and *Lucian* all agree that it had seven, a number which corresponds with that of the planets. Such was the Lyre which *Mercury* invented and gave to *Orpheus*; after his death, the people of Lesbos hung it up in *Apollo's* temple. *Pythagoras* later found it in a temple in *Egypt* and improved it by adding an eighth string. *Timotheus the Milesian* added four new strings to the original seven. *Josephus*, in his *Jewish Antiquities*, mentions a Lyre with twelve strings. By the time of *Anacreon*, the number had grown much larger, for that poet himself informs us that he sang across the full range of the *Twenty Strings* <note>original: "Twenty Strings" refers to the *magadis*, an ancient Greek instrument</note>.

Nevertheless, we find that the <term>Telyn</term> <note>original: "Telyn"; the Welsh word for Harp</note> or Welsh Harp was always unique to our Bards. However, there was probably no difference between the Harp in its ancient, primitive form and the Greek Lyre; for *Diodorus Siculus* records that the Celtic Bards played on "instruments similar to lyres" <note>original Greek: *ὀργάνων ταῖς λύραις ὁμοίων* (organon tais lyrais homoion)</note>.

In the time of the Welsh Princes, a hereditary Harp was preserved with great care and veneration in the household of every Prince and Lord. It was to be bestowed successively on the Bards of the family and was as essential among the possessions of a gentleman as a coat of arms.

The triple or modern Welsh Harp has three rows of strings: the two outside rows are tuned to the same pitch (unison), while the middle row provides the flats and sharps. Its range extends to five octaves. Some of its current features were likely additions from recent centuries. This celebrated instrument has been recently improved by the invention of pedals, which allow it to be changed into different keys without re-tuning. This has rendered it much less complicated and inconvenient by reducing it to a single row of strings.

In expression and variety, the Harp has no rival. This will be acknowledged by all who know how the heart is soothed by its delicate and softer sounds, as well as animated by its more powerful and brilliant tones. This is elegantly expressed by a Welsh Bard in the following stanza:

The <term>Crŵth</term> <note>A traditional Welsh bowed string instrument, often seen as a precursor to the violin</note> is the second in rank of the Welsh musical instruments. I believe it to be the ancestor of the violin. It has a most agreeable melody and was frequently used as a tenor accompaniment to the Harp. It has now become extremely rare in *Wales*. Its length is 20 1/2 inches, and its breadth at the bottom is 9 1/2 inches, tapering to 8 inches toward the top. Its thickness is 1 7/10 inches, and the fingerboard measures 10 inches in length.

The <term>Crŵth</term> has a much wider range than the violin and is capable of great perfection; therefore, it deserves to be studied. It has six strings, namely:

There was likewise a <term>Crŵth Trithant</term> <note>original: "three-stringed Crŵth"</note>, which was the ancient **Bass Viol**. The performers on this instrument were not held in the same estimation and respect as the Bards of the Harp and the standard <term>Crŵth</term>. This was because the three-stringed <term>Crŵth</term> did not require equal skill, and consequently, its musical power was less significantly felt.

The <term>Pibgorn</term>, or **Hornpipe**, is so called because both of its ends are made of horn. When blown, the air passes through it and vibrates the tongue of a reed concealed within. It has seven holes and measures about 19 inches in length. Its tone is a medium between the flute and the clarinet and is remarkable for its melody. This rural pipe is unique to the **Isle of Anglesey**, where it is played by shepherds; it adds greatly to the innocent delight of pastoral life.

The <term>Tabwrdd</term> (**Drum** or **Tabret**) was used either in war or to accompany other instruments in concerts, festivals, and similar occasions. We find in the laws of King <note>King Hywel Dda (Howel the Good), a 10th-century Welsh ruler famous for codifying Welsh law.</note> **Howel** that harps and voices were principally used by the ancient Welsh to inspire courage before a battle. There is reason to think, however, that <term>Crŵths</term>, pipes, and drums were used for the same purpose.

The last instrument, which perhaps should have been mentioned earlier, is the <term>Corn Buelin</term> or **Bugle Horn**. This instrument was sometimes called *Corn Hirlas*, *Corn Cyweithas*, and *Corn Cychwyn*; names which signify the **Long Blue Horn**, **The Horn of the Household**, and the **Marching Horn**. It was made from—and received its general name from—the horn of the buffalo, bugle, or wild ox <note>See the Laws of King Howel.</note>, an animal that was formerly common in **Wales**. In the time of King **Howel**, it was the office of the Master of the Royal Hounds to sound his bugle horn in war, for a march, and to give the alarm and signal for battle. He likewise used it in hunting to animate the hunters and the dogs and to call the pack together. The Master of the Hounds had the same power of protection (sanctuary) within the sound of his horn while he was hunting as the Chief Bard possessed while performing on his Harp. When his oath was required in a court of justice, he swore by his horn. By the old Welsh hunting laws <note>Published at the end of Dr. Davies’s Dictionary.</note>, it was decreed that every person carrying a horn was obliged to know the **Nine Chases**; if he could not give a proper account of them, he would lose his horn. There were three bugle horns belonging to the King: his drinking horn, the horn for calling together the household, and the horn of the Master of the Hounds <note>Howel’s Laws.</note>.

This instrument occasionally had lids at its ends and served as the cup from which our ancestors drank mead. They valued it as much for this purpose as for its shrill and warlike sound. The jovial horn was sometimes a subject for the Welsh poets. There is a very fine, spirited poem in the Reverend Mr. Evans’s *Specimens of the Welsh Bards* entitled *Hirlas Owain* (**The Long Blue Horn of Owain**), composed by **Owain Cyfeiliog**, Prince of Powys. It is elegantly translated in Mr. Pennant’s last *Tour in Wales*. If I may take the liberty to borrow some lines from it, they will give the reader an idea of how our famous ancestors used to feast after battle in days of yore:

Morfa Rhuddlan, or the Red Marsh, located on the banks of the river Clwyd in Flintshire, was the scene of many battles between the Welsh and the Saxons. At the memorable conflict in 795 AD, the Welsh were unsuccessful and their monarch, Caradoc, was slain.

It is unknown whether this celebrated tune took its name from this event or some later occasion. The words now adapted to the tune are verified from a fragment published in the *Letters from Snowdon*. This plaintive style, which is so dominant in Welsh music, is well suited to melancholy subjects. Our music probably received a pathetic quality <note>original: "tincture"</note> from our distresses under the oppression of the Saxons.
