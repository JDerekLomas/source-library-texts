#### Chapter 11: On the Expert Commander

The manual states: If the use of military force is not rooted in morality, virtue, benevolence, and righteousness, then even if a ruler manages to dominate the world, a person of noble character <note>original: *junzi* (君子); the Confucian ideal of a virtuous person</note> will not associate with them.

When the moral authority of the Zhou Dynasty declined, the local feudal lords took it upon themselves to dictate social rites and music, and they began to launch unauthorized military campaigns against one another. This era of conflict began with Duke Yin of the state of Lu.

During this period, different states developed their own military strengths: The state of Qi became powerful by employing "skilled strikers" <note>original: *jiji* (伎擊); professional soldiers or mercenaries rewarded for individual martial prowess</note>. The state of Wei surged ahead with its "martial infantry" <note>original: *wuzu* (武卒); elite heavy infantry selected through grueling physical tests</note>. The state of Qin achieved victory through its "elite crack troops" <note>original: *ruishi* (銳士); highly disciplined and lethal vanguard soldiers</note>.

In these times, military thinkers regarded Sun Tzu and Wu Qi as the ultimate authorities on strategy. However, only the philosopher Master Xun <note>original: *Xun Qing* (荀卿), better known as Xunzi</note> truly understood the "Kingly Way" <term>Kingly Way: A Confucian concept of governance through moral influence and benevolence rather than brute force</term> and criticized these developments. He argued that relying on the "skilled strikers" of Qi was a sign of a state destined for ruin.

# Treaty on Military Preparedness, Volume 14: Commentary on Military Formulas
## Taibai Yinjing <note>The *Taibai Yinjing* (Manual of the White Planet) is a comprehensive military manual written by Li Quan in 759 CE during the Tang Dynasty.</note>, Part 2

The *Spring and Autumn Annals: Zuo Commentary* <note>original: "春秋左傳" (*Chunqiu Zuo Zhuan*); one of the most important historical sources for early Chinese history</note> states:
"If you, my Lord, use virtue to pacify the feudal lords, who would dare not submit? If you instead use force to pressure the State of Chu, they will use the Fangcheng Mountains as their city wall and the Han River as their moat. Even with a massive army, you will find no use for them."

For this reason, Duke Dao of Jin <note>r. 572–558 BCE; credited with restoring the hegemony of the State of Jin</note> sent Wei Jiang to establish peace with the various Rong tribes <note>non-Chinese peoples residing on the frontiers</note> in order to bring stability to the Central States <note>original: "諸華" (*Zhu Hua*); referring to the states of the Zhou cultural sphere</note>. Within eight years, he convened the feudal lords nine times. Their cooperation was as harmonious as music, with nothing out of tune. Even the Jiang Rong tribe submitted.

Conversely, Duke Hui of Jin <note>r. 650–637 BCE; known for his lack of integrity and poor leadership</note> failed to be a ruler who neither encroached on others nor suffered rebellion. This led to the campaign at the Battle of Xiao <note>A significant battle in 627 BCE; though the text here uses it to contrast with peaceful harmony</note>. It was like men hunting a deer: the Jin people captured it, but the Rong tribes held it by the antlers <note>original: "晉人捕之戎人角之"; implying a victory achieved through crude struggle rather than effortless authority</note>.

A ruler who possesses the **Way** <note>the *Dao*; the natural and moral order</note> is able to win people over through **virtue**.
A **benevolent** ruler is able to bring people into harmony through **righteousness**.
A **wise** ruler is able to overcome others through **strategy**.
An **authoritative** ruler is able to control others through **influence** <note>original: "勢" (*shi*); often translated as "strategic advantage" or "power-circumstance"</note>.

Winning through battle is easy; winning through harmony is difficult. As the saying goes: "The former kings displayed their virtue and did not put their military on display." For when the military is sheathed and then moves, its movement carries awe. If it is constantly put on display, it becomes a toy; and when it is treated as a toy, its power is lost.

Listen to the drums and watch the command banners. If the signal is to advance, then advance; if the signal is to retreat, then retreat. If the banner points left, move left; if it points right, move right. Anyone who ignores orders and moves forward, backward, left, or right according to their own whim shall be beheaded.

Within a five-man squad <term>Wu: the basic unit of five soldiers in ancient Chinese military organization</term>, if there is someone who does not advance, the squad leader <term>Wuzhang: the corporal or leader of a five-man squad</term> shall kill them. If the squad leader does not advance, the decarch <term>Shizhang: a leader of ten men, or two squads</term> shall kill them. If the decarch does not advance, the unit commander <term>Dubo: a middle-ranking officer commanding a larger tactical unit</term> shall kill them. 

The generals supervising the columns shall stand at the rear with blades drawn. They are to observe anyone who violates orders or fails to advance and behead them.

If one unit is under attack and the other units do not come to their rescue, they shall be beheaded.

During battle, weapons, bows, and crossbows must not leave the formation. If they are taken out of the formation and the squad leader or decarch does not report or stop it, they shall share the same punishment <note>This implies the leaders would also face execution for failing to maintain the integrity of the line's armament.</note>.
