# PREFACE.

It is now the ninth year since I boarded a ship in the company of the Most Illustrious Prince, the Duke of Albemarle <note>Christopher Monck, 2nd Duke of Albemarle (1653–1688)</note>, Supreme Governor of the Island of Jamaica, to serve as Physician to the same Prince. During the voyage, while staying on the islands of Madeira, Barbados, Nevis, Saint Christopher, and Jamaica, in my spare hours and those free from medical practice (which otherwise, by reason of my duty, was my primary concern), I observed natural things as much as I was able and as my poor health permitted. I committed these observations mostly to paper, though sometimes, due to the inconveniences of the location, I entrusted them to memory. In a short space of time in these new regions, situated at a great distance from our Europe, I found some plants, minerals, and animals to be exactly the same as those I had very often seen and handled in Europe. I also quickly discovered, from the many things noted by me and believed to be new, that in travel accounts—

For this reason, the **investigation of plants** <term>Plantarum investigatio: the systematic study and identification of flora</term> is abandoned by most as a difficult task, to the great detriment of medicine and the liberal arts. Therefore, being about to publish this small book, I deemed it necessary—lest I present plants already described as if they were new and undescribed—to collect their **synonyms** <note>original: "Synonyma"; here meaning the various names given to the same plant by different authors</note> from the accounts of voyagers and those recording natural history from the beginning. This was done so that the confusion which would otherwise necessarily arise might be removed, and so that the reader may see how much has been said among authors of this kind whom I happened to examine. 

In handing these down, if the description, the illustration, the title, or even just the name (from which the native localities of the herbs are known to us) of the author cited by me exactly matches the plant observed by me, I have no doubt that it is one and the same species of plant. If, however, it differs in a very few details, so that it is right to suspect such a difference, I consider such a variation to be rather a **variety** of the same plant, by reason of the climate, the soil, the cultivation, the age, the specimen, whether the stalk was well or poorly dried, or a defect or changed position of its parts, or finally, if my observations are less accurate, the matter thus...

# PREFACE

In such cases, these synonyms are followed by a question mark, so that the Reader may know I am doubtful whether this plant is one and the same as that observed by me, or rather a variety of the same, or a <term>sport of nature</term> <note>original: "naturæ lusus"; a term used by early naturalists to describe unusual biological variations or mutations that seemed to defy classification</note>. I would ask Authors who treat this kind of subject matter not to take such hesitation poorly, since I myself—although for the most part an eyewitness, equipped with specimens collected by my own hand in the places where they grow spontaneously, as well as with brief descriptions—remain uncertain about many of the plants reviewed in this little work as to whether they are distinct species. Nor do I doubt that many errors of every kind may be found in this little labor, brief though it is; for these, I hope the utility of the subject, its obscurity, and its confusion (but most especially my own insignificance and my inadequacy for untangling so many great difficulties) may obtain the Reader's pardon.
