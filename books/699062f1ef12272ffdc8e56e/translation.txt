<meta>This page appears to be a section from a defense of Jewish and Christian antiquity, likely from Origen’s "Against Celsus" (Contra Celsum). The text argues against the philosopher Celsus, who dismissed Jewish history as fraudulent while accepting the histories of other ancient nations.</meta>

...surpassed those whom he mentions in his narratives of antiquity: yet he considers the history of the Jewish people alone to be false and fabricated. For if all historians have preserved the truth of the events that took place among their own people, why do we distrust only the prophets of the Jews? 

If Moses and the prophets recorded so many things that occurred among them and in their own times with such great skill, why will we ourselves not admit the same concerning the writers of other nations? Should the Egyptians be believed to have written in good faith about the Jews if they criticized them in their histories? Yet, when the Jews wrote the same about the Egyptians—noting that they suffered many evils from them most unjustly, and therefore affirming that God afflicted them with punishments—are they to be regarded as liars and empty-headed? 

Nor should this be said only of the Egyptians. We shall also find that the Syrians joined battle with the Jews and waged wars, and that these events are indeed recorded in the ancient Syrian annals of history. Likewise, Jewish writers record that the Syrians were their enemies—lest I seem to speak by anticipating what the prophets also foretold.

Notice, therefore, how Celsus <note>Celsus was a 2nd-century pagan philosopher and a prominent early critic of both Judaism and Christianity.</note>, seized by love for his own kind, believes certain Gentiles to be wise, but condemns and scorns the Jews as being utterly insane. But hear what he himself says: "It is indeed a longstanding and ancient saying which the wisest nations and cities and more prudent men have held in common." 

Nor did he even wish to call the Jews a "most wise nation," or even comparable to the Egyptians and Syrians, or the Indians and Persians, the Odrysians <note>A powerful Thracian tribe from the Balkan region.</note> and Samothracians, or the people of Eleusis. But how much better than Celsus was the <term>Pythagorean|A follower of the philosophy of Pythagoras, who emphasized mathematics, ethics, and the immortality of the soul.</term> Numenius? He presented himself as a most thoughtful man in many matters, and he investigated and weighed several doctrines, collecting from many sources what he judged to be most certain and true. 

When, in the first book he wrote *On the Good*, he mentioned various nations and encompassed many things concerning God as an incorporeal being, he included the Jews among those very nations. Indeed, he was not at all reluctant to use their prophetic writings or sayings in his own works, or to employ their figures of speech.
