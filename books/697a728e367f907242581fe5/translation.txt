Although there has been almost no century since the birth of Christ <note>original: "C. ## I.

Although there has been almost no century since the birth of Christ that has not endured heretics and schismatics, or remained free from perverse minds, the records collected for us by the writers of Church History and other diligent observers of events testify more than enough that the previous **17th Century** was more fertile than the rest. Among those disturbing our nourishing Church—to say nothing of others—are the **Rosicrucians**. For the nearly infinite number of writings published regarding their cause reveal how much turmoil they created in the century now passed. Since I have discovered so many varying opinions among writers concerning their origin and progress—some of which are so constructed as to either bring manifest harm to the Church or wound the reputation of men who have served the Church excellently (to which end the commentary found in **Arnold’s History of Heretics, Part II, Book XVII, Chapter XVIII** <note>original: "Arnoldi Haeresiologia." This refers to Gottfried Arnold’s influential and controversial work, *Unpartheyische Kirchen- und Ketzer-Historie* (Impartial History of the Church and Heretics), published in 1699.</note> primarily looks), or which rely upon I know not what trifles and fables, while others are found worthy of greater investigation and study—I considered that I would be doing something worthwhile if I were to examine the more powerful and prominent opinions commonly held about them. Likewise, I shall weigh the various judgments published here and there—as is commonly done regarding new things—on the scale of historical truth. I shall do this so that I might present to the Kind Reader, in this modest commentary, a single view of what we usually seek out laboriously from various writers regarding this **Brotherhood** <note>original: "Φιλαδελφία" (Philadelphía). A Greek term meaning 'brotherly love,' here used as a title for the Rosicrucian society.</note>.

Lest I proceed without order: **first**, I shall be concerned with their name; **second**, I shall inquire into what time the Fame of these Brothers especially emerged or became renowned; **third**, I shall present various opinions concerning the first Author of this society; **fourth**, I shall indicate their defenders along with their principal writings, at which time I shall also make clear their adversaries and their works; **fifth**, I shall propose their doctrines plainly; and in the **sixth** and final place, I shall discuss their customs, together with the judgment of **Master Arnold** concerning them, insofar as they fall within our circle.

II. And in the beginning, we must deal with the *Name* of the Rosicrucians. In Latin, they are commonly called *Brothers*, or *Little Brothers*, *Rosicrucians*, or, according to others, the *Society* or *Fraternity of the Rosicrucians*. In French, they are known as *the Brothers of the Rose-Cross* <note>original: "les Freres de la Rose-Croix"</note>. In German, they are called **the Rosicrucians / the Brothers of the Rose-Cross / the highly enlightened Society of the Holy Rose-Cross** / or, as it is called by the author of the *Fama and Confession* <note>The "Fama Fraternitatis" (1614) and "Confessio Fraternitatis" (1615) were the foundational manifestos of the movement.</note> right on the front of the book itself, **the Brotherhood of the praiseworthy Order of the Rose-Cross**. I will gladly omit other names and titles of praise which they claim for themselves, or which are heaped upon them by others, though these will be touched upon here and there throughout this treatise. This one thing I might add: some write *Brothers of the Roseate Cross*, or, omitting the 'e', *of the Rose Cross*, as if they were two separate words. It will soon be clear that this spelling is the best and most in line with their own thinking.

However, where this name comes from is no minor dispute among those who study this subject. The first opinion, which is common but legendary and furthest from the truth, is the one they themselves provide in their *Fama and Confession*: that they were named after Christian Rosencreuz (or Rosencruz, without the 'e'). Regarding this man, his followers tell stories as if he were another Apollonius of Tyana <note>Apollonius was a 1st-century philosopher and reputed miracle-worker; here he is used as a shorthand for a legendary or semi-divine figure.</note>. He was born into a noble family of the "Knights of the Golden Stone" <term>aurei lapidis Equitis: likely an alchemical reference to the Philosopher's Stone</term> and later became a monk. When he saw that the Roman Catholic Church was in need of reform, at the age of sixteen, by the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, he undertook a journey to Palestine to the tomb of the Lord. According to Kromayer in his *Scrutiny of Religion* (p. 142), he was joined by other Brothers. From there he came to Damascus (see *Fama*, p. 12), and there he was initiated by the Magi into "Book M."
