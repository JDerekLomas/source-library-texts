*...tly, that in the furthest reaches of the Ocean you have seen to it that such a distinguished library be adorned and furnished: truly a magnificent work, and clearly a royal one, than which no virtue, no dignity, and no reputation can be made either more increased, or more remarkable, or finally more stable. ### EPISTOLA

...nor have I devised a common or trivial subject, but rather one in which I have strived to work with such diligence that the reading will be not only useful, but also pleasant. For indeed, as Strabo <note>Strabo (c. 64 BCE – 24 CE) was a Greek geographer and philosopher who argued that literature should provide pleasure to entice the reader into learning.</note> testifies, such pleasure is the "bait" of reading and learning; it ensures that less boredom arises and that you may persist in your reading more intently. Therefore, so that these things might not be a source of distaste to scholars, I have endeavored to entice and hold the reader’s attention both through the novelty of the subject matter and, as much as possible, through the purity of my language.

Many things were brilliantly conceived and divinely described by great minds in previous centuries; however, many of those works began to be neglected and held in contempt because they were devoid of any rhetorical ornament or refinement of language. Truly, those authors took too little care for their own legacy or for the convenience of students. For, as Cicero says, <margin>Tusculan Disputations, Book 1</margin> to commit one's thoughts to writing when one can neither arrange nor illustrate them, nor entice the reader with any delight, is the mark of a man who intemperately abuses both his leisure and literature. As Fabius <note>Marcus Fabius Quintilianus, known as Quintilian, a celebrated Roman teacher of rhetoric.</note> so skillfully writes: <margin>Book 2, chapter 17</margin> "Even though the precepts for living are honorable in themselves, they nevertheless carry more weight in shaping minds whenever the clarity of speech illuminates the beauty of the subject." <note>original: "Ipsa vitæ præcepta licet per se honesta sint, plus tamen momenti obtinere ad formādas mentes, quoties pulchritudinem rerum claritas orationis illuminat."</note>

He cites classical authorities like Strabo, Cicero, and Quintilian to emphasize that a pleasing prose style prevents reader boredom and ensures the preservation of knowledge.</summary>
