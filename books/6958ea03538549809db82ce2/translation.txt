# In the name of the Merciful One, by whose supreme will one receives grace and the perfection of doctrine. The beginning of the words of John, son of Mesue, son of Hamech, son of Belly, son of Abdela, King of Damascus.

<note>original: "In nomine dei misericordis"</note> In the name of the merciful God, etc. Medicine is addition and subtraction, namely, from the human body. This is how it is understood. The proposed words are written by Galen in his commentaries on the sects, and by Hippocrates in the same place. They are also written by Galen in his book <note>original: "napentice"</note> on the authority of the ancient elders. In these words, the definition of medicine is touched upon. Although these words can be explained in various ways, nevertheless, adapting them to my purpose, they are understood thus: medicine is addition and removal or subtraction. That is, medicine is that which concerns addition to and removal from the human body. The explanation of these words is as follows: the reason is that the very art of medicine is concerned with the preservation and introduction of natural things, and the gathering and removal of things against nature; but the art of medicine is this. Therefore, etc. The major premise is known because addition is nothing other than the preservation of natural things or their acquisition when they are not in the body. Just as subtraction is nothing other than the removal or evacuation of things against nature. The minor premise is known because the end of medicine itself is the attainment of health in sick bodies, or its preservation in healthy bodies. For health is a certain natural thing. Therefore, the physician preserves it as something to be kept in a healthy body, or applies it in a sick body, which confirms those causes for the sake of health, such as composition and proportionality, and confirms its accidents or effects. Therefore, these operations signify this. And just as the science of medicine is concerned with the removal of things against nature, and principally with the removal of disease, for which a cure is principally owed; therefore, for the sake of a good cure of the disease, it is concerned with the cause and the accident of the disease, since these also are things against nature. From which words it is understood that this knowledge is necessary to the physician, namely, concerning things natural and things against nature in the human body; because the benefit of medicine is to heal the cause of the disease first, and then the disease joined to it. These, however, are things against nature. Therefore, by removing the existing cause, he removes the disease while it depends on that cause. Therefore, he further removes the accidents of the disease, because by removing the disease, the accident of that disease's cause is removed. Wherefore, etc. Considering this, therefore, John Mesue of Damascus wished to create a certain book, and it should be about simple medicines <term>solutiuīs</term> <note>laxative or purgative medicines that "loosen" the bowels or humors</note> removing the cause of the disease, and consequently the removal of all things against nature; principally those which depend on a corporeal cause. Thus they are such as are subject to the present exposition. From these words, several points appear. First, what the order of this book is to other books, because this book precedes the <term>Antidotarium</term> <note>a book of compound recipes or antidotes</note>; because this book is about simple laxative medicines, while that one is about compound medicines; and simples precede compounds. It also precedes the book on "Appropriates" <note>medicines specific to certain organs</note>; because here it is determined concerning the preservation and cure of illnesses themselves and their accidents in general; whereas in the book on Appropriates, it is determined concerning the cure of illnesses of every internal member from the head to the feet in particular. Now, general things precede particular things. And it precedes for another reason: because this book is about the instruments and the causes, namely, those which are only in quality and the manner of operating; now, the knowledge of instruments and causes precedes the knowledge of quality and the manner of operating. For this reason, it is not that the book on Appropriates is outside of it; therefore, consequently, it precedes it also because the book on Appropriates is about the removal of material illnesses. But in summary, this book is only about the cure of material illnesses. It also appears what the necessity of this book is: because it was necessary for the physician to prepare for the causes of diseases and to remove their accidents by the removal of their causes. This, however, could not be done except through the knowledge of medicines that are perfectly laxative. It also appears what the material cause of this book is: that the human body is actually sick or ready to become sick quickly due to corporeal causes, in which the corporeal sedation is sought through simple laxative medicines and the knowledge of them. For the human body is the subject of this book, because all medicine is subject to it. In this part, it is this body that is the subject of this book, either actually sick or ready to become sick; because such bodies need laxative medicines; whereas that which is healthy does not. This body is also the subject of this book insofar as it is curable by simple laxative medicines curing the cause of the disease. And by this, it is distinguished from the book on Appropriates, because there the healing of illnesses that are without matter is also treated through compound laxative medicines. It is also distinguished from the Antidotarium, because there the healing of illnesses through compound laxative medicines is treated. It also appears what the efficient cause was: which was Master John Mesue, as is said in the title. The formal cause is twofold: namely, the form of the treatise and the form of treating. The form of the treatise is the division of this book into two principal treatises. The form of treating is the method of proceeding, which is manifold: discerning, namely, defining. For it inquires into the definition and the essence of laxative medicine, and it is called definitive and narrative, because it is a collection of those things which have been said by other ancients; but rarely is it probative; though it is a positive collection of many examples, as will be seen. The final cause is twofold: namely, the end of the work and the end of the worker. The end of the work is that, having learned those things which are said in this book, the physician may operate rightly in the cure and removal of things against nature, which depend on a cause, along with the removal of its cause; or in the preservation of bodies from illnesses by the elimination of the cause. The end of the worker is the satisfaction of friends or the exercise of his own intellect. To which first part it is supposed that it is placed under all medicine, whether theoretical; but rather practical: because this book is about the quality and manner of operating upon the human body through laxative medicines. This, however, is what is practical and not theoretical.

What the title of the book is: Here begins the book on the solution of simple laxative medicines which John Mesue made. In the title of the book, the material cause is touched upon with the mention of simple medicines; the efficient cause is touched upon when it says "John Mesue." Now I come to the division of the book. This book is divided into two parts: namely, into the proem <note>preface</note> and into the treatise. The treatise begins there: "We say that laxative medicine..." The first part invokes the help of God and places the name of the book in its title. In the second part, he places his intention and the cause of his intention, starting at: "A word fell among those inquiring." He explains the first part thus: in the beginning, for the sake of goodwill, he receives grace from the listeners, and doctrine, namely, the teaching itself receives perfection. And this is a fitting invocation: because the help which is sought from God redounds to the good of the teacher himself and the utility of the disciple acquiring the perfection of doctrine from the teacher. In the words "The beginning of the words," etc., it can be explained in two ways. In one way, so that the name of the maker of this book is placed here, both name and surname: so that the sense is that the beginning of these words was of John, son of Mesue, who was the son of Hamech, which Hamech was the son of Belly, which Belly was the son of Abdela, King of Damascus, etc. Or it can be explained another way, so that the sense is that although the words of this book were of John, son of Mesue, they were nevertheless dedicated to the son of Hamech, etc. For it is absurd and vain to say that any physician making this book was the son of a king or the son of a king's son, although sometimes that might be; and in this way of explaining, I can say that although the words of this book were of John, son of Mesue, they were nevertheless dedicated to the son of Hamech, who was the son of Abdela, King of Damascus. It does not matter in which way we explain it. Then when he says:

### A word fell among those entering to know what it is that they have remembered; certain many wise men have written about the rectification of simple laxative medicines.

In this part, he places his intention and the cause of his intention. He first places the intention, and secondly explains the necessity of his intention. In the first part, he intends such a reason: The knowledge of those things which are very necessary to the physician must be handed down in the art of medicine. But laxative medicines are very necessary to the physician. Therefore, their knowledge must be handed down in the art of medicine. The major premise is known by this, that he says "A word fell among those inquiring," which is that which is necessary to the physician. For that word ought to be handed down among wise physicians, which is very necessary to them. He makes the minor premise when he says "that they have remembered," etc. First, it should be known that the term "word" can be explained in three ways. In one way, "word" is not only a sign of a mental concept, so that "word" is nothing other than a name or voice carrying a mental concept. And then the sense is that words or voices fall among the ancients entering into what is; but this explanation is very weak. In the second way, it can be explained thus: that the "word" of all is taken here for the mental concept itself which exists in the soul. And then "word" is understood as the concept of first principles, since it is proposed here absolutely; for it is understood in its best state; and the best mental concept is the concept of first principles. And then the sense of the text is such: "word," that is, the mental concept of first principles, falls among the wise who inquire what is theirs; because they greatly desire to know the principles of their own principles. And this explanation agrees with the text when it is said in the preface of the Metaphysics: "All men by nature desire to know." Now men desire to know four things about a thing: if it is, what it is, of what sort it is, and why it is. For example, in medicine, the physician himself, having the concept of first principles, desires to know about laxative medicines: first, if it exists or not. Having known this, because it exists, he desires to know what it is, and this is called the essence. Afterward, he desires to know about it of what sort it is, namely, if it has passions and properties which can be proven to be in it, namely, as to its harms and benefits. Finally, he desires to know about it why it is: as why this passion or property is in this laxative medicine, or why...

And through **Mesue**, he invites those who have knowledge to help with laxative medicines. For one thing itself has that which is first, that is, it is light, and through this, he invites him to know it. Because also that thing has what it is, and through this, he invites him through that which it is, or gives him understanding. I know now that through error this letter [text] might be taken here for an act that will be among men. It stands so that the word is the same as "attention" and "dispute" among men. This cause is commonly said. And so the sense of the letter is such: the word and attention fell among those entering, namely that which is. For many among wise physicians have had error and dispute regarding laxative medicine. And so, so that error does not happen concerning them, it is good to hand down to them a practical and ordered science.
In this, it seems possible for **Johannes Mesue** to explain somewhat the necessity of his intention. And this is the best exposition among all and more adapted to the following letter. Then when he says:

¶ **In the arrangement, however: neither does any of them hand it down fully: he must have published from this intention: but through diverse chapters: they are remembered in diverse places: leaving this teaching dispersed.**

¶ In this part, Mesue summarizes the special necessity of his intention: and he intends such a reason. Knowledge of the art of medicine must be handed down to those physicians to whom the ancients did not hand down a perfect and ordered knowledge. But concerning simple laxative medicines, the ancients did not hand down a perfect and ordered knowledge, nor concerning their resolution. Therefore, etc. The major premise is known, because in art there ought not to be a defect. The minor is known through itself. Then the reason is proportioned to the reason which **Galen** <note>original: "G." referring to Galen</note> makes in the *Tegni* <note>The *Ars Medica*, a foundational medical textbook</note>, where he proves that it is necessary in that book to hand down a distinctive doctrine. "That one work," etc. there he shows the formal cause which is the mode of acting. In the treasures of your bounty is all happiness. We shall place our work, moreover, into two sections. In the first, the science of the power of rectifying medicines with their conditions and modes. But here is one general doubt: whether knowledge of simple laxative medicines ought to be handed down in the art of medicine. For it seems that it should not. Knowledge of those things which are poisons or poisonous things ought not to be handed down in the art of medicine; but laxative medicines are of this kind; therefore, etc. The major is known by the authority of **Haly** <note>Haly Abbas, a famous Persian physician</note> in the first discourse of his *Royal Disposition* <note>The *Liber Regalis* or *Kitab al-Maliki*</note>. In the second chapter, he says that this is one of the precepts: namely, not to provide a poisonous or deadly drink to anyone, nor to speak of it. The minor appears because laxative medicines act on our body by even bringing force against nature. Everything, however, that brings force against nature is poisonous. On the contrary is Mesue here and other authors who hand down knowledge about them. I say to this that knowledge of laxative medicines ought to be handed down in the art of medicine. The reason is this: knowledge of those medicines ought to be handed down in the art of medicine, and the mode of their appropriation, which are effective of things contrary to nature from the human body; but such laxative medicines are these. Therefore, etc. The major appears because through this the physician follows his end, which is the attainment of health in sick bodies, or preservation from sicknesses. The minor appears because these laxative medicines even eject bad humors from the body which actually cause sickness or are prepared to cause it. Everything of such a kind is a thing contrary to nature.
The second reason is this: knowledge of those medicines ought to be handed down in the art of medicine about which it happens that a physician acts rightly or errs. And concerning laxative medicines, it happens that a physician acts rightly or errs. The major is known, since art is the right reason of things to be made. It concerns the good and the difficult. Whence it stands so that concerning it the craftsman can act rightly or err. The minor appears because Galen and Mesue and other ancients proceeded rightly concerning them with the mode of their appropriation and with due testing, care, and time, and other things which are considered in their appropriation; whereas many others, not considering such things, have erred concerning them. Whence it seems more common to err concerning the use of laxative medicine than concerning the use of alterative or resolutive medicine; because they do not see the effect of alterative or resolutive medicine instantly, nor do others believe that these medicines do anything. Therefore, if the sick person is harmed among them, the physician does not acquire great blame for this; whereas if it succeeds poorly for the physician, or great honor if it succeeds well through its use. It is very necessary for physicians to have knowledge of them: both as to their absolute essence, as from the part of the substance and virtue that places them; and as to the correction of their harms and the mode of their appropriation. Similarly also as to the removal of their harms in the hour of their purging and after purging; teaching also to cure their accidents and harms while they are present, and teaching to preserve from them. This, however, Mesue does in this book, which first contains four discourses. Then to the reason: "About those," etc. I say that the major premise is false. You prove it by authority, namely Haly and others. I say that it matters much to say "do not give a deadly drink" and "do not know it." Indeed, the physician ought not to give it, because the end is destroyed. But if Haly says that he ought not to know it, on the contrary, he ought to know it so that we may avoid it; or it can be said that the major is granted regarding those things which are absolutely poison. But if they are poisonous medicines not through themselves but through being resolved with other medicines, their malice is removed; it is right to hand down knowledge about them. For so Mesue will do in the text. For he will teach how to know **scammony** and **agaric** and similar things, which are better among them, and how a man can remove poisonous parts from them which are found in them.

### Chapter 1: On the selection of medicines, which is done by understanding their judgments according to their proper essence.

<margin>
C. We say that laxative medicine, etc. After Johannes Mesue <note>Yuhanna ibn Masawayh, a famous 9th-century physician</note> set down his preface in which he showed his intentions and the cause and necessity of his intention regarding the deceased. In this part, he sets down his treatise delivering, namely, the doctrine of simple laxative medicines as far as their absolute knowledge is concerned: and as far as the manner of applying them to the body. And he divides this book into two parts. For the knowledge of laxative medicines is twofold: one is universal, which indeed applies to all or many laxative medicines, in which knowledge of them is sought; and this is in that which seeks knowledge of the power of the operation, the benefits, and the harms of the same from the beginning: or of scammony <note>a harsh herbal purgative</note>, and so of others. First, Johannes Heliodorus <note>likely a commentator</note> delivers that universal knowledge of simple laxative medicines. Second, the particular knowledge... in the second section. First, he treats simple laxative medicines as far as what they are. He treats their accidents and proper properties. Second, he treats them as far as the manner of preparation and administration of them to the body. Mesue says the first part is in two. First, he delivers to us the knowledge of simple laxative medicines as far as their formal principle, because according to what purging is done. Second, he delivers to us the knowledge of them as far as the properties and effects they produce. For the knowledge of simple laxative medicines insofar as they are laxative is twofold: one is general, where we do not descend to explaining any specific laxative medicine; the other is special, in which a descent is made to the species without their differences. First, therefore, he delivers the first general knowledge. Second, he notifies us of these simple laxative medicines through this formal principle, because in this part he removes a certain false understanding of these men. First, he proposes what the formal principle is, through which laxative medicines become laxative. Second, he proves that this is their principle. Regarding the first part, it should be noted that from the words of Mesue he notifies what this principle is by which laxative medicine operates: and he notifies it negatively and positively. First, by showing that it is not fitting that a laxative medicine purges from its primary virtue or quality, because not from its temperament <term>complexio: the balance of hot, cold, moist, and dry</term> or elementary virtue, but because it possesses this specific power or specific form which was attributed to it in its cause by the <unclear>first</unclear> <unclear>cause</unclear> of its constitution, as from the intelligence that gives forms: it also acts in the <unclear>body</unclear> by drawing out that which is contrary to it.
</margin>

We say that a laxative medicine is not so because of its temperament <note>original: "re complonali"</note> as such. Nor as a contrary acting on a contrary because it is opposite, but because it is such. And neither because a similar thing attracts this or is more eradicative: or opposite, but because it is such. And neither because it is heavy or light, moving upwards or downwards: but because it is such.

This is done because it has this hidden power <term>virtutem occultam: a property that cannot be explained by the four basic qualities</term> or specific form attributed to it in the manner aforesaid: nor even because of such a humor, because it is similar to the humor from which it attracts it, but because it is such as was said. Nor was it because it draws the humor to itself by its weight, or because it is light, driving it upwards, but because it is such. And thus in these words, a threefold power is explained and removed from laxative medicine, in that it is laxative and does not operate through those. For first, he explains the first power by saying that it is not from the temperament or elementary virtue. For someone might think that since the laxative medicine itself is mixed from elements from which it is tempered by their qualities, it would be such through its temperament. He explains second the second power by which someone might think that laxative medicine would be such when he says: "And neither because it is heavy or light, drawing the humor down by its gravity, or driving them up by its levity." Neither because it is opposite nor because it is similar. Thirdly, he removes the third power from laxative medicine by which someone might think it operates, because it is not contrary when he says: "And neither because a similar thing is attractive or eradicative," eradicating, namely, the humors from the members and acting upon them. For it is not so. Second, I say that Mesue notifies this principle by which laxative medicine operates positively when he says: "but because it is such," that is, through its specific form which it has. But regarding this "suchness," so to speak, Mesue does not specify for two reasons. First, because the cause will be more investigated in the process of the chapter or in the following chapter where he sees that this quality is a specific or hidden form found in the medicine itself; which he will not clarify in the process, nor will he explain it for another reason, because it exists <insert>of this which exists as medicine, which is the driver</insert>. It follows from these things said in the text, for since the power that exists in medicine is fourfold: namely, first as hot, cold, etc.; second, its gravity or levity, and so of others; and third, because it is eradicative or attractive of milk or semen; and fourth, which is the specific power. Since the laxative medicine itself is removed from the first three by its formal principle as Mesue posits, there remains the fourth power in it, which is the specific form through which it evacuates this humor. And from this, a threefold doubt is raised. One is: whether laxative medicine is contrary to the humor by the temperament of its elementary virtue or not. The second is: whether it acts through similarity or contrariety which it has to the humor or not. The third is: whether it acts through the specific form it has or not.

<margin>C</margin> It is endowed—not in a twofold way, as the philosophers say—with power, namely, elementary and celestial. Hence indeed common, here truly proper. For indeed, everything hot and cold is heating and cooling. But a laxative is not so because it is hot, nor because it is cold, but because it is thus endowed with celestial power, with its mixture regulating it. And because of this, one is laxative, while another is provocative <note>inducing a specific discharge</note>. Another is one thing, another another, and so on. And this is because from celestial power, such a thing is carried above the temperament.

<margin>C</margin> In this part, Mesue proves what he said, namely, that laxative medicine was proper to its humor through a hidden power or specific form that is in it: and he does this first. Second, he removes a certain opinion. In the first part, he intends such a reason: every lower mixture has some operations or causes them through elemental and temperamental power, or through celestial power. Laxative medicine is a certain mixture performing its own proper operation, and it does it either through elemental power or through celestial power found in it. But not through elemental power: for although a mixture becomes heating or cooling through the hot or cold existing in it formally or virtually, it is not therefore laxative because it is hot or cold, as will appear below; but because it is informed by celestial power in it, a hidden power or specific form in that mixture. Regarding this reason, Mesue proceeds thus: first, he sets forth or declares the major proposition; second, the minor. But here it should be noted: first, Mesue says that every mixture here below is informed by a twofold power: elementary and celestial power. The cause is at hand, because every mixture has a twofold principle by which it can perform its operation: namely, an internal principle, which holds itself on the part of the matter and the proper and contained agent; the other is a superior or universal principle, and this is common: the celestial body. Whence even Aristotle in the second book of *Physics* says that man generates man from matter and the sun. Therefore, Mesue rightly says that a mixture is endowed and informed by elementary and celestial power, so that from these two it may perfect its operations. Second, it should be noted that Mesue says a mixture is endowed with celestial power: sometimes it is endowed with it commonly, sometimes properly. For a specific form is sometimes common, sometimes proper. Common: <margin>Every mixture has two primary [powers]</margin> such as when it is in many of the same genus or the same species: thus we say that in all fragrant things there is a specific form through which they have the power to attract the womb, and in stinking things there is a specific form by which they repel the womb. But a proper specific form is that which is not in all individuals of the same species in the same way: for not every magnet moves toward the North Pole, because some move toward the South Pole. Also, there is a certain magnet that attracts human flesh and some not, as Albert <note>Albertus Magnus</note> posits in his book *On Minerals* on the authority of Apollonius, and yet all are of the same species. Nevertheless, that preparation is the preparatory principle serving the specific form for the action, while performing that operation. It does not flow into the specific form absolutely by merit, but it flows into the merit prepared by the preparation. Therefore, we cannot say that the specific form itself is anything other formally than a certain thing added upon the preparation: through which it performs such an operation; or even if the preparation is not the operative principle of the occult operation, it nevertheless greatly assists the occult operation. This [is the] medical solution. Just as it happens truly from the medical solution, which with the specific form has a hot and dry composition annexed to them, they more [effectively] attract and evacuate than if they did not have such a composition; yet by the very property they have, they evacuate more than others which are not so hot and dry. ¶ Then when he says,

### Plato says: He endowed things indeed with natural properties. I said, namely, that according to what acts purely from its species, that which is proper [to it] is done. Or <margin>of none</margin> because nothing of a thing is a better proper action than that which the species regulates. To certify this, however, is not the task of the physician, but of him who deals with higher things. <note>Referring to the philosopher or natural scientist.</note>

In this part, he adds a sufficient reason for this and argues thus: If a laxative medicine is appropriated to a determined humor, evacuating it from distant places, attracting it to determined places, and then transmitting it through determined places, then the medicine performs its proper operation through its specific form and not through an elementary virtue. But it is so. Because then there is a greater appropriation than by any other preparation. For he endowed things, as above, with properties: namely, that which acts, acts that which is also proper to it, and is moved by its species, that is, by its specific form. Such acting, however, belongs not to the physician, but to him who acts higher than himself, i.e., the physician. The minor premise is known in itself. Then when he says,

### To think otherwise is inconvenient: because like is too much like the other, and so conversely. But contraries flee each other and do not attract.

In this part, Mesue <note>Yuhanna ibn Masawayh, a famous Persian Christian physician.</note> removes a certain false opinion. But he intended to place this matter in the third [book] on simple medicine. And he says this: that it is inconvenient to think that a medicine evacuates a humor because it is similar, so that it might [be thought] that safflower attracts phlegm and turbith attracts phlegm because where there is likeness, it attracts the like to itself and not the contrary, because contraries <margin>He led by assimilation</margin> flee each other and do not attract. But regarding likeness, it is twofold: one is likeness in manifest qualities. In this way, a medicine never evacuates or draws a humor because it is similar to it in a manifest quality; nor would this be well. Whence Galen thought well: for he would have been crude if he had said that safflower and turbith attract phlegm because they are similar in manifest quality, since phlegm is cold and moist, and turbith is hot and dry. And they are contrary in the mode of substance. The other is likeness in occult quality or specific form, which is discussed at length there by Galen in the 3rd [book] on simple medicine. Whence safflower or turbith evacuates phlegm because they are similar in quality, namely the occult or specific form. And through this is solved the question previously moved, if well noticed: namely, whether a laxative or solutive medicine evacuates the humor proper to it because it is like or because it is contrary. ¶ Then when he says,

### Mesue says: One ought to know further: that the action of a solutive in matter is done with discretions and sublimations regulating nature. For the eduction of these is a work of nature. The drug, however, is that which becomes the same. For it is the same to nature as the instrument is to the craftsman.

In this part, Mesue removes a certain false understanding of his words. For he had said above that a medicine evacuates a humor by property or specific form. Now someone might believe that it did this solely through the specific form, and the governing virtue of the whole body operated nothing in it. Now he removes this. And first he does this. Secondly, he proves what he had said. Thirdly, he answers a doubt. The text is clear. For he says well there. But the truth is: he says thus regarding the first part, that the evacuation of humor by a solutive medicine is done principally through the work of nature regulating it; but through the drug it is done as if through an instrument, in which the [natural] virtue is the principal agent, but the drug is the instrumental agent. For if it were supposed that in some place external to us there were bile on one side and rhubarb on the other, the bile would never move toward the rhubarb. And therefore it is necessary that this be done by the benefit of nature regulating principally, but instrumentally by the benefit of the drug. ¶ Then when he says,

### For Hippocrates says: It is indeed the role of nature to cure, not the physician.

<margin>worthy</margin>

### We say the work of the physician is to provide for nature.

In this part, he proves what he had said: for he had said just now that in the ejection of humor by a drug, nature is the principal agent and the drug is instrumental. Now he proves this; and he does two things according to the two reasons he introduces to prove this. The first he places there. ¶ "But the light which it provides..." In the first part, he intends such a reason: that <margin>The curing [force] is virtue</margin> which principally evacuates in our body is that which principally cures. But nature principally has the task of curing; medicine, however, instrumentally. Therefore, etc. The major premise is known: because that which of itself evacuates humor from our body is of itself curing the sicknesses of our body. The minor appears by the authority of Hippocrates, who says: "It is indeed the role of nature to cure and not the physician." It is more worthy for the physician to provide for the work of nature, namely, the due instrument. Similar also is where Avicenna places it in the 4th [book], 1st fen, 5th chapter on the cure of putrid [fevers] in general: "He would not have known the cures even..." and not the medicine. The physician, however, makes the instruments reach the virtue. ¶ Then when he says,

### ¶ But the light which it provides overcomes the work of nature: either in quantity or malice; it renders the operations unbridled to its prejudice.

In this part, he brings another reason to prove the same and argues thus: if a solutive medicine exhibited in too great a quantity and quality—which is regulated by the virtue—behaves so that its operation becomes unbridled or destructive, then the evacuation of the humor which is done through the solutive medicine is done by nature principally. But it is so as Mesue places it in the text. Therefore, etc. In "malice," i.e., in quality. ¶ Then when he says,

### ¶ But quantity beyond natural requirement causes transgression: and gives the harms of multitude. Maliciousness indeed [gives harms] both of multitude and of one and another mode.

In this part, he answers a doubt. He had said just now that when a medicine increases in quantity or quality beyond what the virtue requires, it causes unbridled operations and bad accidents. Now someone might believe that it would induce unbridled operations and bad accidents in the same way when increased in quantity as when increased in quality. But he removes this and says no. For when a medicine is only increased in quantity beyond what the virtue requires, it induces the harms of multitude; that is, it increases the harms in quantity. And understand this of itself and principally. Because as a consequence, it could induce accidents of diverse modes. For even if hellebore is exhibited in great quantity to our body, it induces vomiting and principally causes an increase in vomiting; yet as a consequence, it can induce various and diverse accidents through vomiting, because it could induce spasm, syncope, or tremor. But if in the medicine there is only malignancy of its quality, then it induces harms of one and another mode and quantity increases those. And concerning this there is a doubt: does a laxative evacuate humor through the composition it has, and especially a hot one? Because there is no greater doubt. It seems that it is so. Through that, a medicine has to evacuate humor through which it attracts it; but a laxative medicine attracts the humor through heat. Therefore, etc. The major is known: because this medicine has to evacuate what it attracts. The minor appears: because heat is proper among attractive causes, as Avicenna places in the first [book] of the Canon, first fen, chapter 8 on the natural virtues serving, and chapter 8 on operations. ¶ Also, if a medicine evacuates humor by resolving it through its heat, then through heat it evacuates it by resolving it; but it is so. Therefore, etc. The minor is noted: because evacuation through the solution of humor and its resolution do not differ except according to more and less. Also, they differ by another [reason]: because one is manifest, like that which is done through solution; the other is occult, like that which is done through resolution. Now more and less manifest and unmanifest do not diversify the principle of operation. When it is asked of the medicine and other authors, I say to this that the principal [reason] why a medicine evacuates humor is twofold: namely, general, by which the medicine is evacuating not a determined humor; and another, namely, special: by which the medicine is evacuating a determined humor from determined places through determined places, also attracting it to determined places. ¶ Regarding this, we speak of the first principle of the operation of solutive medicine: I say that it can perform such an evacuation through a preparation, especially a hot one. The reason is this: if a solutive medicine through its hot composition can introduce a vacuum in the body, then it can evacuate humor through its hot composition. But it is so. Therefore, etc. The major is noted: because a solutive medicine which evacuates by attracting induces a vacuum not absolutely, but a subtle vacuity. The minor appears: because it is in the nature of heat to dilate and open and induce emptiness, and thus by reason of vacuum to induce and make places empty. But if we speak of the second principle by which a solutive medicine evacuates: I say that it does not evacuate through composition, but through the first specific [form]. And the reason is this: every operation which is appropriated to any thing, and is not common, is done by the proper species or specific form; but for a solutive medicine, in this way, it is appropriated to a determined humor, also evacuating it from determined places. Therefore, etc. The major is known by the authority of Plato: for everything that is moved by its species and its specific form acts what is proper to it. The minor is known in itself. ¶ Also this is sought thus: if a solutive medicine evacuated through a proper humor, it would evacuate through composition: either it would evacuate it through the likeness it has to it in a prior quality, or through the contrariety it has to it. It cannot be said that it evacuates it through the likeness it has to it in quality: because then in this way it would evacuate bile and not phlegm, since it is its contrary, because phlegm is cold and moist. Item also epithyme, since it is hot and dry, if it evacuated [by likeness], it would not evacuate melancholy but bile, because bile is hot and dry; and violets and whey and juice of orach would not evacuate bile, but phlegm, since they are cold and moist like phlegm. Nor can it be said that a solutive medicine evacuates through a contrary humor, through the contrariety it has to it in quality. Because in this way, scammony, which is hot, would not evacuate bile; nor even rhubarb, since these medicines, if they are cold in quality, are [not] contrary to bile, but similar to it. And through this the reasons are solved: because all conclude that a solutive medicine, with respect to its common operation which is to evacuate any humor indeterminately, can well do this through its hot composition. Whence this happens: it is the nature of a hot solutive medicine evacuating humor indeterminately to evacuate them by resolving through its heat.
