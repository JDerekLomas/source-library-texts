# P R E F A C E.

Botany <note>The study of plants</note> is without question one of the most useful and agreeable branches of Natural History. This is so well known today that I shall not pause to prove it, as these two advantages are explained at great length in a very large number of books dealing with the history of plants. I shall only report here what gave rise to the composition of this work, and in what manner I labored toward the execution of the design which the King has always held: to increase the sciences during his Reign.

I owe the first inclination I had for the study of plants to the curious demonstrations that the Reverend Father Philippe Sergeant—a very skillful Pharmacist and a Monk of our Order <note>The Order of Minims</note> from the Province of France—and Mr. François de Onuphriis, a Roman Physician, performed in our Royal Convent of the Trinity of the Mount <note>Trinità dei Monti</note> in Rome. From that time on, I gradually left the study of Mathematics, which until then had been my primary occupation, to apply myself to Botany. Obedience having recalled me to my Province, I obtained permission from my Superiors to travel along the coasts of Provence and the mountains of the Alps to discover the most curious plant specimens there. I had even resolved to create a new *pinax* <note>original: "pinax"; a Greek term for a "tablet" or "register," here referring to a comprehensive botanical index or catalog</note>, or general collection of plants, with illustrations; I already had a considerable number of them drawn when, while botanizing along the coasts of Marseille, the opportunity arose to make the voyage to America. Mr. Bégon—so well known to scholars, and who finds moments amidst his great duties to devote to the study of the sciences—was at that time...

# PREFACE

->Intendant of the Galleys at Marseille.<- <note>The Intendant was a high-ranking administrative official; in this context, likely Michel Bégon, for whom the Begonia is named.</note> He desired, in order to satisfy the King's orders, to find someone who could undertake a voyage to our Antilles Islands (where he had previously served as Intendant) to conduct a search for everything rare and curious that Nature produces there. He made this proposal to Mr. Surian, who was highly capable, not only in the knowledge of plants, but also in the secrets of chemistry <note>In the 17th century, "chemistry" often referred to the preparation of medicines from plants.</note>; and he gave him at the same time a commission to find someone who was in a position to assist him in the execution of this design. Mr. Surian made the proposal to me: I lent my hand to it with pleasure, and we undertook the voyage some time later by the orders of His Majesty.

We have so many beautiful and faithful accounts of the Antilles Islands, that it is easy to judge that I found there an abundance of material to satisfy my curiosity. I remained there for about two years, across two voyages that I made, and during that time, I drew and described nearly six hundred different plants, a portion of which I am providing in this volume; and as I knew by my own experience that it is very difficult to properly know a plant by small figures, I wanted to draw them in their natural size; if not in their entirety, at least in part. One will perhaps be surprised that I provide only the simple outline almost without shading, but I was very pleased to engrave them in such a manner that one could add the coloring more easily, as we see in all the works of Fuchs <note>original: "Fuchsius"; referring to Leonhart Fuchs, a 16th-century German physician and botanist famous for his herbal illustrations.</note>, which are engraved likewise in simple outline, and most of which are illuminated <note>Hand-colored.</note>. I have endeavored to describe them as succinctly as possible, without however omitting anything necessary to explain all their particularities. I also wished, for the greater satisfaction of the curious <note>The "curious" referred to the community of scholars and collectors of the era.</note>, to arrange them under known genera, and I have given them Latin names <note>original: "noms Latins"</note> suiting their genera. I was nevertheless obliged to establish a new genus for some...

I do not intend to take away from the Authors who have written before me on the plants of America the glory which is due to them. I admit that I have benefited from their insights; but as most have only given the names of these plants in the common language of that country—which means that those who have never seen them in nature have great difficulty in distinguishing which genus they belong to—I believe that the Reader will be somewhat grateful to me for having reduced them into genera and species known in Botany.

It remains for me to inform the public that if they derive any pleasure from this work, they owe the obligation to the late Lord de Seignelay, Minister and Secretary of State, and to Lord de Pontchartrain, who succeeded him. The former obtained for me, through the liberality of the King, the funds to cover the costs of my travels, and the latter has had the kindness to also honor me with his protection before His Majesty for the engraving and for the printing of this first volume.

This plant resembles a small palm tree in its stature: its trunk is straight and is only furnished with branches at the summit, where they are arranged in a circle in the same manner as those of palm trees. This trunk is about eight to ten feet in height and nearly half a foot in diameter, of equal thickness from the bottom to the top, and covered with grey, membrane-like scales. Beneath these scales, one finds a tan-colored surface, dotted with several small, rather deep holes arranged in a network <note>original: "raiſeau" (réseau), referring to the vascular patterns left on the trunk</note>, and interspersed with the vestiges of old branches, which are less deeply recessed.

When this trunk is cut crosswise, one discovers in place of the pith a very white, relatively firm flesh with a sweetish taste, full of a white and sticky sap, and surrounded by a lattice <note>original: "laſſis" (lacis)</note> of black veins, wavy and as hard as wood.

# DESCRIPTION

They are about ten feet long, smooth, flat on top with a rounded back, and tinted a green that leans toward reddish-brown. They support on either side about thirty branches arranged alternately opposite one another and placed quite close together; these are the thickness of a goose quill, and the longest are about two feet in length.

These latter branches are further furnished on both sides with several ribs arranged alternately opposite each other and very close together. These ribs are smooth, of a rather bright green, and their length is approximately seven to eight inches: they support on each side leaves quite similar to those of Mattioli's <term>Male Fern</term><note>Pietro Andrea Mattioli (1501–1577) was a famous Italian physician and botanist; his "Male Fern" refers to Dryopteris filix-mas.</note>, seven to eight <term>lines</term><note>A "line" is an archaic unit of measurement equal to 1/12 of an inch.</note> long, two wide at their base, and ending in a blunt point: their edge is carved with a sawtooth indentation: their color is a pleasant green, and their back is laden with a double row of <term>small bumps</term><note>Original: "bossettes." These are the sori, or spore-producing structures, found on the underside of fern fronds.</note> which reduce into a dark chestnut-colored dust. I observed, as far as my sight would allow, that before these bumps are reduced to dust, they are preceded by very small gray flowers composed of a single hollow leaf like a small hemispherical cup and full of a quantity of very small gray filaments.

Before the young branches of this tree extend in length, they resemble the <term>volute of an Ionic capital</term><note>The spiral, scroll-like ornament found on the top of Greek columns.</note>, or a Bishop’s <term>crosier</term><note>A hooked staff carried by high-ranking clergy; in botany, this coiled shape of a young fern frond is now commonly called a "fiddlehead."</note>: they are entirely covered with small pointed scales, reddish-brown and silvery at their edges.

There are many of these plants in Martinique along the stream that passes through Fort St. Pierre, and in the humid forests along the road to Capesterre. I have also seen them in several places on the island of Hispaniola <note>Original: "S. Domingue."</note> toward a district called "The Massacre" near Port-de-Paix.

This is the plant that Gonzalo Oviedo calls *Fern Tree* <note>Original: "Alberi del Felce."</note> in the *History of the Indies*, book nine, chapter fourteen. The Reverend Father Du Tertre of the Order of St. Dominic also speaks of it in his third treatise on the Antilles, chapter one, paragraph three.

One day I had a great number of these plants cut down and left to dry, and having had them burned, I extracted ashes with a very acrid and pungent taste, which could undoubtedly be used to make glass.

### III.

->Tree-like fern, broad-leaved, and prickly.<-
<note>Latin: *Filix arborescens, latifolia, aculeata.*</note>
->*Tree fern, thorny, and with broad leaves.*<-

Its roots are fibrous, black, and spread out like turf; they sometimes produce a trunk, and sometimes stems that form a kind of black thicket, very thorny and as thick as a man's body.

These stems are nearly three inches thick at their base, and are black and very prickly: they gradually taper up to the height of a man; and it is at this height that they separate into three branches: the middle one does not divide further, but those on the sides divide a little higher up into two others which, with the middle one, form a kind of five-pronged fork.

Throughout their entire length, these branches put forth opposing shoots arranged alternately, laden with leaves set in the same manner, whose indentations closely resemble the leaves of the plant that Gaspard Bauhin <note>A famous Swiss botanist (1560–1624) known for classifying thousands of plants.</note> calls *Rough Holly-fern* <note>Latin: *Lonchitis aspera*.</note>. These leaves are membranous, smooth, brownish-green, about ten inches long by three inches wide; their vein runs the entire length of the leaf, distributing smaller veins to each indentation.

I have not been able to observe if it has upon its back the dust <note>The "dust" refers to spores, as ferns do not produce seeds.</note> that one usually notices on plants of the same species. A great quantity of them are found in the forests of Martinique on the way to Capesterre, after having descended the Morne de la Calebasse <note>A "Morne" is a Caribbean term for a small mountain or hill; Calebasse refers to the Calabash tree.</note>.
