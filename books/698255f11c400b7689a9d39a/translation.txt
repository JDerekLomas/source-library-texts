...in this way! **Athenian Stranger:** We judged that the sacrilegious person, and likewise the enemy of well-established laws, would justly die. And when we were about to write many laws of this kind, we held ourselves back, considering that these passions <note>Plato uses the word "passions" here to refer to both the internal impulses and the resulting crimes or sufferings.</note> are infinite in both number and magnitude. They are the most just of all passions, yet also the most shameful of all. Will not things that are honorable and just appear at one moment as they should, and at another as their opposites?

**Clinias:** So it seems.

**Athenian Stranger:** Many, therefore, inconsistently call things honorable and just as if they were in disagreement.

**Clinias:** So it clearly appears.

**Athenian Stranger:** But let us look at this again, O Clinias, to see how we agree on these matters.

**Clinias:** What agreement do you mean?

**Athenian Stranger:** I think this was clearly stated by me in our previous discussions.

**Clinias:** In what way?

**Athenian Stranger:** Because if it was not said above, at least consider me to be saying it now.

**Clinias:** What is it?

**Athenian Stranger:** That all bad men are bad entirely against their will <note>original: "non sponte"; this reflects the Socratic paradox that no one does evil knowingly.</note>; and if this is so, it is necessary that this follows as well:

**Clinias:** What?

**Athenian Stranger:** That to be unjust is an evil, and to be evil involuntarily is of this nature. Truly, that which is voluntary can in no way happen involuntarily. Therefore, he who considers injustice to be involuntary will think that the man acting unjustly does so against his own will. I must now also agree with myself: since I admit that everyone commits injury not by their own will <note>original: "non sponte"</note>, even though someone might object to us for the sake of ambition or contention: that while unjust men are indeed unjust against their will, many of them nevertheless commit injury voluntarily <note>original: "sponte"</note>. For my opinion is not this, but that. How then shall I agree with my own words, if you, O Clinias and Megillus, ask me this: "If these things are so, Stranger, what do you advise us regarding the city of the Magnesians <note>The fictional Cretan colony for which the characters are drafting a constitution.</note>? Should we give them laws or not?" I would indeed advise giving them laws. Then you would say: "Will you then distinguish for them between injuries committed voluntarily and those committed involuntarily? And will you prescribe greater penalties for the former and lesser for the latter? Or will the penalties for all be equal, since many thousands of injuries are committed altogether voluntarily?"

**Clinias:** You are right, Stranger.
