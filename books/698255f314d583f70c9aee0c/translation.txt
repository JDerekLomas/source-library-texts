# OF SEMIRAMIS
## *Hermetically sealed,*
### *Which*
## IF A WISE MAN OPENS IT,
### *And not*
## CYRUS, THE AMBITIOUS AND AVARICIOUS,
## *That king will find inexhaustible treasures of wealth,*
## *sufficient for his needs.*
<note>This refers to a legendary story where Queen Semiramis (or Nitocris) left an inscription on her tomb claiming that any king of Babylon who lacked money could find it within. When Cyrus the Great opened it, he found no money, but only a message rebuking his greed. Here, it is used as an alchemical metaphor: only the "wise" seeker, not the greedy one, can find the true treasure.</note>

...description, we draw out from the tomb and present to the literary world to be viewed and opened; *Which if a Wise man, and not Cyrus—the ambitious and greedy—opens, he will find the inexhaustible treasures of the riches of Kings, as much as suffices.* <note>The author continues the allegory from the title page: alchemical knowledge is a "treasure" that remains hidden from those motivated only by greed, like the Persian King Cyrus in the legend.</note> I add to this constant health; these you might call the two main pillars of a happier life.

But perhaps there will be "Sages" on high to whom opening this Tomb will seem like women's work or child's play. I would have them know, however, that this science is *a very deep river, in which the Lamb walks*—meaning the simple person makes progress—*and the Elephant swims*—meaning the most learned men drift among their own opinions, errors, and doubts, and are even submerged. <note>original: *Amnem altissimum, in quo Agnus ambulat... & Elephas natat.* This is a famous medieval proverb, often applied to the Bible, suggesting it is shallow enough for a lamb to cross safely but deep enough for an elephant to drown.</note> While one man is still doubtful about the true subject of the <term>Great Work</term> <note>The *Magnum Opus*, the process of creating the Philosophers' Stone.</note> (and is indeed entirely off-track), another is laboriously anxious about acquiring <term>Sophic Mercury</term> <note>original: *Mercurio sophico*. Not common quicksilver, but a specialized alchemical substance believed to be the "seed" of metals.</note> and exhausts himself in vain labors, knowing neither what it is nor what form it takes. Another man torments himself with empty sorrow over the nature of <term>Philosophic Fire</term>, the magical elements, the key, or the dissolving <term>menstruum</term> <note>An alchemical solvent.</note>—asking from where it should be drawn, whether it is sweet or corrosive, cold or fiery. I have often groaned over the things such men's hands have produced and the fruitlessness of their sweat, where in the end they find nothing but vanity and affliction of spirit. Therefore, moved by a sting of charity and pity for them, I freely share the light mercifully granted to me, so that they may find the key to the sanctuary of that sacred science—even though I may be accused by many of being guilty of violating the vow of silence.

Indeed, your own curious industry and tireless interest, most Excellent Men, regarding the curious secrets and works of <term>many-skilled Nature</term> <note>original: *Polydædalæ Naturæ*. A reference to Daedalus, the mythical craftsman, implying nature is a complex, artistic builder.</note>—which has invited the whole literary world to share its more hidden things through your sweet allure—has also driven me to make you, the true sons of all-encompassing science, the heirs of this most curious work of all nature which I possess.

As for who I am, do not ask. I am a man who strives to benefit others; your friend, and an admirer of your virtues, known to many, at least by name.
