He is a Pontiff whom I—on account of his admirable nature and exquisite learning joined with the keenest judgment, and the highest integrity of life and character, which I observed when he returned from Poland and passed through Bologna—judged most worthy of such a high pinnacle of dignity. I have long hoped for an opportunity to be given to me by which I might demonstrate this desire and respect of mine: which I finally do now, most humbly offering these my night-watches <note>original: "vigilias"; a common scholarly metaphor for works produced by burning the midnight oil</note> at your Holiest feet on bended knees.

Finally, there is the condition of the work itself, which likewise not only invited but incited me to this plan, deliberation, and decision. I would wish Your Holiness to judge how much labor and expense I have consumed therein: for I have indicated not only the Greek and Latin names of the birds treated, but also the Hebrew, Arabic, Italian, and finally those of all nations. In addition to this, I have described the birds, and those I was able to obtain I have placed before the eyes of the readers in drawings; I reveal their nature and habits, what food they use, by what art they are captured, how they ought to be seasoned, and what nourishment they provide to those who eat them (a part which will be treated more fully in the second work); I teach which diseases they may cure. I explain emblems, symbols, icons, coins, adages, and mystical matters from both sacred and profane sources, as well as hieroglyphics. From this, whatever can be said concerning Birds can be sought here, so much so that the Most Illustrious Paravicino—whom I gladly name a hero on account of the most exact judgment in which he excels—when I showed him this work, said that I had left no room for others to speak.

However, nearly all the scholarly works on animals published by others until now have been dedicated either to the Supreme Pontiffs your predecessors, or to Emperors, or Kings, and similar Most Serene Princes. And although there is a common proverb, *Good wine needs no hanging ivy* <note>original: "Vino vendibili non opus esse suspensa hedera"; a Latin version of "Good wine needs no bush," meaning a good product needs no advertisement</note>, the matter seems to be quite the opposite in this business. Indeed, scholarly works, though praiseworthy in themselves and worthy of posterity, unless they are published under the care and authority of great men, for the most part either perish suddenly or at least are little valued. For these great men perform the same service for literature that water does for lupins; for just as this kind of legume, when soaked in water, loses all its bitterness, in the same way they make literature more pleasing and effective for everyone by their favor. Seeing this, the wisest men, both ancient and modern, protected their learning with the authority of princes.

Hence it is that Pliny’s *Natural History* has always flourished, flourishes still, and will flourish forever, because it was dedicated and entrusted to Vespasian Caesar. What age shall destroy the golden poems of Oppian? None surely, none; for the patronage of Antoninus Caesar rendered them immortal. Nor perhaps would we possess Aristotle’s commentaries on animals—never sufficiently praised or to be praised—unless Alexander the Great had interposed his Royal Majesty both in their writing and their publication. **SINCE** it has been so ordained by the perpetual and plainly immutable law of nature, Dear Reader, that nothing is born immortal, and that all things which have at any time arisen in this hemisphere are subject to destruction: it truly could not be otherwise but that even the most distinguished monuments of the most learned men should lie under the same necessity of perishing as all other things. For even if God, that supreme artisan of all things, and His servant Nature, intended that nothing should be created more lasting or more vital than these works—and that nothing, in short, should less dread the injury of time—they nevertheless set a limit even for them. They willed that they be born under the same law: to grow obsolete through the vice of age and to be swept headlong by the blind accidents of wars, fires, and other injuries. As I considered all these things to be only too true, and reflected a little more deeply on the causes of such great disasters, there finally came to mind that golden sentiment of Sallust, which he put forward concerning greed as the material of all malice and depravity: that it is greed which overthrows faith, integrity, and the other noble arts. For that first age of the ancient Greeks, Egyptians, and Romans—the foster-mother and cultivator of all arts and virtues—flourished and stood in its vigor only so long as Kings, Princes, and other men lived content with their own state. They did not seek the property of others, nor step beyond the boundaries of their own dominion, nor provoke others in war for the sake of enlarging their kingdom, nor gape at accumulating things through right or wrong. Instead, cherishing peace at home, they bestowed the highest honor upon the inventors and cultivators of good disciplines and other virtues, and offered them the most ample rewards. But after that monstrous plague—greed, I say—invaded the minds of men, they began to prefer wealth (the incitement of evils) to virtues, to neglect and value as nothing all honorable arts and those skilled in them, and to bend their efforts only toward widening the borders of their fields, extending their boundaries, and piling up riches. Yet truly, those men have seemed to me to deserve true and solid glory who, by their own labor and expense, adorn and enlarge their Fatherland and the Commonwealth; but especially those who apply themselves so that the writings of the ancients may be brought to light by their labor, and propagated by their expenses for the never-dying memory of posterity—of whom, certainly, the number is most rare.

Wherefore our most eloquent Pliny complains that only a memory survives of many of nature's offspring which were best known to those ancients. He marvels greatly that, given the majesty and expanded Empire of the Roman People, not only those things which the ancients knew, but even those which were hidden from them, ought to be known. He declares those men happy and blessed because they were able to search out the secrets of nature with very little labor; but he calls himself and his contemporaries unhappy and wretched on account of the increased labor, because they were forced to hand down to posterity not only what was lost due to changed human customs, but also those things which were then new. How much honor and value the knowledge of these things held among the most ancient kings and wise men is spoken of everywhere in the monuments of any classical writers. Lest we waste time in reviewing them all, I add only a few here, beginning from the liberality of that Great Alexander, King of the Macedonians. It is well known that he not only rebuilt the fatherland for the sake of Aristotle's name and showed a kind testimony of spirit (as Pliny says) toward such great clarity of things, but also brought the greatest help to him as he searched into the sacred things and causes of nature, lest his diligent study and most honorable discipline should lack their proper rewards. Indeed, how great a thing is it that when he composed those fifty books on animals, which are called by the Greeks <term>polyplanakta</term> <note>original: "πολυπλάακτα", likely "πολυπλάνακτα" meaning "wandering far and wide" or "comprehensive"</note> books, eight hundred talents (according to Athenaeus) were spent upon them? Nor will this seem wonderful to us, if only we give credit to Pliny when he reports that several thousand men throughout the whole tract of Asia and Greece were ordered to obey him—namely, those who lived by hunting, fowling, and fishing, and those who had care of preserves, herds, fish-ponds, and aviaries—so that nothing anywhere in the world might be unknown to so great a man.

We read that the Roman Emperors also subsequently held this science not only in the highest honor, but also (as Galen reports) sought out at great expense books in which either plants, animals, or any other natural thing were depicted or discussed, as well as the men who could explain them. The Emperor Antoninus, son of Severus, exercised a singular liberality toward Oppian on account of his completed poem on animals; he not only remitted his father's exile but also ordered as many gold pieces to be paid to him as there were verses in the poem. Cheered by these gifts, the Poet wrote out his poem in golden characters; for that reason, as well as for its elegance and excellence, it was rightly and deservedly called "golden." What shall we say of the familiarity of Nerva and Hadrian with Aelian, of Vespasian with Pliny, of Trajan with Plutarch, and of many other Emperors and Kings with others skilled in this knowledge? Familiarity, did I say? Rather, the conferring of the highest honors and a munificence to be praised in every age. From this, surely, it came to pass that many most learned men, driven partly by admiration of nature and partly by the rewards offered, wrote histories most diligently—some on plants, others on animals, others on another offspring of nature. Among these, to pass over others, Pliny is worthy of the greatest praise; by plucking all the best things from the better authors, he wove together a complete natural history in thirty-seven books. We would have no author to compare with him if, had a longer life been given by God, he could have revised certain things and applied his judgment to them. ...they weigh the time, as he wrote to us, what shall we say of the age of our great-grandfathers after the time of Pliny? <note>Gaius Plinius Secundus, author of the Roman *Natural History*.</note> Those men, intent on domestic affairs rather than these sciences and other noble disciplines, and being in a state of utter, senseless barbarism, were the reason we were submerged in such great errors and lost all the fruits of the arts and sciences. <margin>God's Benevolence.</margin> Therefore, to God the Best and Greatest—the author and preserver from whom all knowledge of things proceeds to mortals, and who willed that we should no longer lack this treasure—we owe immortal thanks. For having finally pitied us, He raised up various men in various parts of the world to restore those lost things, and to bring back to the light those things which had been submerged in the deep mud of obscurity and ignorance and had lain hidden for many centuries. <margin>Epistle 12 to the Corinthians.</margin> Among these men, we have seen some who (according to the various talents and graces of His majesty, as St. Paul says) <margin>God bestows gifts in diverse ways.</margin> have utterly uprooted that barbarism; others who have endured immense labors in Sacred Theology, others in Jurisprudence, and others in Medicine. Some have renewed Anatomy (the knowledge of which had been entirely abolished); others have provided knowledge of the New World; others have illustrated philosophy and these abstruse offspring of nature, the knowledge of which (alas!) had been entrusted not to physicians or learned men, but to apothecaries and old women, to the great detriment of the sick. <margin>Authors of natural histories.</note> Among these, as if they were principal lights, I cannot pass over Ruellius, Pierre Belon, Jacques Dubois, Guillaume Rondelet, and Jacques Daléchamps among the French. Among the Germans: Valerius Cordus, Hieronymus Bock <note>Original: "Tragum"</note>, Joachim Camerarius the younger, the brothers Johann and Caspar Bauhin, Rembert Dodoens, Matthias de l'Obel, Carolus Clusius, and Melchior Wieland. Among the Italians: Julius Caesar Scaliger, Pietro Andrea Mattioli, Bartolomeo Maranta, Antonio Brassavola, Andrea Cesalpino, Andrea Bacci, Giambattista della Porta, Girolamo Mercuriale (the primary Professor of Medicine in the nourishing University of Pisa), Giovanni Coster of Lodi, Fabio Colonna, Massari of Venice, and Luca Ghini, formerly my teacher, who was the first to publicly profess the history of plants both at Bologna and Pisa; also Luigi Anguillara his disciple, and Giacomo Antonio Cortuso, both formerly Prefects of the Paduan garden. Among the Spaniards: Amatus Lusitanus, Andrés Laguna, Garcia de Orta, and Francisco Valles. Some of these most illustrious men contributed all their study, <margin>The author's inclination.</margin> all their effort, industry, and care to illustrating the causes, actions, powers, and forms of those things produced on the earth, others in the air, and others in the water. Indeed, from my "tender little nails" <note>A Latin idiom meaning "from earliest childhood."</note> I have deemed nothing more excellent than such noble exercise, nor have I thought there was almost any other thing that places God better and more illustriously before our eyes, showing His supreme power, exquisite goodness, and inexplicable wisdom. Therefore, as soon as I had tasted the humanities—though I had devoted myself entirely to Civil and Canon Law for seven continuous years and had made such progress that everyone encouraged me to receive the insignia of its highest degree—nevertheless, having tasted the first principles of philosophy (namely Logic), I was inflamed with such a love for philosophical study that, against the will of my friends and despite their sharp rebukes, I said farewell to Jurisprudence and preferred Philosophy to it. When I noticed that Philosophy consists of these particular things, I was greatly inflamed by a natural instinct to understand them; I directed every word, every deed, and every effort toward them as if toward the goal or target of the Highest Good, like a sailor directing his course toward a certain star, warned by the precept of the Stoics who judged that a certain fixed end ought to be proposed to every man. But when I recognized this talent given to me by that supreme Author of all good things—namely, the talent for searching out the abstruse and hidden secrets of nature (which is the duty of a true philosopher)—and that a life long enough to perceive and understand them had been supplied to me, I could not fail to show some small sign of a grateful soul for these benefits received from the Divine Majesty, lest I be ungrateful (for as St. Paul says, "neither he who plants nor he who waters is anything, but God who gives the increase"). I believe I shall partly achieve this if I leave to posterity those things which I have acquired throughout the whole course of my life with the greatest labors and highest expenses. Truly, since I have always been a most bitter enemy of idleness, and have spent all my time day and night understanding these hidden things (hardly taking account of my health, nor fearing the intense cold of harsh winter or the intolerable heat of parched summer, nor fleeing those very arduous labors), I have written many works—not only on natural things, but also other philosophical and medical works—which I believe are not useless or unnecessary, especially for philosophers, physicians, and other literate men. <margin>The author's works and the order of printing.</margin> Among these, it seemed to me that those which treat of animals should be published in the first place, because they ought to be preferred to the others in nobility. <margin>Four kinds of animals.</margin> Since the divine Plato makes four kinds of animals corresponding to the four elements of this world—namely, one terrestrial or living on land, called by him *pedestrian and land-dwelling* <note>Original Greek: πεζὸν καὶ χερσαῖον</note>; the second *aquatic* <note>Original Greek: ἔνυδρον</note>; the third *winged or air-faring* <note>Original Greek: πτηνὸν ἢ ἀερόπορον</note>; and the fourth and last *fiery* <note>Original Greek: τὸ πύρινον</note>—I thought it not ill-advised to begin with the winged kind, because it offers itself to me as first in that order (for I do not believe the "fiery" kind exists in the nature of things). <margin>What animals are contained under the name of winged creatures.</margin> But since under this genus there are also certain flying insects—some of which are called *un-sheathed* <note>Original Greek: ἀνέλυτρα</note> or having exposed wings, like the bee, and others *coleoptera* <note>Original Greek: κολεόπτερα</note>, that is, having wings hidden in a sheath, like the beetle—the order of dignity seems to require that I now treat of Birds, as they fly not with cartilage like those insects, but with feathers. This is truly a very arduous proposal, but the more difficult a thing is, the more worthy it is for a generous mind to test its strength; so that if it aspires to the desired end, it may seem to have achieved nothing common or vulgar, but something magnificent and honorable. If it cannot reach that end, let the distinguished effort, worthy of Hercules, nevertheless be praised. <margin>The reason why the author dealt with Birds before quadrupeds.</margin> But perhaps someone here might argue: why did I not treat of quadrupeds rather than birds? For quadrupeds are those which, because they serve the use of men as much as possible, provide labor, and supply daily nourishment, claim the first place in the genus of animals. <margin>The Author has written on general and special Ornithology.</margin>
...I shall betake myself to that field of plants and fossils. Since, therefore, I have composed a work on general history concerning birds—an outstanding and thoroughly philosophical labor, if I mistake not, to be committed to the press at another time if it please God—someone might say that I am inverting the order of nature if I now prefer this specific *Ornithology* <note>The study of birds</note> to that general one. To such a person I would answer that I am led to do so by various and most weighty reasons, which I now turn to enumerate. For when I weigh within myself the dignity, beauty, and utility of this particular history—which it offers to other sciences (as may be seen in its own chapter)—and the immense expenses which have nearly exhausted my coffers, how, I ask, could I best consult the interests of all scholars and my own affairs, if I did not by the best right prioritize this particular work? For this part provides an entry point to a better understanding of the universal whole. <margin>Why the Author begins in this work with an enumeration of the ancients.</margin>

<margin>An assessment of the Author's expenses.</margin>
Indeed, no one can estimate the magnitude of the expenses I have bestowed upon it, unless they have at some time undertaken a similar province. For I have found that Pliny, Avicenna, Averroes, Albertus Magnus, and several other approved writers have often recorded things based on the reports of others, passing them down to posterity as if they were true and proceeded "from the tripod" <note>An ancient Greek idiom meaning an oracular or infallible truth</note>—for example, the claim that a viper is killed by its young in this work, <margin>The Author [avoids] trifles in this work.</margin> or that the female bites off the male's head, or that the Beaver castrates itself, and similar trifles which have been held as true by many. Therefore, for those who desire to hand down to posterity a history of these sensible things created in this vast hemisphere, I deemed it absolutely necessary that they be seen with one's own eyes, handled with one's own hands, and finally, that one perform the dissections <term>anatomy: the structural study of the birds' bodies</term> oneself.

<margin>Comparison</margin>
For just as those who compose books on the republic and the method of governing it—unless they are skilled in governing a city through their own practice—often hallucinate gravely and lead the reader into vast labyrinths of error (as we once learned regarding Pittacus the lawgiver, who, transcribing many things from the reports of others, gained an ill reputation among men); so those who write about the same matter at their own peril <note>i.e., through personal risk and firsthand experience</note> tell the truth and are extolled with the greatest praise by all, as is evident with Solon and Lycurgus. <margin>Why Pittacus is ill-regarded among the learned.</margin> Wherefore, since nothing seemed to belong more to the duty of a Philosopher than to set aside all else and apply oneself assiduously to the truth—since truth is placed so deep, as if buried and hidden in darkness, so that nothing is quite so difficult as to track it down by investigation and to know it once investigated—it was not said rashly by Democritus that "truth lies in a deep well." We see also that these decrees of the ancient Philosophers agree with the decrees of Christians, who assert that in all of life there is nothing greater or better than to take up the protection of divine truth and to rest in its majesty. <margin>Solon's and Lycurgus's [praise] / Democritus's / saying about / truth.</margin>

<margin>The Author's diligence.</margin>
Hence, in almost every history of our birds, so that I might make known both the internal and external parts and be an eyewitness to them, I have consumed a vast sum of money. This was spent both in various pilgrimages undertaken to diverse regions of the world chiefly for the sake of birds (and other natural things), and in describing them, painting them in their own colors, and in preparing, sketching, carving, and finally printing them on pear-wood blocks. For this reason, I paid a yearly salary of two hundred gold pieces for more than thirty years to a unique painter in that art. I also engaged at my own expense the most celebrated draftsmen, Lorenzo Bennini of Florence and Cornelius Swint of Frankfurt; I also occasionally used the services of Jacopo Ligozzi, the excellent painter to the Most Serene Duke of Tuscany, in this same province in Florence, so that the birds might be depicted with the greatest possible skill. Finally, I had, and still have, the distinguished engraver Christopher Coriolanus of Nuremberg and his nephew, who have carved them so gracefully and elegantly that they seem made not in wood, but in copper. <margin>Draftsmen of the birds. / Artists / who bestowed / their labor in / perfecting this / History.</margin>

<margin>The Painter's fraud.</margin>
To these great expenses other reasons are added; among which this one especially must not be passed over in silence: that my painter, in order to make a greater profit for himself, sold many images to many nobles and scholars. I feared that they might eventually commit these to the press, as has happened to me at other times. For some people have published as their own many plants which they either learned about from me in this most celebrated Academy of Bologna (where I have taught publicly for forty-six years) or received as a gift; they anticipated me, who had intended to bring them to light at some point, so that they might "reap the harvest from my sowing."

Furthermore, I knew that among the Italians (I speak of those who wrote professionally) there was no one who had dealt with birds; among the French, only Pierre Belon, but in his own language; and among the Germans, only an *Ornithologist* <note>Likely referring to Conrad Gessner</note>, but presenting everything confusedly without any method—namely in alphabetical order—and providing images of only a few, and those common ones. To these things, finally, I add the hope of profit (which will assist me in publishing other works) and the prayers of many learned men, as well as the authority and encouragement of our most illustrious Senate. (To the Senate I both owe and wish to be obedient, on account of the many benefits conferred upon me and the help provided for this work, both of their own accord and by the command of Pope Sixtus V). Having seen these labors, they strongly urged me to publish what they recognized as pleasing, useful, and fruitful for the Republic of Letters. In the explanation of these things, I shall mix the useful with the pleasant, not unmindful of the Horatian rule, for he says: <margin>The generosity of the Bolognese Senate. / The benefits of Pope Sixtus V.</margin>

<margin>The utility of this work.</margin>
For those things which have utility joined with pleasure cheer the mind of the reader, are more easily committed to memory, and cling more tenaciously. For this volume contains many ethical, economic, political, and even military documents drawn from the monuments of ancient literature, which are extremely useful for informing and governing both private and public life and morals. You will also find many healthy and medical precepts for protecting and preserving health. Students of various languages will also find a variety of synonyms expressed in almost every tongue. Many other benefits may be seen here which I do not mention now, as I shall deal with them abundantly in their proper place. All these things are delivered in a very lucid and clear method (unless my mind deceives me). Those things which pertain to physical speculation—such as Equivocations, Synonyms, Genus, Differences, Location, Cognates, and Derivatives—are brought forward first. Then follow those things which usually provide pleasure to readers, such as Morals, Uses, Mysteries, Hieroglyphics, Histories, Symbols, Coins, Icons, Emblems, Fables, and Fables <note>original: "Apologi"</note>. <margin>The order to be observed in treating the work.</margin>
