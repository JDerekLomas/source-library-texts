# The Method of the Grass Drill

<note>The "Grass Drill" (Cǎojiào) was a traditional large-scale military exercise held in open fields or wilderness. By framing the drill as a hunt, commanders could train soldiers in complex maneuvers, signaling, and terrain navigation in a high-stakes environment.</note>

Each soldier is responsible for guarding three feet of the perimeter. With twelve generals, and each general commanding a separate body of one thousand men, there is a total force of twelve thousand soldiers. Together, they guard a perimeter of thirty-six thousand feet. When these feet are converted, they equal six thousand paces <note>In this period, one "pace" (bu) typically equaled six feet.</note>. This total distance amounts to fifteen *li* and an additional six hundred paces. The diameter across the center of the enclosure measures five *li* and thirty paces.

The "Left and Right Victory-Determining Generals" <note>original: "Juéshèng Jiāng" (决勝將)</note> serve as the leaders of the formation. Below them, the Left and Right Generals manage the individual soldiers and officers, organizing them into ranks and files. All movements are regulated by the sounds of gongs and drums and the visual signals of banners and flags.

When the encirclement first begins, the troops "spread their wings" <note>A flanking maneuver where the army extends its lines to wrap around a target.</note> to follow the natural contours of the mountains and forests. Regardless of whether the distance is far or near, the terrain is partitioned into specific sections to form the hunting enclosure. The Scout Officer <term>Yuhou: A high-ranking officer responsible for reconnaissance, logistics, and selecting the site for camp or drills</term> first selects and settles upon the location. Once decided, those skilled with the bow and arrow are designated as the "Enclosure Cavalry," while the infantry carry spears and flags to guard the perimeter line.

**Any soldier who allows animals to escape the enclosure will be punished along with the officers guarding that section. Large game captured shall be surrendered to the public <note>The "public" refers to the state or the high command.</note>, while small game may be kept as private property. This system is used to observe the soldiers' discipline in advancing and halting.**

In all training for formations: the **shorter soldiers** are placed in the front, and the **taller soldiers** are placed in the back. ## Part Two

When entering the formation, the troops shout "Guard!" (Hu). When the guards retreat, they shout "Who goes there?" (He). When the "Who" unit is rescued, they shout "Engage!" (Jiao). When the spear-banners are lowered, they shout "Slay!" (Sha). When the spear-banners are raised upright, they shout "Halt!" (Yu). When the units pivot, the left wing performs a "Sunward" turn <note>original: 陽轉 (yángzhuǎn), likely a clockwise or outward turn</note> and the right wing performs a "Shadow-ward" rotation <note>original: 陰旋 (yīnxuán), likely a counter-clockwise or inward turn</note>. A single drum beat signals an advance of one step. A single gong beat signals a retreat of one step. When the signal flags are crossed at a point, the soldiers shout "Acknowledged!" (Ruo). When the flags spin, they shout "Slay!" Those positioned under the flag-squads are strictly forbidden from speaking without orders.

At one blast of the horn and one beat of the drum, the squads are commanded to lower their spears <gloss>the spear-tips should be about five inches from the ground</gloss>. 

At one horn blast and two drum beats, the crossbowmen move to the front of the formation, advancing together three steps away from the main unit. They space themselves according to the formation's density. At one beat of the "standing drum," they nock their arrows; at the second beat, they release the arrows and shout "Slay!" After three volleys of arrows, they stop and retreat back into the main formation. 

At one horn blast and three drum beats, the archers deploy using the same method as the crossbowmen, shouting "Halt!" and "Slay!" three times each. 

At one horn blast and four drum beats, the long-sabers <term>Modao: a large, double-edged long-handled saber used during the Tang and Song dynasties to counter cavalry charges</term> and great-axes deploy. They listen for the drum to signal three "Halt!" shouts and three "Slay!" shouts. The sabers and axes are positioned alternately. Upon hearing the gong, they retreat. At a single drum beat, they shout "Halt!" and raise their spears to await further orders.

The 174 cavalrymen mentioned above are divided into three divisions, with each division organized into four ranks. Upon hearing the sound of the drum, the first division moves out. Each soldier fires three arrows. When finished, the front two ranks level their spears <note>original: 架鎗 (jiàqiāng); this involves bracing or positioning the spear for an active thrusting attack while mounted.</note> and give a great shout while thrusting three times. The rear two ranks also give a great shout, turn their horses, and fire two arrows backward <note>original: 背發 (bèifā); this refers to the famous "Parthian shot," where a mounted archer shoots behind him while retreating.</note> before returning to their original positions.

The second and third divisions then move out in sequence; the firing of arrows is generally the same. <gloss>However, when the second division moves out, after the front two ranks have leveled their spears and finished their thrusts, they do not fire backward; the third division then repeats the pattern of the first division.</gloss> In all cases, the three divisions go out for a second round of the drill, which continues until the drumming stops. <gloss>Those in the rear ranks of the first division who have already turned their horses and fired arrows do not fire during the second round; instead, those in the second division who have not yet fired will take their turn.</gloss>

# Drill Procedures for the Battle Array

The maneuver to the right covers one hundred and eighty-eight paces. When the **sound of the drums** begins, the **flag bearers** advance to the front to establish the boundary line of the engagement.

At the **first drum beat**, the troops <term>straighten their spears and sabers</term> <note>original: "zhi qiang-dao" — a command to bring weapons to a ready or vertical position</note>. At the **second drum beat**, the spear and saber units all crouch down. The archers and crossbowmen remain standing, waiting for the signal of the **striking wood** <note>original: "ji-mu" — wooden clappers used as a secondary signal to coordinate volleys</note>.

The archers and crossbowmen each fire three volleys of arrows. When the drum sounds again, the line advances a short distance. After another three volleys are completed, they wait for the next drum signal to move forward. The melee troops run forward, **trailing their spears and sabers** <note>Holding the weapons low and behind while running to maintain speed and readiness</note>, with the archers and crossbowmen following behind them. 

The entire unit halts upon reaching the boundary flags. Upon hearing the drum, the melee troops straighten their weapons; at the next drum beat, they crouch once more while the archers stand and fire another three volleys. 

When the drum sounds again, everyone stands and lowers the points of their spears. At the **sound of the gong** <note>original: "wen-jin" — in traditional Chinese military signals, the drum commands the advance and the gong (or "metal") commands the retreat</note>, the formation retreats to its original position and halts.

The closing sequence is as follows:
*   **First drum beat:** Straighten spears and sabers.
*   **Second drum beat:** Sit to <term>disperse the formation</term> <note>original: "jie-zu" — likely a command to loosen ranks or dismiss the specific tactical grouping</note>.
*   **Third drum beat:** Straighten spears and sabers once more.
*   **Fourth drum beat:** Banners and spears are held as they were originally.

The drumming then ceases.
