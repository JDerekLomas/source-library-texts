### THOMAS TAYLOR

> “Philosophy,” says <term>Hierocles</term> <note>Hierocles of Alexandria (5th century AD) was a Neoplatonist philosopher who wrote a famous commentary on the "Golden Verses of Pythagoras."</note>, “is the purification and perfection of human life. It is the purification, indeed, from material irrationality, and the mortal body; but the perfection, in consequence of being the resumption of our proper felicity, and a reascent to the divine likeness. To effect these two is the province of Virtue and Truth; the former exterminating the immoderation of the passions; and the latter introducing the divine form to those who are naturally adapted to its reception.”

Of philosophy thus defined, which may be compared to a luminous pyramid, terminating in Deity, and having for its basis the rational soul of man and its spontaneous unperverted conceptions,—of this philosophy, August, magnificent, and divine, Plato may be justly called the primary leader and <term>hierophant</term> <note>original: hierophantēs; a priest who reveals or interprets sacred mysteries.</note>, through whom, like the mystic light in the inmost recesses of some sacred temple, it first shone forth with occult <note>In this context, "occult" means hidden or secret, referring to knowledge reserved for those who have been properly initiated or prepared.</note> and venerable splendour.[1] It may indeed be truly said of the whole of this philosophy, that it is the greatest good which man can participate: for if it purifies us from the defilements of the passions and assimilates us to Divinity, it confers on us the proper felicity of our nature. Hence it is easy to collect its pre–eminence to all other philosophies; to show that where they oppose it, they are erroneous; that so far as they contain any thing scientific they are allied to it; and that at best they are but rivulets derived from this vast ocean of truth.

[1] In the mysteries a light of this kind shone forth from the <term>adytum</term> <note>The innermost sanctuary or "holy of holies" of a Greek temple, where only priests were permitted to enter.</note> of the temple in which they were exhibited.

To evince that the philosophy of Plato possesses this preeminence; that its dignity and sublimity are unrivaled; that it is the parent of all that ennobles man; and, that it is founded on principles, which neither time can obliterate, nor sophistry subvert, is the principal design of this Introduction.

To effect this design, I shall in the first place present the reader with the outlines of the principal dogmas of Plato's philosophy. The undertaking is indeed no less novel than arduous, since the author of it has to tread in paths which have been untrodden for upwards of a thousand years, and to bring to light truths which for that extended period have been concealed in Greek. Let not the reader, therefore, be surprised at the solitariness of the paths through which I shall attempt to conduct him, or at the novelty of the objects which will present themselves in the journey: for perhaps he may fortunately recollect that he has traveled the same road before, that the scenes were once familiar to him, and that the country through which he is passing is his native land <note>Taylor is referring to the Platonic doctrine of "anamnesis" or recollection: the idea that the soul lived in a divine realm before birth and that all true learning is actually a process of remembering those eternal truths.</note>. Let us also, imploring the assistance of the same illuminating power, begin the solitary journey.

Of all the dogmas <note>In this context, "dogmas" refers to established philosophical doctrines or core teachings rather than rigid religious beliefs.</note> of Plato, that concerning the first principle of things as far transcends in sublimity the doctrine of other philosophers of a different sect, on this subject, as this supreme cause of all transcends other causes. For, according to Plato, the highest God, whom in the **Republic** he calls the good, and in the **Parmenides** the one, is not only above soul and intellect, but is even superior to being itself. Hence, since every thing which can in any respect be known, or of which any thing can be asserted, must be connected with the universality of things, but the first cause is above all things, it is very properly said by Plato to be perfectly <term>ineffable</term> <note>From the Latin 'ineffabilis,' meaning something that cannot be expressed in words or described.</note>. The first hypothesis therefore of his, **Parmenides**, in which all things are denied of this immense principle, concludes as follows: “The one therefore is in no respect. So it seems. Hence it is not in such a manner as to be one, for thus it would be being, and participate of essence; but as it appears, the one neither is one, nor is, if it be proper to believe in reasoning of this kind. It appears so. But can any thing either belong to, or be affirmed of that, which is not? How can it? Neither therefore does any name belong to it, nor discourse, nor any science, nor sense, nor opinion. It does not appear that there can. Hence it can neither be named, nor spoken of, nor conceived by opinion, nor be known, nor perceived by any being. So it seems.” 

And here it must be observed that this conclusion respecting the highest principle of things, that he is perfectly ineffable and inconceivable, is the result of a most scientific series of negations, in which not only all sensible and intellectual beings are denied of him, but even natures the most transcendentally allied to him, his first and most divine progeny. For that which so eminently distinguishes the philosophy of Plato from others is this, that every part of it is stamped with the character of science. The vulgar indeed proclaim the Deity to be ineffable; but as they have no scientific knowledge that he is so, this is nothing more than a confused and indistinct perception of the most sublime of all truths, like that of a thing seen between sleeping and waking, like Phaeacia to Ulysses when sailing to his native land <note>A reference to Homer's *Odyssey*, where the island of Phaeacia appears to the weary Ulysses as a distant, mist-covered land.</note>,

> ->That lay before him indistinct and vast,<-
> ->Like a broad shield amid the watr’y waste.<-

In short, an unscientific perception of the ineffable nature of the Divinity resembles that of a man, who on surveying the heavens, should assert of the altitude of its highest part, that it surpasses that of the loftiest tree, and is therefore immeasurable. But to see this scientifically, is like a survey of this highest part of the heavens by the astronomer; for he by knowing the height of the media between us and it, knows also scientifically that it transcends in altitude not only the loftiest tree; but the summits of air and aether, the moon, and even the sun itself.

Let us therefore investigate what is the ascent to the ineffable, and after what manner it is accomplished, according to Plato, from the last of things, following the profound and most inquisitive <term>Damascius</term> <note>Damascius (c. 458 – after 538 AD) was the last head of the School of Athens and a key figure in late Neoplatonism.</note> as our leader in this arduous investigation. Let our discourse also be common to other principles, and to things proceeding from them to that which is last, and let us, beginning from that which is perfectly effable and known to sense, ascend to the ineffable, and establish in silence, as in a port, the <term>parturitions</term> <note>Literally "labors of childbirth"; here it refers to the difficult process of bringing forth or birthing truth through philosophical labor.</note> of truth concerning it. Let us then assume the following axiom, in which as in a secure vehicle we may safely pass from hence thither. I say, therefore, that the <term>unindigent</term> <note>Self-sufficient; lacking nothing. From the Latin 'indigere' (to need).</note> is naturally prior to the <term>indigent</term> <note>Lacking or needing something else to be complete.</note>. For that which is in want of another is naturally adapted from necessity to be subservient to that of which it is indigent. But if they are mutually in want of each other, each being indigent of the other in a different respect, neither of them will be the principle. For the unindigent is most adapted to that which is truly the principle. And if it is in want of any thing, according to this it will not be the principle. It is however necessary that the principles should be this very thing, the principle alone. The unindigent therefore pertains to this, nor must it by any means be acknowledged that there is any thing prior to it. This however, would be acknowledged if it had any connection with the indigent.

Let us then consider body—that is, a <term>triply extended substance</term> <note>A substance that has the three dimensions of length, breadth, and depth.</note>—which is endowed with quality; for this is the first thing we can describe and perceive with our senses. Is this, then, the ultimate principle of all things? No, for it is composed of two things: the body itself, and the quality that exists within that body as its subject. Which of these, therefore, is naturally prior? Both are <term>indigent</term> <note>From the Latin 'indigens,' meaning lacking, needy, or dependent on something else for its existence.</note> of their own parts; and that which exists within a subject is dependent upon that subject. Shall we say then that body itself is the principle of the first essence? That is impossible. For, in the first place, a true principle does not receive anything from that which comes after it. But body, as we have said, is the recipient of quality. Therefore quality (and the state of existing alongside it) is not derived from the body, since quality is present in the body as something distinct. Secondly, body is divisible in every way; its various parts depend on each other, and the whole depends on all its parts. Because it is dependent and is completed by things that are themselves dependent, it cannot be entirely self-sufficient.

Furthermore, if a body is not a single unit but merely a collection of parts joined together, it requires—as Plato says—a "connecting one" to hold it together. It is also something common and formless, acting as a kind of raw matter. It requires, therefore, "ornament" and the possession of a "form" so that it is not merely "body," but a body with a specific quality—such as a fiery body, or an earthly body; in short, a body adorned and clothed with a particular character. Thus, the things that join with it serve to finish and beautify it. Is that joining element, then, the primary principle? This is also impossible. For it does not stand on its own, nor does it exist in isolation, but lives within a subject on which it depends. 

However, if someone were to argue that "body" is not a subject but rather one of the basic elements within a thing—for example, the "animal" element found in both horses and men—then each would still be dependent on the other (the subject and that which is inside it). Or rather, the common element ("animal") and the specific traits (being "rational" like a human or "irrational" like a horse) would be dependent on one each other. Elements are always dependent on one another, and anything composed of elements is dependent on those elements. 

In short, this physical nature that is so obvious to us is neither "body" (for body by itself does not move the senses) nor "quality" (for quality does not possess a physical <term>interval</term> <note>In this context, "interval" refers to physical extension or spatial dimension.</note> that the senses can measure). Therefore, the thing we actually see is neither just body nor just color; rather, it is a "colored body," or "color made physical," that triggers our sight. Universally, the "sensible" object—which is a body with a specific quality—is what moves our senses. From this, it is clear that the thing which actually excites our senses is something incorporeal. For if it were merely "body," it would not yet be an object of sense. Body requires the incorporeal, and the incorporeal requires body. An incorporeal nature is not, by itself, perceivable by the senses. It is, however, different from body because these two have different powers, and neither exists before the other; rather, as elements of one physical thing, they are present together. The one gives physical extension to that which has no extension, while the other introduces a variety of shapes and forms to that which was formless. 

Thirdly, these two together are not the principles of reality, since they are not self-sufficient. They need their own elements and something to guide them toward the creation of a single form. Body cannot do this, as it is inherently powerless; nor can quality, since it cannot exist apart from the body. Therefore, the "composite" (the thing made of both) either produces itself—which is impossible, as it is scattered and divided—or it is produced by a higher principle that existed before it.

Let us then suppose this principle to be what we call **Nature**, which is the source of motion and rest in things, existing essentially within them rather than by accident. This is something simpler and is the "builder" of composite forms. However, if this "Nature" exists only within the things it builds and does not exist separately from them or prior to them, it is not truly self-sufficient—even though it has a superior power to shape and build them. It exists only alongside them and is inseparable from them; when they exist, it exists, and when they perish, it perishes. This is because it is completely "leaning towards" them and cannot maintain its own independent existence. For the power of increasing, nourishing, and generating similar things—and the principle prior to these three, which is **Nature**—is not entirely incorporeal. It is almost like a certain quality of the body, differing only because it allows the composite thing to be moved or stay at rest from within. The "quality" of a physical thing provides what is visible in matter, but **Nature** provides an internal, natural energy, whether in terms of movement through space, or...

Nature, however, is inseparable from a physical subject and is <term>indigent</term> <note>Lacking or dependent; not self-sufficient.</note>, so it cannot be the ultimate principle, since it depends on that which is subordinate to it. For it would not be strange if, as a secondary principle, it depended on a higher principle above it; but it would be very strange if it were dependent on things that come *after* it, and for which it is supposed to be the source.

By these same arguments, we can show that the first principle cannot be the **irrational soul**, whether it is the part that feels (sensitive) or the part that desires (<term>orectic</term> <note>From the Greek 'orektikos,' referring to the faculty of appetite, desire, or impulse.</note>). Even if it appears to have some degree of separation because of its power to move and its <term>gnostic energies</term> <note>Powers related to knowing or perceiving.</note>, it is nevertheless bound to the body and has something inseparable from it. It is unable to **convert itself to itself** <note>A Neoplatonic term for self-reflexivity or self-awareness; the ability of a consciousness to look inward at its own nature rather than being focused only on external objects.</note>; instead, its energy is mixed with the physical subject. It is evident that its essence is of this kind; for if it were liberated and free within itself, it would display an independent energy and would not always be directed toward the body. Sometimes it would turn toward itself, or even if it remained directed toward the body, it would at least judge and examine its own nature. 

The activities of the mass of mankind, though they are preoccupied with external things, still demonstrate that there is something "separate" within them. This is because they consult with themselves about how to act and realize that deliberation is necessary to achieve a perceived good or avoid an evil. In contrast, the impulses of other animals are uniform and spontaneous; they move in sync with their physical organs and require only their senses to seek pleasure and avoid pain from external objects. If the body shares in pleasure and pain and is affected by them, it is clear that these <term>psychical energies</term> <note>Activities belonging to the soul (psyche).</note> are exerted in a way that is mingled with the body. They are not purely "of the soul," but are also corporeal. Perception belongs to the animated body—or to the "soul made physical"—though in such perception the character of the soul still holds sway over the physical. Just as in physical bodies, the physical character dominates in terms of spatial extension and existence. Because the irrational soul has its being in something different from itself, it is dependent on that subordinate thing; therefore, such a thing cannot be the ultimate principle.

Prior to this essence, however, we see a certain form that is separate from a physical subject and is "converted to itself"—this is the **rational nature**. Our soul presides over its own energies and corrects itself. This would not be possible unless it could turn inward upon itself; and it could not turn inward unless it had an essence separate from the body. Thus, it is not dependent on what is subordinate to it. 

Shall we then say that the rational soul is the most perfect principle? No, because it does not exert all its powers at once, but is always lacking the greater part of them. The ultimate principle must want for nothing; but the rational nature is an essence that is in want of its own activities. 

Some might argue that the soul is an eternal essence with never-failing powers that always match its essence—being "self-moved" and "ever-living"—and is therefore not "indigent." However, the first principle must be *perfectly* self-sufficient. A soul that undergoes change in its activities cannot be the most proper principle. 

Therefore, it is necessary that there be something prior to this, which is in every respect immutable in its nature, life, and knowledge, and in all its powers. This is what we call an eternal and immutable essence: the highly honored **Intellect**. Aristotle, having ascended to this level, believed he had discovered the first principle. For what could be missing from that which perfectly contains within itself its own "fullnesses" or <term>pleromata</term> <note>From the Greek 'pleroma,' meaning a state of fullness or the sum total of spiritual powers.</note>, and which is not changed by anything being added or taken away? 

Yet, is this Intellect not also "one and many," a "whole and parts," containing within itself things that are first, middle, and last? The lower parts of this fullness stand in need of the higher, and the higher need the lower, and the whole needs its parts. Things that are related are dependent on each other. Even the "one" here is dependent on the "many," because it exists within the many. Or it could be said that this "one" collects the "many," but not by itself—only in conjunction with them. 

Therefore, even in this principle, there is much that is dependent. Since the Intellect generates its own "fullnesses" within itself to complete the whole, it is dependent on itself—not only is the generated part dependent on the generator, but the generator depends on the generated to achieve the completion of the whole. Furthermore, the Intellect both *understands* and *is understood*; it is both the thinker and the object of thought. Thus, the "thinker" (the intellectual) is dependent on the "object" (the intelligible) as the thing it desires to know; and the object is in want of the thinker because it wishes to be...

Shall we, therefore, next direct our attention to the most simple of beings, which Plato calls the **one being** <note>original: "ἓν ὄν" (*hen on*)</note>? For since there is no separation there throughout the Whole, nor any multitude, or order, or doubling, or **conversion to itself** <note>The ability of a consciousness to turn inward and be aware of itself; here, the author argues that the "one being" is so unified it doesn't even have the "two-ness" required for self-reflection.</note>, what dependency could appear to me in that which is perfectly united? Is it not, however, necessary here to attend to the conception of Plato: that the "united" is not the **One** itself, but that which is **passive** [2] to it? <note>In this sense, "passive" means "receiving" or "acted upon." Also, if being is composed from the elements of **limit** (the bound) and **infinity**, as appears from the *Philebus* of Plato—where he calls it "that which is mixed"—it will be dependent on its own elements. 

Besides, if the concept of "being" is different from the concept of "being united," and the "Whole" is both united and being, these parts will depend on each other, and the whole called the **one being** is dependent on the two. And though the **One** in this mix is better than "being," the **One** still needs "being" in order for the **one being** to exist. But if "being" here comes upon the **One** like a form—much like the specific quality of "man" comes upon the collection of "rational-mortal-animal"—then the **One** will be dependent on "being." 

If, however, to speak more properly, the **One** is two-fold: this "One" being the cause of the mixture and existing before being, while that "One" provides the rightness or structure to being—if this be the case, even this nature is not perfectly free from dependency. 

After all these, it may be said that the **One** itself will be perfectly self-sufficient. For it does not need that which comes after itself for its existence, since the **truly One** is by itself separated from all things. Nor does it need anything inferior or more excellent within itself, for there is nothing in it besides itself; nor is it in want of itself. But it is **One** because it has no "doubleness" within itself. For not even the relation of "itself to itself" must be asserted of the **truly One**, since it is perfectly simple. This, therefore, is the most self-sufficient of all things. 

Hence, this is the principle and the cause of all, and this is at once the first of all things. If these qualities, however, are present with it, will it truly be the **One**? Or may we not say that all things exist in the **One** according to the nature of the **One**? And that both these qualities exist in it, along with such other things as we say of it—for instance, that it is the most simple, the most excellent, the most powerful, the preserver of all things, and **the Good** itself? 

If these things are true of the **One**, it will also be "indigent" of the things that come after it, according to those very titles we give to it. For a "principle" is only called a principle because of the things that proceed from it; a "cause" is only a cause of the things it causes; and the "first" is only the first of the things arranged after it [3].

And if it should be said to be "all things" because it contains the seeds of all things within itself, it will be so according to the **One** alone. It will be the "one cause" of all things prior to all, and will be this and no other, according to the **One**. 

So far, therefore, as it is the **One** alone, it will be self-sufficient; but so far as it is self-sufficient, it will be the first principle and stable root of all principles. But by looking to our souls, fables originated as follows: While we are children, we live according to the <term>phantasy</term> <note>In ancient philosophy, "phantasy" refers to the faculty of the soul that processes mental images or imagination.</note>, but this "phantastic" part of us is preoccupied with figures, types, and things of this kind. So that this part of us may be preserved, we use fables because this part of the soul delights in them. It may also be said that a fable is nothing more than a false discourse shadowing forth the truth: for a fable is the **image of truth**. But the soul itself is the image of the natures that exist prior to herself; and hence the soul properly rejoices in fables, acting as an image that finds joy in another image. As we are nourished by fables from our childhood, it is necessary that they should be introduced. And so much for the first problem, concerning the origin of fables.

In the next place, let us consider what the difference is between the fables of philosophers and those of poets. Each has something in which it excels, and something in which it is lacking compared to the other. Thus, for instance, the poetic fable excels in this: we must not be satisfied with the literal meaning, but must pass on to the hidden truth. For who, endowed with intellect, would believe that **Jupiter** <note>The Roman name for Zeus.</note> was so desirous of having a connection with **Juno** <note>The Roman name for Hera.</note> that he would do so on the ground, without waiting to go into the bed-chamber? Thus, the poetic fable excels because it asserts such shocking things that it does not allow us to stop at the surface level, but leads us to explore the hidden truth. But it is defective in this: it deceives those who are young. Plato, therefore, rejects fables of this kind and banishes **Homer** from his *Republic*; because youth, upon hearing such stories, will not be able to distinguish what is <term>allegorical</term> <note>A story where characters and events represent deeper moral or spiritual meanings.</note> from what is not.

Philosophical fables, on the contrary, do not harm those who go no further than the literal meaning. Thus, for instance, they assert that there are punishments and rivers under the earth: and if we stick to the literal meaning of these, we will not be harmed. But they are lacking in this: because their literal meaning does not cause harm, we often content ourselves with it and do not seek the hidden truth. We may also say that philosophical fables look to the "enemies" of the soul. For if we were entirely intellect alone, and had no connection with imagination, we would not require fables, because we would always be associating with purely intellectual natures. The second species of <term>analysis</term> <note>In the Platonic sense, "analysis" is a method of inquiry that works backward from a conclusion to the principles or "hypotheses" that must be true for that conclusion to exist.</note> is as follows: It is necessary to make the thing investigated the subject of <term>hypothesis</term> <note>From the Greek 'hypothesis', literally meaning a "foundation" or a starting assumption used to build an argument.</note>; to survey such things as are prior to it; and to demonstrate these from things posterior, ascending to such as are prior, till we arrive at the first thing and to which we give our assent. But beginning from this, we descend <term>synthetically</term> <note>To descend "synthetically" means to start from a proven primary principle and logically deduce all the consequences that follow from it.</note> to the thing investigated. Of this species, the following is an example from the *Phaedrus* of Plato. It is inquired if the soul is immortal; and this being hypothetically admitted, it is inquired in the next place if it is always moved. This being demonstrated, the next inquiry is if that which is always moved, is self–moved; and this again being demonstrated, it is considered whether that which is self–moved is the principle of motion, and afterwards if the principle is <term>unbegotten</term> <note>Meaning something that was not "born" or created by a cause outside itself, but has always existed.</note>. This then being admitted as a thing acknowledged, and likewise that what is begotten is incorruptible, the demonstration of the thing proposed is thus collected. If there is a principle, it is unbegotten and incorruptible. That which is self–moved is the principle of motion. Soul is self–moved. Soul therefore (i.e. the rational soul) is incorruptible, unbegotten, and immortal.

Of the third species of analysis, which proceeds from the hypothetical to that which is <term>unhypothetical</term> <note>The "unhypothetical" refers to the ultimate Truth or the "First Principle" which does not depend on any other assumptions for its reality.</note>, Plato has given a most beautiful specimen in the first hypothesis of his *Parmenides*. For all things are posterior to the one; viz. things known, knowledge, and the instruments of knowledge. And thus, beginning from the hypothetical, he ends in that which is unhypothetical, and truly ineffable.

The free will of man, according to Plato, is a <term>rational elective power</term> <note>The faculty of choice, known in Greek as 'proairesis,' which allows the soul to deliberate and decide between different courses of action.</note>. It desires both <term>true and apparent good</term> <note>A core Platonic concept: 'True good' benefits the soul and aligns with reality, while 'apparent good' is something that seems desirable (like a harmful pleasure) but is actually detrimental.</note>, and it leads the soul toward both. Through this power, the soul either ascends toward the divine or descends toward the material; it either errs or acts with rectitude. This elective power is the very thing that characterizes our essence. It is because of this power that we differ from both divine natures and purely mortal (animal) natures. Neither of those possesses this two-fold inclination: the divine, because of its excellence, is fixed only in true good, while the mortal nature, because of its defect, is fixed only in apparent good.

Intellect characterizes the divine, but sense characterizes the mortal; as Plotinus says, the former is our king, and the latter is our messenger. We are established in this elective power as a middle ground. Having the ability to lean toward both true and apparent good, we follow the guidance of the intellect when we seek the former, and the guidance of the senses when we seek the latter. Therefore, the power within us is not capable of all things. A power that is truly omnipotent is characterized by unity; it is all-powerful precisely because it is one and possesses the form of the Good. However, our elective power is two-fold, and for this reason, it cannot achieve everything. Because it fluctuates between true and apparent good, it falls short of that divine nature which exists prior to all things. Our power would be all-powerful if it were not an elective impulse but were instead "will" alone. For a life lived according to will alone is a life lived according to the Good, because the will naturally tends toward the Good. Such a life makes our core nature most powerful and <term>deiform</term> <note>Meaning 'god-like' or sharing in the form and nature of the divine.</note>. According to Plato, it is through this that the soul becomes divine and, in another life, governs the world alongside the deity. These are the outlines of the primary doctrines of Plato’s philosophy.

In the beginning of this Introduction, I noted that in drawing these outlines I would lead the reader through novel and solitary paths. They must be solitary, as they have been largely abandoned from the reign of the <term>Emperor Justinian</term> <note>The Byzantine Emperor who, in 529 AD, closed the philosophical schools in Athens, effectively ending the public teaching of Neoplatonism for centuries.</note> until the present time. They will undoubtedly appear "novel" to readers of all kinds, especially to those who have been "nursed in the bosom of matter." These are the students of experiment and the darlings of the senses—the legitimate descendants of the <term>earth-born race</term> <note>A metaphorical reference to the Giants of Greek mythology who fought the Olympian gods; here it refers to materialists who recognize only the physical world.</note> that once warred against the Olympian gods. Such people have stared at the dark and deformed face of their "nurse" (the material world) for so long that they can no longer behold the light of truth. They have become so drowsy from drinking too much from the <term>cup of oblivion</term> <note>A reference to the river Lethe, which causes souls to forget their divine origins before being reborn.</note> that their entire life is nothing more than a transition from one sleep to another, or from dream to dream, like men moving from one bed to another. To people like this, the road we have been traveling will seem like a deceptive path, and the objects we have studied will seem like nothing more than fantastic visions—seen only by the imagination, and as empty as the "dreams of a shadow."

However, the following arguments may perhaps awaken a few who are less lethargic than the rest from this sleep of the senses. It may enable them to lift their mental eye out of the "dark mire" in which they are plunged and catch a glimpse of this most important truth: that there is another world, of which this one is merely an obscure resemblance, and another life, of which this one is but a fleeting mockery.

My current discourse is therefore addressed to those who consider "experiment" (empirical observation) to be the only solid criterion of truth. First, these men seem to be ignorant of the unchanging laws of <term>demonstration</term> <note>In classical logic, a demonstration (apodeixis) is a proof that shows why something must be true based on necessary and universal premises.</note>. Every proof consists of principles that are not only prior to others but are "first" principles. If a proposition can be proven by other assumptions, it might be "prior" to the conclusion, but it is not a "first" principle. On the other hand, truths that require no proof because they are self-evident are rightly called the first, the truest, and the best. The ancients called such indemonstrable truths <term>axioms</term> <note>From the Greek 'axioma,' meaning something worthy or self-evident; these are the foundation stones of logic.</note> because of their majesty and authority, as they provide all the force and power to the <term>syllogisms</term> <note>A form of reasoning in which a conclusion is drawn from two given or assumed premises.</note> that follow them.

Next, these men do not seem to realize that a <term>universal demonstration</term> <note>A proof that applies to an entire class of things rather than just one specific instance.</note> is better than a partial one. A demonstration is superior when it is derived from a better cause, and a universal cause is more far-reaching and excellent than a partial one. The difficult search for the "why" of any subject only stops when we reach universal truths. But here perhaps the experimentalist will say, admitting all this to be true, yet we no otherwise obtain a perception of these universals than by an <term>induction</term> <note>The logical process of drawing a general conclusion from a set of specific observations.</note> of particulars, and <term>abstraction</term> <note>The mental process of "pulling away" a general concept from physical objects (e.g., looking at many red apples to think of the color "red").</note> from sensibles. Thus, the proposition that "the angles of *every* triangle are equal to two right angles," if it is indubitably true—that is, if the term *every* in it really includes all triangles—cannot be the result of any abstraction; for abstraction, however extended it may be, is limited, and falls far short of universal comprehension. In short, if the words *all* and *every*, with which every page of theoretic mathematics is full, mean what they are conceived by all men to mean, and if the universals which they signify are the proper objects of science, such universals must subsist in the soul prior to the energies of sense. Hence it will follow that induction is no otherwise subservient to science, than as it produces credibility in axioms and petitions; and this by exciting the universal conception of these latent in the soul. The particulars, therefore, of which an induction is made in order to produce science, must be so simple, that they may be immediately apprehended, and that the universal may be predicated of them without hesitation. The most general division of the writings of Plato is into those of the **Sceptical** kind, and those of the **Dogmatical**. In the former sort, nothing is expressly either proved or asserted, some philosophical question only is considered and examined; and the reader is left to himself to draw such conclusions, and discover such truths as the philosopher means to insinuate. This is done, either in the way of inquiry, or in the way of controversy and dispute. In the way of controversy are carried on all such dialogues, as tend to eradicate false opinions; and that, either indirectly, by involving them in difficulties, and embarrassing the maintainers of them; or directly, by confuting them. In the way of inquiry proceed those whose tendency is to raise in the mind right opinions; and that either by exciting to the pursuit of some part of wisdom, and showing in what manner to investigate it; or by leading the way, and helping the mind forward in the search. And this is effected by a process through opposing arguments.[23]

# Introduction to the Philosophy and Writings of Plato

The dialogues of Plato, with respect to their subjects, may be divided into the **speculative**, the **practical**, and those of a **mixed nature**. The subjects of these last are either general (covering both types) or differential (distinguishing between them). The general subjects are either fundamental or final. 

The **fundamental** subjects include:
*   Philosophy
*   Human nature
*   The human soul

The **final** subjects include:
*   Love
*   Beauty
*   The Good

The **differential** <note>These are dialogues that examine the distinctions between different types of thought or action.</note> subjects regard knowledge as it relates to practice. These consider two main questions: first, whether virtue can be taught; and second, whether a failure of will is actually caused by a failure of judgment.

The subjects of the **speculative** <term>speculative<note>Philosophical inquiry aimed at understanding truth or theory for its own sake, rather than for immediate practical use.</note></term> dialogues relate to either words or things. Regarding words, they cover etymology, <term>sophistry<note>The use of clever but misleading arguments; Plato often wrote dialogues to expose this practice.</note></term>, rhetoric, and poetry. Regarding things, they cover science, true being, the principles of the mind, and the nature of the physical world.

The **practical** subjects relate to:
1.  **Private conduct**, or how the mind governs the person.
2.  **Duty toward others** in personal relationships.
3.  **Civil governance**, or the public conduct of a nation.

Under these three categories, Plato organizes specific practical topics: virtue in general, sanctity, temperance, courage, justice, friendship, patriotism, and piety. Finally, he addresses the leadership of a civil government, the structure of a state, law in general, and specific civil laws.

Thus, to give the reader a scientific <note>In this context, "scientific" means a systematic, comprehensive, and organized method of study.</note> and distinct view of Plato’s writings, we have attempted to show their natural distinctions. One can view them by their inward essence, their outward appearance, their subject matter, or their ultimate goal—or, as we have used in this <term>Synopsis<note>A brief summary or general survey of a subject.</note></term>, their genius, character, subject, and design.

It is important to note that just as the "highest good" benefits everything (though some things benefit more than others depending on their readiness), Plato’s dialogues are so full of this "sovereign good" that they benefit even those who cannot fully grasp their depth. They can calm a wild <term>sophist</term> like Thrasymachus <note>A character in Plato's *Republic* known for his aggressive argument that "might makes right."</note> in the *Republic*; they can humble those who are "ignorant of their own ignorance"; and they can help people become better citizens even if they never reach the heights of theoretical philosophy. Like a divine light, they purify and uplift anyone ready to receive them.

After this general overview, let us consider the **preambles** (introductions), the many **digressions**, and the **style** of his writing. 

Regarding the **preambles**, they might seem unnecessary at first, but closer study shows they are essential to the design of the dialogues. For example, the introduction to the *Timaeus* uses imagery in the <term>Pythagoric<note>Relating to the teachings of Pythagoras, who used mathematical and symbolic language to explain the universe.</note></term> tradition to explain the theory of the world. The first part of the *Parmenides* (the discussion of "ideas") is actually a preamble to the second part (the study of "The One"). 

As **Plutarch** <note>A famous Greek historian and essayist (c. 46 – 119 AD).</note> said when discussing Plato's writing on the Atlantic island:
> These preambles are superb gates and magnificent courts with which he purposely embellishes his great edifices, so that nothing may be wanting to their beauty, and that all may be equally splendid.

The scholar **Dacier** <note>André Dacier (1651–1722), a French classical scholar who translated Plato.</note> observes that Plato acts like a great prince who builds a palace and, as the poet **Pindar** suggests, "adorns the entrance with golden pillars." It is only right that the first thing a visitor sees should be magnificent, signaling the grandeur that lies ahead.

Regarding the frequent **digressions**, these also serve the main goal of the dialogue when examined closely. They provide the mind a rest from the labor of intense investigation. Plato, with enchanting art, leads the reader toward the "Temple of Truth" through the "delightful groves and valleys of the **Graces**" <note>In Greek mythology, the Graces are goddesses of charm, beauty, and creativity.</note>. This winding path is often the shortest way to the goal; for one must consider not just the straightest road in theory, but the road best suited for the traveler.

Regarding the style of Plato, although it is technically the least important part of his work—since the philosophical content should always come before the choice of words—he still competes for the top prize of excellence among the greatest masters of language. Because of this, his style was deeply admired by the best writers of ancient times. According to the historian Ammianus, if Jupiter <note>The Roman name for Zeus, king of the gods.</note> himself were to speak in the <term>Attic tongue<note>The dialect of Greek spoken in Athens during its golden age.</note></term>, he would speak exactly like Plato. Aristotle viewed Plato's style as a middle ground between poetry and prose. Cicero praised him equally for his beautiful language and his deep ideas, and Longinus, a famous critic, called him a rival to Homer. Longinus believed that Plato drew his strength from the same fountain of inspiration as Homer, comparing him to a new challenger entering the ring against a champion who is already universally admired.

Despite this praise, Plato has also been accused—as Longinus mentions—of being carried away by a <term>Bacchic fury<note>A state of inspired, ecstatic energy or wild enthusiasm, named after Bacchus (Dionysus), the god of wine and theater.</note></term> of words, leading him to use excessive or difficult metaphors and a grand, allegorical style. Longinus defends this by saying that anything naturally massive and grand is rarely "pure" or perfect in every small detail. He argues that someone who is always perfectly accurate is at risk of being "small" or trivial. He adds: "Is it not also likely that those with average or moderate talent, because they never take risks or reach for the heights of excellence, usually stay safe and error-free? # Introduction to the Philosophy and Writings of Plato

As I have already observed, whole systems come before their individual parts, and universal principles come before particular details. For this reason, I have placed those dialogues first that function as "wholes"—those that present a complete system—and afterwards those where these systems branch out into specific details. Thus, after the *First Alcibiades* (which was generally considered by ancient scholars as the proper introduction to all of Plato's philosophy), I have placed the *Republic* and the *Laws*. These two works systematically cover Plato's views on morality and politics. After these, I have ranked the *Timaeus*, which contains his entire <term>physiology<note>In this historical context, "physiology" refers to the study of the natural world and the physical universe (from the Greek "physis," meaning nature), rather than just the human body.</note></term>, along with the *Critias* because of its close connection to it. Next in order is the *Parmenides*, which contains his complete system of theology.

So far, this arrangement follows the natural way the human mind moves when acquiring the most sublime knowledge; the arrangement that follows focuses primarily on the hierarchy of things. After the *Parmenides*, the *Sophist* <note>Original: "Sophista"</note>, *Phaedrus*, *Greater Hippias*, and the *Banquet* <note>Commonly known today as the "Symposium."</note> follow. These can be seen as smaller "wholes" that are subordinate to and contained within the *Parmenides*, which, like the universe itself, is a "whole of wholes." For in the *Sophist*, "Being" itself is investigated; in the *Banquet*, "Love" itself; and in the *Phaedrus*, "Beauty" itself. All of these are "intelligible forms" <note>In Plato’s philosophy, these are the perfect, eternal templates or "Ideas" of things that exist beyond the physical world.</note> and are therefore contained within the *Parmenides*, where the full range of the intelligible world is revealed. The *Greater Hippias* is grouped with the *Phaedrus* because the *Phaedrus* discusses the entire concept of beauty, while the *Greater Hippias* focuses on how beauty exists within the soul.

After these comes the *Theaetetus*, which investigates "science" (or knowledge) as it exists in the soul; knowledge itself, in its highest form, was previously celebrated by Socrates in one part of the *Phaedrus*. The *Statesman* <note>Original: "Politicus"</note> and the *Minos*, which follow next, can be seen as branches growing out of the *Laws*. In short, all the dialogues that follow either examine more specifically the doctrines that were systematically covered in the ones already mentioned, or they flow naturally from them as their original source.

However, because it did not seem possible to arrange these dialogues that rank as "parts" in the same precise order as the "wholes," I thought it better to group them by common themes. For example, the *Phaedo*, *Apology*, and *Crito* all relate to the death of Socrates. The *Meno* and *Protagoras* both deal with the question of whether virtue can be taught. Others are grouped by their character, such as the *Lesser Hippias* and *Euthydemus*, which are <term>anatreptic<note>From the Greek "anatreptikos," meaning "overturning." These are dialogues meant to refute and dismantle the false arguments of others.</note></term> dialogues, or the *Theages*, *Laches*, and *Lysis*, which are <term>maieutic<note>From the Greek "maieutikos," meaning "midwifery." This refers to Socrates' famous method of "birthing" ideas and truth from his students through targeted questioning.</note></term> dialogues. The *Cratylus* is placed last, not simply because its subject is the origin of words (etymology), but because a large part of it deals with deep theology. By this arrangement—after ascending to the divine levels and their unspeakable source in the *Parmenides*, and then descending through the human soul in the subsequent dialogues—the reader is led back to the Divine in this final dialogue. This mimics the natural order of all beings: an unceasing return to the principles from which they first originated.

After the dialogues <note>[28] Since my goal is to provide the reader with a translation of only the genuine works of Plato, I have not translated the *Axiochus*, *Demodoeus*, *Sisyphus*, and so on <note>original: "&c."</note>, as these are clearly fake <note>original: "spurious"</note> dialogues.</note> come the *Letters* <note>original: "Epistles"</note> of Plato, which are in every way worthy of that prince of all true philosophers. They are not only written with great elegance and occasionally with magnificent language, but also with the dignity of a mind aware of its own superior gifts and the authority of a master teacher. They are also filled with many excellent political observations and contain some of his most difficult <note>original: "abstruse"</note> doctrines. Although these are presented in a mysterious <note>original: "enigmatically"</note> way, the style of delivery helps explain his theological dialogues, just as those dialogues help explain the letters.

**The Divine** <note>original: τὸ θεῖον</note>, is being subsisting in conjunction with the one. For all things, except the one, *namely* essence, life, and intellect, are considered by Plato as suspended from and secondary to the gods. For the gods do not subsist in, but prior to, these, which they also produce and connect, but are not characterized by these. In many places, however, Plato calls the participants of the gods by the names of the gods. For not only the Athenian Guest in the *Laws*, but also Socrates in the *Phaedrus*, calls a divine soul a god. “For,” says he, “all the horses and charioteers of the gods are good,” etc. And afterwards, still more clearly, he adds, “And this is the life of the gods.” And not only this, but he also denominates those nature gods that are always united to the gods, and which, in conjunction with them, give completion to one series. He also frequently calls daemons <note>In the Platonic sense, daemons are intermediary spirits between gods and men, not necessarily evil.</note> gods, though, according to essence, they are secondary to and subsist about the gods. For in the *Phaedrus*, *Timaeus*, and other dialogues, he extends the appellation of gods as far as the daemons. And what is still more paradoxical than all this, he does not refuse to call some men gods; as, for instance, the Elean Guest in the *Sophist* <note>original: Sophista</note>. From all this, therefore, we must infer that with respect to the word god, one thing which is thus denominated is simply deity; another is so according to union; a third, according to participation; a fourth, according to contact; and a fifth, according to similitude. Thus every superessential <note>That which exists beyond or above the level of Being or Essence.</note> nature is primarily a god; but every intellectual nature is so according to union. And again, every divine soul is a god according to participation; but divine daemons are gods according to contact with the gods; and the souls of men obtain this appellation through similitude. Each of these, however, except the first, is as we have said, rather divine than a god; for the Athenian Guest in the *Laws*, calls intellect itself divine. But that which is divine is secondary to the first deity, in the same manner as the united is to the one; that which is intellectual to intellect; and that which is animated to soul. Indeed, things more uniform and simple always precede, and the series of beings ends in the one itself.

**Doxastic**. This word is derived from *opinion* <note>original: doxa</note>, and signifies that which is apprehended by opinion, or that power which is the extremity of the rational soul. This power knows the universal in particulars, as that every man is a rational animal; but it knows not the *why* <note>original: dioti</note>, or why a thing is, but only the *that* <note>original: oti</note>, or that it is.

**The Eternal** <note>original: τὸ αἰώνιον</note>, that which has a never–ending subsistence, without any connection with time; or, as Plotinus profoundly defines it, infinite life at once total and full.

**That which is generated** <note>original: τὸ γεννητόν</note>. That which has not the whole of its essence or energy subsisting at once without temporal dispersion.

**Generation** <note>original: γένεσις</note>. An essence composite and multiform, and conjoined with time. This is the proper signification of the word; but it is used symbolically by Plato, and also by theologists more ancient than Plato, for the sake of indication. For as Proclus beautifully observes (in his manuscript *Commentary on the Parmenides*), “Fables call the ineffable unfolding into light through causes, generation.” “Hence,” he adds in the Orphic writings, the first cause is denominated time; for where there is generation, according to its proper signification, there also is time.”

**A Guest** <note>original: Ξένος/Xenos</note>. This word, in its more ample signification in the Greek, denotes a stranger, but properly implies one who receives another, or is himself received at an entertainment. In the following dialogues, therefore, wherever one of the speakers is introduced as a *Guest*, I have translated this word guest, as being more conformable to the genius of Plato's dialogues, which may be justly called rich mental banquets, and consequently the speakers in them may be considered as so many guests. Hence in the *Timaeus*, the persons of that dialogue are expressly spoken of as guests.

**Hyparxis** <note>original: ὕπαρξις</note>. The first principle or foundation, as it were, of the essence of a thing. Hence also, it is the summit of essence.

**Idiom** [<term>Characteristic Property <note>original: Ιδιωμα (Idioma)</note></term>]. The characteristic peculiarity of a thing.

**The Immortal** [<term>The Deathless <note>original: Το αθανατον (To athanaton)</note></term>]. According to Plato, there are many levels of immortality, reaching from the highest heights down to the lowliest things. The final echo of this immortality is seen in the everlasting nature of the physical universe; according to the teachings of the Elean Guest <note>The "Elean Guest" is the main speaker in Plato’s dialogue titled "The Statesman" (Politicus).</note> in the *Statesman*, the universe participates in this through the Father of the universe. This is because the existence and life of every physical body depend on a separate cause, as matter is not naturally able to organize, beautify, or preserve itself. However, the immortality of individual souls, like our own, is more obvious and perfect than that of the physical bodies in the universe. This is proven through many arguments in the *Phaedo* and the tenth book of the *Republic*. The immortality of these individual souls is more fundamental because they contain the cause of eternal existence within themselves. Yet, even higher than these is the immortality of "daemons" <note>In Plato's philosophy, "daemons" are not evil spirits, but intermediary beings or "guides" that exist between the human and divine realms.</note>; for these never lean toward death, nor are they filled with the nature of things that are born and decay. Even more venerable and superior to these is the immortality of divine souls. These souls are "primarily self-moving" and contain the sources and principles of life that are directed toward physical bodies, allowing those bodies to participate in a renewed sort of immortality. Finally, the highest level of all is the immortality of the gods. In the *Symposium* <note>original: "the Banquet"</note>, the character Diotima does not even ascribe the same kind of immortality to daemons that she does to the gods. Therefore, the immortality of the gods is entirely separate and stands above all else. Alongside the immortality of the gods sits eternity itself, which is the fountain of all life and immortality—both the life that lasts forever and the life that eventually dissolves into nothingness. In short, the "divine immortal" is that which generates and holds together perpetual life. It is not immortal simply because it "shares" in life, but because it provides divine life and makes life itself divine.

**Imparticipable** [<term>The Unshared <note>original: Το αμεθεκτον (To amethekton)</note></term>]. That which does not exist in a shared state with a lower nature. For example, "unshared intellect" is a type of mind that does not exist within a soul.

**Intellectual Projection** [<term>Mental Intuition <note>original: νοερα επιβολη (noera epibole)</note></term>]. Because the perception of the intellect is immediate—like an arrow darting directly toward its target—this direct insight is called "projection."

**The Intelligible** [<term>The Object of Thought <note>original: Το νοητον (To noeton)</note></term>]. In the writings of Plato and his followers, this word has several meanings. First, anything that is free from the physical senses and exists separately from them is called "intelligible"; in this sense, the soul is intelligible. Second, the Intellect itself, which exists before the soul, is intelligible. Third, that which is even more ancient than the Intellect—which fills the mind and perfects it—is called intelligible. This is the level that Timaeus (in Plato's dialogue of the same name) calls a "paradigm" <note>A "paradigm" is an eternal blueprint or model used by the creator of the world.</note>, existing before the creative intellect and its activity. But beyond even these is the "divine intelligible," which is defined by divine union and *hyparxis* <note>Hyparxis refers to the very peak or "flower" of existence; the most fundamental reality of a thing.</note>. This is the ultimate object of desire for the mind, containing it and completing all of being. Therefore:
1. The **highest intelligible** is the fundamental existence (*hyparxis*) of the gods.
2. The **second** is true being and the first essence.
3. The **third** is the Intellect and all intellectual life.
4. The **fourth** is the order belonging to the soul.

**Reasoning** [<term>Logismos <note>original: Λογισμος</note></term>]. When applied to divinity, as Plato does in the *Timaeus*, this signifies a cause that distributes and organizes things.

**On account of which; with reference to which; through which; according to which; from which; or in which.** [<term>The Six Causes <note>original: δι ο̈, υφ' ου, δι ου, καθ' ο̈, ἐξ ου (di ho, hyph' hou, di hou, kath' ho, ex hou)</note></term>]. 
- By the first ("on account of which"), Plato refers to the **final cause** (the purpose). 
- By the second ("with reference to which"), the **paradigmatic cause** (the model).
- By the third ("through which"), the **demiurgic cause** (the creator/maker).
- By the fourth ("according to which"), the **instrumental cause** (the tool).
- By the fifth ("from which"), **form**.
- By the sixth ("in which"), **matter**.

**Appetitive** [<term>Orectic <note>original: ορεξις (orexis)</note></term>]. This word is derived from the word for appetite or desire.

**Paradigm** [<term>Pattern <note>original: παραδειγμα (paradeigma)</note></term>]. A pattern, or that model which is referred to when a thing is being made.

**The Perpetual** [<term>The Everlasting <note>original: το αϊδιον (to aidion)</note></term>]. That which exists forever, but does so through a connection with time.

**Prudence** [<note>original Greek: Φρόνησις (Phronesis)</note>]. In the works of Plato and his followers, this word frequently means the habit of discerning what is good in all moral actions; it also often signifies intelligence or intellectual perception. The following excellent explanation of this word is given by Jamblichus <note>Jamblichus (c. 245–325 AD) was a Syrian Neoplatonist philosopher who influenced how later scholars interpreted Plato’s mysticism.</note>: 
> Prudence, having a prior existence, is born from a pure and perfect intellect. Because of this, it looks toward the intellect itself, is made perfect by it, and treats the intellect as the measure and most beautiful model for all its activities. If we have any communion with the gods, it is achieved especially through this virtue; through prudence, we are made most like them. The knowledge of things that are good, profitable, and beautiful—and their opposites—is obtained through this virtue. The judgment and correction of the work we must do are also directed by it. In short, it is a governing leader of men and of the entire arrangement of their nature. By referring cities, households, and the private life of every person to a divine model, it shapes them into the best possible likeness, wiping away some flaws and purifying others. Thus, prudence makes those who possess it similar to divinity. (Jamblichus, quoted in Stobaeus, p. 141.)

**Psychical** [<note>original Greek: ψυχικός (psychikos)</note>]. Pertaining to the soul.

**Science** <note>In this context, "science" refers to *Episteme*, or certain, foundational knowledge, rather than the modern experimental sciences.</note>. This word is sometimes defined by Plato as that which identifies the causes of things. At other times, he defines it as a subject whose essence is perfectly stable, combined with the ability to explain causes through reasoning. Again, he sometimes defines it as a field whose principles are not mere assumptions (hypotheses); according to this definition, he asserts there is one "science" that climbs all the way to the very first principle of things. This science considers the true first principle to be "unhypothetic" (not requiring further proof), deals with "true being," and produces its arguments based on cause. According to the second definition, he calls <term>dianoetic</term> <note>Knowledge gained through discursive reasoning, such as mathematics or logic.</note> knowledge "science"; but according to the first definition alone, he gives the name of "science" to <term>physiology</term> <note>The study of nature and the physical world (*physis*).</note>.

The **telestic art** [<term>The art of mystic initiation <note>original: τελεστική (telestike)</note></term>]. The art or practice pertaining to mystic ceremonies and sacred rituals.

**Theurgic** [<term>Divine-working <note>original: Θεουργία (theourgia)</note></term>]. This word is derived from "Theurgy," referring to a religious operation or ritual intended to unite the practitioner with the divine as much as is humanly possible.

**Truth** [<note>original Greek: ἀλήθεια (aletheia)</note>]. Plato, following the ancient theologians, views truth in many layers. According to his teaching, the **highest truth** is defined by unity; it is the light shining from "the Good," which grants purity (as he says in the *Philebus*) and union (as he says in the *Republic*) to things that can be understood by the mind. The **second kind of truth** is that which flows from those same understandable things and illuminates the intellectual orders; this is first received by an essence that has no shape, color, or physical touch—this is where the "plain of truth" is located, as described in the *Phaedrus*. The **third kind of truth** is that which is born within souls; through intelligence, this truth comes into contact with true reality. For the "psychical" light (the light of the soul) is the third level down: the intellectual light is filled by the highest light, and the soul’s light is filled by the intellectual. The **last kind of truth** is that which is full of error and inaccuracy because of the physical senses and the changing nature of the objects it studies. For material nature is constantly flowing and is not naturally able to stay the same for even a moment.
