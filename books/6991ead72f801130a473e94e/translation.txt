### On the percussion of water and air

The water that strikes in the middle of the surface of an immobile board <note>a fixed obstacle that does not move when hit</note> divides into 2 parts, and these 2 parts divide in equal proportions, flowing toward the opposite edges of said board.

---

<margin>
on the circular motion
</margin>

When the water has reached the edges of the board, it can no longer maintain its straight course, and thus it curves back upon itself, creating two eddies <note>term: revertigini (vortices). Leonardo identifies these as the primary way energy is dissipated after an impact</note>. These eddies turn in contrary directions, yet they maintain a perfect circularity if the strike was exactly in the center.

---

<margin>
oblique impact
</margin>

If the board is placed at an angle <note>original: "obliquo"</note>, the water will not divide into equal parts; rather, the greater part will flow toward the lower side of the slant, and the eddies formed there will be larger and more powerful than those on the higher side. This occurs because the water finds a more direct path along the slope than it does in resisting the upward turn.

---

<margin>
the nature of air
</margin>

The same is seen in the air, though it is invisible to the eye. If you observe the smoke of a fire as it strikes a flat ceiling, you will see it divide and curl back in the same manner as the water. This proves that air and water are governed by the same laws of percussion and reflection, differing only in their weight and density.

---

<margin>
resistance
</margin>

The resistance of the board is the cause of the water's reflection. If the board were to move at the same speed as the water, there would be no percussion and, consequently, no division of the flow and no eddies would be generated. Thus, percussion is born from the opposition of two powers <note>the force of the moving fluid versus the stability of the solid object</note>.

# ON THE MOVEMENT OF WATER

Observe the movement of the surface of the water, how it resembles the behavior of hair, which has two motions: one depends on the weight of the strands, the other on the direction of its curls; thus the water has its swirling eddies, one part of which is due to the force of the principal current, while the other is due to the incidental motion and return flow. <note>Leonardo frequently used "analogy" as a scientific tool, comparing the macrocosm of nature (water) to the microcosm of the human body (hair).</note>

<margin>The curls of the hair should be depicted like waves of water.</margin>

### OF THE SHADOWS IN FOLDS

The shadows found within the folds of garments <insert>and in the tresses of hair</insert> are always darker than the shadows on the outer surfaces. <note>This refers to "chiaroscuro," Leonardo's technique of using strong contrasts between light and dark to achieve a sense of volume.</note>

### OF THE REPRESENTATION OF HAIR

The hair should be treated with great lightness, showing the individual strands as they twist and turn in the air, yet they must be grouped in such a way that they form a unified mass of light and shadow. <gloss>The artist must avoid "wire-like" lines.</gloss>

If the surface of a ball were made of crystal and were full of water and were placed in the middle of the air, anyone looking into that ball would see in it the things that are outside, but they would see them by a straight line, because they do not bend in passing from a rare medium into a dense medium except when they strike obliquely upon the surface of the dense body; but if by <term>perpendicular</term> <note>original: "perpendiculari" - lines that strike a surface at a 90-degree angle</note> lines they strike such a surface, they do not bend at all, and thus it is seen in looking through the center of a ball of crystal or glass full of water, which ball is of equal thickness throughout. But if the glass were thicker in one place than in another, then the eye would not see the things outside except by bent lines, and they would bend all the more as the surfaces of the glass were more oblique to one another; and this same thing happens to the air when it is of different density between the eye and the object, as is seen in the air that trembles above furnaces and above fields heated by the sun. And if you wished to see if the air is of different density in the same place, look at the air through a small hole in a ball of copper or wood, and you will see the air that seems to tremble; and this happens because the air heats up in certain places more than in others, and those places, because they are rarer due to their heat, and the other places which are colder are denser; and the eye that looks through air of different density sees the things outside through twisted lines, and because such air divides itself into various parts, the eye sees the things outside variously.

---

Because every brief light, sending its species <note>original: "spetie" - a Renaissance term for the visual image or "likeness" of an object traveling through the air</note> through a small hole into a dark dwelling, spreads it by the opposition of the places outside—that is, if the light is in the east it will come to strike the western wall, and if it is in the south it will come to strike the northern wall; therefore, the objects having come through the opening from outside between the eye and the visual faculty, they cross each other in a brief space of conjunction; therefore it is necessary <unclear>that</unclear> one species passes through the other.

---

If the species of the objects came by the <term>pyramidal</term> <note>original: "piramidale" - referring to the cone-shaped path light takes as it converges toward the eye</note> line to the eye, one object-bearer would occupy the other because, coming by straight lines, they would clash together; but they come by infinite ways in such a mode that every object is everywhere and all in every part, therefore each passes through the other without hindrance.

---

Regarding the visual faculty: as the eye, through its pupil, sends its species at the same time to see a multitude of objects, it is not possible that these species do not intersect one another: and if they intersect, they must impede one another.

Here the answer is that the visual faculty is a single line, which comes through the center of the pupil to the middle of the <term>crystalline lens</term> <note>original: "cristallina" - the lens of the eye, which Leonardo believed was the primary organ of sight</note>, and that which comes by such a line is seen well and distinctly; all the other objects that are seen around this line are seen by oblique lines and are not seen well. And this happens because the eye has only a single <unclear>point</unclear> of visual faculty, and this point is in the middle of the pupil; and if the pupil saw through many points, it would see one object on top of another.

The eye has in itself a single visual line, which is that which ends upon the object that the man looks at, and that is the <term>central line</term> <note>original: "centralis" - the primary axis of vision</note>. All the other objects that are seen at the same time are seen by other lines which go between the <term>central line</term> and the circumference of the pupil.

#### The Experiment of the Needle

If you take a needle and place it between the eye and a small hole made in a sheet of paper, and you look through that hole at a light, you will see the needle <insert>appearing to be</insert> behind the hole, and it will seem to be larger than it is, and it will be seen upside down. # . On the proportion of light and shadow

Because the eye does not see things unless they are illuminated and unless they send their <term>images<note>original: "spezie". In Leonardo's time, this referred to the "species" or formal likenesses of objects that were believed to travel through the air to the eye.</note></term> to said eye, I say that the air is filled with infinite images of the bodies that are within it. These images are in every part and the whole is in every part; and if the air were luminous in every part, it would be of one and the same brightness. But because the images of luminous bodies and illuminated bodies are in every part of that air, these images encounter one another without obstructing each other.

From every point of luminous bodies, the angle of a pyramid is caused which ends in the eye; and if the eye is closer to the luminous body, the angle will be more <term>obtuse<note>A wider angle, occurring when the object is near.</note></term>, and if it is more remote, it will be more <term>acute<note>A sharper, narrower angle, occurring when the object is far away.</note></term>.

The air is filled with the likenesses of bodies that are mirrored within it, and therefore I say that if a body is of great length and great width, its images will be in every part of the air; and therefore every part of the air sees the whole body and the whole body sees every part of the air.

If the air were luminous in every part, it would not allow any shadow to be seen; but because the lights are in certain locations in the air, bodies therefore cast shadows.
