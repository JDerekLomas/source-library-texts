## Selecting Leaves

The silkworm takes the mulberry as its food. People from the region of <term>Huzhou<note>Located in modern-day Zhejiang province, Huzhou was historically the premier center for high-quality silk production in China, famous for its "Hu Silk."</note></term> do not refer to the plant as "mulberry" but simply call it "the leaf." Every place of trade is called a "leaf market," the brokers are "leaf agents," and the warehouse owners are "leaf masters." Even the smallest tools are named after "the leaf." This local custom of prioritizing the leaf is done entirely out of reverence for the silkworm. 

However, there are leaves that are suitable for silkworms to eat and those that are forbidden. If one does not distinguish the silkworm’s nature and feeds them a mixture of types, the silkworms will inevitably fall ill. Those who manage silkworms must inspect their stock at all times and must not harbor a negligent attitude, which would bring harm to the silkworms.

## Wet Leaves

Silkworms have an aversion to moisture; you must not feed them wet leaves. There are several types of wetness: rain, dew, and fog. A note in the *Essential Compendium of Agriculture and Sericulture* <note>original: "農桑輯要" (*Nongsan Jiyao*), an influential agricultural manual compiled in 1273 during the Yuan Dynasty to standardize farming techniques.</note> states: Leaves carrying rain or dew are both wet and cold. If eaten, the silkworm's body will turn a brownish color and it will suffer from "water-diarrhea." This is particularly harmful as the silkworm approaches maturity <note>The text refers to the final stage before the silkworm spins its cocoon.</note>.

On the ground, spread a layer of reeds <note>original: "蘆薍" (*luwan*); various types of aquatic grasses or rushes used to create a clean, breathable floor surface.</note>. Take the leaves and spread them out loosely in rows; do not pile them high or stack them thickly. This is called "arranging the leaves" <note>original: "綜葉" (*zongye*); a method of organizing the harvest to ensure air circulation.</note>. The purpose of arranging the leaves is to prevent them from fermenting <note>The original text uses "steaming," describing the natural heat generated by a dense pile of green leaves which can lead to rot.</note>. If they do not ferment, no heat will be generated to create condensation. Generally, if leaves have surface moisture, they should be dried in a breeze, and they will soon be ready.

### Contaminated Leaves

There are several types of contaminated leaves. Bird droppings and the slime of slugs <note>original: "蛞蝓" (*kuoyu*); common garden slugs whose mucus trails were thought to be toxic to silkworms.</note> are particularly harmful; if silkworms eat these, they will bloat and die. Therefore, any leaves stained with various types of excrement must be urgently inspected and discarded.

### Muddy Leaves

If mulberry leaves are stained with mud, they will irritate the silkworms' mouths <note>Literally "hinder the mouth."</note> and they will refuse to eat. Leaves from low-hanging branches near the ground, or those that have fallen and become soiled with mud, must be washed clean with fresh water. Furthermore, during the silkworm months, if a northwest wind blows, dust will fill the air. The people of the Lake region <note>Referring to the Huzhou region, famous for its high-quality silk production.</note> call this "Yellow Sand Sky." This dust is fine and clings stubbornly to the leaves, covering both the front and back. If silkworms eat this, they will fail to grow. These leaves must be rinsed <note>original: "淘汰" (*taotai*); specifically meaning to wash or sift in water to remove impurities.</note> repeatedly and then air-dried before feeding.

### Foul-smelling Leaves

If mulberry leaves are allowed to ferment <note>The original term is "steaming" (*fazheng*), referring to the natural heat and moisture buildup in a dense pile of green matter.</note> for a long duration, they will turn black and develop an odor. *The Essentials of Agriculture and Sericulture* <note>A major agricultural treatise compiled during the Yuan Dynasty.</note> states: "Leaves that are damp and foul-smelling will cause various diseases; silkworms must never be allowed to eat them." 

Generally, when harvesting leaves from distant locations and transporting them by boat, the leaves are most prone to becoming overheated and fermented. Halfway through the journey, one must select a cool, shaded spot to take the leaves out, spread them loosely to air, and then reload them onto the boat. This process is called "releasing the heat" <gloss>The character "heat/baking" (*hong*) is formed from the phonetic components *hu* and *gong* <note>This is a traditional Chinese *fanqie* pronunciation guide.</note></gloss>. This term implies that the fermented leaves are as hot as if they were being baked; they must be brought out to cool, otherwise they will turn black and foul.

### Hot Leaves

If leaves are harvested and bundled during the midday sun, they will be hot. If the leaves are piled up and allowed to ferment, they will also become hot. Silkworms have an aversion to eating such leaves. In either case, the leaves should be spread out and aired on cool ground to dissipate the trapped, sweltering heat.

### Wilted Leaves

<margin>*The Essentials of Agriculture and Sericulture* says: "Leaves withered and dried by the wind and sun cause abdominal blockages <note>A condition where the silkworm's digestive tract becomes obstructed, often fatal.</note>."</margin>

Silkworms should be fed fresh leaves. If leaves are kept overnight, their vitality and nutrients <note>Literally "fat" (*zhi*).</note> dry up. If they are withered by the wind, their juices will be parched. Silkworms will not eat these, and they should be discarded.

# Rich Leaves

If mulberry trees are continuously fertilized with manure, the leaves become "fat" <note>Meaning lush, thick, and overly rich in nutrients.</note>. If silkworms eat these, they will immediately become "Sturdy Greens" <gloss>The silkworm's body becomes thick and strong, the color turns a deep, dark green, and it fails to enter its "sleep" or molting phase.</gloss> Therefore, if manure has just been applied to the mulberry field, the leaves must not be harvested. After seven days have passed, there is no longer a restriction.

# Sleeping and Waking

Among the insects born from eggs, silkworms have a fixed rhythm of activity and rest. Their rest occurs when they "close their mouths" and stop eating; this is called "sleeping" <note>The dormant state just before a silkworm sheds its skin.</note>. Their activity occurs when they "shed their skin" and seek food; this is called "waking." This cycle is the essential pivot of their growth and development. Among egg-born creatures, the silkworm is considered a "divine insect." Although its sleeping and waking arise from its natural instincts, they must be regulated by human effort to ensure the timing is not inconsistent. 

*The Silkworm Classic* <note>An ancient Chinese manual on sericulture, likely referring to the *Canjing*.</note> says: "If the sleeping and waking are inconsistent, the silk yield will be halved." Generally, the transition between sleeping and waking takes about one full day and night. During this time, the environment must be quiet and undisturbed for the silkworms to be properly situated. If the timing is inconsistent—meaning some are early and some are late—then the early ones will stop eating while the late ones are still seeking food. If one feeds them at this time, it causes chaos; the movement and agitation will cause those trying to sleep to lose their rest. By the time the late ones have finally fallen asleep, the early ones have already "woken up" and are seeking food. Because the late ones are just beginning their sleep, if one cuts off the food supply and does not provide more, by the time...

### The First Sleep
<note>This refers to the first of four molting periods in a silkworm's life cycle.</note>

From the time the <gloss>silkworms</gloss> are first hatched until their first sleep, if fire is used <note>To provide artificial warmth.</note>, it takes approximately three days; if no fire is used, it takes about seven or eight days. Whenever the silkworms are about to sleep, one should sweep away the <term>waste trays</term><note>original: "shabu" (沙篰); bamboo trays filled with "sand" or silkworm droppings and leaf remnants.</note> to clear away impurities and foulness. Silkworms raised with fire enter their sleep rapidly and uniformly; as soon as you see delicate, reddish silk threads appearing, you can clear the trays. For silkworms raised in cooler conditions, the onset of sleep is slightly staggered. One must wait until about twenty percent of them have begun to <term>anchor</term><note>original: "mian" (緜); the process of spinning a fine silk web to secure themselves to the tray.</note> before deciding to stop feeding. It is absolutely vital not to stop too early. If you stop too early, many are still eating; if you continue to feed them in large quantities, the <term>waste trays</term> will become too thick, and the silkworms will inevitably suffer from <term>suffocating heat</term><note>original: "yuzheng" (鬱蒸); a condition caused by the fermentation of moist leaves and waste, creating stagnant, humid heat.</note>. Conversely, you must not be too late. If you are late, they will sleep amidst the waste in the trays, making it impossible to inspect or move them properly.

The regulation of this process depends entirely on perfect timing. Whenever a silkworm is ready to sleep, it first spits out silk to grip the tray and anchor its feet; this is called <term>forming the web</term>. Once they begin <term>webbing</term>, you must stop both the fire and the feeding. <gloss>If you do not stop the fire, you will harm the silkworms; if you do not stop the feeding, those who have already finished webbing will crawl back up to eat again.</gloss>

## The Second Sleep

The silkworms' second sleep occurs more rapidly than the first sleep, generally within four to five days [of the first]. The various methods for removing waste, sifting through the trays, and halting the feeding of leaves remain the same as those used for the first sleep.

### Ceasing the Fire

When silkworms reach the stage of <term>Ceasing the Fire</term><note>original: "chuo huo" (輟火); a critical management phase where the heat and feeding are stopped to allow the larger larvae to enter their molt.</note>, the procedure differs from that of the younger silkworms. Separating the sticky masses of silkworms is a process that requires a great deal of time. If the "sleep-heads" <note>silkworms entering their dormant molting state</note> remain in these clumps for too long, they are highly susceptible to damage from dampness and heat. Furthermore, the awakening that follows the "Ceasing Fire" stage happens much faster than earlier stages <gloss>A local proverb says: "The 'Ceasing Fire' hits from a distance," which means the awakening occurs with great speed</gloss>. It often happens that before the workers have finished gathering and sorting the brood, some silkworms have already begun to wake. Therefore, one should wait until a majority have entered their sleep and then complete the sorting quickly. At most, provide only one or two more feedings before they go dormant. Because the "sleep sand" <note>the layer of waste, including droppings and leaf scraps</note> is thin at this stage, the inspection and gathering are easier, and there is less risk of the silkworms being harmed by trapped heat. # The Great Sleep

The management of the **Great Sleep** <note>the fourth and final molt before spinning cocoons</note> follows the same principles as the "Ceasing Fire" stage, including the weighing of the brood. Every one tael <note>a traditional unit of weight, approx. 37 grams</note> of silkworms at the "Ceasing Fire" stage should ideally grow to weigh four taels by the time they reach the Great Sleep. If they exceed four taels, the brood is considered "long" <note>thriving and robust</note>; if they weigh less than four taels, they are "diminished." This ratio is used as a constant measure to predict whether the final harvest will be bountiful or poor. When distributing the "sleep-heads" in baskets, the spacing should be moderate: use three units of area for the "Ceasing Fire" stage and five units for the "Great Sleep." Inside the baskets, scatter Cornelian cherry <note>original: "zhu yu" (茱萸); a medicinal plant used here to ward off pests or dampness</note> or inch-long pieces of cut rice straw, then cover them with lime and speckled bran.

The "sleep" <note>the dormant period when a silkworm molts its skin</note> of silkworms values uniformity; it is best when they enter sleep together and wake up together. If the feeding of mulberry leaves is uneven, those that have eaten their fill will sleep early, while those who have not had enough will sleep late. Within a single basket, those beginning their sleep <term>sleep-heads (眠頭); silkworms just starting the molting process</term> and those still eating become a chaotic and crowded mix. If you continue to feed them, the active "neighbors" will grow larger and risk damaging the dormant sleep-heads. If you stop feeding them, the active ones will lack food and become even less able to enter their sleep. In such cases, you must quickly remove the active eaters from the surface to feed them elsewhere, while placing the sleep-heads from the bottom into a separate container; this method is called **"Separating the Old Bottoms."** Although the silkworms gathered from the surface cannot be compared to "green silkworms" <note>original: "qing can" (青蠶); healthy, fully active silkworms</note>, their final harvest will never be as abundant as that of the "Old Bottoms" <note>the silkworms that molted on schedule at the bottom of the tray</note>.

## The Molting and Awakening of Second-Stage Silkworms

The nature of **second-stage silkworms** is vastly different from that of the first stage. In the first stage, the times for molting and waking are clearly divided into morning and evening. For the second stage, if they do not enter sleep during the evening hours, any that show signs of sleep during the day will generally be able to sleep uniformly by dusk. However, if signs of sleep appear during the *Shen* hour <note>the period from 3:00 PM to 5:00 PM</note>, but by late evening only half have entered their sleep, the remainder will not sleep until noon the following day. These two groups must be separated and stored in different containers. If every molting period were as irregular as this, the work of separating them would be endless. Hence the local proverb: *"Second-stage silkworms involve eighteen divisions"* <note>a metaphor for the many different groups or "batches" one must manage due to uneven growth</note>. The nature of the third and fourth stages is much the same.

# Testing the Sleep-Heads and the Awakening

When assessing the **sleep-heads** <note>silkworms just entering their dormant molting stage</note>, a body that is firm and solid, appearing dark green when held up to the light, indicates they are free of disease. If they are limp, stretched out, and reddish-yellow in color, then they are ill. When gathering the sleep-heads, if the mulberry leaves are sparse and dry and the <term>sand-mats (沙茵); the bedding layer consisting of silkworm excrement and leaf remnants</term> are firm and clumped together, they are healthy. If the leaves are sparse but damp and the waste stains the hands, they are diseased.

A popular folk method involves taking a bowl of clear water and selecting ten sleep-heads. After thoroughly cleaning away the **foot-binding silk** <note>original: "chan jiao si" (纒脚絲); the silk the worm spins to anchor itself to a surface before molting</note> from them, drop them into the water. If all ten sink, it signifies a perfect harvest; if nine sink, it is a ninety-percent harvest, and so on in descending order. Although this is not necessarily an absolute rule, it can be kept as a useful method of trial.

As for the **awakening** <note>original: "qi niang" (起孃); the process of waking and rising after the molt is complete</note>, those that have just molted and resemble a horse with its head held high and its tail spread, with a color like "mutton-fat jade" <note>a high-quality, creamy white jade</note>, are healthy. If the head droops, the tail is tucked, and the body is yellow like the color of "old storehouse rice," the common folk call this "wearing a yellow grass-cloth shirt." Once these are given leaves to eat, green fluid will immediately leak from their tails.

For **fire-raised silkworms** <note>silkworms raised in rooms where charcoal fires are used to maintain warmth</note>, one must place a sheet of fresh white paper in the basket. If the silkworms wake and rise together on the paper without leaving a single speck of filth or water marks, they are in good condition. If there are marks, it is a sign of "heat injury" <note>damage caused by excessive or uneven heat from the warming fires</note>, and it is acceptable to discard them.

Silkworms grow with remarkable speed; with every feeding, they grow larger. Spreading is also not fixed; it may be done once at midday or once at dusk, depending on whether the weather is cold or warm. The *Wuben Xinshu* <note>original: "New Book of Essential Agricultural Labors," a classic agricultural treatise from the 14th century</note> states: "When lifting silkworms, one needs many hands and speed; when spreading silkworms, one must use a light touch." These two words—**Light** and **Fast**—are the finest methods for thinning and spreading. If one is reckless or slow, the silkworms will suffer harm. Those who rear them must be cautious.

### Leaving Space for Thinning

When placing silkworms in the baskets, do not allow them to become too crowded. This is especially critical for young silkworms, as they have little strength and cannot withstand being squeezed together. If they are placed so that their heads cluster and tails overlap, the stronger ones will compete for food, while the weaker ones will inevitably be pushed down into the refuse and suffer from hunger. Therefore, when spreading the silkworms, one must not crowd them against the edges of the basket. A margin of two or three inches <note>approximately 6 to 10 centimeters</note> should be left around the four sides to serve as space for the silkworms to spread out during feeding.

## Merging and Turning the Baskets

"Merging the baskets" <note>original: "He mian" (合緜)</note> refers to the technique of placing two baskets face-to-face to flip and transfer the silkworms. This method is appropriate for the period from when the silkworms are newly hatched until their second <term>molt; the process where a silkworm sheds its skin to grow</term>. Because young silkworms have weak bodies, they cannot endure being manually handled or "stripped" from their bedding. 

Once a layer of <term>speckled chaff; rice or grain husks used as a protective bed</term> has been used to create a partition, take a sheet of new paper and spread it over the surface of the silkworms <gloss>the paper must be larger than the basket</gloss>. Place an empty basket on top. Two people should then hold the two baskets together, pressing them tightly, and flip them over so the top and bottom are reversed. Then, lift off the original basket. Following the partition made by the speckled chaff, roll away the <term>frass; the accumulated waste of silkworm droppings and leaf scraps</term>. Immediately sprinkle a new layer of speckled chaff and cover it with the basket. The two people then flip it back upright as before.

One must take advantage of the momentum to rotate them smoothly; they must not be jolted, or the <term>silkworm tray; the woven bamboo frame of the basket</term> may be damaged. Then, remove the empty basket and the paper, and spread the silkworms out to ensure they are neither too crowded nor too sparse. <gloss>There is also a method where two people stretch thick mulberry-bark paper tight and use it to cover the worms.</gloss> Spread a cloth over the silkworms. 

For <term>fire silkworms; silkworms raised early in the season using supplemental heat</term>, the merging method is also used, but without using speckled chaff as a divider. Instead, use threads to separate the layers. Use twenty or thirty threads, increasing or decreasing the number based on the quantity of silkworms. Tie one end of the threads into a single knot and spread them evenly among the silkworms. When it is time to flip and rotate them, simply lift the knot of the threads to remove the frass. Speed is the most essential factor in this process.

## Net-Based Transferring

Once the silkworms have passed their **second molt**, their bodies have grown significantly, and they are each housed in large baskets. At this stage, transferring the bedding <note>original: "fen mian" (分緜); the process of cleaning waste and providing fresh space</note> becomes difficult to manage, and manually stripping away the waste by hand is far too slow. Using nets to transfer the bedding is much more labor-saving and efficient. 

The net must be spread flat over the surface of the silkworms. Mulberry leaves are then fed directly onto the net; the silkworms will all crawl up through the mesh of the net to reach the food. After two or three feedings, the mesh of the net will be completely covered by the veins of the leftover leaves, which prevents the silkworms from falling back through the holes. Two people then lift the four corners of the net and move it into an empty basket. <gloss>The corners of the net must not hang outside the basket, for fear that they might obstruct entry and exit or become snagged on things.</gloss> For the next cleaning and transfer, another net is spread in the same way; thus, each basket requires the alternating use of two nets.

# Placing on the Ground

Silkworms are generally best kept in baskets. The people of **Hangzhou** <note>A major center of silk production in Zhejiang province.</note> raise their silkworms in baskets from the time they are newly hatched until they are fully grown. However, after the **Great Sleep** <note>The final molt before spinning cocoons, when the silkworms reach their maximum size and appetite.</note>, the silkworms are large and the baskets are numerous. When these are arranged in rows and divided among frames, carrying them for feeding and removing the waste becomes extremely difficult. Unless one has a large workforce, it is inevitable that everyone will struggle with the exhausting labor.

According to the customs of **Huzhou** <note>Another vital silk-producing region, known for different traditional techniques than Hangzhou.</note>: small silkworms are kept in baskets, but immediately after the Great Sleep, they are removed from the baskets and placed directly on the floor. This is called <term>Xiadi (下地); literally "placing on the ground," a method of floor-rearing silkworms to save labor.</term>. 

Some move them to the ground a full day after the first feeding following the Great Sleep; others move them after only three feedings. By estimating the number of silkworms and the size of the room, the floor space is partitioned and bedding is spread out. The areas around doorways, wells, and stoves are filled so completely that there is barely any space left for people to move. From this point on, there is no more need to carry baskets or clear away waste. One only needs to feed them diligently morning and evening while waiting for them to mature. This is a very simple and convenient method.

# Spreading the Silkworms

For silkworms reared on the floor, the primary rule is to keep them spread thinly and evenly. If they are too crowded, the mature silkworms will huddle together, and their collective body heat becomes intense. When silkworms are subjected to this heat, it prematurely forces them into maturity <note>The text suggests that excessive heat accelerates their biological clock, causing them to stop eating and start spinning before they have gained enough mass.</note>. Because they have not consumed enough mulberry leaves, the resulting cocoons will be thin and the silk yield will be reduced. 

Therefore, when first placing them on the floor, do not fill the entire room. One must leave about a foot of space around the perimeter—above, below, left, and right—to provide room for the silkworms to expand. Since the silkworm’s body will double in size from the <term>Great Sleep (大眠); the fourth and final molt of the silkworm larva</term> until it reaches full maturity, this extra space allows the farmer to gradually spread them from the center toward the edges each day. Furthermore, it is in the nature of silkworms to like climbing upward; thus, the upper half of the room should be spread especially thinly, as the silkworms will naturally migrate there to fill the space evenly.

## Insulating Against Dampness

From the time the silkworms are first placed on the floor until they reach maturity is a period of about six or seven days. Over time, the <term>ji (穊); the thick accumulation of silkworm droppings and leftover mulberry leaf stems</term> becomes quite deep. If the weather becomes humid and sultry, a "damp-heat" vapor will begin to rise from within this layer of waste. 

One should take three or four <term>dou (斗); a traditional dry measure, roughly equivalent to 10 liters</term> of finely chopped dry cogon grass and sprinkle it evenly over the surface of the silkworms. Then, feed them with fresh leaves. The silkworms will all climb upward to reach the food, leaving the chopped grass beneath them to serve as a barrier against the rising dampness.

As silkworms age, they are categorized into "raw" and "ripe" stages based on their color. Those with a greenish tint are considered **raw**, while those with a reddish, translucent hue are **ripe** <note>At this stage, the silkworm's body becomes slightly see-through because its silk glands are full of liquid silk</note>. This often leads to the defect of **"sharing a palace"** <note>original: *tong gong* (同宫); when two silkworms spin a single double-cocoon, which produces tangled silk that is difficult to reel</note> and the discharge of fluid that stains and ruins the quality of the cocoons.

### Mounting Silkworms Should Avoid Crowding

When placing silkworms onto the "mountain" racks <note>original: *shan peng* (山棚); the trellises or frames where cocoons are spun</note>, the density must be properly balanced. If they are packed too tightly, the harm caused is the same as that of overripe silkworms. Generally, in a room measuring fourteen feet <note>The OCR reads "four feet," but given the rack size, this likely refers to "one zhang and four feet," a standard room width</note> with a rack twelve or thirteen feet wide, the space can only accommodate **one basket of silkworms**. It cannot hold more than this.

### The Racks Should Avoid Bright Light

When mature silkworms see bright light, they tend to wander restlessly without settling. This prevents them from immediately **wrapping their bodies** <note>original: *guo shen* (裹身); the act of spinning the initial silk envelope around themselves</note>. Therefore, the mounting area should be kept dark. Once all the silkworms have been moved onto the racks, all windows and doors should be covered to block out the light.

When the silkworm emits silk from its mouth, the fibers will shrink if they encounter a draft. ## Heating the Racks

Just as young silkworms require warmth, older silkworms require it just as much. Warmth makes it easier for them to form their cocoons. Therefore, once the mounting process is complete, one must use charcoal fires to heat the racks. Furthermore, mature silkworms discharge a great deal of fluid; the cocoons will become damp, and without the heat of a fire, they will not dry. Fire basins should be used in the room, with their number increased or decreased based on the size of the space. The basins should be placed approximately two feet <note>about 60-70 centimeters</note> away from the racks; they must not be placed too high, nor too low. A bucket of water should be stored under the racks as a precaution against the risk of fire. In the art of silkworm cocooning, heating the racks <note>original: *zhuó shān* (灼山), literally "scorching the mountain"</note> is the most critical step. If this is neglected, even if the harvest appears abundant, the cocoons will be sickly and the resulting silk will lack substance and weight.

### Heat the Silkworm, Not the Cocoon

Fire on the mountain frames should be used while the silkworms have not yet enclosed themselves in their silk; it is not suitable to apply intense heat once the cocoons are already formed.

Therefore, when the silkworms are first exposing their bodies to begin spinning, the fire should be strong. Once they are enclosed within the silk, the heat must not be too intense; maintaining a constant warmth on the screens should be the standard.

# Containing the Heat

Beneath the mountain frames <note>original: *shānpéng* (山棚); the tiered structures where silkworms spin cocoons</note>, one must not allow the heat of the fire to dissipate or wander in all directions. Use screens and mats to shield the area, ensuring that the warm air is forced to rise upward. When the screens and the straw bundles are thoroughly warmed, the silkworms will immediately begin to "spread the blossoms" <note>original: *tān huā* (攤花); the process of spinning the initial, loose supportive silk layers</note> and wrap their bodies within the silk.

---

## Equalizing the Intensity of the Fire

When using fire for the mountain frames, the absolute priority is uniformity. If the heat is allowed to fluctuate—suddenly burning hot or suddenly dying out—the resulting cocoons will be difficult to reel. This will inevitably lead to "thread-bunching" <gloss>the upward gathering of threads is called *shāntóu* (參頭)</gloss> and "jumping threads," which cause a hundred different defects <gloss>this refers to cocoons bobbing and jumping unevenly during the silk-reeling process</gloss>. A strong fire should be kept burning for one full day and night. After a full twenty-four-hour cycle, the amount of charcoal should be reduced. After two days, the fire may be extinguished.

---

## Observing the Weather Conditions

The fire on the mountain must be adjusted by carefully judging the weather. If the weather is cold, the fire should be strong; if it is hot, the fire should be slight; if it is dry, the fire should be slow. If one encounters damp and muggy weather, the fire should remain strong even if the temperature is already high <note>In traditional sericulture, heat is used to drive out humidity, which prevents the silk from becoming damp and difficult to unwind</note>. One must estimate the needs of the moment through intuition; it is difficult to adhere to a single rigid rule.

## Harvesting Cocoons from the Mountain Frames

## Harvesting the Cocoons

When using "mountain sheds" <note>wooden or bamboo structures where silkworms spin their cocoons</note> equipped with decorative bamboo lattices, one must first remove the reed mats. This allows the dead silkworms, their waste <note>original: *shā* (沙), literally "silkworm sand," used in traditional medicine</note>, and any fungi to fall to the ground. Only then should the harvesting of the cocoons begin. For those who do not use bamboo lattices, the harvest may be conducted directly from the mats. The most important principle in harvesting cocoons is to value cleanliness over speed. Only when the mature silkworms have reached a "ten-parts" <note>fully complete</note> harvest will the cocoons be free of defects; otherwise, many diseases of the cocoon will appear.

The <term>Essential Skills for Scholars and Farmers</term> <note>original: *Shì nóng bì yòng* (士農必用); a classic agricultural manual originally compiled in the 14th century</note> states that there are six types of cocoon defects that occur within the spinning frames:
1.  **Frame-stained:** Stained by waste.
2.  **Fallen-frame:** Cocoons that drop before completion.
3.  **Wanderers:** Silkworms that crawl around without spinning properly.
4.  **Red Pupae:** Prematurely darkened pupae.
5.  **Calcified:** Silkworms hardened by white muscardine disease.
6.  **Blackened:** Silkworms that have rotted inside.

When harvesting, defects such as frame stains, fallen cocoons, wanderers, red pupae, and calcified worms do not present a major obstacle. However, the **blackened** cocoons—where the silkworm has died and turned into a "black smear" <note>original: *wū liàn* (烏練)</note>—must be handled with extreme care. If the harvester is reckless or hasty, these cocoons will surely burst, and the decaying liquid will stain the clean, healthy cocoons. One must dismantle the frames slowly and pick these out individually. In this way, the spread of contamination is avoided, and the remaining cocoons will stay lustrous, pure, and flawless.

## Selecting the Seed

There are many different varieties of **silkworm seed** <term>original: *cán zhǒng* (蠶種); the eggs of the silkworm moth</term>. Their places of origin are also diverse. The **"Red Mortar"** variety <note>original: *dān chǔ zhǒng* (丹杵種); likely named for the reddish hue of the eggs or a specific regional brand</note> comes from places like **Nanxun** and **Hubin**. The **"White-skinned"** variety, the **"Three-molt"** variety <note>silkworms that spin cocoons after only three molts instead of the standard four</note>, and the **"Mud"** variety come from **Qianjin**, **Xinshi**, and surrounding areas. **Yuhang** also produces the White-skinned variety as well as the **"Small Stone"** and various hybrid breeds. 

In every village, there are people who make a living by selling these seeds; the profits from this trade are even greater than those from selling silk. When the season for the moths to emerge arrives, the local farmers compete to go to various places to pre-order their eggs, a practice called **"fixing the seed"** <term>original: *dìng zhǒng* (定種)</term>. Each sheet of egg-paper, if small, is worth a thousand copper coins; larger sheets can cost fourteen or fifteen hundred coins. 

There are also those who purchase various types of eggs and travel to different villages and towns to hawk them. These are commonly called **"street-peddled seeds"** <term>original: *dū lù zhǒng* (闍路種)</term>, and their price is very cheap. However, these seed-selling families are only interested in profit. They frequently use cocoons that are unfit for silk production to produce these eggs. They do not set aside healthy, superior silkworms specifically for breeding. 

While the success or failure of a silk harvest depends on how the worms are raised and fed, it is also essential that the seed itself be of high quality so that the worms do not easily fall ill. To use a human analogy: if one’s **"congenital energy"** <term>original: *xiān tiān zhī qì* (先天之氣); the innate constitution or vitality one is born with</term> is sufficient, then sudden exposure to wind, cold, heat, or dampness cannot easily cause harm. Therefore, families who raise silkworms should ideally set aside their own silkworms to produce eggs for the next generation. One must not buy seed carelessly, for doing so often leads to later regrets.

### Selecting the Silkworms

To reserve stock for breeding, one must first select the silkworms. Only when the breeding silkworms are free of disease will the resulting eggs be healthy. Therefore, after the **"Big Sleep"** <term>original: *dà mián* (大眠); the fourth and final molting period before the silkworm spins its cocoon</term>, one should pick out silkworms that are uniform, robust, and healthy. Feed them daily with **"Swallow-head" leaves** <note>original: *yàn tóu yè* (燕頭葉); the tender, vigorous leaves found at the very tips of mulberry branches, believed to be the most nutrient-rich</note>. These leaves possess great vitality; when the silkworms eat them, their own strength becomes vigorous.

One must be even more diligent and cautious in all tasks, such as cleaning the trays and moving the worms. Once they reach maturity, they should be placed on separate racks to spin their cocoons. Maintain warmth using a small, gentle fire to ensure they can successfully complete their cocoons.

### One-Morning Birth

Moths emerge from their cocoons over a period of approximately three days. The first day is called **Breaking the Chest** <term>original: *pò yì* (破臆); the initial stage of emergence where only a few early moths break through the cocoon</term>, and the number of moths produced is small. The second day is known as the **Peak Period** <term>original: *wàng cháo* (旺朝)</term>, and the third day is the **Final Period** <term>original: *mò cháo* (末朝)</term>. 

When selecting moths to produce eggs, each sheet of paper must be completely filled during the single day of the **Peak Period**. If the eggs are laid on the same day, then the silkworms will all hatch at the same time the following year. If the eggs are laid gradually over two or three days, they will not hatch simultaneously. Therefore, the moths that emerge on the very first day and those that emerge last are not suitable for use.

### Barren Eggs

Moths emerge from their cocoons in the morning and begin laying eggs by the evening. Eggs laid without the moth having mated are called **Barren Eggs** <term>original: *dàn zǐ* (淡子); literally "pale" or "tasteless" eggs</term>. The moths that emerge in a single morning may not necessarily be an equal split of male and female. If there are more males than females, the extras can be kept to mate with females the following day. However, if there are more females than males, those without a mate will nonetheless produce barren eggs by evening. These barren eggs do not undergo the typical color changes of fertilized eggs; over time, they simply wither and deflate, and they will never hatch into silkworms.

Cocoons are categorized into those suitable for reeling into fine silk, those that cannot be reeled but are used for silk floss, and those unsuitable for either that are relegated to coarse wadding. Their physical qualities are distinct, and their uses are entirely different.

For cocoons intended for reeling silk, size and shape—whether round or elongated—are of little concern. Instead, density, thickness, and luster are considered paramount. A dense and thick cocoon yields a greater weight of silk, while a lustrous, clean cocoon ensures a vibrant color. There are five types of cocoons used for **Silk Floss** <term>original: *mián* (綿); high-quality silk padding used for garments</term>:

1.  **Double Cocoons** <term>original: *tóng gōng* (同宮); literally "sharing the same palace"</term>. These occur when multiple worms spin together. When one tries to draw a single thread, the various strands become entangled, resulting in the "sickness" of coarse knots and uneven thickness.
2.  **Stained Cocoons** <term>original: *niào zhū* (尿猪); literally "pig-urine"</term>. If the salty waste of the silkworm touches the cocoon, it immediately mildews and cannot be drawn out or reeled.
3.  **Indented Cocoons** <term>original: *cǎo āo* (草凹)</term>. These bear the physical impression of the straw frames. At the site of the indentation, the silk threads are fused together; whether the spot is constricted or flat, the fibers cannot be separated into distinct, clear strands.
4.  **Pierced-End Cocoons** <term>original: *chuān tóu* (穿頭)</term>. The threads at the end are tangled and fuzzy. When one attempts to pull the lead thread, the entire cocoon often jumps up, which is enough to snap the delicate silk line.
5.  **Maggot-Bored Cocoons** <term>original: *qū zuān* (蛆鑽)</term>. These are cocoons where parasites have bored holes to escape. When placed in the reeling water, they immediately fill and sink, leaving no way to grasp or pull the silk ends.

All of the above are unsuitable for fine silk and are instead used as material for silk floss.

There are three types of cocoons used only for **Wadding** <term>original: *xù* (絮); the lowest grade of silk used for bulky, coarse stuffing</term>:

1.  **Sunken-Red Cocoons** <term>original: *āo chì* (凹赤)</term>. These are soft, weak, and paper-thin; they cannot withstand the processing required for quality floss.
2.  **Shadow-Head Cocoons** <term>original: *yìng tóu* (映頭)</term>. These contain putrid, decaying remains. The foul, turbid fluids stain the silk and damage other cocoons, causing a great deal of trouble and loss.
3.  **Loose Cocoons** <term>original: *mián jiǎn* (緜繭)</term>. These are structurally chaotic and weak. Once they enter the water and are stirred, they immediately clump together into tangled knots. 

These final three types are unfit for either reeled silk or quality floss and are used only for wadding.

## Reeling Silk

The process of boiling cocoons to extract silk was called *reeling* <note>original: *sāo* (繰)</note> in ancient times; today it is referred to simply as "making silk." ### Changing the Water

The color of the silk is determined by the water. If the water is clear, the silk will be bright; if the water is turbid, the silk will be dull. Therefore, the water must be changed frequently. However, if one waits until the water is already muddy before changing it, the difference between the clear and the turbid water will be too extreme, and the silk will not be able to maintain a consistent color from start to finish.

Those who are skilled in the production of silk today constantly observe the color of the water. As soon as they see it change even slightly, they remove **one-third** of the water and refill the basin with clean hot water. This frequent adding and replacing is called a **"running horse exchange"** <term>走馬換 (zǒu mǎ huàn): A technique of continuous, rapid replacement of water, named after the swift movement of a galloping horse.</term>. By doing this, the color of the water remains consistent from beginning to end, and the color of the silk remains uniform throughout, without alternating layers of brightness and dullness appearing.

# Raw and Cooked: Weight and Quality

As a general rule, if the cocoons are "raw," the silk will be heavy; if they are "cooked," the silk will be light. <note>In this context, "raw" refers to silk that is reeled quickly before the natural sericin (silk glue) breaks down too much, while "cooked" refers to cocoons left too long in the boiling water.</note> The "cooking" of the cocoons is caused by slowness in the reeling process: the hands are hesitant, the feet are sluggish, and the rotation of the reel is not swift. When the cocoons remain in the cauldron for too long, they inevitably become overcooked.

Therefore, striking the filaments <note>original: 打緒 (dǎ xù); the process of finding the ends of the silk fibers from the cocoons in the water.</note> should be fast, and the foot-pedaling should be firm and steady. It is often said that in the study of books, there are "three arrivals": the arrival of the heart, the arrival of the mouth, and the arrival of the eyes. <note>This is a famous maxim by the Song dynasty scholar Zhu Xi regarding focused study.</note> Making silk also requires "three arrivals": the arrival of the eyes, the arrival of the hands, and the arrival of the feet.

The eyes must focus intently while the hands frequently stir the cocoons to add and join new filaments; the feet must pedal firmly to keep the reel turning quickly. When this is done, the cocoons are all drawn into the thread and exhausted immediately without becoming overcooked. The resulting silk will naturally be heavier in weight. <note>original: 銖兩 (zhū liǎng); ancient Chinese units of weight. A "zhu" was a very small unit, 24 of which made one "liang" or tael.</note>

These should be picked out immediately to avoid tangling or breaking the thread. 

If the thread ends are both **brittle and prone to tangling**, they will tangle if the water is too hot and snap if the water is too cool. The person tending the fire must keep the heat moderate and steady; one must be patient and work slowly. Regarding **waste-stained threads** <note>original: 尿緒 (niào xù); cocoons soiled by the silkworm's own waste</note>, these are originally good cocoons but have been stained. You must select those with fewer spots and wash them lightly with vinegar, after which they can be reeled. 

If the cocoons are stained by **dead silkworms**, their color will be muddy and dull. They must not be mixed with good cocoons; they should be sorted out and processed separately, with the fresh water in the basin changed frequently. As for others, such as **yellow cocoons**, their silk produces a very vibrant color, but they must not be processed together with white cocoons. **Green cocoons** have a lush and tender color; using them for the outer layer of a skein adds great luster. ### Making Silk Floss

Silk floss and reeled silk are equally essential for clothing and use. Any cocoons that are unsuitable for reeling into silk should be set aside. Once the reeling of the better cocoons is finished, these remainders should be used to **make silk floss** <term>silk floss (制綿): unspun silk used as padding for winter garments or for spinning into coarser thread</term>. 

The floss made from the first batch of silkworms is tough and durable, while the floss from the second batch is loose and soft. There are also those who produce it by spinning directly from the cocoons. In ancient times, it was said that only those fifty years of age began to wear silk garments, but today the people of **Huzhou** <note>original: 湖人 (Húrén); referring to the residents of Huzhou, a world-renowned center for silk production</note>—regardless of whether they are old or young—rely on this material to ward off the cold. Its qualities of being light, warm, and comfortable against the body are far superior to those of **cotton** <note>original: 木棉 (mùmián); literally "wood-cotton," referring to the cotton plant or tree cotton, which was a cheaper but less insulating alternative to silk</note>.

Fill a bowl with half a cup of **fragrant oil** <note>original: 香油 (xiāngyóu); typically sesame oil, used here as a softener</note> and mix it with clear **lye** <term>lye (灰汁): an alkaline solution made from wood ashes, used to break down the sericin (silk glue) on the cocoons</term>. # Boiling the Cocoons

The cocoons must be thoroughly boiled before they can be transformed into silk floss. **Double-cocoon silk** <term>Double-cocoon (同功): silk harvested from a single cocoon spun by two silkworms together; it is prized for its strength and volume</term> is considered the highest quality. **Stained-thread** <note>original: 尿緒 (niàoxù); cocoons stained by the silkworm's waste</note> and "shadow-head" floss are secondary. **Moth-exit cocoons** <gloss>which tangle easily</gloss> are of the lowest quality. <gloss>Moth-exit cocoons are those where the moth has already chewed its way out. When processing ten units of Double-cocoon silk, one must wrap them in a layer of existing floss to prevent the fibers from tangling.</gloss> From ten units of fine cocoons, one will yield only about thirteen or fourteen ounces of finished silk floss.

To begin, boil the lye water in a **cauldron** <note>original: 釜 (fǔ); a large metal cooking pot</note> and place the cocoons inside. When they are nearly cooked, take a large bowl containing one cup of fragrant oil and fill the rest of the bowl with the hot lye water. Pour half of this mixture evenly into the cauldron and allow it to come to a boil several times. Flip the cocoons over and pour in the remaining half of the oil-and-lye mixture. 

Continue boiling, ensuring the cocoons are extremely well-cooked. Some processors use **cedar oil** <note>original: 柏油 (bǎiyóu)</note> for this task. In the boiling process, if the lye is too weak, the floss will be murky; if it is too strong, the floss will eventually become moldy. If too little oil is added, the floss will feel coarse and "thirsty." If the cocoons are undercooked, the silk will form lumps and be uneven. However, if they are overcooked, the floss becomes brittle and loses its loft. One must achieve the perfect balance for the best results. <gloss>An old farmer notes: Using animal lard or sesame oil is actually inferior, as the silk turns yellow when treated with sesame oil. Lye can also cause discoloration; therefore, in recent times, some people have begun boiling the cocoons purely in clear water.</gloss> Vegetable oil is considered the best additive, followed by cedar oil.

# Rinsing

Only clean cocoons produce white silk floss. Once the cocoons have been boiled thoroughly, the impurities within them have already been expelled by the heat. While they are still hot, take them to the river to rinse them. When rinsing, use a **sieve** <term>sieve (篩): a traditional bamboo mesh tray used for draining and sorting</term> to hold the cocoons. Place the sieve on the surface of the water and sway it back and forth to wash them several times; you must not knead or stir them. Lift the sieve and bring it to the bank, then press down to squeeze out the murky water. Sway them in the water again and press them again. The cocoons are only truly clean when the water being pressed out of them runs clear.

## Stretching the Hand-Floss

When the silk floss is stretched over the palm of the hand, it is called **hand-floss** <note>original: 手綉 (shǒuxiù); while literally meaning "hand embroidery," it refers here to the sleeve-like shape the floss takes when stretched over the hand</note>. Prepare a large water basin and place a silk sieve over the top. Take the cleaned cocoons and place them in the sieve. Peel each cocoon open one by one and stretch it over the palm of your hand, removing the **pupa** <term>pupa (蠶蝟): the "hedgehog-like" remains of the silkworm after it has finished spinning</term> and the **cast-off skin** <note>original: 蠶蜕 (cántuì)</note>. Generally, for **double-cocoons** <term>double-cocoons (同功): high-quality cocoons spun by two silkworms together</term>, one can stretch fifteen or sixteen over the hand; for small cocoons, one can stretch over thirty.

Take a bucket of fresh water and, while the floss is still on your hand, submerge it and use both hands to expand and rotate it. This makes the floss round, wide, and loose, which makes it easier to layer later. If there are so many cocoons that they cannot all be peeled in one day, they must be soaked in **weak oil-lye water** <note>original: 淡油灰水 (dànyóu huīshuǐ); a diluted version of the alkaline solution used for boiling</note> until the following day before peeling.
