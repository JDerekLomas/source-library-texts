Translated by the Khotanese Tripiṭaka Master Śīladharma at the Longxing Temple Monastery in Beiting <note>Beiting was an administrative center in the northern regions of the Tang Dynasty, near modern-day Jimsar, Xinjiang.</note>

# **The Sutra of the Vast and Pure Dharani of the Buddha’s Diamond Altar**

This *Vajra Altar Vast and Pure Dharani Sutra* was recently translated at **Anxi** <note>The "Protectorate General to Pacify the West," a Tang Dynasty administrative region in Central Asia, modern Xinjiang.</note> by the monk **Danji** (secular surname Liu). From that time until the current ninth year of the **Zhenyuan** era of the Great Tang [793 CE], it has been approximately

<column-break/>

thirty years. This sutra is the supreme cause for the **secret and definitive meanings** <term>Definitive meaning: *Nītārtha*, teachings that express the ultimate truth directly and clearly. <note>original: "了義"</note></term> of the **Mahayana** <note>The "Great Vehicle" of Buddhism.</note> of all Buddhas and **Tathagatas** <note>An epithet for the Buddha, meaning "One who has thus come."</note>. In the kingdoms of **Khotan** <note>An ancient Buddhist kingdom on the Silk Road.</note> and Anxi, it is currently widely upheld and practiced. However, east of these regions, its proclamation has not yet been heard. 

There was a monk named **Lizhen** (secular surname Li, courtesy name Fu), formerly the **Chief Administrator** <note>A high-ranking provincial official.</note> of **Xizhou** and concurrently the Prefect of Qianting County, who resigned from his office to enter the monastic life. Because he encountered this sutra, he developed a deep longing and reverence for it. Wishing to create a great benefit and ensure its wide circulation—and fearing that paper manuscripts might deteriorate over the years, causing the teachings to be lost—he used his remaining personal funds to reverently establish a **refined chamber** <note>A quiet room for meditation, study, or ritual.</note> at the **Miaode Temple** in that prefecture, modeled after the purchase of the **Jetavana** park <note>A famous monastery site donated to the Buddha; here used as a metaphor for a sacred building project.</note>. He commissioned skilled craftsmen to engrave this sutra upon fine stone so that it might be preserved forever. 

A man named **Wei Jibu** of the Tang dynasty, seeing this auspicious opportunity, came to participate and assist in the writing of the stone-engraved sutra. The original manuscript of the sutra consisted of approximately twenty-three sheets of paper. Because the text was quite lengthy, Wei Jibu feared it could not be finished quickly and that the preparations were insufficient. For several days, he was filled with worry. Suddenly, in a dream one night, an old man spoke to him, saying: "Master Xu, seek the writing of this..." [the text continues west/onward with] more than fifty characters...

My doubts were dissolved, and I was suddenly struck by a great realization. Following the words of this dream, I no longer harbored any hesitation <note>original: "計芊," likely referring to doubt or secondary thoughts</note> and resolved to transcribe it with the utmost devotion. Thus I knew that the sacred power had truly been added, and the benevolent spirits had secretly assisted me. It is rare to find such a profound response and clear evidence of the divine. 

The original Sanskrit manuscript of this sutra was kept in a repository in **Khotan** <note>An ancient Iranian Buddhist kingdom on the Silk Road, modern-day Hotan, Xinjiang.</note>. There was a novice monk who took the text from the repository to read it. <gloss>Disbelieving, he slandered it and was immediately struck mute; he threw the manuscript over a wall, where it caught on a tree. The monk, filled with remorse and regret, threw himself down beneath the tree, his body broken as he offered his lamentation and repentance. At that time, there were over two hundred **Hinayana** <term>Hinayana: Literally "Lesser Vehicle," a term used by Mahayana Buddhists to refer to early schools of Buddhism that focused on individual liberation. <note>original: "小乘"</note></term> monks who all abandoned their former practices and turned toward the **Mahayana** <term>Mahayana: "Great Vehicle," the branch of Buddhism that emphasizes the path of the Bodhisattva for the salvation of all beings. <note>original: "大乘"</note></term>. As for the rest of the monks and laypeople, they chanted and upheld this Dharma. 

There was once a monk who upheld this sutra; when he approached the end of his life, he purified himself, rinsed his mouth, and with a focused mind, sat in the **lotus position** <note>The "vajra" or cross-legged meditation posture.</note> and passed away. Miraculous occurrences such as these are extremely numerous and cannot be fully recorded here. Previously, during the turmoil in **Xizhou** <note>Also known as Gaochang or Turpan.</note>, people’s hearts were hurried and confused; the text was not properly put away and was nearly lost. However, there was a fellow monk named **Guanglin**, an elder, who said:
