->*The place, time, person, and cause of the making of this book.*<-
->*The Latin title <note>original: "Silva Gadelica"</note> which is at its head has the same meaning*<-
->*as the phrase "The Treasury of the Gaels."*<-

The **PLACE** was great London of the Saxons. The **TIME** was when the age of the Lord was one thousand eight hundred and ninety-two years [1892]. The **PERSON** was Standish, son of Hayes, son of Dermot the younger O'Grady, of the Limerick tribe; though he was born in the territory of MacBrien of Ara <note>Mac Bhriain ua gCuanach</note>, specifically at the very foot of the beautiful hill of Knockgreine. The O'Gradys, moreover, who are also called the *Cinel Dungaile*, are a branch of the descendants of Aodh <note>Síl Aodha</note>, to whom belong MacNamara and many others. The descendants of Aodh themselves are a division of the *Dál gCais* of Thomond: namely, O'Grady (Ainéislis), son of Murtough, son of Edruth, son of Maelmaith, son of Grada (from whom the O'Gradys are named), son of Tresach, son of Art, son of Conn, son of Follachtan, son of Cormac, son of Sechnasach, son of Flaithri, son of Ruadghus, son of Fodalbach, son of Dúnghal (from whom the kindred is named), son of Tiopraid, son of Forannán, son of Fionán, son of Brendan, son of Eochaidh, son of Caisín, son of Cas (where all the *Dál gCais* meet), son of Conall of the Swift Horses, son of Lughaidh the Swift, son of Aonghus the Lawgiver, son of Ferchorb, son of Cormac Cas, son of Oilill Olum, son of Eoghan Mór, who was called Mogh Nuadhat.

The **CAUSE OF ITS MAKING** is the necessity of the benefit that exists at this present time for every one of the Gaels, however weak, to join together and apply themselves diligently to their day of battle <note>meaning: to make a collective effort</note>, to see if they might be able to rescue some fragment or small piece of the art and scholarship of the ancients from this point forward, without it being drowned or permanently lost; for now the day is drawing to a close, and the sweet mother tongue is almost exhausted.

Alas, after all that, there is no group on the land of Ireland, nor among the descendants of Gaedhal Glas <note>the mythical ancestor of the Gaels</note>, son of Niul, son of Fenius Farsaid, who today would be more likely to be a prominent people with even a small spark of desire for the sweet stories or the sweet history of their country. So it is, if what David O'Bruadair <note>a famous 17th-century Irish poet</note> sang two hundred years ago regarding the children of Connacht was true:
