# Illustration of a Large Ritual Bell
<note>original: "鏞圖" (Yong tu). The *Yong* is a specific type of large-scale bronze bell. In the hierarchy of the "Eight Sounds" (ancient China's classification of musical materials), the *Yong* represents the pinnacle of the "Metal" category.</note>

<term>Yong: A large bronze bell.</term> <note>In traditional ritual music (Yayue), the *Yong* was often referred to as the "Big Bell." It was typically struck to signal the end of a musical piece or a movement within a ceremony, providing a deep, resonant conclusion that anchored the orchestral sound.</note>

<note>The woodcut illustration depicts the bell suspended from an elaborate ceremonial frame. These frames were architectural marvels in their own right, featuring:
1. **Bird Finials**: Known as *bihe*, these decorative birds at the top corners often held colorful silk tassels.
2. **Carved Supports**: The horizontal beam is held aloft by vertical posts that terminate in decorative bases.
3. **Mythical Pedestals**: The entire structure rests upon two carved creatures, likely lions or tigers, which symbolize strength and stability, ensuring the heavy bronze bell remained secure during use.</note>

---

<note>The bell's body is adorned with rows of rhythmic protrusions called "mei" or "bells bosses." These are not merely decorative; they are precisely cast to help dampen the overtones and allow the bell to produce a clear, authoritative pitch when struck with a wooden mallet or a swinging beam.</note>

# Illustration of the Bo Bell
<note>original: "鎛鐘圖" (Bo zhong tu). The Bo is a specific type of large, singular ritual bell. While it resembles the *Yong* bell described on the previous page, it is distinguished by its flat bottom edge and its specific role in directing the musical ensemble.</note>

<term>Bo Bell: A large, single-hanging ritual bronze bell.</term> <note>The Bo bell belongs to the "Metal" category of the "Eight Sounds." In the grand ritual music of the court (known as *Yayue*), the Bo was struck to signal the beginning of a musical section, whereas the *Yong* bell was often used to signal the end. Together, they acted as the structural bookends of a performance.</note>

<note>The woodcut depicts the bell's ornate construction and suspension system:
1. **The Suspension Loop**: Called the *gan* or *niu*, this top handle is traditionally cast in the shape of a crouching feline or a dragon, symbolizing the connection between the earthly sound and the heavens.
2. **The Striking Point**: Unlike Western bells, which use internal clappers, the Bo is struck on the outside of its rim with a heavy wooden mallet.
3. **The Body Patterns**: The surface is divided into sections by decorative bands. These patterns often include "cloud and thunder" motifs, representing the natural forces the music was intended to harmonize.</note>

---

<note>Historians of the Ming dynasty, when this encyclopedia was compiled, looked back to the Zhou dynasty (c. 1046–256 BCE) for the ideal proportions of these instruments. They believed that if the bell's shape and weight were perfectly balanced, the resulting sound would bring the human heart into alignment with the "Way" of nature.</note>

<term>Twelve Pitches (Shier Lü): The twelve-tone musical system of ancient China.</term> <note>Unlike the Western chromatic scale, these twelve pitches were associated with the twelve months of the year and the cycles of cosmic energy (Yin and Yang). # Metal Chun Bells 　　　　　　　　　 Chunyu

# The Bell (Zhong)

<note>original: "鍾" (Zhong). Unlike Western bells, ancient Chinese bronze bells are often lens-shaped (almond-shaped) in cross-section rather than circular. This design allows a single bell to produce two distinct musical pitches depending on where it is struck.</note>

The *Record of Trades* states: The Fu clan <note>original: "鳧氏" (Fu Shi). The hereditary lineage of master artisans or "Bell-casters" described in the *Rites of Zhou*.</note> manufactures bells. The two projecting "horns" at the base are called the **Xian**; the space between these horns is called the **Yu**. The area above the rim is called the **Gu** <note>literally the "drum," the primary striking zone</note>; the area above the drum is called the **Zheng** <note>the central rectangular panel</note>. The area above the central panel is called the **Wu** <note>the "dancing" crown or flat top of the bell body</note>. The stem rising above the crown is called the **Yong**; the very top of this stem is called the **Heng**. The loop used for hanging the bell is called the **Xuan**; the creature-shaped ornament on this loop is called the **Xuan-chong** <note>literally "loop-insect," usually depicted as a dragon-like creature</note>; the post of the loop is called the **Gan**. The decorative bands on the bell are called **Zhuan**; the spaces between these bands contain the **Mei** <note>the protruding bosses or "nipples" that help tune the bell’s resonance</note>, which are also known as the **Jing**. The recessed striking point on the rim is called the **Sui**.

<term>Kao Gong Ji (Record of Trades): An ancient technical encyclopedia included in the *Rites of Zhou*. it provides precise mathematical formulas for the dimensions and metal compositions of ritual objects.</term>

Divide the length of the **Xian** into ten parts; remove two parts to determine the height of the **Zheng**. Use the height of the **Zheng** to determine the width between the **Xian**. Subtract two-tenths from that width to determine the distance across the **Gu**. Use the width of the **Gu** to determine the length of the **Wu** crown, then subtract two-tenths to determine the **Wu's** width. Use the length of the **Zheng** to determine the length of the **Yong** (stem). The circumference of the **Yong** should equal its length. Divide that circumference into three parts and remove one to determine the circumference of the **Heng** (the top of the stem). Divide the length of the **Yong** into three parts; place the **Xuan** (suspension loop) at the point where two parts are above and one is below.

The variation in thickness is what causes the vibration; it is the source of the clarity or depth of the tone. The widening or narrowing of the mouth is how the resonance is produced. There is an entire theory regarding the bell.

<note>The ratios described here represent a sophisticated understanding of acoustics. By varying the ratio of the "Xian" to the "Zheng," and the "Gu" to the "Wu," ancient smiths could precisely control the pitch and duration of the sound.</note>

# The Stone Chime

The *Record of Trades* <note>original: "考工記" (Kao Gong Ji), a classic text on ancient craftsmanship and technology</note> states: The chime-makers <note>original: "磬氏" (Qingshi)</note> fashion the chime so that its angle <note>original: "倨句" (juju), the curve or "knee" of the L-shaped stone</note> measures one and a half right angles. Its width is taken as one unit, the shorter limb <note>term: "股" (Gu), literally the "thigh"</note> as two units, and the longer limb <note>term: "鼓" (Gu), literally the "drum"</note> as three units. One-third of the width of the shorter limb is subtracted to determine the width of the longer limb. One-third of the longer limb's width determines the thickness. If the pitch is too high, the craftsman grinds the sides; if it is too low, the ends are ground. The "thigh" is not the striking area; it is short and wide. The "drum" is the striking area; it is long and narrow.

Zheng Sinong <note>A prominent Han Dynasty scholar and commentator</note> says: The "thigh" is the upper, larger part of the chime, and the "drum" is the lower, smaller part. Although their sizes and lengths are different, their thickness remains uniform. A stone chime tuned to the **Yellow Bell** <note>term: "黃鍾" (Huangzhong), the fundamental pitch upon which the entire ancient Chinese musical system was based</note> is always two inches thick.

# The Qin
<note>The Qin (original: 琴, *Qin*), today often called the *Guqin*, is a seven-stringed zither and the most prestigious instrument in Chinese history, associated with sages and scholars. Unlike many instruments played for entertainment, the Qin was a tool for self-cultivation and philosophy.</note>

The construction of the **Qin** measures three feet, six inches, and six-tenths of an inch in length, symbolizing the days of a full year <note>original: "象期之日", referencing the roughly 366 days in a year</note>. Its width is six inches, symbolizing the **Six Directions** <term>Six Directions: East, West, South, North, Heaven, and Earth</term>. It possesses five strings, symbolizing the **Five Elements** <note>Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water</note>. The width of the waist is four inches, symbolizing the **Four Seasons**. The variation in width between the front and the back symbolizes the relationship between the **superior and the inferior**. The top is rounded and the bottom is flat, symbolizing the **Heaven and Earth** <note>In ancient cosmology, the sky was perceived as a round dome over a flat, square earth</note>. 

There are thirteen inlays <term>inlays: (original: 徽, *hui*) small studs along the side of the strings that mark the positions for harmonics and stopped notes</term>, which symbolize the twelve months of the year, with the remaining one symbolizing the **leap month**. 

The **Small Qin** has five strings, the **Middle Qin** has ten strings, and the **Great Qin** has twenty-seven strings. Some say the Qin was created by **Fuxi** <note>A legendary creator-god and first of the Three Sovereigns of ancient China</note>; others say the five-stringed version was created by **Shun**, and the seven-stringed version was created by **King Wen and King Wu of the Zhou dynasty**. In the **Song dynasty**, a two-stringed version was first produced, as well as a twelve-stringed version to represent the twelve musical pitches. **Emperor Taizong** <note>of the Song Dynasty</note> increased the strings to nine.

# The *Sheng* (Mouth Organ)

The *Book of Rites* <note>original: "禮記" (Liji), one of the core canons of Confucianism</note> states: "The *Sheng* and its reeds originated from the clan of Nüwa." <note>Nüwa is a mother goddess in Chinese mythology, traditionally credited with inventing the sheng to mimic the sound of the phoenix.</note> The *Shuowen Jiezi* <note>An early 2nd-century dictionary</note> states: "It is the sound of the first lunar month, when all things are born (*sheng*); therefore, it is called the *sheng*." <note>This reflects the traditional Chinese belief that certain instruments correspond to specific months and cosmic energies; here, the "Sheng" instrument is linked to "Sheng" (生), meaning birth or growth.</note>

It consists of thirteen reeds with pipes arranged inside a gourd. The reeds are fixed to the ends of the pipes. The *Gong* <term>Gong: The fundamental tonic note in the traditional Chinese five-tone scale</term> pipe is located in the center. An instrument with thirty-six reeds is called a *Yu*.

In some arrangements, the *Gong* pipe is positioned on the left side. Instruments ranging from nineteen reeds down to thirteen reeds are called *Sheng*. The large *Sheng* is referred to as the *Huang* <note>literally "the reeds"</note>, while the small *Sheng* is called the *He*. The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary</note> specifies: "A *Sheng* with nineteen reeds is called a *Chao* (Nest); one with thirteen reeds is called a *He* (Harmony)."

### Large *Yu* and Small *Yu*

The *Yu* is also a form of *Sheng*. Modern *Sheng* and *Yu* use wood as a substitute for the natural gourd, which is then finished with lacquer. This is significantly more durable and superior to using a natural gourd.

# The Ancient *Fou*
<note>original: "古缶" (Gu Fou)</note>

The *Fou* <term>Fou: a pottery vessel or wine jar used as a percussion instrument</term> produces the sound of Earth; it is the sound associated with the "Beginning of Autumn" <note>The Eight Sounds were traditionally linked to the eight seasons/directions; Earth corresponds to the transition of late summer/early autumn</note>. The ancients called it the *Fou*. As an instrument, it is hollow within and capable of containing, while its exterior is round and responsive to vibration. It is from this vessel that the balanced central tone emerges.

## The Large and Small *Xun*
<note>original: "大塤小塤" (Da Xun, Xiao Xun)</note>

According to the *Rites of Zhou* <note>original: "周官" (Zhou Guan), a classic text on bureaucracy and ritual</note>, the *Xun* <term>Xun: a globular clay vessel flute or ocarina</term> was used for instruction by the Junior Master, performed by the Blind Musicians <note>In ancient China, court musicians were often blind, as it was believed their lack of sight heightened their sense of hearing and musicality</note>, and played by the Reed-Pipe Masters. 

The instrument features a flat bottom and six holes. It is hollow inside and tapers to a point at the top, resembling the shape of a steelyard weight <note>The counterweight used on traditional Chinese scales</note>. The sound of the large *Xun* corresponds to the pitches of *Huangzhong* (Yellow Bell) and *Dalu* (Great Lyre), while the small *Xun* corresponds to the pitches of *Taicu* (Great Terminal) and *Jiazhong* (Pressed Bell) <note>These refer to the first four pitches of the twelve-tone Chinese temperament system</note>.

As seen in the woodcut illustration, the Spirit Drum is a sophisticated piece of engineering and art. Its construction reflects its specific ritual function:

**Structural Features:**
*   **The Drum Cluster:** The instrument consists of six drum faces joined into a single assembly. The number six is numerically significant in Chinese cosmology, often associated with the "Six Directions" (North, South, East, West, Heaven, and Earth) and the energies of the terrestrial realm.

### The Pellet Drum
<note>original: 鼗鼓 (Taogu)</note>

The <term>Taogu</term> <note>also known as a rattle drum or "shaking drum"</note> shown on the left is the processional counterpart to the Road Drum. In the context of the Road Drum's rituals, it is specifically referred to as the **Road Pellet Drum** <note>original: 路鼗 (Lutao)</note>.

**Design and Use**
*   **Triple-Tiered Construction:** Unlike common hand-drums, the ritual Road Pellet Drum consists of **three drum-bodies** <note>original: 三枚 (san mei)</note> pierced by a single long handle. 
*   **The Mechanism:** Small beads or "ears" are attached to the sides of each drum by strings. When the handle is rotated or shaken, these pellets strike the drum faces in rapid succession.
*   **The Finial:** The top of this instrument's pole is adorned with a small ceremonial figure, distinguishing it from the bird-topped larger drums.

In ritual practice, the pellet drum acts as a precursor; it is "shaken" to signal the start of the music and to coordinate the rhythm of the larger drums, ensuring that the earthly music is harmonious enough to reach the ears of the ancestral spirits.

# Pellet Drum
<note>original: 鼗鼓 (Taogu)</note>

<vocab>Pellet Drum, Rattle Drum, Ritual Music, Ancestral Worship</vocab>

### The Pellet Drum and its Ritual Context
The **Pellet Drum** <note>also known as a "rattle drum" or *taogu*</note> is a traditional percussion instrument characterized by two small beads or "pellets" attached to the sides of the drum body by short cords. When the drum is rotated or shaken, these pellets strike the drum heads to produce a sharp, rhythmic sound. Within the classical hierarchy of instruments, the Pellet Drum serves as the essential companion to the larger **Road Drum** <note>Lugu</note>.

**Ritual Function**
As established in the *Rites of Zhou*, this instrument is not a mere toy or folk item, but a vital component of the state’s ceremonial music. It is used specifically in sacrifices dedicated to **Human Spirits** <note>original: 人鬼 (Rengui); referring to the spirits of ancestors and the deceased who are honored in the lineage</note>. While the massive drums provide a deep, resonating foundation for the ceremony, the Pellet Drum provides a high-pitched, clear signal used to punctuate transitions in the ritual and to "awaken" or alert the spirits to the start of the musical performance.

**Symbolism and Artistic Design**
The woodcut illustration captures the high-status version of the instrument used in imperial or official settings:
*   **The Phoenix Frame:** The drum is suspended within an ornate wooden frame. The tops of the vertical supports are carved into the likeness of **phoenix heads** <note>original: 鳳首 (Fengshou)</note>. The phoenix, a symbol of auspiciousness and cosmic balance, indicates that this instrument is intended for the "refined music" <term>Yayue</term> performed at the imperial court.
*   **Decorative Base:** The base of the stand features stylized carvings, likely representing clouds or mountains, which elevate the instrument and separate it from the mundane world.

**Historical Significance**
The *Sancai Tuhui* records this instrument as part of a lineage of ritual objects that stretches back to Chinese antiquity. By the Ming Dynasty <note>the period when this book was published, 1368–1644</note>, the precise construction of the drum—including the material of the skin and the length of the pellet cords—was carefully documented to ensure that the music remained in harmony with the cosmic order.

# The Jin Drum
<note>original: 晉鼓 (Jingu)</note>

### The Jin Drum and its Ceremonial Significance
The **Jin Drum** <note>literally "Drum of Progress" or "Drum of the Jin State"</note> is a major percussion instrument of ancient China, categorized as one of the "Six Drums" in the *Rites of Zhou*. In the classical hierarchy of ritual music, it is noted for its impressive size, traditionally cited as being six feet and six inches in length with a diameter of four feet.

**Structure and Ornamentation**
As shown in the woodcut illustration, the Jin Drum is a large, barrel-shaped instrument mounted horizontally upon a decorative central pedestal.
*   **The Support Frame:** The drum is pierced by a central vertical pillar that rests on a heavy, tiered base. 
*   **Auspicious Motifs:** Extending from the upper part of the pillar are four curved brackets, each topped with a carved bird figure. These represent the **Phoenix** or **Vermilion Bird**, symbolizing the sun and the direction of the south.
*   **The Flaming Jewel:** The entire structure is crowned with a **flaming pearl** <note>original: 火焰珠 (Huoyanzhu)</note>, a common motif in East Asian art representing spiritual energy, wisdom, and the "brilliance" of the music being performed.

**Functions in Ritual and State**
The name *Jin* <note>meaning "to advance" or "progress"</note> reflects the primary functions of this instrument:
1.  **Military Coordination:** Historically, the Jin Drum was used during royal hunts and military maneuvers. It served as the signal for troops to advance or to begin their march.
2.  **Sacrificial Rites:** In the context of the state's ritual music <note>original: 雅樂 (Yayue)</note>, the Jin Drum was used to establish the tempo and provide a deep, resonating foundation for the ensemble. While other drums like the **Road Drum** <note>Lugu</note> were reserved for the spirits of the deceased, the Jin Drum was often employed in broader ceremonial contexts to honor the order of the state.

# The Ying Drum
<note>original: 應鼓 (Yinggu); literally "The Responding Drum"</note>

<term>Ying Drum: A ritual barrel drum used in ancient Chinese state ceremonies. Its name, "Ying" (Response), refers to its function of echoing or "answering" the lead Jin drum in the ritual orchestra.</term>

### The Significance of the Responding Drum
In the classical hierarchy of musical instruments established in the *Rites of Zhou*, the **Ying Drum** serves as the essential companion to the larger Jin Drum. If the Jin Drum represents the "leader" that initiates the sound, the Ying Drum represents the "responder" that harmonizes and completes the cadence. 

According to ancient standards, the Ying Drum is slightly smaller than the Jin Drum, traditionally cited as measuring six feet and five inches in length. Together, they coordinate the movements of dancers and the pace of the ritual.

**Architectural Support and Ornamentation**
The woodcut illustration showcases the specialized stand required to support such a significant instrument:
*   **The Suspension Frame:** The drum is suspended from a horizontal crossbeam, which is held aloft by two sturdy vertical pillars. 
*   **Phoenix Finials:** The top of the frame is adorned with two outward-facing **Phoenix heads** <note>original: 鳳首 (Fengshou)</note>. In the symbolic language of the Ming Dynasty, the phoenix is a creature of high virtue and is associated with the South and the element of Fire, matching the "yang" energy of the drum's sound.
*   **Auspicious Feet:** The base of the wooden frame is carved with **cloud motifs** <note>original: 雲頭 (Yuntou)</note>. These "cloud-feet" signify that the ritual music is a bridge between the earthly realm and the heavens.

**Ritual Usage**
The Ying Drum was placed to the east of the Jin Drum during ceremonies. It was not merely a musical tool but a piece of **Ritual Apparatus** <note>original: 器用 (Qiyong)</note> used to maintain the "Middle Harmony," ensuring that the state's offerings to the ancestors and gods were performed with perfect timing and decorum.

# The Fu (The Beat-Bag)
<note>original: 拊</note>

The **Fu** is shaped like a leather bag and is filled with rice husks; it is struck to provide the rhythm for music. As an instrument, the **Fu** typically takes the form of a bundle of husks; consequently, its sound is similar to a drum but is softer and more harmonious. # Clappers

<note>original: 拍板, Paiban</note>

Miscellaneous records state that Emperor Ming <note>original: 明皇, Minghuang; referring to Emperor Xuanzong of the Tang Dynasty (r. 712–756), a famous patron of music and the arts.</note> ordered Huang Fanchuo <note>A celebrated court musician and actor known for his wit.</note> to compile the *Compendium of Clappers*. These are struck to substitute for the clapping of hands <note>original: 抃, bian; an ancient term for rhythmic hand-clapping.</note>; to clap is to "strike the beat." Because the sound of this instrument is used to regulate the rhythm of a dance, it likely originated from the practice of striking the beat by hand. The *Old Matters of Yecheng* <note>original: 鄴城舊事, Yecheng Jiushi; a historical record concerning the ancient capital city of the Wei and Northern Qi dynasties.</note> states that in the Hualin Garden during the reign of Emperor Wu of the Northern Qi <note>r. 550–559 AD.</note>, a pond was excavated to represent the North Sea. Situated within was the "Hall of Contemplation" which used boats as its foundation. Seven wooden figures were fashioned there, and one of them held clappers. Thus, this instrument had already appeared as early as the Northern Qi dynasty.

# Striking Bowls

<note>original: 擊甌, Ji'ou</note>

Striking bowls is likely a legacy of the ancient practice of striking the *fou* <note>original: 缶; an ancient Chinese earthenware vessel used as a percussion instrument.</note>. At the beginning of the Dazhong era of the Tang Dynasty <note>847–860 AD.</note>, the musician Guo Daoyuan was highly skilled in this art. He used twelve bowls from the Yue <note>Yue ware, a famous type of green-glazed celadon.</note> and Xing <note>Xing ware, famous for its "white as snow" porcelain.</note> kilns. By adding or removing water from the bowls to adjust their pitch, he struck them with iron chopsticks. Their sound was more exquisite than that of the *fangxiang* <note>original: 方響; a set of tuned iron slabs or chimes arranged in a frame.</note>. Scholars of the Jin period recorded the method here, though it is suspected that the practice truly began with Guo Daoyuan.

# **The Ruanxian**
<note>original: 阮咸; a circular-bodied lute</note>

During the reign of Empress Wu, a man from Shu <note>modern-day Sichuan</note> named Kuai Lang discovered a bronze vessel inside an ancient tomb. It resembled a *pipa* <note>a pear-shaped lute</note> but was perfectly round. The people of that time did not recognize it, but Yuan Xingchong remarked, "This is an instrument created by Ruan Xian" <note>Ruan Xian was one of the "Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove," a group of 3rd-century scholars and musicians</note>. He then commissioned craftsmen to recreate the instrument using wood. Because its shape resembles the full moon and its clear sound is reminiscent of the *qin* <note>zither</note>, it was named the "Moon Lute" <note>original: 月琴, Yueqin</note>. The historian Du You noted that the paintings of the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove depicted Ruan Xian playing an instrument identical to this one, hence it was called the "Ruanxian." Some say that because Ruan Xian was of a stout build, he created this instrument to adapt the resonant sounds of the *qin* into a more portable form. It features four strings and thirteen frets. One plays it by leaning it against the knee and plucking the strings—a technique called *bo* <note>plucking/strumming</note>—which was intended to replace the great difficulty of playing the *qin*. People today simply call it <gloss>the Ruan</gloss>.

# <term>The Pipa</term> <note>original: 琵琶. A pear-shaped lute that is one of the most iconic instruments in the Chinese musical tradition.</note>

The **Pipa** is a four-stringed instrument known for its expressive range and technical complexity. Its name is a phonetic representation of its two primary playing techniques: *pi* (plucking the strings forward with the right hand) and *pa* (plucking the strings backward).

As seen in the woodcut illustration, the instrument features a shallow, pear-shaped wooden body with a short neck. Historically, it evolved from Central Asian designs that traveled along the Silk Road, eventually becoming a staple of both refined court ensembles and popular storytelling traditions. By the Ming dynasty, the <term>Pipa</term> had developed a sophisticated system of frets that allowed for a wide variety of scales and melodies.

# <term>The Painted Horn</term> <note>original: 畫角, Huajiao. A wind instrument, often made of bamboo or wood, used for military signaling and ceremonial fanfares.</note>

The **Painted Horn** is a tapered wind instrument used to produce powerful, resonant tones. Unlike the refined stringed instruments used in chambers, the <term>Painted Horn</term> was traditionally used in open spaces. It was originally a military instrument used by nomadic peoples to signal movements and sound the watches; however, it was eventually integrated into the ceremonial music of the imperial court.

The instrument is typically constructed from wood or bamboo and wrapped in leather or cloth, which is then painted with decorative patterns—hence the name "Painted Horn." Its mournful and stirring sound was often associated with the atmosphere of frontier life and the solemnity of official processions.

# <term>Earth Drum</term> <note>original: 土鼓 (tugu)</note>

This is an ancient musical instrument. The scholar <term>Du Zichun</term> <note>A prominent Han dynasty commentator on the Rites of Zhou</note> stated that it uses earthenware for the frame and animal hide for the two drumheads, which can then be struck. 

The *Appended Phrases* of the <term>Classic of Changes</term> <note>original: 易繫辭 (Yi Xici); one of the core philosophical commentaries of the I Ching</note> says: "With straw mallets and earth drums," referring to the simplicity of ancient ritual. 

The "Positions in the Hall of Distinction" chapter of the *Book of Rites* <note>original: 禮記·明堂位 (Liji Mingtangwei)</note> states: "The earth drum and the mallets of sedge-grass <note>original: 蒯桴 (kuaifu)</note> were the music of the Clan of <term>Yiqi</term> <note>An ancient title associated with the legendary Emperor Shennong, the Divine Farmer</note>." 

In the *Rites of Zhou*, under the "Offices of Spring," it is recorded that the <term>Master of the Flutes</term> <note>original: 籥章 (yuezhang); an official in charge of ritual music and agricultural ceremonies</note> was responsible for the earth drums and the flutes of Bin. In the second month of spring, they would strike the earth drums during the day and sing the *Odes of Bin* <note>original: 豳詩 (Binshi); poems from the Classic of Poetry describing agricultural life</note> to welcome the rising summer heat. In the second month of autumn, they would do the same to welcome the approaching cold.

# <term>Paulownia Horn</term> <note>original: 梧桐角 (wutong jiao)</note>

Currently, in various rural villages of the <term>Yuandong</term> region <note>Likely referring to a specific area east of a local landmark or a regional name in the Ming dynasty</note>, farm children roll <term>Paulownia</term> bark into horns during the spring months. When they blow them, the sound echoes across the fields and wilderness. 

Our predecessors wrote the following lines:
"South of the village, north of the village, the Paulownia horns;
Behind the mountain, before the mountain, the white cabbage flowers."

These lines describe the scenery of the season. From this, we can see that this custom of making horns has existed for a very long time.

The **Bamboo Bronze Drum** is made by cutting a large section of bamboo to a length of approximately three or four feet. One carves the body of the bamboo itself to create two "strings."
