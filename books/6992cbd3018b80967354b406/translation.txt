# I.

By *highest* good, I mean the *ultimate end* <note>original: "Endzweck"</note>, at which all thinking and acting, all thoughts and endeavors <note>The German phrase "Dichten und Trachten" is a common idiom referring to the entirety of human intention and imagination.</note> of man should aim—and indeed, not only those of the individual, but those in which the aims of all humanity should unite. With the definition of this good, the highest *moral principle* <term>Sittenprincip: the fundamental rule or standard for ethical behavior</term> is determined at the same time. People have sought to express this highest good, as well as the actions directed toward it, through various formulas, such as: acting according to God's will, becoming like God, knowing God, loving God, acting rationally, acting according to nature, feeling oneself to be a member of the (organic) whole to which one belongs; acting in the spirit of and for the preservation of that whole; fulfilling the true destiny of man, fulfilling the true destiny of things, acting for one's own pleasure, acting for the pleasure of others, seeking the greatest possible sensory pleasure, the greatest possible spiritual pleasure, tranquil pleasure, active pleasure, and other such things.

Upon closer inspection, only the principles of **pleasure** <term>Lust: joy, pleasure, or gratification</term> or **happiness** <term>Glückseligkeit: a state of profound bliss or well-being</term>, as they are also called, appear immediately clear and understandable; for what pleasure and happiness are—and what is more or less of them—everyone feels directly. ## II.

Let us consider the recognized basic rules of morality, such as: be temperate, chaste, just, truthful, charitable, respect the life and property of others, be obedient to authority and the laws, bear faith, hope, and love toward something divine, etc. We will find none which, if followed, would not have the effect of securing, preserving, and promoting the pleasure-state <term>Lustzustand: a state of enjoyment, pleasure, or well-being</term>, the happiness of humanity as a whole, and even down into the finest details. These are precisely the rules through whose most general observance the pleasure-state of humanity is secured, even in its most general relations and deepest foundations. They are roots which, to be sure, when hacked off and kept in the stack <note>Schober: a stack or rick, usually of hay; here used metaphorically to describe the dry, disconnected storage of a philosophical system</note> of a system, betray nothing of pleasure; but whoever looks upward from them in the garden of life sees them truly bearing the flower-crown of pleasure, and recognizes that they exist only to bear it; and finally calls everything rotten that does not stir up sap and strength in the striving for this crown. Only the crown can reveal whether the roots themselves are good.

As with all foundations, however, it is easier to recognize the importance of moral basic rules as such if one, instead of paying attention to what stands as long as they stand, pays attention to what collapses when they themselves fall. Now, how would it stand with the pleasure-state of the world if those rules ceased to be valid—how would it stand in a world where no law of temperance ruled, where no one could trust the word of another, where no one was safe in their property, their wife, or their life, where no laws and authorities were any longer able to order, restrain, and keep life in safe tracks, where no faith, no love, no hope toward something divine prevailed; how would things stand in a world in which only one of these rules had become rotten, and was not at least followed on average? ### III.

What is found in all basic moral rules, linking and binding them all together, I now condense into a general, supreme principle—one that gives birth to all rules of action from within itself, while also linking, binding, judging, and measuring them. This principle, however, should not merely reflect written rules, but should be cultivated by man, entering into the innermost sense, the thought, and the heart, down to the most minute and fundamental details of life.

**Man should, as much as it lies within him, seek to bring the greatest pleasure, the greatest happiness into the world at large; seek to bring it into the whole of time and space.**

Reducing displeasure <note>original: "Unlust." In this context, it refers to pain, discomfort, or anything that detracts from well-being</note> is, however, equivalent to increasing pleasure.

The first main consequence of this law is: man should educate himself and others so that he gains the greatest possible pleasure from such striving and acting, the greatest possible skill <term>Fertigkeit: refers to a practiced proficiency or ease in performing a task, suggesting that moral behavior should become second nature</term> in it, and the greatest possible knowledge of what promotes happiness and the state of pleasure <note>original: "Lustzustand"</note> in the world—whereby he simultaneously becomes the best possible person.

Not in such a way that he would need to relate his thinking and acting to the *word* "pleasure"; but rather to the *thing itself*. Even without knowing that the basic moral rules <note>original: "moralische Grundregeln"</note> in the final and decisive instance aim at pleasure, one can still be called "good" if they have gained the pleasure and skill to follow them, and know how to recognize what, in an individual case...

### IV.

In connection with the previous page, the supreme viewpoint through which religion is linked with morality and, consequently, with life, is formed as follows: that God as Spirit—whether he exists above the Whole or is the Whole itself—himself takes pleasure <term>Lust: in this philosophical context, "Lust" refers to pleasure, joy, or positive affect rather than carnal desire</term> in the promotion of the pleasure of this Whole through the forces inherent within it; that all the displeasure <term>Unlust: the opposite of "Lust," encompassing pain, suffering, or negative affect</term> of the world is to him only a means to eventually generate a higher pleasure; and that he also possesses the power and wisdom to turn everything toward this purpose; man calls this "turning things for the best."

Such a God is simultaneously the best possible God and the archetype for the best human being; he is a punishing judge of what is bad, and yet remains kind and merciful even in his punishment, insofar as the punishment must—sooner or later, here or there—turn the person toward improvement.

According to this way of thinking, all those commandments whose observance is primarily tied to the preservation and promotion of the happiness of the Whole—that is, the universal **basic moral rules**—will now be regarded primarily as divine commandments.

Conversely, if one perceives divine commandments within the basic moral rules from the outset for other reasons, one can discover by contemplating their collective meaning that God’s will truly intends for human actions to work together, as far as possible, for the happiness of humanity.

Thus, the statement of our **principle** itself is to be regarded as only the most general expression of the supreme divine commandment.
