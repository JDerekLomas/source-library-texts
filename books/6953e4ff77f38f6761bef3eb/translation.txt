# Books recommended by Newton himself to be read before the *Principia*:

<margin>Geometry</margin> Euclid’s *Elements*.
Elements of Conic Sections <note>The study of shapes like ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas formed by intersecting a cone with a plane.</note>.
Elements of Curves, either by Jan de Witt, or Philippe de la Hire’s Latin treatise on Conic Sections, or Dr. Isaac Barrow’s *Epitome of Apollonius*.

<margin>Algebra</margin> Erasmus Bartholinus’s *Introduction* — then peruse such passages as are scattered throughout René Descartes’s *Geometry* and other algebraic writings of Frans van Schooten — including all the commentaries and solutions of the problems.

<margin>Astronomy</margin> A short account of the Copernican System <note>The heliocentric model where the Earth revolves around the Sun.</note>, and of Pierre Gassendi’s *Astronomy*.
As much of Nicholas Mercator’s work as relates to the same system and newer discoveries made by telescopes.
These are sufficient for understanding Newton’s book; however, Christiaan Huygens’s *The Pendulum Clock* <note>original: "Horologium Oscillatorium"</note> will make one much better prepared.

On the first reading — understand the propositions along with some of the <gloss>Definitions</gloss> and demonstrations which are easier than the rest.
Proceed from the easier to the harder — after the first 60 pages, pass on to the Third Book. When the design of that book is understood, then turn back to other propositions at your leisure.
See: Newton’s Correspondence in Cotes <note>Refers to the correspondence between Isaac Newton and Roger Cotes, who edited the second edition of the *Principia*.</note>.

# DEDICATION

...commands and receives the universal acknowledgments of all who profess or value its several branches.

They justly admire that, amidst a close attendance to the cares of your profession—in which you now fill the most honorable seat—you are indefatigably promoting the improvement of natural knowledge <note>original: "natural knowledge"; this refers to the study of the physical world, what we now call science.</note>. You do this by carrying out some laudable <note>praiseworthy</note> designs of your own, by assisting and encouraging others, and by adding new stores to that immense treasure already brought into your extensive collection of whatever is rare and valuable in nature or art <note>original: "art"; here meaning human skill, craftsmanship, or technology rather than just fine art.</note>.
