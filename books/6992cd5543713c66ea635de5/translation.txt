# Emperor Gaozu of the Han

Emperor Gaozu of the Han <note>original: "Han Gaozu" (漢高祖). This refers to Liu Zhiyuan, the founder of the Later Han dynasty, not to be confused with the founder of the ancient Han dynasty over a thousand years prior.</note>, whose posthumous titles are "The Wise, Literary, Sagely, Martial, Illustrious, and Solemn Emperor," was surnamed Liu. His given name was Gao, though his original name was Zhiyuan.

In the second year of the Kaiyun era <note>945 CE</note> under the Lesser Emperor of the Jin dynasty, he was first enfeoffed as the King of Beiping. In the third year of that era, the Lesser Emperor was taken captive by the Khitan <note>a nomadic people from the north who established the Liao dynasty</note> and moved to the north. The Khitan then moved to usurp the imperial throne within the Central Plains.

Gaozu then planned to raise a righteous army. Consequently, he ascended the imperial throne in Taiyuan <note>a major city in modern Shanxi province</note>. He titled his dynasty the Great Han and proclaimed the year as "the twelfth year of Tianfu" <note>By using the era name "Tianfu"—the name used by the founder of the previous Jin dynasty—Liu Zhiyuan was signaling that he was the legitimate successor to the throne, intentionally ignoring the era names of the "Lesser Emperor" and the Khitan invaders.</note>. 

The capital was established at Bian <note>modern-day Kaifeng</note>. The dynasty lasted for a total of two generations. 

According to a careful reading of the *Study of Administrative Geography of the Five Dynasties* <note>original: "Wudai Zhifang Kao" (五代職方考)</note>, at the beginning of the Liu family's reign, the prefectures of Qin, Feng, Jie, and Cheng <note>territories in the west, in modern Shaanxi and Gansu</note> were lost once again to the Kingdom of Shu <note>the Later Shu, a rival state in the southwest</note>. During the reign of Emperor Yin <note>the second and final ruler of this dynasty</note>, one additional prefecture was established, which was Xie Prefecture. In total, one hundred and six prefectures constituted the territory of the Han.

Emperor Taizu of the <term>Zhou Dynasty [Later Zhou, 951–960 CE]</term> was surnamed Guo, named Wei, and held the courtesy name Zhongwen. His ancestors were natives of Yaoshan in Xing Prefecture. Some records suggest his original surname was Chai, and that as a child, he followed his mother when she remarried into the Guo family, subsequently adopting their surname. He established his capital at Bian <note>modern-day Kaifeng</note>. His dynasty lasted for a total of three generations.

According to the *Examinations of the Office of Geography during the Five Dynasties*, when the Guo family replaced the <term>Han</term> <note>referring to the Later Han dynasty</note>, ten prefectures were lost to <term>Liu Min</term> <note>founder of the rival Northern Han kingdom</note>. Later, Emperor Shizong <note>the second Zhou ruler, often considered one of the era’s finest leaders</note> seized the prefectures of Qin, Feng, Jie, Cheng, Ying, and Mo, as well as fourteen prefectures in the Huainan region. He additionally established five new prefectures <gloss>Ji, Bin, Tong, Xiong, and Ba</gloss>, while three were abolished <gloss>Jing and Wu were merged into one; Xing was also merged</gloss>. This brought the total to 118 prefectures under the Zhou state. When the Song dynasty rose to power, they inherited this administrative foundation.

At the end of the "Stone-Jin" <note>the Later Jin dynasty, founded by the Shi family</note>, the kingdom of Min had already perished. Among those that remained were seven states:
*   The area south of the Yangtze River, consisting of twenty-one prefectures, belonged to the <term>Southern Tang</term>.
*   The area south of Jian <note>likely referring to the Jianmen Pass</note> and the Western Shannan Circuit, consisting of forty-six prefectures, belonged to <term>Shu</term> <note>the Later Shu kingdom</note>.
*   The ten prefectures of northern and southern Hunan belonged to <term>Chu</term>.
*   The thirteen prefectures of eastern and western Zhejiang belonged to <term>Wuyue</term>.
*   The forty-seven prefectures north and south of the Ling Mountains belonged to the <term>Southern Han</term>.
*   The ten prefectures north of Taiyuan belonged to the <term>Northern Han</term>.

Although their tribute, taxes, and household registers were not submitted to the Ministry of Revenue <term>hubu; the central government body responsible for national finances and population records</term>, they were nonetheless reached by the Emperor’s civilizing influence and were overseen by the military headquarters of the various border prefectures.

### Reservoir-Ponds and Embankments

The *Shuowen Jiezi* <note>The first comprehensive Chinese dictionary, compiled by Xu Shen around 100 CE.</note> states that a "reservoir" (*bei*) is a pond in the wild, while a "pond" (*tang*) is like a weir or dam. A reservoir must have an embankment to hold the water, hence they are collectively called "reservoir-ponds" (*beitang*). 

The *Rites of Zhou* <note>A foundational classic detailing the administrative systems of the Zhou dynasty.</note> mentions that "stagnant pools" (*zhu*) are used to accumulate water, while "dikes" (*fang*) are used to hold water back. Commentators explain that a stagnant pool is a reservoir pond that captures flowing water, and a dike is the embankment built along the pool's side.

The reservoirs of today are the same as those of the past. If we examine historical records and commentaries, we find several famous examples:
* In Lujiang, there is the Que <gloss>pronounced *què*</gloss> Pond <note>A famous irrigation project in modern Anhui province, credited to the statesman Sunshu Ao.</note>.
* In Yingchuan, there is the Hongxi Pond.
* In Huangling, there are the Lei and Aijing Ponds.
* In Yangping and Pei Commandery, there is the Qianlu Pond.

There are many others <gloss>it is difficult to list them all here</gloss> whose names are cited in history. These systems were used to irrigate fields; the largest served several thousand *qing*, while smaller ones served several hundred *qing* <note>A *qing* is a traditional unit of area, roughly equal to 15 acres or 6.6 hectares.</note>. The remains of these ancient works still exist today and continue to provide benefits.

If people today were to survey the terrain and imitate these ancient systems, they would provide enough water to irrigate tens of thousands of fields. Compared to building polder fields <note>A "field enclosure" or polder (*tianwei*) is a tract of low land reclaimed from a body of water, which requires complex drainage.</note>, this method saves significantly on labor and costs. Furthermore, these reservoirs can be used to raise fish and turtles, and to cultivate crops like water chestnuts and lotus roots.

**Hidden drains** for conducting water are underground channels. Generally, in regions of water and land, if one encounters high ridges or terrains that separate fields, gardens, or settlements <note>original: 聚落 *juluo*, referring to clustered villages or habitations</note> such that water cannot pass through, one should dig a tunnel beside a bank or at the curve of a stream. According to ancient accounts, one *mu* <note>a traditional unit of land area, roughly 0.16 acres</note> of **pit-field** land measures fifteen paces in width. # Enclosure Fields

<term>Enclosure fields</term> <note>original: 圍田 *weitian*</note> are created by raising earth to form a protective barrier that surrounds farmland. In the region between the Yangtze and Huai Rivers, much of the land consists of low-lying marshes and swamps. Because these areas are adjacent to water, they are frequently submerged by unpredictable flooding, which hinders regular plowing and sowing.

Wealthy and influential families survey the local terrain and construct earthen dikes that form a continuous, unbroken circle. The interior of such an enclosure can contain hundreds or even thousands of <term>mu</term> <note>A Chinese unit of land area; during the Ming dynasty, 100 *mu* equaled one *qing*, roughly equivalent to 15 acres.</note> and <term>qing</term>, all of which are transformed into productive agricultural land. 

In later times, when various generals were stationed for military defense, they ordered their troops to divide the labor and raise the earth, imitating this same design. Consequently, these lands are classified differently depending on whether they belong to the government or to private citizens. There are also <term>polder fields</term> <note>original: 圩田 *yutian*; a term often used interchangeably with enclosure fields in the Yangtze Delta.</note>, which involve stacking earth to create embankments to ward off external waters; these are very similar to the enclosure fields. 

Whether a season brings flood or drought, these fields can be defended against the elements. Whenever a single harvest produces a surplus, it is not only sufficient to feed the local district but can also provide for neighboring prefectures. Truly, this is the superior agricultural method of <term>Jiangyou</term> <note>Literally "West of the River," a historical administrative region roughly corresponding to modern-day Jiangxi province.</note> and a source of permanent benefit for the future. For enriching the state and the people alike, there is nothing that surpasses this system.

### Chest Fields

**Chest Fields** involve constructing earthen embankments to protect the land; they resemble <term>polder fields<note>Known as *wei* (圍) fields, these were large-scale reclaimed lands common in the Yangtze River delta, protected by dikes.</note></term> but are smaller in scale. # Silt Fields

The <term>Book of History<note>The *Shujing* or *Classic of History*, specifically the "Tribute of Yu" chapter, which describes the geography and soil of ancient China.</note></term> states that in the region between the Huai River and the sea—which is <term>Yangzhou<note>One of the Nine Provinces of ancient China; in this context, it refers to a vast area covering parts of modern-day Jiangsu, Anhui, and Zhejiang.</note></term>—the soil consists of nothing but mud and silt. Generally, any planting done in low-lying waters requires this kind of muddy earth. 

However, in coastal regions, there is a specific method for managing these types of fields. Sand and mud carried by the tides accumulate around islands or in submerged, winding areas, creating plots of varying sizes measured in <term>mu<note>A traditional Chinese unit of land area; during the Ming Dynasty, one *mu* was approximately 614 square meters.</note></term>. 

Upon these flats, salt-tolerant grasses grow in thickets. When the tide comes in, it gradually traps and deposits more silt among the vegetation. Initially, one plants water barnyard grass. Once the <term>salinity and alkalinity<note>original: "chilu" (斥鹵), referring to salt-encrusted soil that is typically barren.</note></term> have been fully leached out, the land can be converted into productive grain fields. 

To protect these fields, walls are constructed along the coast, or wooden stakes and posts are driven into the ground to resist the surging tides. Ditches are dug along the edges of the fields to collect rainwater; these are used for irrigation during droughts and are known as "Sweet-Water Ditches" <note>In this context, "sweet" (tian) refers to fresh water, as opposed to the "bitter" salt water of the sea.</note>. The profits from the harvests of these fields can be ten times greater than those of ordinary land.

Furthermore, along the banks of the great rivers in the Central Plains, in the bends of the Huai River where waters converge, and in the curves of various lakes and marshes, silt and sediment accumulate where stagnant or swirling waters gather. When the water recedes, these areas become silt flats. These can also be planted with <term>barnyard millet<note>original: "can" (穇), a hardy grain often used as a substitute for rice or wheat in marginal lands.</note></term>. After autumn, when the mud dries and the ground cracks, one can scatter and sweep wheat seeds into the soil. The resulting harvest is just as effective as that of regular cultivated fields.

### Terraced Fields

**Terraced fields** <note>literally "ladder fields" (梯田, *titian*)</note> refers to the practice of carving the mountainside into a series of steps to create farmland. In regions where mountains are plentiful but flat land is scarce, except for areas of rocky debris <term>leishi: large boulders or piles of stones often found at the base of slopes</term> and steep cliffs which remain barren, the rest of the earthen mountains—from the horizontal foothills up to the precarious peaks—can be fashioned into a single system. By cutting the slope into multiple levels, one can then plant and cultivate crops. 

If the terrain consists of half soil and half stone, one must stack stones in sequence to encase the soil and form the fields. There are also mountain slopes so steeply inclined that one cannot even find a solid foothold; during the seasons of sowing and planting, people must hunch over and crawl upward like ants to hoe the soil for <gloss>sowing or</gloss> weeding. 

Because these mountain fields vary in height, yet always resemble the steps of a ladder when climbing from below, they are collectively called **"Terraced Fields."** 

If there is a water source at a higher elevation, one can plant non-glutinous and glutinous rice <note>original: *jingtu* (秔稌), referring to the two primary categories of Asian rice</note>. If only dry-land cultivation is possible, the soil is also suitable for millet and wheat. Truly, when the flat fields and usable lowlands are exhausted, the people turn to the mountains. The common folk of the mountain villages must strive to reclaim and tenant these lands, for it is still far better than not farming at all. Through the application of human effort and the nourishment provided by rain and dew, there will surely be at least some small harvest.

**Silt Fields** <term>Shatian (沙田); literally "sandy fields," these are fertile alluvial lands formed by the accumulation of river silt.</term> are located in the south, in the region between the Yangtze and Huai Rivers. They are either situated along the banks of the Great River <note>The Yangtze River</note> or stand as islands in the middle of the stream. They are surrounded on all sides by dense, crowded clusters of reeds, which serve to protect the embankments. The soil there is constantly moist and fertile, ensuring a bountiful harvest. 

Generally, where ridges and dikes are constructed, one can plant rice and glutinous millet. In the areas between these, where settlements are formed, mulberry and hemp can be cultivated. Some of these fields are intersected by lake-canals; in times of drought, they can be easily irrigated. Others are surrounded by large waterways; in times of flooding, the water can be drained. Therefore, there is no worry of either flood or drought, which makes them superior to other types of farmland. 

What were formerly called "collapsing river fields" <note>referring to land that is lost to erosion or gained through sedimentation as the river shifts</note> are abandoned or reclaimed inconsistently. Consequently, the total acreage of such land is never constant, and the tax rates are not fixed; this is precisely what is meant by these descriptions.

# General Discussion of the Mountains of China

Master Zhu <note>Zhu Xi (1130–1200), the preeminent Neo-Confucian philosopher whose views on geography were highly influential in the Ming Dynasty</note> said: The *River Map* <note>original: "He Tu"; an ancient cosmological diagram said to have been revealed on the back of a mythical dragon-horse</note> states that <term>Mount Kunlun</term> <note>In Chinese mythology and early geography, Kunlun was considered the "pillar of the sky" and the source of all major rivers and mountains</note> is the center of the earth. Generally, the distance from China to Khotan <gloss>pronounced "Tyan"</gloss> is ten thousand *li* <note>A unit of distance, roughly 500 meters</note>. When Khotan sent envoys to present tribute, they reported that Mount Kunlun lies over 4,300 *li* to the west of their land. Currently, China is located to the southeast of Kunlun. All mountains under heaven have Kunlun as their ancestor; they spread out into <term>three main trunks</term> <note>A traditional geographical theory that China's terrain is divided by three primary mountain systems: the North, Central, and South Trunks</note> to enter China. As for the mountains that enter the lands of the barbarians <note>Refers to non-Chinese nations or regions outside the Ming administrative sphere</note>, they cannot be verified and are not worth discussing. 

Now, speaking of the mountains of China: the various mountains of the Hebei region <note>Literally "North of the River" (the Yellow River)</note> descend from the high ground of the northern prefectures of Huan, Wu, Lan, and Xian. The waters to the west of the mountain ridge flow into the <term>Dragon Gate</term> <note>Longmen; a famous gorge on the Yellow River known for its dramatic cliffs</note> and the West River. The waters to the east of the ridge flow through the regions of You and Ji <note>Ancient provinces covering modern-day Beijing, Tianjin, and Hebei</note> and into the East Sea. One branch to the west forms Hukou and Mount Taiyue. The next branch encompasses the headwaters of the Fen and Jin Rivers and extends south to form the mountains of Xicheng and Wangwu, then turns west to form Mount Leishou. Another branch forms Mount Heng <note>The Northern Great Mountain of the Five Sacred Peaks</note>. Another branch forms Mount Tai...

The Taihang Range <note>One of the most significant mountain ranges in North China, acting as a natural boundary between the North China Plain and the Loess Plateau</note> extends for a thousand *li*. These mountains are exceedingly high; the region of Shangdang <note>Literally "High Party," a high-altitude plateau in modern Shanxi province</note> sits directly upon the mountain ridge. The various prefectures of Hedong <note>East of the Yellow River</note> and Hebei <note>North of the Yellow River</note> are situated along the mountain's branches. Its longest branch forms the Yan Mountains <note>The range north of modern Beijing</note>, which cover the area of Pingluan <note>Modern eastern Hebei</note> and the region north of the Great River <note>The Yellow River</note>.

As for the mountains to the south: the high mountains of Guanzhong <note>The "Land Within the Passes," the strategic heartland surrounding modern Xi'an</note> all originate from the Shu-Han region <note>Modern Sichuan</note>. One branch reaches as far as Chang'an <note>The ancient capital, modern Xi'an</note> before ending. Another branch from Guanzhong descends through the Hangu Pass <note>A strategic pass protecting the Wei River valley</note> to reach Mount Song and Mount Shaoshi <note>The Central Sacred Mountain and its neighboring peak</note>, finally ending in the east at <term>Mount Tai</term> <note>The Eastern Sacred Mountain in Shandong, traditionally considered the most important of the five sacred peaks</note>. Another branch winds through Panzhong <note>The upper reaches of the Han River</note> to the north of the Han River, descending until it reaches Yangzhou.

The various mountains of the Jiangnan region <note>The lands "South of the Yangtze River"</note> have their ancestor in the Min River and emerge from the <term>Min Mountains</term> <note>A mountain range in northern Sichuan, historically considered the source of the Yangtze</note>. The Min Mountains run along both banks of the Yangtze River. One side branch extends to various locations north of the river. This main branch then scatters through the regions of Hunan, Fujian, and Guangdong, finally ending in the provinces of Liangzhe <note>Modern Zhejiang and southern Jiangsu</note> and Jiankang <note>Modern Nanjing</note>. 

One of its branches forms Mount Heng <note>The Southern Sacred Mountain</note> and terminates to the west of Lake Dongting and the Jiujiang <note>Nine Rivers</note> region. Another branch crosses the Gui Ridge <note>The mountains of Guangxi</note>, encompasses the source of the Xiang River, and passes northward through the lands of Yuan and Yun <note>Historical prefectures in modern Jiangxi</note> to end at the Lufu range <note>Mount Lu in Jiangxi</note>. Another branch runs from the south toward the east, encompassing the source of Lake Pengli <note>Modern Lake Poyang</note> and crossing through She and the <term>Yellow Mountains</term> <note>Mount Huang, famous for its dramatic granite peaks and pines</note> to end at Jiankang.

## General Discussion of the Mountains and Waters of China

Mr. Jin of Renshan <note>Jin Lixiang (1232–1303), a prominent Neo-Confucian scholar known for his works on geography and history</note> said: The enduring forms of Heaven and Earth are certainly linked and interconnected, yet their internal logic also possesses distinct <term>pulse-lines (maituo)</term> <note>Traditional Chinese geography viewed mountain ranges as "dragons" or veins through which the earth's vital energy (qi) flows, similar to the circulatory system of a human body</note>. Speaking of the trajectory from the Kunlun Mountains <note>The massive mountain range in Western China, traditionally considered the "ancestor of all mountains" and the source of the Yellow River</note> toward the northeast: from Mount Jishi to the north lie the Huang River, the Sea of Stars <note>Xinghai</note>, and Qinghai Lake, reaching all the way to the Haowei River; these are all the sources of the Yellow River. 

Entering the lands of the Xiongnu <note>The nomadic confederation to the north of China's historical borders</note> to the east, they become the Yin Mountains. Further southeast, splitting off from Daibei and Yunshuo <note>Regions in modern Shanxi and Inner Mongolia</note> and running southward, they become the Northern Sacred Peak <note>Mount Heng in Shanxi, the northernmost of the five sacred mountains of China</note> and extend to the Taihang Mountains. These form the backbone of the Hebei region.

The sayings of geomancers <term>Kanyu: The traditional term for Feng Shui, meaning the study of the canopy of heaven (Kan) and the chariot of earth (Yu)</term> certainly should not be followed blindly. However, even Confucius <note>original: 宣尼 (Xuan Ni), a respectful posthumous title for Confucius</note> left writings on divining burial sites, and Master Cheng <note>Cheng Yi, a famous Song dynasty philosopher, who outlined the "Five Concerns" for protecting a grave from elements like water, insects, or roots</note> issued warnings regarding the "Five Concerns" of burial. When the Duke of Jin <note>likely referring to the statesman Sima Guang</note> composed a memorandum on relocating a city, he still prioritized the observation of Yin and Yang; and when Ji Dan <note>the Duke of Zhou, a foundational figure of Chinese civilization</note> established the capital of the Zhou dynasty, he pre-calculated the positions relative to the Chan and Jian rivers. Given these historical examples, how could books like the *Classic of Burial*, the *Blue Bag*, or the *Golden Casket* <note>these are foundational and esoteric manuals of ancient geomancy</note> be entirely discarded?

The *Book of Changes* <note>original: 大易 (Da Yi)</note> says: "Heaven and Earth determine positions; Mountains and Marshes exchange vital energy." Truly, "determining positions" is the basis of the theory of burial sites, and "exchanging vital energy" is the basis of the theory of Dragon Veins <note>long-reaching ridges of mountains thought to carry the earth's energy (qi)</note>, surrounding hills, and watercourses. 

If benevolent people and filial sons obtain these diagrams and study them—looking down to observe the winding networks of the mountains and rivers, and looking up to consult the Five Elements and the constellations—they can then survey the orientations and determine the proper configurations of the landscape. While one need not strictly adhere to every popular saying of masters such as Guo, Bu, Zeng, Yang, Liao, or Lai <note>These represent the "Six Masters" of geomancy, including Guo Pu (276–324 CE) and Yang Yunsong (834–900 CE)</note>, the grand principles of geomancy will be clear in their minds. For now, let us preserve these records.
