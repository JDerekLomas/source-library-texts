**Zhuge Liang** <note>181–234 CE; a legendary strategist and the Chancellor of the state of Shu Han during the Three Kingdoms period.</note> led a campaign to suppress the Southern tribes. In every location, he was victorious in battle. Entering through **Yuexi**, he executed the rebel leader **Yong Kai** and others. **Meng Huo**, who was deeply respected by both the local tribes and the Han people, gathered the remaining forces to resist Zhuge Liang. Meng Huo remarked, "Previously, I was unaware of the true state of your forces, which is why I was defeated. ### Three Kingdoms Part IV (Conclusion)

The four commanderies of **Yizhou**, **Yongchang**, **Zangke**, and **Yuexi** <note>These administrative divisions covered parts of modern-day Yunnan, Guizhou, and southern Sichuan.</note> were all pacified. **Zhuge Liang** immediately identified the local **chieftains** <term>original: "qushuai" (渠率)</term><gloss>Refers to the leaders of the non-Han tribal groups in the south.</gloss> and appointed them to official positions.

Someone <note>Likely an advisor or subordinate concerned about the risks of local autonomy.</note> counseled **Liang** against this policy. **Liang** replied:

"If we leave outsiders <note>Referring to Han Chinese officials from the central government of Shu Han.</note> to govern, we would be forced to station soldiers there. If soldiers are stationed, we would lack the food to sustain them—this is the **first difficulty**. The tribal people have only recently suffered wounds and defeat; many have seen their fathers and brothers killed or lost. If we leave outsiders in charge without a military garrison, disaster will surely follow—this is the **second difficulty**. Furthermore, these tribal leaders have committed many crimes of deposition and murder; they harbor **deep-seated guilt** <term>original: "leizhong" (纍重)</term> and suspicion. If we leave outsiders to rule them, they will never truly trust one another—this is the **third difficulty**. My current intention is to leave no soldiers and transport no grain. I aim to see the social order roughly established and ensure that the tribal and Han peoples remain at peace with one another. That is all."

Thereupon, **Liang** recruited all the most talented men of the region, such as **Meng Huo**, to serve as his own staff and officials. The region in turn provided **gold**, **silver**, **cinnabar**, **lacquer**, **oxen**, and **warhorses** to support the expenses of the military and the state. Until the end of **Liang's** life, the southern tribes did not rebel again.

**Sun Quan, the King of Wu**, having heard of the **state of mourning in Wei** <note>referring to the death of Cao Pi, Emperor Wen of Wei, in 226 CE.</note>, personally led a military force to attack **Jiangxia**. **Wen Ping**, the Grand Administrator <note>a high-ranking provincial official with both civil and military authority.</note> of **Jiangxia**, held the defense firmly. ### Three Kingdoms Part IV

There were discussions [within the Wei court] about mobilizing troops to rescue the besieged positions. The Emperor of Wei, **Cao Rui** <note>Also known as Emperor Ming of Wei, the grandson of Cao Cao.</note>, said: "**Sun Quan** is accustomed to naval warfare. That he now dares to launch a land attack is merely an attempt to catch us while we are unprepared. He is already held in check by our general **Wen Ping**. In a struggle between offense and defense, the defender's strength is effectively doubled; he will not dare to remain for long." Indeed, after a short time, Sun Quan retreated.

**Zhuge Liang** led his various armies to station themselves in the north at **Hanzhong**. When the Emperor of Wei heard that Liang was in Hanzhong, he wished to mobilize a large-scale expedition to attack him. He consulted **Sun Zi** <note>A high-ranking official and close advisor to the Wei emperors, known for his cautious and strategic mind.</note> on the matter.

**Sun Zi** replied: "In the past, the **Martial Emperor** <note>Referring to Cao Cao, the founder of the Wei state's power.</note> took the region from **Zhang Lu**, but he only succeeded after facing great peril. He often remarked that the region of **Nanzheng** <note>The administrative center of Hanzhong.</note> was effectively a **Prison of Heaven** <term>original: "tianyü" (天獄)</term><gloss>A metaphor for a location that is naturally fortified and extremely difficult to escape or conquer due to its rugged terrain.</gloss>, and that the road through the **Xie Valley** was like a five-hundred-mile stone tunnel. 

Now, if we advance our army toward **Nanzheng**, the path is already treacherous and obstructed. It would be better to simply utilize our existing troops, divide their commands, and order our great generals to occupy the various **strategic points**..."

# The Strategic Debate at the Qin Mountains

The state of **Wei** had appointed **Xiahou Mao** <note>The son of the famous general Xiahou Yuan; he was a son-in-law of the Wei imperial family.</note> as the Commander of **Guanzhong** <term>Guanzhong</term><gloss>Literally "Within the Passes," the strategic heartland surrounding the capital of Chang'an.</gloss>. **Chancellor Liang** <note>Zhuge Liang, the brilliant Prime Minister of Shu Han.</note> was preparing to launch an invasion of Wei and consulted with his subordinates on the plan of attack.

**Wei Yan** <note>A brave but headstrong general of Shu.</note> proposed: "Mao is the Emperor’s son-in-law; he is cowardly and lacks any strategic foresight. If you grant me **five thousand** elite troops and **five thousand** more to carry supplies, I can strike directly from **Baozhong**. By following the **Qin Mountains** eastward and then turning north through the **Ziwu Valley**, I can reach **Chang'an** <note>The great western capital and a major military objective.</note> in no more than **ten days**. 

When Mao hears that I have arrived so suddenly, he will surely abandon the city and flee. We can seize the grain stored in the **Hengmen** granaries and the supplies left by the fleeing civilians; this will be enough to sustain our troops. By the time reinforcements can assemble from the East, it will take about **twenty days**. In that time, your Excellency can arrive with the main army via the **Xie Valley**. If we do this, then in one single stroke, everything west of **Xianyang** can be secured."

**Liang**, however, considered this a **perilous plan**. He believed it was better to proceed safely along the **level roads**, whereby they could easily take **Longyou** <note>The region west of Mount Long, modern-day eastern Gansu.</note>. He felt this was a **ten-to-one certainty** of victory without any risk of disaster. Therefore, he did not utilize **Yan’s** plan. 

Instead, he publicly announced that they would take the route through **Mei** via the **Xie Valley**, and he dispatched Generals **Zhao Yun** and **Deng Zhi** to lead a decoy army <note>A "Yijun" or feint army meant to distract the enemy while the main force attacked elsewhere.</note>.

# The First Northern Expedition and the Battle of Jieting

### The Invasion of the West

...occupied **Jigu** <note>The "Ji Valley," used as a feint to distract the Wei forces.</note>. The State of **Wei** dispatched **Cao Zhen** <note>A senior general and relative of the Wei imperial family.</note> to supervise the various armies and defend **Mei** to resist them. **Liang** <note>Zhuge Liang, the Chancellor of Shu Han.</note> then led the main army to attack **Mount Qi**. His military formations were perfectly organized, and his orders were clear and solemn. 

<margin>The brilliance lay in the silence.</margin> 

Initially, the State of Wei believed that because **the Emperor Zhaolie** <note>original: "昭烈" (Zhaolie); the posthumous title of Liu Bei, the first emperor of Shu Han.</note> had already passed away, the Shu forces would remain quiet for several years. Indeed, there had been no news of movement. Consequently, they were largely unprepared. When they suddenly heard that Liang had set out, the entire court and the countryside were filled with terror. Following this, the commanderies of **Tianshui**, **Nan'an**, and **Anding** all raised their armies to respond to Liang’s call; the entire **Guanzhong** <term>Guanzhong</term><gloss>The "Land Within the Passes," a strategic region central to the defense of the Wei capital.</gloss> region was shaken with shock.

### The Turning Point at Jieting

**Cao Rui**, the Emperor of Wei, traveled to **Chang'an** and dispatched the General of the Right, **Zhang He**, to lead fifty thousand infantry and cavalry to resist the invasion. Liang sent his Military Consultant **Ma Su** <note>A close protégé of Zhuge Liang known for his theoretical knowledge of tactics.</note> to supervise the various armies and engage Zhang He at **Jieting**. 

However, Su violated Liang’s strategic instructions. His movements were confused and disruptive; he abandoned the water source and moved his troops up the mountain rather than descending to hold the fortified city. Zhang He cut off their supply route to the water and launched a strike, dealing them a massive defeat. 

As a result, Liang was forced to evacuate over a thousand households from **West County** and return to **Hanzhong**. Originally, Liang had believed Su’s talent and strategic skill to be extraordinary and had held him in high regard. However, when the Emperor Zhaolie was on his deathbed, he had told Liang: "Su’s words exceed his actual abilities..."

# The Records of the Three Kingdoms

### The Execution of Ma Su

...[the late Emperor Liu Bei had warned,] "[Ma Su] should not be given significant responsibility; you must observe him carefully." **Liang** <note>Zhuge Liang (181–234), the Chancellor of Shu Han.</note>, however, did not believe this to be the case. He appointed **Su** <note>Ma Su, a talented but theoretical strategist whose blunder at Jieting led to the expedition's failure.</note> as an Army Advisor, and the two would often converse from dawn until the deep of night. 

But following the recent defeat, Liang had him arrested and executed. Liang wept bitter tears for him. When the time came for the funeral sacrifices, Liang personally comforted Su’s orphaned children. 

**Jiang Wan** <note>A high-ranking official and eventual successor to Zhuge Liang.</note> spoke to Liang, saying: "In ancient times, when the State of Chu executed its general **Dechen** <note>Dechen was a Chu commander whose suicide/execution after a defeat was celebrated by his enemy, Duke Wen of Jin, because it removed a capable threat.</note>, Duke Wen of Jin rejoiced. Today, while the world is still in upheaval and far from settled, to put to death a man of such strategic intellect—is this not a cause for great regret?"

Liang replied through his tears, "The reason **Sun Wu** <note>Sun Tzu, the legendary author of *The Art of War*.</note> was able to achieve victory throughout the world was because his application of the law was transparent and absolute. Today, the four seas are divided and the clash of arms has only just begun. If we begin to abandon our laws now, by what means shall we suppress the rebels?"

### The Merit of Wang Ping

Prior to these events, Assistant General **Wang Ping** had repeatedly counseled Ma Su, but Su had been unable to use his advice. When the defeat occurred and the various units scattered in chaos, only the thousand-odd men under Ping’s command remained organized. They beat their drums and held their positions. The Wei general **Zhang He** suspected an ambush and did not dare to press his advantage. 

Because of this, Ping was able to slowly and methodically gather the scattered soldiers from the various camps and lead them back. For this conduct, Liang appointed Ping as an Army Advisor and promoted him to the rank of Marquis. Meanwhile, Liang requested of the Emperor that he himself be demoted by three ranks <note>A traditional Chinese gesture of taking official responsibility for a military failure.</note>.

An imperial decree was issued, appointing [Zhuge Liang] as General of the Right, acting in the capacity of the Chancellor. <note>Following his defeat at Jieting, Zhuge Liang requested a formal demotion to take responsibility, though he remained the executive leader of the Shu Han military.</note>

In the twelfth month, **Liang** <note>Zhuge Liang (181–234), the Chancellor and chief strategist of the state of Shu Han.</note> led tens of thousands of troops out through Sanguan Pass to besiege **Chencang** <note>A vital strategic stronghold guarding the path to the Wei capital region.</note>, but he was unable to capture it. Although Zhao’s soldiers numbered only about a thousand, Liang launched a fierce assault. He deployed **Cloud Ladders** <note>yunti; massive, folding siege ladders often equipped with counterweights.</note> and **Battering Rams** <note>chongche; heavy mobile rams used to breach gates and walls.</note> against the city. Zhao countered by firing **Incendiary Arrows** <note>huojian; literally "fire arrows," these carried flammable materials to ignite wooden siege engines.</note> at the ladders, and the men upon them were all burned to death. Zhao also used ropes to link together heavy stone millstones, which were dropped to crush the battering rams, causing them to shatter.

Liang then constructed **Siege Towers** <note>jinglan; tall wooden towers used by archers to fire down upon defenders.</note> a hundred feet high to fire into the city, and he used dirt mounds to fill the defensive moats, intending to scale the walls directly. Zhao responded by building secondary inner walls. Liang then ordered the digging of **Tunnels** <note>dixue; sapping or mining under walls to create an entrance or cause a collapse.</note>, hoping to emerge inside the city, but Zhao dug a horizontal intercepting trench within the city walls to block their path.

The two sides attacked and resisted one another day and night for over twenty days. The State of Wei dispatched General **Zhang He** to relieve the siege. Before he arrived, Liang’s provisions were exhausted, and he withdrew his forces. General **Wang Shuang** pursued Liang’s retreat, but Liang struck back and beheaded him.
