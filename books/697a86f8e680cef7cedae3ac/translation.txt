§. 8. But what finally shall we say regarding the *Prophecy of Enoch*, of which Jude makes explicit mention? Three possibilities may be considered. 1. That the Apostle cited a certain book existing at that time, whether it actually originated from Enoch or was published by someone else under his name. 2. That Enoch's prophecy was passed down through <term>tradition <note>original Greek: paradosin</note></term> until the time of the Apostle. 3. That the Apostle received Enoch's words through a unique divine revelation. Joseph Scaliger, following others, adopts the first view; he believes the Apostle cited the "Fragment of the Watchers" <note>The 'Egregoroi' or Watchers are a class of angels mentioned in the apocryphal Book of Enoch</note> (*page 244*). Gregorio Primaticcio says the same in his *Commentary on the Epistle of Jude* (*page 167*). However—not to bring too many arguments here—the words quoted by the Apostle do not actually appear in the fragment about the Watchers. The second view is favored by Michael Medina in *On Right Faith in God* (book 6, chapter 24), by Giovanni Maria Scribonio in his *17th Sermon on the Epistle of Jude* (*page 259*), and others. They argue that this prophecy was presented as something well-known and famous among those to whom Jude was writing; however, this cannot actually be gathered from the Apostle's words. We simply rest with the final option. The Illustrious Bagius correctly notes (*page 94*): *Jude, being a man led by the Spirit of God, had no need to resort to antediluvian <note>pre-flood</note> writings or traditions, for he enjoyed an extraordinary and immediate readiness in writing and speaking, as well as the <term>remembrance and teaching <note>original Greek: hypomnēsei kai didaxei</note></term> of the Holy Spirit itself.* Furthermore, from the Apostle’s citation of Enoch's prophecy, no one can easily prove that Enoch committed that prophecy of his to writing. The Apostle does not say, "he <term>wrote <note>original Greek: egrapse</note></term>," but "he <term>prophesied <note>original Greek: proephēteuse</note></term>"; nor does he introduce Enoch as <term>writing <note>original Greek: graphonta</note></term>, but as <term>speaking <note>original Greek: legonta</note></term>. In the same way, Peter relates that Noah was a <term>herald of righteousness <note>original Greek: kēryka tēs dikaiosynēs</note></term> (*2 Peter 2:5*). Yet it does not immediately follow from this that Noah published volumes of sermons. Therefore, from the fact that we read that Enoch prophesied, it cannot be inferred that he wrote a prophecy. Let learned and wise readers judge. For our part, we now pull in our sails, since we are sailing into the harbor <note>a classical metaphor for concluding a long literary work</note>.

§. 2. The *Tonic* accent raises the affected syllable during the pronunciation of the word. Sometimes the last syllable is raised, and then the word is called <term>*Milra*<note>original Hebrew: "מלרע" [mil-ra], meaning "from below"; refers to words accented on the final syllable</note></term> and is <term>oxytone<note>original Greek: "ὀξύτονος" [oxytonos]; a linguistic term for a word with the stress on the last syllable</note></term>; or the second-to-last syllable is raised, and it is called <term>*Milel*<note>original Hebrew: "מלעל" [mil-el], meaning "from above"; refers to words accented on the penultimate syllable</note></term> and is <term>paroxytone<note>original Greek: "παροξύτονος" [paroxytonos]; a word with the stress on the next-to-last syllable</note></term>. # OBSERVATIONS

->I.<-

A single word admits only one accent that is functionally rhythmic <note>original: "actu Tacticum"; referring to the accent that determines the musical beat or stress.</note>.

II. The accent is usually placed where the stress [tone] is located. However, the accents *Zarqa*, *Segolta*, *Telisha Qetanna*, and *Pashta* are placed on the last letter, while *Telisha Gedola* is placed on the first, even if the stress is not there. For example: <term>אַבְרָהָם</term> (Abraham), <term>הַמֶּלֶךְ</term> (the king).

III. *Yitiv*, which is considered a substitute for *Pashta*, is always placed before the first vowel of the word; in this way, it is distinguished from *Mahpakh*, which otherwise shares the same shape. For example: <term>מֶלֶךְ</term> (king) has <term>Yitiv</term>, while <term>מֶלֶךְ</term> (king) has <term>Mahpakh</term>.

IV. *Pashta*, when falling on a word with *Milel* <note>original: "Millel"; stress on the penultimate (second to last) syllable</note> stress, is doubled; sometimes this also happens with *Segolta*, *Zarqa*, and others. For example: <term>מֶלֶךְ</term> (king), <term>בַּעַל</term> (master/lord), <term>שָׁבַר</term> (he broke).

V. *Geresh*, when preceded by *Munach* or by no accent at all (as it admits various patterns), is doubled when it falls on the last syllable of a word. For example: <term>דִּבֶּר</term> (he spoke). This is commonly called <term>Gershayim</term>.

VI. Because of certain disjunctive accents <note>original: "distinctivos"; accents that indicate a pause</note>—such as *Silluq*, *Atnach*, *Segolta*, *Zaqeph Qaton*, as well as *Revia*—the vowels *Patach* and *Segol* are changed into *Qamets*, and *Sheva* is changed into *Segol*; in verbs, they change back into the vowel from which they originated. For example: <term>אֶרֶץ</term> (land), <term>בָּחָרוּ</term> (they chose), <term>יֵצֶר</term> (inclination), <term>יֵדָעוּ</term> (they will know). See on this matter the excellent Master Sennertus <note>Andreas Sennert (1606–1689), a famous German Hebraist and orientalist.</note> in his <term>Grammar, book 1, chapter 9, page 130 and following.</term>

VII. A vocal *Sheva* <note>original: "Scheva mobile"</note> following a long vowel is considered here to constitute a syllable; otherwise, neither the *Sheva* itself nor the vowel that stands in its place (as in <term>וּמוֹשָׁב</term> [and a seat] or <term>וְעָמְדוּ</term> [and they stood]) is counted.

VIII. Words joined by a *Maqqef* <note>A Hebrew hyphen that joins two or more words together into a single rhythmic unit.</note> are treated here as a single word.

IX. *Merkha Mahpakh*, when falling on the first syllable, throws its *Mahpakh* onto the last syllable of the preceding word, *Yerekh Ben Yomo*, if it finds that syllable empty.

# GENERAL NOTE.

To each Lord <note>original: "Domino"; a technical term used by early grammarians for "distinctive" accents that indicate a pause in reading</note>, as far as was possible, we have assigned two other subordinate Lords. One is closest to it, which can be called the **minor sub-distinctive**, expressed by us in a cursive character; the other is further removed, which can be called the **major sub-distinctive**, noted by us with a different character. The foundation of this matter is that more precise distinction which never allows more than two words to cohere <note>i.e., to be read together without some level of syntactical break</note>. Hence, quite often, a very small mental *semi-comma* <note>a "half-comma" or the slightest possible pause in thought</note> must be conceived.

Where the position of a major or minor distinctive should be established according to a certain logical rule, we believe it cannot be determined with such certainty *a priori* <note>based on theoretical rules alone before looking at the text</note>. For unless most things are left to the intention of the sacred authors—which often refutes our own judgment regarding the proportion of the distinction and is attributed to an emphasis that we cannot always penetrate—the matter will sometimes be full of unresolvable <note>original Greek: *ἀδιαλύτοις* (adialytois)</note> doubts. 

Thus, demonstration and practice are more successfully established *a posteriori* <note>based on observing the actual marks found in the biblical text</note>. It is enough for us to know, for example, that <term>Atnach</term> <note>The primary mid-verse divider</note> distinguishes more than <term>Segolta</term>, and this more than <term>Revia</term> <note>original: "Refia"</note>, and this more than <term>Paseq</term> <note>original: "Pfik"; a vertical line used as a minor separator</note>, and so on; and that these various Accents suggest to us various degrees of distinction. Furthermore, we judge the manner of distinction from the presence of the Accents *a posteriori* rather than inferring this or that Accent *a priori* by presupposing a certain degree of distinction. 

Therefore, we will not be able to arrange the Accentuation of any verse—regarding the proper position of both the "Kings" <note>major pauses</note> and "Ministers" <note>conjunctive accents that join words together</note>—by our own skill in the same way it is found in the text itself. If that were possible, there would have been no need to add Accents in the first place, since anyone would have already had their position sufficiently perceived. We ourselves were once under the persuasion that a certain stretch of verses, if written out without Accents, could be furnished with those same Accents according to certain rules without looking at the book; but it often happened that we discovered our judgment concerning the proportion of the distinction was something else <insert>entirely</insert> <note>The sentence concludes on the following page with the word "judgment"</note>.

Regarding Silluk <note>the accent marking the end of a biblical verse</note>, such a variety of patterns occurs here that, unless the analogy found in other cases came to our aid, the matter would be altogether hopeless. Therefore, we must speak of each of the "Lords" <term>Lord: a primary distinctive accent that governs the punctuation and melody of a phrase</term> in detail. To do this concisely:

I. We will indicate "Ministers" <term>Minister: a servant accent that leads into a "Lord" accent</term> sometimes with small circles (o.), minor distinctives with small crosses (x.), and major distinctives with obelisks (†.) for the reader. 

II. Words that do not have more than one syllable before the tonic accent—or have none at all—we shall call "Laboring" <note>original: *Laborantes*; these are short words where the lack of preceding syllables makes it difficult to place multiple accent marks</note>.

III. When a "Lord" uses a minor sub-distinctive and then requires a further sub-distinctive below that, we will call such a sequence "mediated"; however, when it uses both its minor and its major sub-distinctive directly, we call it "immediate."

IV. When we refer to a word as "second" or "third," it should be counted backward (as the nature of accentual analysis requires) from the word carrying that "Lord" accent under which the others fall.

### ON THE PRESENCE OF MERKA-MAHPAKH AND ATNAKH.

VI. In the middle division of a whole Poetic verse <note>The "Metric" or "Poetic" books are Psalms, Proverbs, and Job, which use a different system of accents than the rest of the Bible.</note>, if that division occurs beyond the fourth word from the Silluk <note>the end-of-verse marker</note>, the Merka-Mahpakh ֓ is used; the "Archduke" Atnakh ֢ then mediates between the Merka-Mahpakh ֓ and the end: *Ps. 24:8, Ps. 70:5.*

VII. In the middle division of a verse, Atnakh is used if it occurs on the fourth word from the Silluk, or even the third—unless the last words are "laboring" <note>original: 'laborent'; this means the words have too few syllables to support the usual accentual pattern</note>, *Prov. 26:16, ch. 21:16, Psalm 72:20, Ps. 121:4.* Or sometimes it is used beyond these: *Psalm 22:21, Ps. 86:4, Ps. 28:8, Prov. 15:16, Job 5:19.*

VIII. Sometimes a Geresh-marked Revia <note>original: 'Resia Gereschatus'</note> alternates for Atnakh and employs, as the situation requires, its sub-distinctive and subordinate accents, both regular and irregular: *Ps. 31:23, Ps. 35:24, Job 34:10.*

### ON THE DOMINION OF MERKA-MAHPAKH AND ATNAKH.

IX. *Merka-Mahpakh* and *Atnakh*, when they are to place the first distinctive mark on the third word from themselves, follow a regular sequence, both where they require only the first [subordinate] and where they require a major one as well. Compare regarding the former: *Psalm 11:15, Ps. 123:4, Ps. 53:3, Ps. 100:3*; regarding the latter: *Ps. 3:5, Ps. 34:18, Ps. 118:23, Job 10:12, Ps. 71:60, Job 35:10.* The same is true when the distinction is mediated after the first word. See regarding the former: *Psalm 31:3, Ps. 57:5*; regarding the latter: *Psalm 19:5, Ps. 86:12, Job 19:3.*

X. *Merka-Mahpakh* at other times, instead of a minor ֓, places before itself the immediately greater ֢ with a preceding "servant" <note>a conjunctive accent</note> ֧. Compare *Ps. 29:2, Ps. 78:50, Ps. 87:4, Ps. 9:21.* In such cases, Zarka <note>original: 'Sarka'</note> is sometimes placed before it, which surpasses the Revia itself in its power of division. *Ps. 72:9.*

XI. *Atnakh*, when "laboring" in place of a preceding Tipcha in the second position, requires the vicarious minister Munach along with a preceding Munach, while the rest of the sequence remains intact. See *Ps. 2:4, 113:4, 41:2, 15:3, 12:24, 104:31.* This is also what the "vicarious Lord" <note>the secondary distinctive accent</note> imitates with its minister Merka.

XII. In the secondary division of the Atnakh <note>original: 'Atnachi'; the Atnakh is a major mid-verse pause</note>, sometimes the <term>Revia</term> <note>a "Lord" or pausing accent</note> or a substitute **Revia** acts in place of the immediate sub-divider, and the **Shalshelet** <note>a rare, triple-rising musical accent</note> divides secondarily; the latter can again be preceded by the sub-dividing **Pasek** <note>original: 'Pfik'; a vertical line used to ensure a distinct pause between words</note>. *See Psalms 72:3; Proverbs 6:27, 1:9.*

### CONCERNING THE OTHER LORDS, AND CERTAIN OTHER MATTERS.

XIII. The **Geresh-marked Revia** <note>original: 'Refia Gereschatus'</note> rarely establishes a connection of multiple words by itself, and sometimes substitutes the "servant" **Merka** <note>a connecting accent</note> for a sub-distinctive pause; the others generally follow a regular sequence. Thus, for a single-dividing **Revia**, *see the examples in Psalms 22:24, 72:7, 79:9, 71:6, 78:31, 110:6;* for a double Revia, *Psalms 2:11, 79:1, 141:4.* For a single-dividing **Zarka** <note>original: 'Sarkæ'</note>, *Psalms 79:6, 35:27, 32:7, 143:10, 29:2, 78:38;* and for a double Zarka, *Psalms 32:6, 75:9.* For a single anterior **Tipcha**, *Psalms 16:19, 27:1, 98:1, 12:5;* for a double, *Job 32:1;* otherwise, the **Tipcha**, when about to divide in the second word from itself, also substitutes a "servant" preceded by the assistant **Mahpakh**, combining it with the fellow-servant **Zarka** where possible. For the **Pazer**, *Psalms 39:13, 141:5, 28:5, Proverbs 30:8, Psalms 22:25, Proverbs 25:19.*

XIV. In a domain that is to be divided secondarily twice, the **Revia**, **Zarka**, and anterior **Tipcha** have the **Pazer** in the first position, preceded by a **Pasek**, *Psalms 140:6, 126:2, 5:12;* and in an immediate division, it is sometimes permitted to repeat the **Pasek** in place of a greater **Pazer**, *Psalms 67:8, 44:3, 40:6.*

XV. In a phrase of two words, the following "Lords" <note>pausing accents</note> place a sub-distinctive accent before a servant: a non-laboring **Silluk** <note>'non laborans' means the word has enough syllables to carry the full musical weight</note> takes a **Geresh-marked Revia**, *Psalms 27:5, 38:23.* The **Merka-Mahpakh** takes a **Revia**, *Psalms 27:14, Job 30:13.* The **Atnakh**, especially when affecting a polysyllabic word or words joined by a <term>Maqqaf</term> <note>a Hebrew hyphen</note>, takes an anterior **Tipcha**, *Psalms 75:17, Job 37:16.* Finally, the **Revia** and **Zarka** take a **Pasek**, *Psalms 28:9, Job 33:27, Proverbs 24:24, Psalms 12:2.*

XVI. A single-word Psalm title and the word **To the Chief Musician** <note>original Hebrew: 'למנצח' (La-menatzeach)</note> are mostly joined with the following word, even if they do not logically cohere.

XVII. A **Pasek** preceded by the "servant" **Kadma** <note>original: 'Kadmatus'</note> is otherwise usually a **Mahpakh-associated Pasek**.

XVIII. The servant **Mahpakh** (and sometimes **Merka**) places a **Zarka** before itself on the same word (acting as a fellow-servant): if before the stress of the word there is a letter with a long vowel or a substitute for a long vowel, *Psalms 10:3, 5;* as well as in words with a **Maqqaf**, whether explicit or implicit, *Psalms 49:14, 62:8.* The same applies to **Merka**, *Psalms 18:2.* A preceding **Revia** prevents this, provided its domain does not consist of several words.

XIX. The **Mahpakh-associated Pasek** is sometimes omitted, leaving only the **Mahpakh**.

XX. The "Lords" that can be repeated under the same ruler in the poetic meter are **Revia**, **Pazer**, and **Pasek**.

### PRACTICE.
### *Psalm 1:1*

The present verse has the <term>Merka Mahpakh</term> <note>A compound musical accent used in poetic books</note> in the place of a more absolute distinction. The <term>Atnakh</term> <note>The primary divider of a verse, acting like a semicolon or period</note> mediates between this and that in the latter part of the section, according to Observation 6. The last word, therefore, from which we begin here, has the <term>Silluk</term> <note>A vertical line under the last word of a verse</note>, which is the terminator of all verses in both prose and meter. The preceding particle **not** <note>Hebrew: *lo*</note> has its serving accent joined to it, specifically a lower <term>Munakh</term> <note>A "servant" accent that connects its word to the following one</note> because it is a monosyllabic word (see page 12, Note a). The preceding word **scorners** <note>Hebrew: *letsim*</note> would require the smaller sub-divider <term>Pasek</term> <note>original: 'Pfik'; a vertical line separating words to ensure a pause</note> under the Silluk, but instead it has the greater <term>Revia Geresh</term> <note>A complex pausing accent</note> according to Observation 1; and the word preceding that has a <term>Merka</term> as its servant, joined to it by grammatical governance. Furthermore, the Atnakh of the word **stood** <note>Hebrew: *amad*</note> would require a Munakh on the preceding joined particle **not** <note>Hebrew: *lo*</note> according to page 12. But in the copies I have been able to compare, there is a Merka. The preceding word **sinners** <note>Hebrew: *hatta’im*</note> has the ordinary minor sub-divider <term>Tipcha</term> <note>A pausing accent meaning 'handbreadth'</note> (see page 26), and the word **and in the way** <note>Hebrew: *u-ve-derekh*</note> has its servant Munakh; in some Biblical copies, a <term>Revia</term> is also added simultaneously, but it is missing in others. In prose, otherwise compare Observation 11. Finally, the word with the Merka-Mahpakh, **wicked** <note>Hebrew: *resha’im*</note>, is preceded by its usual servant <term>Yerakh</term> <note>A connecting accent shaped like a crescent</note> on the word **in the counsel** <note>Hebrew: *be-atsat*</note>, and thus its minor sub-divider <term>Zarka</term> <note>A musical accent meaning 'sprinkler'</note> ordinarily precedes on the word **walked** <note>Hebrew: *halakh*</note> with its servant Merka on the joined particle **not** <note>Hebrew: *lo*</note> (page 13). And the Zarka begins the mediated domain and places a Pasek before it on the word **blessed** <note>Hebrew: *ashrei*</note>, appearing as a <term>Mahpakh</term> here because it is without a preceding servant (Observation 17). And the preceding word, which is more perceptibly separated, **the man** <note>Hebrew: *ha-ish*</note>, now has the greater sub-divider Revia (page 26), and by right, the preceding **blessed** has its servant Mahpakh (page 13); but in many copies, it is destitute of both vowels and accents.

Concerning the dual system of accents in the Decalogue <note>The Ten Commandments, which in Hebrew Bibles often feature two sets of musical accents: one for private study and one for public recitation</note>, we desire a closer field of discussion, for until now the celebrated Buxtorf has not been fully satisfied. It has always been a matter of debate <note>original: ἐξαγώνιον (exagōnion)</note> among us that the double accentuation of the Decalogue implies a different connection of the command and the verse. For some commandments, being quite long, could not be included in a single verse in equal proportion to the others; hence they are often arranged through several <term>pericopes</term> <note>discrete sections or units of text</note>, with the accentuation of the commandment nevertheless continuing through all those verses. Thus the First Commandment continues through four sections from the second, and the third through three. 

On the other hand, some commandments, due to their brevity, were not sufficient for a whole verse; hence, through a unique connection of accents, they were compacted into a single verse, and yet, because they are distinct in themselves, they were equipped with other distinct accentuations. Where, however, a commandment neither exceeds nor falls short of the quantity due to a legitimate section, the accentuation of the commandment and the verse is the same.
