# First Treatise

And although those ancients remained only in the simple way of nature, they nevertheless discovered things that we, though now occupied with so many matters, could scarcely have imagined with our own wits. This happens because the nature and generation of things in the world now seem cheap and simple to us. Therefore, we direct our intellects not toward known things, but toward such things that cannot, or can only with great difficulty, be done. For this reason, it happens to us that it is easier to devise subtle things—and we can imagine things that the Philosophers themselves did not know—than it is to reach the true course of nature and the meaning of the Philosophers.

And so the nature of man is such that he neglects those things he knows and always seeks others; much more so the human intellects to which nature is subjected. For example, you see any <term>Artisan</term><note>Latin: Artifex; referring here to a skilled craftsman or an alchemist practicing his craft.</note> who, when he has already reached the highest perfection of his art, seeks other things, or misuses his skill, or simply stops. In the same way, noble nature always acts, even into the very <term>Iliadum</term><note>A term used by Paracelsus to describe the ultimate limit or the first matter of the universe; the "greatest term."</note>, that is, the ultimate boundary; afterward, it ceases. 

For a certain promise was implanted in nature from the beginning, so that through a complete course it might come to better things and have full rest, toward which it strives with every effort, and rejoices in its end. -># On the impossibility of nature.<-

we have arrived. For the art of *Chemistry* <note>original: "Chymiæ"</note> has now discovered such subtleties that scarcely greater ones could be found; and it differs from the Art of the ancient Philosophers just as a Clockmaker differs from a simple Blacksmith. And although both work with iron, nevertheless neither would understand the other's labors, even though both are Masters in their own craft. Only one thing is lacking to us: that we might know that which they themselves achieved, namely the Philosophers' Stone <note>original: "Lapidem Philosophorum"</note> or the Physical Tincture. While we seek that, we find other things; and if human procreation were not so common, and nature did not maintain her law in that matter, we would now scarcely avoid going astray. I say, therefore, that NATURE is one, true, simple, and whole in its being, which God made before the ages, and enclosed a spirit within it. Know, moreover, that the boundary of nature is God, who is also the beginning of nature; for no beginning is finished except in Him in whom it begins.

### First Treatise

I said there is only one [nature] through which God does all things, not because God would not know how to work without it (since He himself made nature and is omnipotent), but because it pleased Him so, and so He acted. All things proceed from that one nature, nor is there anything in the world outside of nature. And although sometimes abortions <note>In this context, "abortions" refers to failed natural processes or malformed results, like a stunted plant or a failed alchemical transmutation.</note> occur, this is not the fault of nature, but the fault of either the Craftsman or the location. This nature is divided into four places <note>Likely referring to the four Aristotelian elements: Fire, Air, Water, and Earth.</note>, in which it performs all these things that appear and are in shadow; for things are rather in shadow than truly appearing. It is changed into male and female, and is likened to *Mercury* <note>original: "Mercurio"; in alchemy, Mercury represents the fluid, transformative principle that can bridge different states of matter.</note>, because it joins itself to various places, and according to the locations of the earth it produces good or bad things, although there are no truly "bad" places in the earth, as it seems to us. 

However, there are only four qualities, and these are in all things, yet they do not agree; one always exceeds another. Nature, however, is not visible, though it acts visibly, for it is a volatile spirit <term>spiritus volatilis</term> <note>A subtle, active essence that moves through matter to animate it.</note> which performs its office in bodies. It has its place and seat in the divine will. For us in this place, it is useful for nothing else except that we might know its locations, which are more suitable or closer to it; that is, so that we may know how to join thing to thing according to nature, lest wood be mixed with man, or an Ox or Animal with Metal, but rather that each thing should act upon its own kind, and then nature will also perform its duty. The place of nature is no other than, as stated above, in the will of **G O D**. Searchers of nature <note>original: "Scrutatores naturæ"; a term for natural philosophers or alchemists who "scrutinize" the world's secrets.</note> ought to be...
