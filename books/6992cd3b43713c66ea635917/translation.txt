### Page Thirty-Two

According to the records, Mount Songgao is the **Central Peak** among the **Five Sacred Mountains**; it is located within the Henan Prefecture. The *Explanation of Names* states that *Song* means high; it is also written as *Song* [崧]. A mountain that is both massive and tall is called *Song*. The *Comprehensive Discussions in the White Tiger Hall* <note>a record of a scholarly debate in 79 AD</note> asks: "Why is the word 'High' specifically added to the Central Peak?" Because this peak resides in the center of the four cardinal directions and is exceptionally lofty, it is called Mount Songgao.

When **Emperor Wu of the Han dynasty** ascended the Central Peak, he reportedly heard voices calling out "Ten Thousand Years!" <note>a ceremonial wish for long life, usually reserved for the Emperor</note> to him. Consequently, he assigned three hundred households to maintain the sacrificial rites and named the area **Chonggao Town**. During the reign of **Emperor Ling** of the Later Han dynasty, the name was changed back from Chonggao to Songgao.

Dai Yanzhi’s *Record of the Western Expedition* notes: "The eastern part of the mountain is called **Taishi**, and the western part is called **Shaoshi**. They are seventeen *li* <note>approximately 5.3 miles</note> apart, though 'Song' remains the general name for the whole range." They are called *Shi* <term>Room or Chamber: referring to the cavernous formations found within the peaks</term> because there are stone chambers beneath each of them. 

**Shaoshi Peak** stands eight hundred and sixty *zhang* <note>a traditional unit of height; this figure is likely symbolic of its great elevation</note> high, with a summit area of ten square *li*, and it stands adjacent to Taishi Peak. Various Daoist texts state: "Traveling twenty *li* northeast from the Temple of the Mountain God, one arrives at a mountain named **East Longmen**." To its east lies **Mount Santai**. Long ago, when Emperor Wu of the Han was on an eastern inspection tour, he passed this mountain and saw women practicing the arts of immortality; he subsequently named the mountain after them. To the south lies the site associated with **Xu You** <note>a legendary hermit from the time of Emperor Yao, famous for his purity and refusal of political power</note>.

The mountain stands high and majestic, isolated on all four sides. To its north flows the **Ying River**, which is the very place where the sage-king **Yao** attempted to recruit the hermit **Xu You** <note>Legend says Xu You was so committed to his reclusive life that he washed his ears in the Ying River to "cleanse" them after hearing Yao’s offer to give him the throne</note>. The remains of the ancient ritual altars and embankments are still there.

In ancient times, **Prince Jin**, the son of **King Ling of Zhou**, played the *sheng* <term>a reed pipe mouth organ</term> to mimic the call of a phoenix while wandering between the Yi and Luo rivers. The Daoist master **Fuqiu Gong** led him up Mount Song, where he remained for over thirty years, traveling back and forth to **Mount Weishi**, which lies just to the west of Mount Song.

During the Han dynasty, a Daoist priest brought **pattra seeds** <term>seeds of the fan palm, originally from India; their leaves were used as a surface for writing Buddhist scriptures</term> from a foreign land and planted them at the western foot of Mount Songgao. There are four of these trees, which are distinct from all other local timber; they bloom three times a year with white flowers that are fragrant and beautiful.

Traveling five *li* south from **Yanshi** along the Luo River, then crossing the river and proceeding ten *li*, one reaches the tomb of **Prince Luling of the Tang** <note>referring to Li Xian, who reigned briefly as Emperor Zhongzong before being deposed and titled Prince of Luling</note>. The ceremonial stone pillars still stand. Among them is a tall, large stele, though its inscriptions have eroded over time and are no longer legible. 

Proceeding thirty *li* further, one reaches the mouth of **Calyx Ridge** (E-ling), where two mountains stand facing each other and the rocky path is rugged and steep. This pass was carved out during the visit of **Emperor Gaozong of the Tang** to the Shaolin Temple. Following the mountain path for eight *li*, one arrives at the **Shaolin Temple**, situated at the northern foot of Mount Shaoshi. There is a stele there recording that the temple was built by **Emperor Xiaowen of the Northern Wei** for the foreign monk **Batuo** <note>also known as Buddhabhadra, the first abbot of Shaolin and a master of dhyana (meditation)</note>.

...the inscription. When **Emperor Taizong of the Tang** was still the Prince of Qin, he issued a decree to the monastery monks. It stated that **Wang Shichong** <note>A general who briefly claimed the title of Emperor during the transition from the Sui to the Tang dynasty</note> had "usurped power and held no rightful claim." Later, Master **Yuanchang** and other monks captured that wicked rebel and purified this **Pure Land**. To this day, the **Shaolin monks** are renowned for their martial prowess.

The monastery’s **Main Buddha Hall** now serves as the Lecture Hall. Behind the hall, to the left, stands the **Standing in the Snow Pavilion**. In ancient times, the monk **Huike** <note>revered as the successor to Bodhidharma</note> once attended to **Bodhidharma** <term>the legendary monk credited with bringing Zen (Chan) Buddhism to China</term> in the depths of the snow. He refused to leave until he finally inherited his teachings. Among monks today, he is honored as the **Second Patriarch**.

Walking a hundred paces out from the western corridor, there is an open terrace. Here, the foreign monk **Bhadra** <note>the first abbot of Shaolin, also known as Batuo</note> translated scriptures. It is said that sweet dew fell from the heavens during his work, which is how the terrace received its name.

Ascending two *li* <term>a traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly half a kilometer</term> to the northwest, one reaches the **Hermitage of Bodhidharma**. There stands a cypress tree said to have been planted by the hand of the **Sixth Patriarch** <note>Huineng, a pivotal figure in Zen history</note>. Further up is a chamber; outside its door, six large characters are carved into the stone: **"Hermitage of Bodhidharma Facing the Wall."** These were written by **Cai Bian** of the Song Dynasty. Inside the chamber is a statue of Bodhidharma. Before the altar is a stone <unclear>platform</unclear> only two feet high and of equal width; the likeness of Bodhidharma is vividly visible upon it <note>This refers to the legend that Bodhidharma's shadow was burned into the rock after nine years of meditation</note>.

Behind the **Scripture Pavilion**, there is an ancient locust tree, ten *zhang* <term>a unit of length, roughly 3.3 meters</term> high and thirty feet in circumference. Leaving the monastery and traveling fifteen *li* to the east, then turning east for about twenty *li*, one reaches the **Ordination Altar Monastery**, which is now in ruins.

In a building of three bays, there stand four stone steles inscribed with the "Tea Proclamation" <term>Tea Proclamation (*Chabang*): In Zen Buddhist monasteries, these were formal notices or rules regarding the tea ceremony, often displayed as calligraphy.</term> written by the Yuan Dynasty scholar **Li Puyuan**. The characters are four inches in diameter, vigorous and robust, and truly delightful to behold. Beside them is a stele commemorating the imperial decree for the Ordination Altar <term>Ordination Altar (*Jietan*): A sacred platform used for the ceremony of admitting monks or nuns into the Buddhist order.</term>, dating to the second year of the Dali era of the Tang Dynasty <note>767 CE</note>.

A hundred paces to the left of the temple is the Dharma Hall of **Huishan Temple**. Inside is a stele originally from the **Songyang Temple** of the Later Zhou Dynasty. An inscription on the back states it was "moved to this location in the first year of the Linde era of the Great Tang" <note>664 CE</note>; it can truly be called ancient. To the east of the Buddha Hall, a spring emerges from the mountains. It provides water only for the temple monks to draw; for the purpose of irrigation, the water sinks into the ground and does not flow outward.

Traveling eight miles <note>The *li*, or Chinese mile, is traditionally about 500 meters.</note> to the southeast, one reaches the **Shrine of the God of Mount Song**. The shrine is located beneath **Canopy Peak**. Mount Song stands majestically at the center of the Middle Kingdom, with a circumference of nearly two hundred miles. The surrounding mountains stand in a circular formation, appearing like a group of children gathered around an elder. 

**Yellow Canopy Peak** is situated at the southeastern extremity of the mountain. Outside, there is a rock formation with a horizontal tablet inscribed: "Grass Ginseng." Entering through the triple gates, one finds the "**Stone Cypresses**" <note>These are ancient, gnarled cypress trees so old and weathered that they are said to resemble stone.</note>. Some are reclining or bowing, some are tilting, and others are twisting and coiling. Some appear angry and grasping, some seem ready to leap into the air, and others spread out like curtains or canopies. There are nearly two hundred of these trees. Within the triple gates, the shrines of the Four Sacred Peaks are arranged to the left and right.

There is also the **Temple of the Exalted Deity**, where the walls were once painted with images of the ancient Masters of Thunder; however, through the passage of many years, these paintings have peeled and faded away. West of the Shrine to the God of the Peak lies the **Imperial Shrine**, which houses numerous stone steles and inscriptions from successive dynasties. 

Traveling two hundred paces to the south, then turning northeast for another two miles <note>The *li*, or Chinese mile, is traditionally about 500 meters.</note>, one enters **Hut-Rock Temple** at the northern foot of the mountain. This was originally the hermitage of **Lu Hong** <note>A famous scholar-recluse of the Tang Dynasty (8th century) who famously refused imperial summons to live in the mountains.</note>, though it later became a residence for monks. Four miles above this site is the **Upper Hut-Rock Temple**, where a waterfall plunges dozens of feet down the stone cliffs.

Three miles to the north lies the abandoned **Songyang Belvedere** <term>Belvedere (*Guan*): A Taoist monastery or temple.</term>. The only remains there are two stone steles: the "Record of the Belvedere" by the Tang official **Li Linfu** and the Yuan Dynasty "Record of the Hall of the Five Patriarchs." 

At this site stand three ancient cypress trees. The largest is so massive it would take six people joining hands to encircle it. An old stone inscription records that **Emperor Wu of Han** <note>Reigned 141–87 BCE; a pivotal figure in Chinese history who significantly expanded the empire and promoted Confucianism and Taoism.</note> bestowed upon this tree the title "Great General." The next largest tree, about four people's girth, is called the "Second General" by the local Taoist priests. Their forms are so magnificent and strange that they defy being captured by a painter's brush. The third tree is about three people's girth. I have seen many ancient cypresses in my life, but never any as wondrous as these.

Three miles further east, situated on the southern slope of the mountain, is the **Palace of Exalted Fortune**. Behind it lies **Mount Longevity** <note>Wansui Shan.</note>. Long ago, when Emperor Wu of Han visited this place, he heard a voice from the sky calling "Long live the Emperor!" three times, and thus he named the mountain...

In this location, the **Belvedere of Long Life** <term>Belvedere (*Guan*): A Taoist monastery or temple complex.</term> was established. During the Tang Dynasty, its name was changed to **Taiyi** <note>original: "太一" (The Supreme One), referring to a primordial deity in ancient Chinese cosmology and Taoism.</note>. During the Song Dynasty, the imperial portrait of **Emperor Zhenzong** <note>Reigned 997–1022 CE; he was known for his deep patronage of Taoism and for performing the prestigious Feng and Shan sacrifices at Mount Tai.</note> was enshrined here. To maintain the site and offer prayers for the Emperor’s longevity and blessings, officials such as the Superintendent and the Supervisor were appointed to serve at this temple.

Within the mountain grounds, there were originally three famous pavilions: the **Chess-playing Pavilion**, the **Chupu Pavilion** <note>Chupu was an ancient board game popular in the Han and Tang dynasties, somewhat resembling backgammon or dice games.</note>, and the **Floating Cup Pavilion**. Of these historical structures, only the "Floating Cup Stone" remains today <note>The "Floating Cup" (泛觴, *Fanshang*) refers to a literati tradition where wine cups were floated down a winding stream; guests would drink from a cup if it stopped in front of them while composing poetry.</note>.

Descending the mountain and traveling one mile to the east, one can view the **Mother of Qi Stone**. The legend surrounding this stone is recorded in the *Huainanzi* <note>A 2nd-century BCE philosophical text that preserves many ancient Chinese myths.</note>. The tale relates that the wife of the legendary **Yu the Great** turned into this stone while giving birth to their son, Qi, after seeing her husband transformed into a bear. However, the details of this story are considered unorthodox <note>original: "不經" (bujing), meaning the story is not found in the Confucian classics and is thus viewed with skepticism by traditional scholars.</note>, and scholars of later generations have often debated or sought to clarify the truth of the matter.

Mount Sumen is located in Hui County of Weihui Prefecture. It is also known as "Hundred Gates Mountain," for it is the peak from which the <term>Hundred Springs</term> <note>A famous cluster of freshwater springs at the foot of the mountain.</note> first emerge. The terrain there is characterized by deep, secluded rocky valleys and dense, lush forests. Many famous scholars of antiquity chose to live here in seclusion.

I ascended to the Hundred Springs Academy to solemnly pay my respects to the statue of the <term>Ancient Teacher</term> <note>Referring to Confucius.</note>. Afterward, I climbed Mount Sumen and lingered at the "Whistling Terrace," deeply moved as I contemplated the character of <term>Sun Deng</term> <note>A 3rd-century hermit known for his mastery of the "I Ching" and his spiritual whistling.</note>. Historical records state that Sun Deng lived in an earthen cave on Mount Sumen and loved to study the <term>Book of Changes</term> <note>original: "易" (I Ching), an ancient Chinese divination text and philosophical foundation.</note>. He often played a single-stringed zither. 

When the poet <term>Ruan Ji</term> <note>One of the Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove.</note> went to visit him and spoke with him, Sun Deng never offered a word in reply. As Ruan Ji retreated halfway down the mountain, he heard a sound from the peak like the cry of a <term>luan-bird or phoenix</term>; it was the sound of Sun Deng whistling. <term>Ji Kang</term> <note>Another of the Seven Sages.</note> followed him for three years, yet Sun Deng still did not speak to him.

The "Immortal’s Crag" is located on Mount Sumen, where there is an "immortal’s footprint" carved into the rock, with the inscription: "Footprint of the Immortal." Below the Whistling Terrace, winding westward for about a mile and a half, one reaches the "Nest of Peace and Joy" belonging to <term>Shao Yong</term> <note>A renowned Northern Song Dynasty philosopher and cosmologist, often referred to by his posthumous name, Kangjie.</note>, where I bowed before his portrait. 

Shao Yong was a native of Fanyang. In his youth, he followed his father, Shao Gu, and moved to Gongcheng. He eventually built a house above the Hundred Springs and named his dwelling the "Nest of Peace and Joy." He wore simple cloth robes, ate basic vegetarian food, and personally cooked over the fire to provide for his father. At that time, Li Zhicai happened to be serving as the acting magistrate of Gongcheng.

Now, the study of <term>Principles of Numerology</term> <note>original: "數學" (Shuxue). In this context, it refers to the specialized cosmological and mathematical study of the *Book of Changes* (I Ching) pioneered by the Song dynasty philosopher Shao Yong.</note> is taught here. Later generations built a shrine in this location specifically for his veneration. 

Exiting the "Nest" <note>Referring to the "Nest of Peace and Happiness" (*Anle Wo*), the famous retreat of Shao Yong.</note> and traveling east then south, one enters the **Cave of the Three Immortals**. Inside are statues of the three "Perfected Men," **Qiu, Liu, and Tan** <note>Likely referring to Qiu Chuji, Liu Chuxuan, and Tan Chuduan, prominent figures of the Quanzhen school of Taoism.</note>; this is said to be the place where they cultivated their spiritual essence. Some people mistakenly believe this cave to be the earthen hole where Sun Deng once lived, but this is an error.

Descending from the cave, one visits the **Gathering of Immortals Palace of Bestowed Blessings**. This site is actually adjacent to the left side of the Academy and was formerly the site of the Cai family garden. Beneath the western wall of the Academy stand fourteen ancient white poplar trees; they are so massive they could hide an ox from view and are likely several hundred years old.

Passing the Academy, one reaches the **Pavilion of Thinking of Parents**, located at the foot of Mount Sumen. Xu Shike, who served as the Governor of the Weihui Circuit, erected this pavilion in memory of his father, **Lu Zhai** <note>The literary name of Xu Heng (1209–1281), a highly influential Neo-Confucian scholar and official of the Yuan dynasty.</note>, who had once lived in the city of Gongcheng.

Past the pavilion lies the **Temple of the Source of the Wei River**. Throughout the generations, the temple’s deity has been known as the "Duke of the Spiritual Source," and the main hall is named "Clear Radiance." The reason this prefecture and county were given the name **Hui** <note>Hui County (Huixian).</note> is derived from this hall's name. During the Song and Yuan dynasties, the deity was granted the title of King. At the beginning of the Hongwu era <note>The reign of the first Ming Emperor (1368–1398).</note>, the sacrificial rites were reformed, and the title was changed to the **God of the Wei Source**.

**Yandang Mountain** is located ninety *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance, approximately 500 meters or 1/3 of a mile.</note> east of Yueqing County. This mountain is of exceptional and singular beauty, renowned throughout the world. To the south lies **Lotus Blossom Peak**, and below the peak is the **Lotus Blossom Post Station**. From the front of the mountain, one overlooks the vast sea. 

For a long time, however, the location of the **"Goose Marsh"** <note>original: "Yandang"</note> and the **"Dragon Pond"** <note>original: "Longqiu," referring to the famous waterfalls of the region</note> remained unknown. Later, because of woodcutters, it was first discovered that there is a large pond at the summit of the mountain. According to tradition, this is the "Goose Marsh" from which the mountain takes its name. Below it lies a pool of water. 

The various peaks are all steep, rugged, and strangely formed, rising a thousand feet high. With their arched cliffs and massive valleys, they are unlike any other mountains. However, once one enters the valleys, the peaks loom majestically, piercing the very clouds. 

If one seeks the underlying principle of their formation, it must be that the earth and sand were washed away by the powerful rushing of great waters within the valleys, leaving only the solid rock standing tall and majestic. Places such as the **Greater and Lesser Dragon Ponds**, **Water Curtain Fall**, and **New Moon Valley** are all caverns hollowed out by the action of water. Viewed from below, they appear as towering cliffs and precipitous walls; yet if one views them from above, they are level with the surrounding ground. Even the very tops of the various peaks align with the level of the land at the mountain's summit.

**Southern Yandang Mountain** is so named to distinguish it from the Yandang Mountain located in Yueqing <note>Yueqing is the site of "Northern Yandang," one of China's most famous sacred mountains.</note>. It is situated one hundred <term>li (approximately 50 kilometers)</term> southwest of Pingyang. From Qi Ridge in the north to Shi Rock in the south, the entire region is known as Yandang.

Leaving the southern part of Pingyang, one travels by boat past Jing Creek to reach the **Zhijue Zen Forest**. Other formations, too numerous to name individually, take the shapes of ritual jade tablets, ceremonial scepters, spirit mushrooms, or swallows' nests—displaying the very height of nature's craftsmanship.

Beyond these, two stone pillars ten <term>ren (an ancient unit of height, roughly 2.3 meters)</term> tall stand flanking the path. Passing them, one enters the **Stone Column Temple**. 

The temple contains several caves, though travelers consider the Western Cave the finest. It features an upper "stove" and a lower "hollow," where stalactites drip from the rock fissures into a stone trough. This is known as the **Immortal Maiden’s Spring**. In the front of the cave, a "moon window" has been hollowed out of the rock, allowing one to climb up and look out at the surrounding views.

Walking half a *li* to the right, two sheer walls pierce the heavens, with a natural stone bridge spanning across them. Looking down from here, one gazes into deep, dark pits that seem immeasurable. It is said that the summit of the peaks also contains a **Wild Goose Lake** <note>the namesake of the mountain, where migratory birds were said to gather in the marshes</note>, but the path is so overgrown and lost that there is no one to ask for directions. In general, while this southern range is considered smaller than Northern Yandang, in terms of its jagged, towering peaks and steep, soaring beauty, the two Yandang ranges are truly without peer.

# The Temple at the Heart of the River

The Temple at the Heart of the River <term>Jiangxin Temple: Literally the "Temple at the Heart of the River," this is a renowned Buddhist site located on a famous islet in the Ou River.</term> is situated upon a solitary islet in the midst of the Great River of Yongjia <note>Yongjia is an ancient name for the region of modern-day Wenzhou in Zhejiang Province.</note>. The city walls embrace nine mountains, forming the Nine Dipper Gates <note>The city of Wenzhou was traditionally laid out to mirror the stars of the Big Dipper constellation to ensure spiritual protection and prosperity.</note>. The islet and the city stand facing one another, while the tides of the sea gnaw at the island’s base day and night. To the left and right of the temple, pagodas were constructed to anchor the site <note>original: "futu" (浮圖), a Chinese transliteration of the Sanskrit "stupa," referring here to the prominent East and West pagodas on the island.</note>, appearing like the two horns of a dragon.

When the blue sky is still and the vast sea is without waves—as the spring days begin to lengthen and the clear river resembles a polished mirror—the shadows of the pagodas are cast toward the east. Ferries dock and depart amidst a competitive clamor. To lean against the railing of the Pavilion of River and Sky and gaze out is a true joy. 

As for the lights from the hearths across the river, scattered like stars in the heavens, they are best viewed on a cloudy evening. When the sea tides rush and surge, sounding as they head west, one feels as though the islet itself is floating up and down along the Silver River <term>Silver River: The traditional Chinese name for the Milky Way galaxy.</term>; for this experience, a moonlit night is most sublime.

According to the local gazetteer <note>original: *Junzhi* (郡志)</note>, the **Misty Rain Tower** <term>Yanyu Lou (煙雨樓): A famous landmark in Jiaxing, named for the ethereal beauty of the landscape when shrouded in mist and light rain.</term> is located at Racecourse Lake in **Jiaxing**, situated about one *li* <note>approximately 500 meters</note> southeast of the prefectural seat. During the **Five Dynasties** period <note>907–960 CE, a period of political division between the Tang and Song dynasties</note>, **Qian Yuanliao**, the Prince of Jingling and Military Governor of Zhongwu, constructed a raised platform here to serve as a place for sightseeing and looking out over the landscape.

The structure was abandoned during the Jianyan era <note>1127–1130 CE</note>. Later, during the Jiading era <note>1208–1224 CE</note>, **Wang Xilü**, the Minister of Personnel, retired from office and returned to his hometown. He used the original site to rebuild the tower. As members of the local gentry began to visit and stroll about the grounds, various officials successively expanded, managed, and renovated the site, transforming it into the preeminent scenic wonder of the region.

At the end of the Yuan dynasty, during the **Yang-Miao Rebellion** <note>A conflict involving local leaders Yang Wan and Miao Jun during the chaotic final years of Mongol rule</note>, the tower was finally destroyed. In the *wushen* year of the Jiajing era <note>1548 CE</note>, the Prefect, **Zhao**, dredged the inner moat <term>Neihuang (內隍): The inner defensive ditch or moat surrounding a city's walls.</term> and ordered the common people to use the excavated earth to heighten the old foundation. Subsequently, the local scholars and commoners gradually repaired and maintained the site, and it slowly regained its former appearance.
