# Preface

I have previously collected six "Books of Genius." Their titles are the <term>Zhuangzi</term> <note>The foundational Daoist philosophical text</note>, the <term>Li Sao</term> <note>The "Encountering Sorrow," a classic of ancient Chinese poetry</note>, the *Records of the Grand Historian* by Sima Qian, the regulated poetry of Du Fu, <term>Water Margin</term> <note>The classic novel of outlaw heroes</note>, and the <term>Romance of the Western Chamber</term> <note>A celebrated dramatic play of young love</note>. I have already, perhaps presumptuously, added my own commentary and corrections to them. Gentlemen throughout the land have all judged me to be a discerning critic. 

Recently, I took up the *Records of the Three Kingdoms* to read. I see that it presents the truth and points to facts; it is not a work of mere fabrication. It is worthy of serving as the counterpart to the official Classics and Histories. From this point of view, nothing is more marvelous than the Three Kingdoms. 

Some might say: "From the Zhou and Qin dynasties of antiquity, to the Han and Tang dynasties later on, all those works that <term>expand upon history</term> <note>original: "演義" (Yanyi), the genre of historical fiction that elaborates on factual records</note> are similar to the Three Kingdoms. Why is the Three Kingdoms alone considered marvelous?" 

I reply: The Three Kingdoms is a uniquely marvelous drama of contending for the world throughout all of history; and the one who wrote the *Three Kingdoms* is a uniquely marvelous hand at crafting fiction throughout all of history. In other eras, the struggles for the world were relatively mundane, and the writers who took those events to create chronicles were also relatively mediocre. Therefore, they cannot be ranked alongside the Three Kingdoms. I have often surveyed the situation of the Three Kingdoms’ struggle for the world and sighed—

<meta>This page continues the preface by the critic Jin Shengtan. Having established the "Romance of the Three Kingdoms" as a "work of genius" comparable to the Classics, the author now shifts to a philosophical reflection on the "Heavenly Fate" that dictated the unique historical structure of the Three Kingdoms period.</meta>

The transformations of Heavenly Fate are truly unpredictable. When Emperor Xian of the Han lost his grip on power and Dong Zhuo usurped authority, heroes rose in unison and the "four seas" <note>A traditional term for the known world or the Chinese empire</note> reached a boiling point of turmoil. Had the Imperial Uncle Liu <note>Liu Bei, a distant relative of the Han imperial family</note> earlier enjoyed the "harmony of fish and water" <note>A metaphor for the perfect relationship between a ruler and his advisor, specifically Liu Bei and Zhuge Liang</note> and secured the lands of Jing and Xiang, he might have driven deep into the Yellow River and Hebei regions, then annexed Huainan and Jiangdong, subduing the lands of Qin and Yong in succession. Had he done so, it would have merely been a repeat of Emperor Guangwu’s restoration <note>Referring to the founding of the Eastern Han after a period of usurpation</note>, and we would not have seen the marvelous fickleness of Heavenly Fate.

However, Dong Zhuo did not succeed in his usurpation and was executed. Then Cao Cao managed to "kidnap the Son of Heaven to command the lords" <note>original: "挾天子以令諸侯"; Cao Cao’s strategy of using the Emperor as a puppet to justify his military campaigns</note>. Though the Emperor’s title was hollow, the imperial calendar remained unchanged. The Imperial Uncle was forced into a winding path of exile and could not early establish the "Great Righteousness" for the world. Meanwhile, the lands north and south of the Yangtze River were already seized by the states of Wu and Wei. Only a corner in the southwest was left for the Liu clan to find a foothold. Yet, had Kongming <note>The courtesy name of the brilliant strategist Zhuge Liang</note> not emerged to help in the East during the Battle of Red Cliffs, and struck down the enemy in the West at Hanzhong, then the regions of Han and Yi would have likely fallen to Cao Cao. If the state of Wu could not stand alone, it would have become another case of Wang Mang usurping the Han <note>Wang Mang was a Han official who founded the short-lived Xin dynasty in 9 CE</note>. Then, too, the propensity of Heavenly Fate for change would not have been seen. Following the escape at Huarong Pass and the retreat of the "chicken rib" campaign...

<column-break/>

...the protagonists returned to a state of "tripod legs" <note>original: "鼎足" (dingzu); a metaphor for the three-way balance of power, like the legs of a bronze tripod vessel</note>. With their authority and strength matched, the "Three-Part Balance of Power" was finally achieved. Consider that Cao Cao: throughout his life, his crimes were overflowing, and he earned the wrath of both gods and men. He was denounced in proclamations, cursed, stabbed, poisoned, burned, robbed, had his beard cut and his teeth broken, and fell from horses into trenches. He faced death numerous times, yet in the end, he escaped it. His enemies were many, but his supporters were also many. 

It is as if Heaven intentionally wanted to complete this "Three-Way Split," and thus deliberately left this "treacherous hero" <note>original: "奸雄" (jianxiong); a famous epithet for Cao Cao, meaning a man of great talent but manipulative character</note> to act as a parasite upon the Han. Furthermore, Heaven gave birth to Zhou Yu to serve as an opponent for Zhuge Liang, and later gave birth to Sima Yi to trouble the successors of the Cao family. It seems Heaven feared that one of the "tripod legs" might break prematurely, and so brought forth these geniuses to maintain the standoff. 

Since ancient times, there have been those who carved out territories or split kingdoms into twelve, seven, or sixteen states; there were the Northern and Southern Dynasties, the Eastern and Western Wei, and the Former and Latter Han. In those times, gains and losses were sudden; states survived or perished in the blink of an eye. Some lasted less than twelve years, others not even a few months. Never has there been a situation as marvelous as the Three Kingdoms' struggle for the world, where for sixty years they flourished together and fell together. Now...

<summary>The author argues that the Three Kingdoms period was a unique work of "Heavenly Fate," which intentionally kept great rivals like Cao Cao, Liu Bei, and Sun Quan in a sixty-year stalemate to create a historical drama unlike any other.</summary>

<meta>This page continues the preface by the critic Jin Shengtan. Having established the "Romance of the Three Kingdoms" as a work of genius, he now shifts to a philosophical reflection on the nature of history and "Heavenly Fortune," arguing that the specific outcome of the Three Kingdoms period—a three-way stalemate rather than a quick reunification—was a unique masterpiece of divine design.</meta>

Beholding the marvels of this book is enough to make scholars feel delighted when they read it, and even the unlearned folk living in the back alleys feel delighted when they read it. Heroes and great men read it with delight, just as common and ordinary people read it with delight as well. 

In ancient times, when <term>Kuai Tong</term> <note>A famous strategist during the transition from the Qin to the Han dynasty</note> spoke to <term>Han Xin</term> <note>The brilliant general who was instrumental in founding the Han dynasty</note>, the theory of the "tripod’s three legs" <note>The concept of dividing the empire into three equal, competing powers to ensure stability or survival</note> already existed. At that time, however, Han Xin was already a subject of the Han, and it was a matter of righteousness that he could not betray them. <term>Xiang Yu</term> <note>The chief rival of the Han founder; known for his immense strength but lack of political wisdom</note> was crude and lacked strategy; although he had a <term>Fan Zeng</term> <note>A wise advisor known as "Father Fan"</note>, he could not use him. Thus, the momentum of the world had no choice but to unify under the Han. 

Generally speaking, the possibility of the Han’s tripartite division was merely a faint glimmer when the Han house was first rising, yet it finally came to fruition during the Han house’s decline and weakness. Furthermore, the <term>High Ancestor</term> <note>Emperor Gaozu, the first emperor of the Han dynasty</note> rose to power by becoming the "King of Han," while the <term>First Sovereign</term> <note>Liu Bei, the central protagonist of the novel and founder of the Shu-Han state</note> perished despite being the "King of Han." One was able to return and pacify the "Three Qins," <note>The central territories of the former Qin dynasty in the Wei River valley</note> while the other could not seize even an inch of the Central Plains. It is as if Heaven’s creation of the Han was designed to begin in one way and end in another, with the final outcome long ago established in the unseen realms. This led to the people and events of that era—with their varied talents, strategies, and unique circumstances—being vastly different from anything else in thousands of years. Is this not the most marvelous of Heaven's works? The author of this <term>Romance</term> <note>Yanyi, referring to the novel</note> used the brilliance of his writing 

<column-break/>

to pass down the marvels of those events. He did not need to resort to fabrication; he merely wove together the actual facts and interlaced the beginning and the end, so that nothing in it was not marvelous. This is yet another marvel of human affairs never seen before. 

It is simply that while the events are marvelous and the book is marvelous, if there were no one to stand up and offer a critique—or if there were a person, but their heart was not a "brocade heart" and their mouth was not an "embroidered mouth" <note>Traditional metaphors for supreme literary talent and eloquence</note> such that they could not express the inner thoughts of the ancients on their behalf—then this book would eventually be grouped with all those other "romances" from the eras before the Zhou and Qin or after the Han and Tang. How then would people know of its marvels or believe in them? 

I have often wished to search out these marvels to set them right for the world, but I was struck by illness and did not succeed. Suddenly, on a friend’s desk, I saw the manuscript of <term>Master Mao's</term> <note>Mao Zonggang, the scholar who, along with his father Mao Lun, edited and commented on the definitive version of the novel used today</note> commentary on the *Records of the Three Kingdoms*. Observing the swiftness of his pen and the agility of his mind, I found that he had already reached the same conclusions as my own heart. I marveled at it repeatedly. From now on, I know that the title of the "First Book of Genius" indeed belongs to the *Three Kingdoms*. Therefore, I have written these few words of preface and given them to Master Mao, so that on the day of publication, they may be placed at the head of the volume.
