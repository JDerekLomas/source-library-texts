# THE QUESTION

## *Concerning Imperial or Royal power: namely, can Kings or Princes, by any right or title and with a clear conscience, alienate their Citizens and Subjects from the Royal Crown and subject them to the rule of another private Lord?*

BECAUSE AMONG ALL THE GRIEVANCES that Kings can impose upon their peoples, that one seems more grievous—indeed, the most grievous of all—wherein they <term>alienate</term> <note>In this legal context, to "alienate" means to transfer the ownership or jurisdictional control of a group of people or a territory to someone else, effectively "selling" the subjects.</note> Citizens or townspeople from the Royal Crown and subject them to the rule of another private Lord, whether by way of sale, gift, delegation, grant, or privilege: it is asked whether Kings can do this by any right or title, and with a clear conscience? 

The reason for doubting this arises because, while the matter is extremely burdensome and highly damaging to the people, it is nevertheless seen happening everywhere in kingdoms without any moral hesitation. There are, certainly, those who strive to favor courtiers, palace officials, and associates of Kings who are seeking such gifts for themselves. These people, not considering the hardships and intolerable troubles of the inhabitants of the kingdom as they ought, bring forward several special reasons for the affirmative side <note>The "affirmative side" refers to the legal argument that a King *does* have the right to give away his subjects to others.</note>, through which they try to either justify or at least put a good face on these aforementioned transfers of ownership. One of these reasons is if the Sovereign wishes to reward the merits and services of those who have served him, particularly those who have served the state exceptionally well, to whom the Sovereign and the whole <term>Commonwealth</term> <note>original: "Respublica"; referring to the state or the organized political community.</note> are indebted for <term>antidora</term> <note>original: "antidora"; a term derived from Greek meaning "reciprocal gifts" or "recompense for services rendered."</note>. *See law: "but also"* <note>original: "l. sed &"; a standard shorthand for a specific citation in Roman Civil Law.</note>

It is in this relationship that the entire logic of obligation is founded: a citizen is "born to" a homeland and is held to expose himself to danger for its safety because he receives his entire civil good from it, and not from himself, nor in himself, because he is imperfect on his own. Therefore, the city is bound to the kingdom by a lesser right than the citizen is bound to his city or homeland; thus, the obligation is not the same in both cases.

Therefore, the citizen is bound to the city by a greater bond than the city is held to the kingdom.

## §. XXXIII.

1 *An explanation of the legal rule: What one can do through another, etc.*

<margin>I</margin> Regarding the eleventh reason: That which a person can do through another, he can also do by himself.
