Is it not a matter of the greatest importance to overcome these monsters of the schools and wrestling-grounds? Alas, with how many siege engines will they assault me, with how many efforts will they attack me, with how many shames will they pursue me? # P R E F A C E

<margin>catchword: I am</margin>

It is only right that for His sake I have willingly stirred up such great enemies against myself; I will also gladly fall before I desert Him. And I want you to know that I have not been led to write these things by hatred, nor ambition, nor deceit, nor error; neither has a sacrilegious greed nor the arrogance of a wicked mind driven me to this. Rather, my cause is both most just and most true: namely, that I see many people becoming so insolent through human disciplines and sciences <note>original: "humanis disciplinis scientiisque"</note> that they look down upon the eloquence of the Sacred Scriptures and the Canonical Writings of the Holy Spirit. Because these scriptures lack the ornaments of flowery words, the force of formal logic <note>original: "viribus syllogismorum"</note>, affected persuasions, and the foreign erudition of the Philosophers—being instead established simply in the operation of virtue and naked faith—these men therefore not only despise and scorn them as if they were unrefined and ignorant, but even attack them with a certain contempt. We see others who, although they seem more pious to themselves, strive to approve and confirm the laws of Christ by attributing more to the decrees of the Philosophers than to the holy Prophets, Evangelists, and Apostles of God, even though the latter are further apart from the former than a double octave <note>The term "disdiapason" refers to a musical interval of two octaves, used here to illustrate a vast, irreconcilable distance.</note>. # TO THE READER.

If anyone perceives this, they immediately proclaim him a scandalous heretic, offensive to pious ears, and one to be consumed by fire and flames. Therefore, these so-called giants <note>original: "gigantes" — a reference to the mythological giants who rebelled against the gods, here used to describe arrogant scholars</note>, who are enemies of the sacred scriptures, must be attacked; their camps and fortresses must be stormed. It must be shown how great the blindness of men is, that with so many sciences and arts, and with so many masters and authors of each, they always wander away from the knowledge of the truth. 

How great also is their rashness, how arrogant their presumption! They prefer the schools of the Philosophers to the Church of Christ, and they place the opinions of men above, or equal to, the Word of God. Finally, what an impious tyranny it is to imprison the talents of students to predetermined authors, and to take away from disciples the freedom of investigating and following the truth. 

Since all these things are so manifest that they cannot be denied, I must be granted pardon if I seem to speak too freely, or perhaps more bitterly <note>original: "amarulentius"</note>, against certain types of disciplines or their Professors. *Farewell*.

**V**enerable is the opinion, and almost the concordant and unanimous sentiment of all who practice Philosophy, by which they judge that any science whatsoever—according to the capacity and value of each—brings something of divinity to man himself, so much so that these sciences can often carry men beyond the limits of humanity into the choirs of the blessed Gods. From this source have come those various and innumerable praises of the sciences, by which every man strives to prefer those arts and disciplines—in which he has sharpened the powers of his mind through long exercise—above all others, and to extol them even above the heavens themselves, using a speech no less ornate than it is long. 

I, however, persuaded by reasons of a different kind, judge that nothing more pernicious, nothing more pestilential to the life of men and the safety of our souls can happen than these very arts and these very sciences. And therefore, I believe one must act in a reversed order: it is my opinion that the sciences themselves should not be extolled with such great proclamations, but for the most part should be censured. Nor is there any science which lacks a just cause for reprimand, nor again any which deserves praise for its own sake, unless it borrows that praise from the integrity of its possessor. <note>Agrippa argues that knowledge is not inherently "good," but only as good as the person using it.</note> Moreover, I wish my opinion to be received by you with such modesty, that neither...

Do not think that I wish to rebuke others who hold a different opinion, nor that I arrogantly claim anything for myself. Therefore, you will grant me pardon for dissenting from the rest in this matter, until we begin to examine this opinion through each branch of learning in alphabetical order—not merely with common arguments drawn from the surface of things, but with the firmest reasons drawn from the very innermost guts <note>original: "visceribus," literally "entrails" or "vitals"</note> of the matter. I will not use those arguments of Demosthenes <note>The famous Greek orator (384–322 BCE)</note> or Chrysippus <note>A Stoic philosopher known for his dialectical skill</note>, nor that eloquence which would be a disgrace to me as a professor of Sacred Scripture—as if I were following the deceits of speech like flatteries for a lover. For it is proper for a professor of Sacred Scripture to aim to speak correctly, not eloquently; to seek the truth of the matter, not the decoration of speech. For the seat of truth is not in the tongue, but in the heart. Nor does it matter what kind of speech we use in telling the truth, for a lie requires eloquence and "trappings of words" <note>term: "phaleratis," referring to the ornamental metal disks on a horse's harness; here meaning flashy, superficial rhetoric</note> so that it can worm its way into the minds of men. But the speech of truth, as Euripides writes, is simple, seeking neither deceit nor pigments.

If, therefore, I offend your most delicate ears with this undertaken task, stripped of all the flowers of eloquence (which must now be not so much neglected as condemned by us), I pray you bear it with that patience with which a certain Roman Emperor once halted with his army to listen to a poor woman; and as King Archelaus <note>King of Macedon (r. 413–399 BCE), a patron of the arts</note> sometimes wished to hear hoarse men with unpleasant voices, so that by listening to them he might later take more delight in the eloquent. Remember that sentiment of Theophrastus <note>Successor to Aristotle in the Peripatetic school</note>: that even unrefined men can speak among the greatest and most elegant of people, provided they speak with faith and reason. 

And so that I do not leave you hanging with your ears open as if yawning, I shall now bring into the open those tracks and signs by which I, like a hunter with dogs, have tracked down and caught this opinion of mine: provided I first warn you that all sciences are as much evil as they are good. # IN GENERAL

It will scarcely ever be necessary to imbue the mind with that discipline of all sciences which is almost inscrutable (as the **Averroists** <note>Followers of the 12th-century Islamic philosopher Averroes, known for their emphasis on reason and the "double truth" theory.</note> contend). Even **Aristotle** himself says that happiness is something very common, which all can easily attain through a certain discipline and diligence. He calls this an easy and, as it were, common faculty for contemplating the most noble object of all, namely **God**. Indeed, this act of contemplation, so easy and common to all, is not perfected by syllogizing <note>The use of formal logic or deductive reasoning.</note> and demonstrating, but by believing and worshiping.

What, then, is this "happiness" of the sciences now? What is this praise and blessedness of the wise men and philosophers, with which all the schools ring and resound? They echo with the praises of those whose souls the inhabitants of the underworld hear and see being torn apart by dire torments. **Augustine** saw these things and feared, crying out that phrase of **Paul**: *The unlearned rise up and take heaven by force, while we with all our knowledge are drowned in hell.* <note>original: "Surgunt indocti & rapiunt cælos, & nos cum scientia nostra mergimur in infernum." A famous paraphrase of Augustine's Confessions, referencing Matthew 11:12.</note>

If one must dare to speak the truth, the tradition of all sciences is so dangerous and inconstant that it is far safer to be ignorant than to know. **Adam** would never have been driven from the paradise of blessedness unless he had learned to know good and evil with the serpent as his teacher. And **Paul** judges that those who wish to know more than is proper should be cast out from the Church. When **Socrates** had examined almost every discipline, he was only then judged by the oracle to be the wisest of all men when he openly confessed that he knew nothing.

The knowledge of all sciences is so difficult—not to say impossible—that a man's entire life would fail before even the smallest principle of a single discipline could be perfectly investigated. This is what **Ecclesiastes** <note>The biblical Book of Ecclesiastes, traditionally attributed to Solomon.</note> seems to affirm to me here when he says: *I understood that of all the works of God, man can find no reason for those things which are done under the sun; and the more he labors to seek, the less he finds; even if the wise man says he knows, he will not be able to find it.* Nothing more pestilential can happen to man than knowledge: this is that true plague which overthrew the whole and entire human race because of one [tree], which expelled all innocence, and made us [subject] to so many kinds of sin and to death.

So great, moreover, is the ample liberty and free amplitude of truth, that it cannot be grasped by the speculations of any science, nor by any urgent judgment of the senses, nor by any arguments of logical artifice, nor by any evident proof, nor by any demonstrating syllogism <note>A form of logical reasoning where a conclusion is drawn from two premises</note>, nor by any human discourse of reason—except by faith alone.

<margin>The sciences borrow from one another</margin>

The Astrologer <note>In this period, "Astrologus" often referred to both astronomy and astrology</note> places his faith in both. Thus, from Philosophers they finally become torturers and executioners; they wish to force us by violence to admit that which they ought to have taught by reason.

<margin>The danger of learning to the State</margin>
Accordingly, nothing is more hostile or ruinous to the Commonwealth <note>original: "Reipub." or Respublica</note> than letters and the sciences. In a state where men are endowed with erudition and science, affairs are mostly conducted according to their judgment as if they were "wiser" men. Relying on the simplicity of the common people and the ignorance of the multitude, they usurp all the authority of the Magistrate for themselves alone. From this, the state of the Commonwealth migrates from a popular one into an oligarchy <note>A government ruled by a small, elite group</note>, and from there, divided into factions, it easily passes into a tyranny. No one is read to have ever obtained such power anywhere in the world without science, without doctrine, and without letters—except for the one Dictator Lucius Sylla <note>Lucius Cornelius Sulla (138–78 BC), a Roman general and statesman</note>, who alone seized the Commonwealth without learning. Yet the Commonwealth owes this much to his ignorance of letters: that he finally laid down his tyranny of his own accord. Furthermore, all sciences are nothing but the decrees and opinions of men, as harmful as they are useful, as pestilential as they are wholesome, as bad as they are good; nowhere complete, but instead ambiguous and full of error and contention. We shall now show this to be the case by proceeding through each individual discipline of the sciences.

# ON LETTERS

## CHAPTER II.
### On the Elements of Letters.

FOR in the first place, who does not see that the arts of speaking well—I mean Grammar, Logic, and Rhetoric, which are merely the vestibules and doors of the sciences, rather than the sciences themselves—often bring no less pestilence than pleasure? For these arts, there is no other rule of truth than the whims and will of whoever first established them. This is fully evident from the very invention of letters, which are the first elements and instruments of those arts. The first of these were the Chaldean <note>The language of ancient Mesopotamia</note>, invented by Abraham, as Philo <note>Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish philosopher</note> says, which were used by the Chaldeans, Assyrians, and Phoenicians. However, there are some who say that Rhadamanthus first gave letters to the Assyrians. After these, Moses delivered letters to the Jews, though perhaps not in these characters which they use today, which are thought to be the invention of Ezra, whom they consider the writer of almost all the books of the Old Testament <note>original: "veteris instrumenti"</note>. Furthermore, a certain Linus of Chalcis brought letters from Phoenicia to the Greeks, but these were Phoenician until Cadmus, the son of Agenor, gave them new letters in a different character, sixteen in number. To these, Palamedes added four during the Trojan War. Later, Simonides of Melos added just as many. To the Egyptians, a certain man named Memnon first delivered the practice of writing through the figures of animals, as is seen on obelisks; but Mercury first gave them letters—specifically that Mercury who Lactantius says was the fifth Mercury, whom Vulcan, the son of the Nile, succeeded in the kingdom. To the Latins, however, a woman named Nicostrata, surnamed Carmenta <note>A goddess of childbirth and prophecy associated with the Roman alphabet</note>, delivered letters. Thus, seven types of letters excelled in antiquity: Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Syrian, Chaldean, Egyptian, and Getic <note>Referring to the Goths or Thracian tribes</note>. Regarding these, Crinitus <note>Pietro Crinito, a Renaissance humanist</note> reports that he read the following verses in a very ancient Codex:

> Moses was the first to plow the Hebrew letters.
> With a sagacious mind, the Phoenicians founded the Attic ones.
> Those which we Latins write, Nicostrata produced.
> Abraham found the Syrian, and likewise discovered the Chaldean.

Indeed, other peoples and barbarian nations have discovered new letters in more recent times. There also exist other letters of the Franks, whose inventor was a certain man named Doracus, but these are far different from the characters of Wastaldus; and yet others were invented by Hicho the Frank, who came from Scythia to the mouths of the Rhine with Marcomir. Furthermore, Bede described some letters of certain Northmen <note>original: "Normanorum"; likely referring to Vikings or Scandinavians</note>, though the inventor is unknown. 

Many other nations in a similar way either established new characters for themselves or, having received them from the ancients, partially changed or corrupted them—just as the Dalmatians did with Greek letters, and the Armenians with Chaldean <note>A term often used historically for Aramaic or Syriac scripts</note>. Moreover, the Goths and Lombards defiled the forms of the Latin letters. Many ancient letters have besides perished, such as those of the ancient Etruscans, which—according to the testimony of Pliny and Livy—were once held in great esteem among the Romans. Images of these are still seen on ancient monuments, but they are utterly unknown. 

For when the Romans were formerly laying waste to the world, they violently imposed the use of their own letters upon those they conquered, rendering the ancient and proper letters of each nation obsolete. Thus the letters of the Hebrews perished during the Babylonian captivity, and their language was corrupted by the Chaldeans. But from these principles—I mean letters and languages—which are so inconsistent and changeable at all times, there emerged first Grammar, and then the other arts of speaking well which we have mentioned. ## CHAPTER XX.

### *On the Art of Acting.*

The art of acting <note>original: "Histrionica"</note>, moreover, is a dance of imitation and a skill of demonstration, explaining things conceived in the mind through graceful gesturing. It represents all manners and emotions so clearly and transparently that every spectator—even though the actor speaks nothing—clearly understands him through his various movements and gestures. So great is the excellence of acting that there is absolutely no need for interpreters; for it represents the old man, the boy, the woman, the slave, the maidservant, the drunkard, the angry man, and the differences and passions of all characters with such pleasant gesturing that even a spectator at a distance who does not hear the play <note>original: "fabulam"</note> understands its plot from the actor's dance alone.

Hence we read that actors were held in no small esteem. It is well established, on the authority of Macrobius <note>A 5th-century Roman writer known for his work on Saturnalia</note>, that Cicero used to compete with the actor Roscius—who was also very dear to the dictator Sulla—to see whether the actor could express the same idea more often through various gestures, or whether Cicero himself, through his abundance of eloquence, could deliver it with different speech. This rivalry led Roscius to write a book in which he compared eloquence with the art of acting.

Truly, the city of the people of Marseilles <note>original: "Massiliensium"</note>, according to the witness Valerius <note>Valerius Maximus, a Roman historian</note>, stood as such a guardian of dignity that it gave no entrance to actors; largely because their plots for the most part contain acts of debauchery, lest the habit of watching such things should also create the license to imitate them. Furthermore, to practice acting is not only a shameful and wicked occupation, but even to watch and be delighted by it is disgraceful; for indeed, the delight of a wanton mind falls into the category of a crime. Finally, no name was once more infamous than that of actors, and by the laws themselves, anyone who had danced a play in the theater was barred from holding honors <note>This refers to "infamia," a loss of legal and social standing in Roman law</note>.

# CHAPTER XXI.
## On Rhetoricism.

There was also a form of "Rhetorical Dance," not unlike theatrical acting but more restrained, which Socrates, Plato, Cicero, Quintilian, and many of the Stoics considered quite useful and even essential for an orator. This practice consists of appropriate bodily gestures and a composed arrangement of the face and frame; it relies on the vigor of the eyes, the gravity of the countenance, and a tone of voice suited to individual words and sentiments, combined with effective bodily movement—though only to the extent of meaningful gesturing. 

However, this dancing or theatrical style of rhetoric eventually began to fall out of favor among orators. This shift followed the advice given by Augustus to Tiberius, that he should speak with his mouth and not with his fingers <note>Augustus was advising Tiberius to rely on the clarity of speech rather than the overly expressive hand gestures typical of stage actors</note>. Today, it has been almost entirely abolished, except that it is still practiced by certain "little brothers" of the stage (even though actors were once expelled from the Church and denied the holy sacrament of Communion). We see some of these men today proclaiming popular sermons to the common people from the pulpit with a marvelous straining of their voices, shifting expressions, wandering and bold eyes, tossing arms, dancing feet, swaying hips, and various poses—turning, circling, leaning back, leaping, and gesturing with the whole body, as if the restless mind were spinning the body along with it. Perhaps they are mindful of the opinion of Demosthenes, who (as recorded in Valerius Maximus) when asked what was most effective in speaking, replied: "Hypocrisy" <note>original: "hypocrisin." In this context, the Greek term refers to "delivery" or "acting" rather than moral insincerity</note>. When asked a second and third time, he said the same, suggesting that almost the entire power of oratory is owed to this. Geometry holds a special praise among the other disciplines because, while there are great and countless disputes among the sects of almost all other branches of knowledge, all geometers everywhere are in agreement with one another. For Plato believes that sight occurs through a "co-brilliance," <note>original: "conclaritatem"; a term referring to the merging of internal and external light</note> namely, that the light flowing out from the eyes meets the light reflected from bodies in the external air. This occurs because the air in between is easily diffused and transformed, extending along with the fire-like power of sight. Galen feels in harmony with Plato. However, Hipparchus says that rays extended from the eyes reach out to those bodies, touching them as if with a certain palpitation, and return the perception of them to the sight. The Epicureans, on the other hand, assert that "images" <note>original: "simulacra"; the Epicurean theory of thin films or atoms shedding off objects</note> of things that appear cast themselves into the eyes. Aristotle, however, judges that these images are not bodies themselves, but reach the sight from visible things through a quality—an alteration of the surrounding air. Porphyry <note>A Neoplatonist philosopher</note> truly believes that neither rays, nor images, nor anything else is the cause of seeing; rather, it is the soul itself, recognizing itself as visible—and since the soul is one in all things, it recognizes itself in everything that exists.

But the Geometricians and experts in optics, agreeing in a certain way with Hipparchus, describe certain cones formed by the coincidence of rays which are emitted through the eyes. From this, the sight comprehends many visible things at once, but those are seen most certainly wherever the rays coincide. Yet Al-Kindi <note>original: "Alchindus"; a 9th-century Islamic philosopher and pioneer of optics</note> teaches something else entirely regarding aspects, while it seems to Augustine that the power of the soul effects something in the eye which has not yet been fully investigated by students of wisdom.

Therefore, this science contributes greatly to detecting the variety, distance, magnitude, and motion of celestial bodies, as well as their reflections and refractions; it also serves architecture in the measuring of buildings. Most closely, however, it brings the greatest ornament to the art of painting and the manufacture of mirrors, so that without it, these arts cannot be perfected. For it demonstrates by what reasoning we may avoid creating images that are either out of proportion or deformed when they are perceived due to distances and heights.

Furthermore, it utterly ruins laws, customs, and whatever arts of human wisdom exist; for when astrology alone is consulted on when, how, and by what means something should be done, it alone holds the scepter over life, morals, the State, and private affairs—as if drawing all authority from heaven—and all other things that do not acknowledge this "patroness" are considered vain. Now indeed, the heresy of the Manichaeans <note>A major dualistic religion founded by the prophet Mani, which taught that the universe was a struggle between light and darkness; the author argues their denial of free will stems from astrological fatalism.</note>, which entirely removes and takes away the freedom of the will, emanated from nowhere else but the false opinions and doctrines of astrologers regarding fate. From the same spring flowed the heresy of Basilides <note>An early Gnostic teacher in Alexandria; his system involved complex hierarchies of spiritual beings.</note>, who declared there were three hundred and sixty-five heavens, made one after another in succession and likeness. He claimed the number of days in a year was a manifestation of these, assigning to each certain principles, powers, and angels, and inventing names for them. He held that the prince of them all was *Abraxas*: a name which, according to Greek lettering, contains within itself three hundred and sixty-five <note>original: "ter centum sexaginta quinque"</note>, which is exactly the number of local positions of those heavens devised by him. 

These things are recounted so that you may know that astrology is also the mother of heretics. Furthermore, just as all the most brilliant Philosophers explode <note>In the Latin sense of "explodere," meaning to hiss off the stage or reject with contempt.</note> this divinatory astrology, so too Moses, Isaiah, Job, Jeremiah, and the other Prophets of the Old Law detest it. Among the Catholic doctors, Augustine judges that it must be expelled from the Christian Religion. Jerome argues that it is a form of idolatry. Basil and Cyprian laugh at it; Chrysostom, Eusebius, and Lactantius refute it; Gregory, Ambrose, and Severianus inveigh against it. The holy Council of Toledo prohibits and condemns it, as does the synod of Martin; it was anathematized by Popes Gregory the Younger and Alexander III, and punished by the civil laws of the emperors.
