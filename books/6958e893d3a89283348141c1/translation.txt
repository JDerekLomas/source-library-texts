The work *On the Nature of Plants* <note>original: "De NATVRA STIRPIVM"</note>, *Most Christian and Most Invincible King* FRANCIS, which was undertaken long ago under your generous patronage, has recently—with that same favor supporting it—been brought to completion <note>literally "brought to the navel," a Roman metaphor for finishing a scroll</note>. Following the ancient custom of authors, I now dedicate it to your most serene majesty. Since the dignity of letters, their excellence, and their authority have always been such that princes considered it both honorable and glorious for such works to be born for them, fostered by them, and dedicated to them: how much more so for you, who are seized by a wonderful delight in celebrated things? For as soon as you have a moment to breathe from those weightier labors, the duties of your empire, and your cares, whatever snatched leisure you obtain, you spend it all industriously listening to the late-night studies <note>original: "lucubrationibus," referring to works composed by lamplight</note> of distinguished writers. Afterward, you recite them with such a ready memory and adorn them with such refined speech, that everything you say makes those works much richer and much better; they become pure wit, pure wisdom. Therefore, without any suspicion of seeking unearned favor, one may testify that you add a great deal of beauty and elegance to literature. You so cultivate and honor all disciplines that they owe no less to you than to the writers themselves; for in your own style of writing and in your *French eloquence*, you match them, and not infrequently surpass them. You alone among princes ensure that their monuments live, flourish, and are read. Thus, the world knows you as the one King endowed with the greatest experience in affairs and with incomparable prudence...

-># TO THE KING OF THE FRENCH<-
<note>original: "AD GALLIARVM REGEM"</note>

...justice, illustrious glory, incredible happiness, admirable eloquence, the most ready wit, and the purest expression. Nor have there ever existed any of the most distinguished gifts of the mind in any princes of any age—even individually—which do not all shine forth more brightly, spread further, and excel together in you. Because of this, it is heard that you have already brought forth eternal titles for yourself among men: for your piety toward your subjects, as a father to all, a patron of studies, and of all the good arts; furthermore, for your greatness, prudence, and humanity. While all may indeed wonder at these titles and those other supreme gifts of nature, they cannot likewise emulate them.

On account of this excellence of yours, I considered that it would go happily enough for these commentaries of mine if you would receive them into your protection from me—who, though absent, am as reverent as if I were present. Truly, they will understand themselves to be sufficiently commended and approved once they have not displeased your judgment, which is by far the sharpest. 

But since the subject matter which I treat has lain unattempted for many centuries, a path more inclined toward forgiveness will be left to me when I falter in such a great crisis of facts and sometimes, if it should so happen, when I am blinded by human darkness. For not everyone "hits the matter on the head with a needle," <note>A Latin proverb meaning to hit the mark exactly or be perfectly accurate.</note> as the saying goes; nor "will the bow always strike whatever it threatens." <note>A reference to Horace, suggesting that even the best efforts sometimes miss the target.</note> Indeed, even *Theophrastus*, and *Dioscorides* (who is preferred by *Galen* in <term>botany</term> <note>original: "re herbaria," the study of plants and their medicinal uses</note>), and among our own writers, *Pliny*, often diverge so widely that they cannot be reconciled by any persuasion of speech. 

And, by Hercules, sometimes in representing the appearance of a small herb, they delineate the characteristics so variously that they seem to demonstrate anything else rather than the plant itself; or certainly the same plant with a completely manifold likeness, such that most everyone doubts whether such a thing could exist anywhere. Therefore, the disagreement of such great men compelled me to go through the vast solitudes of many regions, to wander the pathless ridges of mountains, to survey inaccessible lakes, to scrutinize the hidden fibers of the earth, to follow the gaping caves of valleys, and sometimes to attempt precipices even at the peril of this little body of mine, so that I might grasp by sight—let alone by knowledge—the very things about which I was to write...

-># E P I S T L E<-

<sig>A iii</sig>

*...Nor was there any less labor and diligence required in unrolling the works of authors, especially Theophrastus <note>Theophrastus (c. 371 – c. 287 BC) was a Greek successor to Aristotle, often called the "Father of Botany."</note>: in whose writings one may see the identifying marks of plants so scattered in various places and torn into so many fragments, that it seems as if a complete plant can be found nowhere on earth. Nevertheless, it was necessary to collect all these pieces and recall them to one another so that they might grow together into a single body. Thus, I have reconstructed many shrubs that had been fused into a varied and shapeless mixture of facts, recovering their parts from wherever possible; and by restoring their colors, as it were, I have made them more recognizable. Then—which was by far the most difficult task—after the illustrations of the plants, I placed the things themselves with their specific qualities before the reader's eyes, ensuring every detail was consistent. Next, I endeavored to teach what French name each plant had adopted for itself: specifically, so that your French apothecaries <note>original: "officinæ tuæ Galliæ"</note> might grasp the correct method of naming—showing how many things they name rightly, and how many more they name otherwise and incorrectly—and then by what terms they ought to designate everything properly. Without these names, even if plants are depicted perfectly in every detail <note>original: "ad unguem," literally "to the fingernail," a Latin idiom for perfection</note>, it would hardly be well understood, especially among such a vast family of species, which little plant is intended to be represented and illuminated by its ornaments. For in demonstrating any object, this has always seemed to me the only true method and the most certain system: to show both what it is and what kind of thing it is. If the men celebrated for their art and genius in the past—who, having shaken off the darkness of the previous century and utterly uprooted barbarism, betook themselves to the classical Greek and Latin writers—had successfully followed this path, or to put it more modestly, had even attempted it, they would have better aided our labor; for they discovered many excellent things in other disciplines and deserved well of ours too. But perhaps this help was expected from the nation of physicians, because this seemed to be their own particular field and instruction; although even those seekers of wisdom, whom Greece first called "philosophers," did not reject this duty as something foreign to them, but rather sought no small glory from this study. And even more so did those kings: who, though they had at times become famous for their illustrious deeds, nevertheless set aside the honor of their royal rank to pursue this study in various places...</note>

# TO THE KING OF THE GAULS <note>original: "AD GALLIARVM REGEM"; referring to King Francis I of France</note>

Having surveyed what I said above, [men of old] sought out herbs that were highly useful to the human race. Once found, they handed them down to posterity, sometimes even fighting to claim them as their own with no less vigor than they fought to defend their empires. It was as if they believed they could earn no greater favor from mortality than by leaving behind something they thought would last forever. Thus, by this pledge of glory, the most renowned men live on even today in the grateful memory of mankind. Otherwise, their names would have perished with them as soon as they departed from the living, had they not left their names bestowed upon plants, which will be remembered for an eternal age—so long, of course, as the earth, that faithful and best parent of all things, continues to produce *lysimachia* <note>Loosestrife; named for King Lysimachus</note>, *eupatoria* <note>Agrimony; named for Mithridates Eupator</note>, *gentian* <note>Named for King Gentius of Illyria</note>, *clymenum*, *polemonia* <note>Jacob's Ladder; named for King Polemon of Pontus</note>, and others of that kind which take their names from kings.

What of *Juba*, who was the first to rule both Mauritanias? He is even more memorable for the brilliance of his studies than for his kingdom, and he achieved eternal fame chiefly because he celebrated the herb named *euphorbia* (named after its discoverer, his own physician) with wonderful praises in a volume privately dedicated to it. How much more will you yourself, through the knowledge of so many and such varied plants revived by your command, reap immortal glory? I remain silent regarding the kings *Philometor, Attalus, Hiero,* and *Archelaus*, who, by writing on this subject in Greek, secured perpetual names for themselves.

Indeed, because among the nations of Europe, your France abounds in a wealth of all kinds of plants, and they spring up everywhere, the people have bestowed names upon almost all of them. These are partly distorted from the Greek, partly from the Latin, and partly taken from their own tongue. So much so, that whatever charms us in the sowing, or tempts us as food, or invites the fodder of four-footed beasts, or pleases with a medical effect, can be spoken of in the French nomenclature. It has very often been my pleasure, Most Illustrious Prince, to follow and imitate your common folk in recounting their native and vernacular names. Therefore, I have frequently consulted with carpenters, gardeners, farmers, vine-dressers, and the rustic crowd regarding these names, and I have taken from them those things which suited the matter at hand. I hope, in this way, to provide a model for the experts of other nations and peoples to inquire after the same from their own folk, and to pour into this Latin language those things which they judge best. For those things of theirs [must be translated] by us...

->E P I S T O L A<-
<note>Epistle or Dedicatory Letter</note>

...who would allow themselves to be taught? Therefore, most great Prince <note>Latin: *Princeps*; likely referring to the author's royal or noble patron</note>, may you look kindly upon the fact that I have digested into a compendium those things handed down scatteredly by the ancients, not infrequently illuminating their obscurities and bringing many hidden things into the light. So far am I from attacking the doctrine and erudition of those ancient writers, that I cannot sufficiently admire or venerate them; for they have not only invited us to examine the nature of plants <note>Latin: *stirpium*; referring to stalks, roots, or plants generally</note> through their written monuments, but have even aided us to such an extent that almost everything must be credited to them, especially regarding cultivation, illustrations, and medicinal powers.

Furthermore, I have judged it necessary to represent the most common plants in such great detail, and to indicate those known to everyone with even greater scrupulousness, since the long passage of years has changed them somewhat, and negligence has confounded, overturned, and utterly abolished everything. Besides these, we have depicted many plants entrusted to written records by posterity since that former age—plants which might compete with those ancient ones in importance and potency—so that nothing should be missing from this history. 

And since the subject matter itself did not demand a diligent style of oratory—indeed, rejecting those little flowers and splendors of eloquence, it hardly allowed itself to be adorned, being content only to be taught—I have disclosed this history nakedly and simply, without any dye of words or any allurements of speech. I was little concerned with how elegantly I spoke, provided I discussed the matter truly and faithfully. 

Moreover, lest our writings be poorly received by overly-critical censors <note>Latin: *nasutulos*; literally "those with big noses," a Roman idiom for people who are overly critical or prone to sniffing out faults</note>, and my name be polluted by the contagion of insult <note>Latin: *sugillationis*; a term for bruising or, metaphorically, slander</note> or the suspicion of an ungrateful mind if I always acted without providing evidence, I have praised the authors from whom I have plucked certain things, interpreting the work and study of nature as something held in common with them. Although I have not repeated their names in every single place, lest names repeated in so many spots should produce disgust, I have nevertheless done so in some places—namely, whenever a matter was controversial or seemed worthy of being credited to its specific author. 

Truly, in this whole work, the sum of my prayers has been to refine antiquity with a more modern polish, and to render posterity more venerable and better equipped through the majesty of the ancients. I did this so that the modern work would not become worthless through a contempt for novelty due to a lack of authority, nor would the ancient work fade away in the decay of obscurity for lack of splendor. But now, lest this early-ripened work remain hidden any longer...
