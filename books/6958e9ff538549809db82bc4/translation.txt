Fever is an extraneous heat. We are excused from those things which are customarily said at the beginnings of books, because they were discussed at the beginning of the first *Canon*. We do not intend to focus on childish matters, but on necessary things according to the members of the fourth book. After Avicenna determined in the third book the illnesses appropriate to specific organic members, he saw fit in this fourth book to determine particular illnesses that are not appropriated to a single member.

I also speak of "decoration" <note>cosmetics or physical appearance</note> just as he himself said in the preface of the *Canon*, because this pleased him. He was also able to place the knowledge of fevers and abscesses before the knowledge of diseases of specific members, just as Rhazes <note>original: "Rhaly"</note> did in the eighth and ninth of the *Theory* and in the third and fifth of the *Practice*. First, therefore, it pleased him to determine the illnesses not appropriated to one member; second, concerning decoration, at the beginning of the seventh Section. The first part is in four, just as there are four such illnesses or particular dispositions not appropriated to one member: namely, fever, abscesses, the loss of continuity <note>wounds or fractures</note>, and poisonous dispositions. First, he determines fevers; second, abscesses; third, the loss of continuity; fourth, poisonous dispositions.

The first part is in two: first, he determines fevers regarding their essence, causes, signs, and cure; second, regarding their termination (the crisis). The first is in two: first, he determines fevers in a general sense; second, in a specific discourse. The general things we consider about fever are its essence, its distinction into types, and its existence in a subject according to the aptitude of the subjects. First, he provides the definition and division of fever; second, he shows the bodies susceptible to it; third, he determines the periods of fevers.

Regarding the definition of fever, it should first be considered that many definitions are given by authors: by Galen in *On the Differences of Fevers*, in the commentary on the *Aphorisms*, and in the *Book of Diet*; also by Johannitius, Averroes in the *Colliget* and the *Canticles*, by Rhazes in the *Signs of the Heart* and the *Theory*, and by Isaac in his discourse on fever. All are good definitions, but this one by Avicenna is more complete. Let us explain it: "Fever" is named from "fervor" <note>boiling heat</note>; in Greek it is called *pyretos* from *pyr*, which is fire. Thus, fever is called a heat of the nature of fire.

<margin>b</margin> **It is heat.** The "Conciliator" <note>Peter of Abano</note> doubts whether fever is heat, since there are "cold fevers," as Avicenna says later. He brings up many childish arguments. We say with Galen that fever is essentially in the genus of "heat contrary to nature," which shows injury to function. If you wish to investigate this, see our question on the subject of fevers, where we deduced that in the heart and the whole body there is a "hot disease" that injures all operations; this disease must be called fever, as there is no more fitting name. Fever is a heat which is a quality, not a body. When it is said some fevers are cold, it is understood either because the matter is cold (like phlegm or melancholy) or because a cold accident follows it, such as shivering.

<margin>c</margin> **Extraneous.** All agree that the "extraneousness" of heat is twofold: one is totally opposed to life, like the heat of the dead; fever is not this, because the works of life continue. The other is an extraneousness opposed to health, which removes the perfection of functions and brings impediment; such is the heat of a feverish person. Therefore, it appears that in a feverish person, the heat is composed of the natural and the contrary-to-nature.

### On the definition and types of fever. Chapter 1.

Fever is an extraneous heat kindled in the heart and proceeding from it by means of the spirit and the blood through the arteries and veins into the whole body: and

A. in such measure it necessarily deprives life. But not every amount of "napellus" <note>monkshood/aconite</note> kills; rather, the works of life stand with it. If it is the nature of heat contrary to nature that it is "febrile heat," it formally impedes the operations of the heart.

B. **Kindled in the heart.** We have already declared that in the whole body there is one natural heat, which is the principle of all operations. This is the health of the complexion. Disordered heat and added extraneous heat is fever. It follows that fever is the primary suffering of the heart, then of the rest of the body. Averroes said well that fever injures all operations. Albert of Bologna says that in every putrid fever, the humors of the heart are in a state of heating. If the heat is in the members, it is "hectic"; if in the spirits, it is "ephemeral."

C. **And proceeding from it into the whole body.** This is common. But it does not seem to proceed to the whole, because sometimes the extremities are found cold. Furthermore, do the blood, veins, and arteries go to all parts of the body? They say that from the inflamed heart, the fever is carried to the whole by means of the blood and spirits in the vessels. Because the heart sends proportionate heat through these, it also sends disproportionate heat through them. A solemn doubt: when a man is near death from a killing fever and the extremities are cold, is he said to have a fever? We say he can be said to have a fever in every part, yet also have a "cold disease" due to the loss of natural heat. A second doubt: can the fever be carried by the spirit alone or the blood alone? Avicenna says "spirit and blood." An external cause can transform the spirit alone (as in ephemeral fever) or the humors alone (as in pestilential fever).

# Book

<margin>a</margin> Suppose that an external heat occurs from the outside, gradually occupying the whole, or finally the whole heart. Take the final state: now the heart is inflamed. Before the heat carries the members away, I ask whether the person has a fever. It is certain that they do, because there is external heat in the whole heart, but the distribution <note>original: "delatio"</note> has not yet occurred. Therefore, those arguments are apparent and resolved by me in our question; for they conclude the opposite of what I believe to be true.

<margin>b</margin> **And it is inflamed.** He declares here that fever is an external heat, and this is what harms natural operations—that is, those which are in us by nature as living beings: natural, and vital. As Isaac <note>Isaac Israeli the Elder, a 10th-century physician</note> says in the declaration of this definition. And Dionysius says it regarding "natural" things, taking "natural" as distinguished against "animal" and "vital" functions. For although all are harmed, the natural ones seem to be harmed more, because the heart is more their principle. Consider this, because it has an opposition depending on the principles of medicine to which we wish to return. And Avicenna <note>Ibn Sina, the primary authority for this text</note> suggests that not just any inflammation is a fever, but inflammation that harms operations. Therefore he adds that it is not the inflammation of febrile heat like the inflammation of a man working or being angry; for the flame in those is not "finalized" <note>reached a pathological extreme</note>, rather they grow warm and thus remain healthy.

### It is inflamed in it with an inflammation that harms natural operations, not like the heat of anger or labor, since it is not finalized and does not impede operation so that it is restrained. And there are some of the greats who divide fevers into two primary divisions: namely, into fever as a disease and fever as an accident <note>a secondary symptom</note>; and they place fevers from abscesses in the category of accidental fever. And the intention of their discourse is that a disease-fever is that between which and its cause (which is not a disease) there is no medium, such as the fever of putrefaction. For putrefaction is its cause without a medium, and putrefaction is not a disease in itself, but rather the cause of a disease. But the fever of an abscess is an accident existing in the affected part, while the abscess follows it; and the abscess is a disease in itself. And to the one contradicting, it is as if he says: if because the fever of an affected part follows its heat and follows from its pain, it is similar to being an accidental fever, then it must be that many ephemeral fevers are accidents; and if it follows the putrefaction which is in...

<margin>c</margin> **And there are some.** He begins to determine the divisions of fevers: first according to the opinion of others, second according to his own and the customary one. The first part has three sections: first he posits their opinion, second he exemplifies it, third he insists against it. He says first that there are "some of the greats," namely Rhazes <note>Al-Razi, 9th-century Persian physician</note> in the 16th book of the *Continens*, second chapter, and the 17th book, fifth chapter.

<margin>d</margin> **And the intention.** He declares what they understand by this: namely, he wants that fever to be a disease whose immediate cause is not a disease. For example: a fever following putrefaction has that putrefaction as its joined cause, and yet putrefaction of a humor is not a disease in itself. But that fever is called an "accident" whose immediate cause is a sickness. For example, a fever whose cause is an abscess; for the fever following an abscess follows something that is a sickness, namely the abscess. For an abscess is a sickness, and this is what he said at the end: the fever of an abscess is an accident of the affected part while it is an abscess.

<margin>e</margin> **And to the one contradicting.** Here he gives an objection against the division—specifically against the claim that the fever of abscesses is an accident. First this, second he shows what should be held in this contradiction. In the first part, he says that he who would wish to contradict would argue thus: if the fever of abscesses is an accident, this is in one of three ways. One is that it follows the heat of the abscess or the pain, and from this it is called an accident. But this is inconvenient, because then many things would be accidents, namely those which arise from pain and heat. For example: from anger, drinking something hot, labor, and similar heating causes, which is nevertheless false. The second way is that the fever of an abscess is called an accident because it follows the putrefaction of the abscess. But for this reason it ought not be called an accident of the abscess, because the abscess as an abscess (which is a composite term) causes that fever accidentally, because it causes it through the putrefaction which is a part of that abscess-forming sickness. But what causes through a part, causes accidentally; or it happens to the abscess that it causes fever because it causes it through putrefaction, which is something external to the abscess insofar as the abscess is a sickness. And thus the abscess causes it mediately, not immediately. The third way is that the fever of an abscess is not called an accident by any of the said ways, but in another way: that the fever of an abscess is called an accident because through the fever itself one comes into the knowledge of the abscess, and we investigate its knowledge through the fever. Just as accidents lead to the knowledge of diseases. And this third way is not valid, because through the fever of putrefaction we investigate the putrefaction itself...

<margin>column 2 upper margin</margin> ...and we arrive at the knowledge of it. And this is understood when he says "but not significantly," meaning it was not understood as an accidental fever through this, but rather through one of those two ways. They did not understand it as an accidental fever except that it follows an abscess—that is, unless it is an accident of the abscess through this. Or because they find the discovery of the abscess through that fever. Therefore the disposition is similar, etc. But it is doubted whether this objection seems to be valid. For if I find an abscess through a fever, then through it I find a disease, and from this it can be called an accident. But if through the fever I find putrefaction, I do not find a disease but a cause, because putrefaction is a cause. And thus such a fever following putrefaction without an abscess is a disease. It must be said that Avicenna leaves the solution of this noted through what he had said: noting that through frequent fever we do not properly find the abscess except the mode of putrefaction of the abscess, not the abscess in itself. For example, a choleric abscess of the liver is not properly found through fever; but when there is fever and other signs, judgment is given that there is an abscess in the liver, then you judge that the matter is choleric, that which putrefies; because that fever is a continuous tertian. You find therefore the putrefaction of the abscess through the fever, then the disposition of putrid fevers without an abscess is similar.

### ...from the abscess: then the abscess is not its primary cause insofar as it is an abscess; rather it extends to the putrefaction which is in it. Therefore its cause, which is essential, is putrefaction; and the abscess is not its cause except by accident. And we say that if they did not mean an accidental fever by this, rather they only understood it because it follows an abscess, they find it through the discovery of the affected part. Similarly, therefore, is the disposition of putrid fevers in comparison to putrefaction. Truly, occupation in such contradictions is that which contributes nothing to the science of medicine, and makes physicians proceed from their art to inquiries which perhaps for a long time divert them from their art. And let us proceed, therefore, according to what is customary. Let us say, therefore, because the fevers of abscesses and obstructions are accidental fevers, and other fevers are diseases, then it is fitting that we say: since all things that are in the human body are of three kinds, namely the containing members, that which is in them of humors...

<margin>f</margin> **Truly, occupation.** He wants that one should not be occupied in this contradiction, because it contributes nothing to medicine alone. For the question is only whether a fever following an abscess can be called an accident; but you know the nature of that fever.

<margin>g</margin> **And the abscessing:** and know the causes and cure, whether it be an accident or a substance, or a disease. It contributes or harms you nothing in medicine. We ought not to dispute about names, as above in the first book.

<margin>h</margin> **And makes them proceed, etc.** Because he will strive to want to interject whether the same thing can be both a disease and an accident; whether an abscess by reason of its totality can cause fever; whether the putrefaction in the abscess which causes the fever is a disease in itself; and many other things to which, when he proceeds, he spends much time, and thus is hindered.

<margin>i</margin> **Perhaps.** Let there not be a man who, by the goodness of intellect and exercise, quickly dispatches himself from these questions; and perhaps it will behoove him to overstep medicine. It is doubted whether the division of fevers into accidental fever and disease-fever is good. It is said that it is, because they find it in the seventh part of the *Aphorisms* <note>of Hippocrates</note>. Also Rhazes in the 16th book of the *Continens*, where he says "I say that this is true and visible, that as often as we divide fever, they are bipartite: whence we say that all fevers will be either from disease or from accident." Avenzoar <note>Ibn Zuhr, 12th-century Andalusian physician</note> posits the same. Dionysius responds: fever is considered in two ways, either in its essence, and thus it is not divided into disease-fever and accidental fever (for what is essentially a disease cannot essentially be an accident, since these are distinct reasons). Or fever is considered in comparison to the physician's knowledge and operation. And thus he can divide the fever. For he will call that fever an accident which follows another disease. For he will cure that fever with the cure of an accident, namely by intending toward the disease which the fever follows. Another view is that which others gave: that this division is good because it is useful to the physician. For in it he varies the cure; and therefore Avicenna immediately concedes this division as customary.

<margin>k</margin> **Let us proceed.** Here he posits the division according to his own opinion: and first he posits the division into primary and principal genera. Second he posits the non-principal divisions. And the first is divided into two. First, by affirming the aforesaid division, he premises the foundation by which he divides fever into primary genera. Second, he makes the division. By "fever of obstructions" he understands when it is immediate, not when it is by means of putrefaction. By "fever of abscesses and obstructions" he understands those following other diseases like a lung ulcer. And what he says, "let us proceed according to what is customary," refers to the said division and to what is to be said. To the said one indeed, because it is customary and useful to the physician. Namely, notwithstanding the contradiction made, we affirm it saying that fevers following diseases are accidents, and others are diseases. But Avicenna does not solve the reason of the contradictor, since he concludes the truth from the nature of the thing; to the physician, however, it is conceded that he may call a disease an accident. And this being affirmed, then let us posit the more principal division. And it appears that it refers to what is to be said, which is customary: namely, that fever is divided into the first three genera.

<margin>l</margin> **Since the cause:** And as if arguing. Just as Galen <note>the Greek physician</note> in the first book *On the Differences of Fevers*, expressing the body in names, and the first natural subjects of heat are three: namely members, humors, and spirits. Therefore there are three genera of fevers according to the different kindling in one of the three subjects. First, therefore, positing the antecedent, he declares it by a simile in the parts of a bath. Second, he posits the general consequence. But it is doubted: since many other things seem to be in us, like hair, superfluities, elements, and others. It must be said that there are only three things in us which are the immediate foundations of natural heat: namely humors, spirits, and members; and therefore according to these three, heat beyond nature is distinguished.

# Treatise I

<margin>a. Humidity. Note: He does not say "earth," so that he might include humors and humidities, and perhaps chyle <note>chyle: a milky fluid containing fat droplets, drained from the small intestine during digestion</note>.</margin>
<margin>b. Diffused vapors. By "diverse vapors" he means those which are not yet spirits—as at the end of the second stage of putrefaction—or those which are corrupt spirits, or not true spirits.</margin>
The subject of the present [discussion] is either extrinsic and not properly [the subject]; properly speaking, surfaces are also not the proper subject of natural heat, nor are parts properly so, except through a certain reduction. From which it can be inferred that a fever ignited in the blood more truly has the essence of a fever than one ignited in yellow bile <note>original: "cholera"</note>, and properly in yellow bile it exists on the surface or similar.

<margin>c. And air. Through this, Avicenna wishes to show that just as one part of a bath—which perhaps seems less significant—can heat the others (as hot air can inflame the walls and the nearby bathwater, even if they themselves were cold, namely the wall and the water), so in the same way one of these parts of ours—for example, the spirits—can inflame the other parts, and thus also the members and the humors. And this is what he adds: "similarly the genus of bodies," because an incorporeal genus is indeed found, such as temperament <note>original: "complexio"</note> and virtue.</margin>
and spirits, the comparison of which is to the walls of a bath. And the hidden humidity, its comparison is to the water of the bath. And the other spirit, vital and natural, and the diffused vapors, their comparison is to the air of the bath. And when the air of the bath is cooled, it is not necessary that it extinguish that which is near it; rather, it is possible for it to remain and return and heat that which is near it. Similarly, there is one of these three genera outside of which no corporeal genus is found in man. ¶ For if a fever is ignited in the radical members by a primary ignition, just as a burning is ignited—for example, as in the walls of a bath, or by a blacksmith's bellows, or by a cook's pot—then that genus of fevers is called **hectic fever** <note>original: "febris ethica"; a fever associated with wasting diseases like tuberculosis</note>. ¶ And if a fever is ignited by a primary ignition in the humors, and then ignites the members from them as it happens—as when hot water is heated in a bath and the bricks are fired because of it, or hot broth in a pot and the pot is heated because of it—then that genus of fevers is called **humoral fever**. ¶ And if a fever is ignited by its primary ignition in the spirits and vapors, and then ignites the members and humors from them—just as it happens when the air of a fire reaches a bath and is ignited in it, heating its air and reaching the water and walls—then that genus of fever is called **ephemeral fever**, since it is ignited in a subtle substance which is resolved with speed. And it rarely passes a day and its night unless it is converted into another genus of fevers. Therefore, the division of fevers is not by a method close to the division occurring through differences. ¶ And fevers are divided in another way. ¶ For it is said that among fevers there are acute fevers, and there are those that are not acute. ¶ And among them are chronic, and those not chronic; and among them are nocturnal, and those are diurnal. And among them are healthy and "right" <note>regular/predictable</note>, and those having horrible accidents <note>symptoms</note>. And among them are remitting, and those that are inseparable. ¶ And among those inseparable ones, there are some which have remissions, and some which have intensifications. And there are those which have exacerbations, and those which are uniform. And there are those which are cold, having rigor or shivering. And there are those which are simple, and those which are composite.

<margin>d. Rhazes <note>original: "Rafis"; 9th-century Persian physician</note>. He declares the conclusion or consequence that gives the mode of each genus of fevers. First, of the hectic, he says: "if it is ignited by a primary ignition," meaning first in the members. He said this to give an example of this fever alone, not complicated by another genus of fevers. He said the same of every other genus, always repeating "by a primary ignition." Hectic (ethica), i.e., habitual, because it is habitually present, or because it possesses the "habit" of the body and its parts. Then he gave an example of humoral fever... He called them "fevers of the humor" to include both putrid and non-putrid ones, although Galen... places *sinochas* <note>continuous non-putrid fevers</note> without putrefaction as intermediates between putrid and ephemeral fever. Then he gave the example of the ephemeral, and gave the reason for its name: because it lasts one day and rarely passes it. It is called ephemeral from the *ephemeron*, a fish that lives for one day, as Isaac says... It is of short duration because the heat is ignited in a subtle thing, i.e., spirits or vapors, which are of a nature for fast resolution. Experience finds that this resolution is completed in one day. Air and water are quickly ignited. One must consider Avicenna's various ways of speaking... for a hectic fever, it suffices for the heat to be ignited in the members; it does not require heat in the humors or spirits... Hectic is when heat is ignited in the radical members, which are properly members as distinct from fat and tallow, and perhaps flesh... But you will ask how hectic is transmitted? It must be said that it requires the spirits and humors to be heated, not for the *existence* of the fever, but for its *transmission*. Transmission is not of the essence of fever, just as being "down" is not the essence of a heavy object, but the potential to be so. In putrid and ephemeral fevers, it is not enough for heat to be ignited in humors or spirits; it must pass to the members, otherwise it would not be a fever since the members are what live. Therefore he said "if it is ignited in the spirits, then ignited in the members," adding "and humors," which was not necessary for the existence of ephemeral fever, though it follows necessarily. He then concluded these things, which will be declared in the question.</margin>

### On the predispositions to fevers. Chapter II.

<image-desc>Large ornamental drop cap 'Q' in red ink with swirling flourishes.</image-desc>

**Q**uestion: Some ask whether the bodies most strongly predisposed to fevers are hot and moist bodies, especially when the moisture is stronger than the heat; and these indeed have foul-smelling sweat, urine, and excrement. ¶ And hot and dry bodies are likewise predisposed to hot fevers; they begin first as ephemeral, then hasten toward putrefaction.

<margin>f. And among them. He presents the seventh division, and it is clear. From the things said above, I want the solution to the question to be noted: whether fever is heat, and whether it is heat beyond nature, and whether it is added, and whether it is a passion of the heart. ¶ It is doubted here whether fever is a single disease. It seems not, because it is a bad temperament, hot and dry... in many places in medicine the subject is multiple and not one, i.e., the body. ¶ To the contrary: because Avicenna says it is heat. Antonius responds that fever, by the mode of its composition, is one disease like an abscess, and therefore dryness stands as the "material" heat... but from its components, it is a composite disease, i.e., hot and dry. Accept this solution if it pleases you. ¶ There is another response by Dy. [Dionysius?] which those of Bologna follow: that fever is one disease because it is either "becoming" or "made." If "becoming," it is one disease because there is one thing moved (the heart) and one thing moving (the body's heating). If "becoming" is one disease, then so is the "made" fever. ¶ Also, when things follow each other in some effect—one by itself, the other by accident—one is not of the essence of the other. Since heating follows fever by itself, but dryness follows the heat, therefore dryness is not of its essence. Against the first: the thing moved is twofold, i.e., the heart as heatable and as dryable. The mover is twofold, i.e., the agent heating and drying (for example, yellow bile). Against the second: the drying of yellow bile does not necessarily follow the heating.</margin>

<margin>e. And they are divided. He presents various other divisions... not in the first or proper way. He presents seven primary divisions: some are acute (absolutely), others not acute (i.e., becoming acute). Others are chronic... This division is not primary because sharpness can exist from accidental diversities like subtlety, heat, the quantity of matter, and location. Second division: some nocturnal, some diurnal. This is also accidental, based on the hour of movement... Third division: some are healthy and "right" (i.e., *synochas*), others are those in which there is doubt due to wicked accidents. This is from symptoms... Fourth division: by continuous and interpolated (intermittent), which is neither primary nor essential. Fifth division: a sub-division in which he hints at three species of *synochas* (continuous fevers): always decreasing (called *paraugmastic*), always increasing (called *augmastic*)... and those standing equally (called *homotenous*)... Sixth division: those that are similar (not having a "type" or pattern) and those having rigor, etc.</margin>

Although heat happens, it does not happen to it (i.e., to the dryness) by itself but from the operation. ¶ Let us therefore state three things. First, that fever is essentially one disease. The reason: fever is a distemper in the natural heat. But for a distemper to exist, the hot quality alone suffices, with every other quality (moisture or dryness) set aside. ¶ Second, that in reality, fever is for the most part found only as a composite disease. It is not "one" in the way whiteness is one quality, for it is not found alone in reality but always joined to another (e.g., hot or cold). The reason: exceeding heat immediately dries, and the drying that is immediately joined to fever is through the resolution of spirits which it immediately causes. One must imagine that in all members there is a mode of natural or healing humidity through the presence of the substance of the spirits, which are moist bodies of the nature of air. Therefore, fever is rarely seen to be free from this drying. This happens not only from a dry cause, but from heat drying the other humidities of the members... Third, we say that if it is possible to imagine a fever being only a distemper in heat without a distemper in dryness, then not every dryness is a disease. If there is a man having many and thick spirits, when a fever arrives, the distemper in heat will stand for some time without the drying of the resolution of spirits. And if someone is of superfluous humidity, the fever will not immediately make him fall into a "dry" disease. Thus, authors who wanted the essence of fever said it is a hot disease; those who saw what happens for the most part in fact said it is a hot and dry disease. Regarding the reason, it must be said that the question does not ask about unity or multiplicity on the part of the subject... but asks about the unity in the essence of fever: whether it is one quality or two.

**And the increase.** He indicates the confirmation of the "increase" from the symptoms and by another reasoning of the matter. From the symptoms indeed when he says: "it is the hour in which the innate heat is moved to resist." By this he wants us to understand that the heat which was previously submerged in the limb (that is, in the place of the matter) is now expanded. It appears like a fire which was previously suffocated under green wood but now conquers and its flame rises high. Therefore, to the touch, the heat is greater and more apparent, the pulse more manifest and consistent, and symptoms appear. Secondly, he identifies the increase from the alteration of the matter when he says "signs of digestion appear," meaning manifest or clear signs of digestion, because such signs are in the time before the increase from the beginning. For signs of hidden digestion can be found in the beginning, as Galen says in the first book on crises, chapter seven. Therefore he says "by a manifest motion," meaning by this not only the manifest appearance of signs of digestion or its contrary, but also the manifest appearance of the aforementioned symptoms. It is doubted: if innate heat moves to resist the matter by a manifest motion, how then will a sign contrary to digestion appear? It must be said that in the motion to resist, it does not always resist successfully; rather, it is resisted by the addition of a degree of corruption in the matter. Or it must be said that "innate heat" here is taken for the innate quality of the feverish person, which is not single but double: namely, natural and contrary to nature. Both rise from the suffocation of the beginning. When therefore it has awakened and now conquers this whole heated aggregate: if the naturalness of the natural heat conquers, then it penetrates the matter and inflames it with a perfect flame (that is, digestive as far as possible), and from this a sign of digestion appears (for example, a white cloud begins to appear). And then we say that if the fever is continuous, in the hour of the decline of the paroxysm of the peak, the disease is stronger than in the peak of the paroxysm of the decline. If the fever is intermittent, we can perhaps understand the same; yet it is safer to say that the peak in that case is discerned more from the alteration of the matter. To the last it must be said: although the peak is known in the present when it is, yet the knowledge is confirmed when it has passed and the decline appears.

# Book III

...division of diseases which he sets forth. Galen <note>G. refers to Galen, the 2nd-century Greek physician</note> in the third book on critical days, and in the commentary on the third book of the *Prognostics*, comment 11, and in many places, says that some diseases are very acute and terminate in the first four days. And this "ephemeral" fever <note>A one-day fever</note> sometimes terminates on the fourth day, such as that which arises from an obstruction or similar cause; yet it is not considered "acute" for the reason he states. And Galen’s discourse in the commentary on the *Aphorisms* regarding acute diseases says some are "absolutely acute," of which Hippocrates <note>Hyp. refers to Hippocrates</note> says they terminate in 14 days. Beyond that law, taking "acute" broadly, they can extend to 20 days; and if they are taken improperly as "tropic" acute diseases, they terminate in 40 days, as Galen said in the first book of the *Prognostics*, comment 27. He also notes that there are other chronic diseases which extend to months and years. Secondly, the cause is known to him who acts from a regimen universally ordered toward the "status" <note>The peak or plateau of a disease</note>, as Hippocrates understands in the first book of the *Aphorisms* when he said: "to those, therefore, whose status is continuous." He speaks in "orders" because acute diseases are of diverse orders: some terminate on the 1st day, some on the 7th, some on the 14th; and similarly, chronic ones vary. Each requires its own feeding, as will appear in the chapter on the feeding of feverish patients. The third cause is the discourse of Galen in the first book on crises, already mentioned above, that several [diseases] complete themselves in one paroxysm <note>A sudden attack or increase in symptoms</note>, i.e., they have their beginning, increase, and peak in one paroxysm, and in the second they decline. Or many complete the peak in one paroxysm, but the other stages occur in several paroxysms before that peak paroxysm. The fourth cause is that certain fevers have a long increase and a long decline, as appears in *augmastic* and *peraugmastic* fevers <note>Technical terms for fevers with specific patterns of increasing intensity</note>; and thus it is understood that some have a long beginning and some a long peak. This cause awaits declaration in the chapter below regarding the fever which is due to putrefaction, etc.

<margin>a **And ephemeral.** Against this: Rhazes <note>9th-century Persian physician</note> in the 15th book of the *Continens*, chapter 7, classifies ephemeral as a long disease. It must be said that here the discourse is about the ephemeral of a single paroxysm, not the whole ephemeral. But against the fact that three universals are taught to be recognized here: it must be said he uses this discourse as a starting point.</margin>

When you see paroxysms prolonged due to thick matter, then the disease will not be acute. All this was said to let you know that the certainty of this sign depends on the verified nature of the matter. And the discourse can be the other member of the division, so that we say material illnesses either have paroxysms—like interpolated and proportional continuous fevers, where paroxysms are long or short—or they do not have paroxysms, like *sinocha* <note>A continuous inflammatory fever</note> and dropsy. And then they either have signs of acute (i.e., hot) matter, and the disease will be acute and short, or signs of cold, thick matter like dropsy, and then the disease is not acute. **And if it remained.** This text can first be explained thus: if the paroxysms invade at the same hour, as in the discourse of that aphorism in the fourth part: "to whom exacerbations or paroxysms..." but this exposition does not seem to be admitted by Avicenna, since this does not speak of anticipation or postponement, nor consequently of equal invasion. Secondly, it is explained to mean that if the illness in its own genus does not have any critical change or any trace of a crisis, it will not be acute but long, as in that aphorism of the fourth part: "in fevers, an abscess not resolved at the first crises," etc. And these expositions are difficult. Thirdly, it can be explained thus: if the paroxysm remains with its disposition, i.e., midway between long and short, then the disease will be of a medium disposition, like a *prolatic* fever <note>A fever that is extended or delayed</note> compared to choleric and melancholic ones. Fourthly, it remains in part, i.e., the sign signifying its disposition, i.e., midway between signs of thick matter and subtle matter, which signifies a matter that is as if medium; the illness will not be of that [high] acuity. And these two expositions are our own.

<margin>b **Apoplexy.** Against this: it is from cold, thick matter. It must be said that some apoplexy arises from hot matter; or it must be said the disease is acute on account of the matter being hot (and such is not apoplexy for the most part), or on account of the accidents or the nobility of the location [the brain]. And such is apoplexy and also quinsy <note>Severe throat inflammation</note>; for they kill quickly by the severity of the accidents, or recovery happens quickly.</margin>
<margin>d **Tertian.** Properly continuous or interpolated.</margin>
<margin>e **And sometimes.** Increases in the circuit: whether the conditions of the periods or paroxysms are four: length and shortness. He teaches to recognize the times of the disease through this. The second is their safety or lack thereof; he teaches to recognize through this in the section "and sometimes from strength." The third is anticipation and postponement; he teaches to recognize through those in the section "and they are recognized." The fourth is the multitude or paucity of accidents; he teaches to recognize from this in the fourth section. It should be considered that Avicenna takes "time" broadly here, i.e., for a great span of time to which a tertian fever can extend; and therefore he said from 3 hours to 14. Yet authors usually take the time that commonly occurs for the most part, as Galen in the second book on crises, when he says 12 hours. Avicenna below: It is doubted whether from the shortness of paroxysms we argue the peak is near, and from length that the peak is remote. It seems not, for Galen in the third book on crises, chapter 3, argues from the length of the parts of a paroxysm that a fever is continuing, or is continuous; but he appropriates shortness to interpolated fever. A continuous fever is shorter and more acute than an interpolated one. In the 18th book of the *Continens*, chapter 6: Furthermore, when paroxysms are long, then it is the peak, because in the peak, labors and accidents reach their limit. To the contrary, for Avicenna, it must be said that in the same illness we do not signify this mode; but when paroxysms are long, then it is the peak as Galen argues. In different ones compared to each other, length and shortness can happen either from the nature of the matter—and then we signify as Avicenna says, that this judgment is for phlegmatic or quartan fevers, or impure tertians signifying a distant peak, while short ones signify a near peak—or it can happen from the mode of motion or location of the matter, whence it is continuous or interpolated. And then we do not judge as Avicenna says; and this judgment given from the length and shortness of the paroxysm is confused, and is not verified unless the nature of the matter is first inspected. Therefore Avicenna said: "they signify that the matter is thick." The first thing that falls under judgment from the length of a paroxysm is that the disease is long; and then this judgment, which was confused, is certified when you see that the matter is thick, from which the contrary signs cease.</margin>
<margin>f **And if it were not.** This discourse can be a discourse in itself, as if to say: if you see signs of thick matter and subtle matter which signifies a matter as if medium, the illness will not be of that acuity. And these two expositions are ours.</margin>

# Treatise on the Fen
<note>The "Fen" refers to a specific division or "book" within Avicenna’s Canon of Medicine.</note>

<margin>a</margin>
**Anticipation** <note>original: *antecessio*; the fever fit arriving earlier than expected</note> signifies **increase**, and not just any symptoms that appear: for there are some illnesses that run their course until the end, etc. Secondly, it must be considered that the text which says: *if it has so remained*, can be explained in two ways. First, if the anticipation is not only absolute, but an anticipation of that which is superfluous: and this is what it means by *the superfluities have remained*. Then the illness will be in its **peak** <note>original: *statu*; the "status" or height of the disease</note>. In another way, according to Avicenna: one should not be certain in giving a judgment of increase through anticipation, however much it may be superfluous, unless there are also added accidents <note>symptoms</note> and the rest. Similarly, a judgment of the peak is not given by the paroxysms remaining at an equal onset unless there are no extreme symptoms. For an equal onset can absolutely signify the beginning of a disease, or a long disease, as appears from the *Aphorisms*, fourth part: *To whom the exacerbations...* Therefore he said: if the anticipation and the superfluities have remained (that is, the symptoms in their extremity), and this speech refers to what he says: *as one precedes the superfluity of the other*. That is, one precedes that which it adds over the other, tending toward an addition, namely of symptoms. But he spoke distinctly about this knowledge of the times of the disease from the perspective of symptoms immediately after; however, he said this about it so that he might certify the knowledge from the perspective of anticipation and postponement.

### ...proceeding according to anticipation superfluously: so that one precedes the superfluity of the other, tending toward an addition: then the illness will be in its **increase**. For among illnesses there are those that run until the end of their hours according to increase: and these are sometimes of the tertian type <note>fever recurring every third day</note> and of the continuous type. But if the anticipation has stayed, and the superfluities have stayed: then without doubt the illness will be in its **peak**. ¶ And that which keeps to one hour is of a long duration: and if they are postponed, then the illness is in its **decline**. Similarly, the disposition of the hours is known from the increase of the symptoms of the fever, and their staying, and their decline, and from the addition of its paroxysms in their length and shortness. And perhaps they vary and do not resemble one another. ¶ And sometimes they are known from their disposition. For when in some paroxysm there occurs sweat or a flux of the belly, and the second paroxysm which follows it is similar in intensity to the first and in its strength or above it: then its evacuation is because of the quantity [of matter] and not because of strength: and the illness is judged to be long. And sometimes they are known from the side of **digestion** and the contrary of digestion according to what we have said; for example: when sputum appears with some digestion, or urine in which there is some cloudiness, then it is the beginning of the increase.

<margin>a</margin> ¶ *And keeping to one hour.* This is that aphorism: *To whom the exacerbations or paroxysms...* And it requires a great deal of discussion. For it is commonly said that the equal onset of paroxysms is a sign of rawness <note>original: *cruditatis*; undigested humors</note>: and with the symptoms remitted, it signifies a long illness, because it exists in the beginning with thick or raw matter. But in his commentary on that aphorism, he wants the fixedness and stability of the onset to signify matter needing a long time for digestion, as if he wants there to be an equal battle between nature and the matter: and the speech is similar to the third [book] of the *Prognostics* and the commentary. This is an equal battle: because nature holds it in the same way in its dispositions with respect to its power. Therefore it is neither defeated so that health occurs, nor winning so that death occurs: therefore the disease is long. But with this, irregularity also signifies, or can signify, a longer disease. And it is a doubt whether an equal onset signifies a longer disease than irregularity: and perhaps it ought to be said that irregularity which happens by a fall of power signifies a shorter end toward death. But irregularity from a diversity of matter can signify a longer disease insofar as it signifies diverse matters: and you should consider this. It is doubted first why anticipation and superfluity signify increase, postponement signifies decline, and equal staying signifies the beginning or the peak. And he says that if the matter were uniform of itself, it would always invade at an equal time. Therefore, when nature manifestly moves against the matter, as it does in the increase, then the paroxysms are accelerated. When nature fails it, then the paroxysms are postponed: and this is in the decline. This speech is much diminished, as it appears: because it is given that it can be reduced to two causes. The first is the equality and inequality of the battle: for in the beginning and the peak the battle seems equal enough; in the beginning indeed equal because not increased, but the matter stays raw; in the peak indeed equal because not able to be increased. And therefore the paroxysms, which are paroxysms of battle, are equal in the hours of onset. In the increase and decline, however, there seems to be a certain inequality of battle: in the increase indeed through the agitation which nature makes in the matter, and therefore the matter moves quickly. Whether it be a good agitation which nature makes in the matter, or a bad one which the matter makes in nature, or a bad putrid heat in the matter. In the decline, however, it is because of victory. The second cause is the quantity and motion of the matter with a similar disposition of qualities which is in the beginning and the peak. For that which is moved first and second in the beginning and peak is equal in quantity and quality: but in the increase and decline it is unequal and dissimilar, as is known: because these are possible to have otherwise; therefore it happens in the beginning that anticipation occurs, and also postponement. And in the increase postponement, and in the decline anticipation. And you should consider this. It is doubted secondly why some fevers proceed always by adding, like certain tertians and quotidians <note>daily fevers</note>: and some always by postponing: as are certain tertians and quotidians. These men say, namely **Thaddeus** <note>Thaddeus of Florence, a famous 13th-century physician</note> and all who follow him: that the cause is the deformity of the matter in thickness and thinness. For if the thinner matter begins to putrefy first, and successively the thicker, in that case the paroxysms will always go by postponing, because the thicker matter will be moved more slowly. But if the thicker matter begins and proceeds to the thinner, they will always go by anticipating. But then Thaddeus insists: how can thick matter be moved before thin? It must be said it is because of some proper disposition, just as in someone **phlegm** putrefies and not **choler** <note>yellow bile</note>. But if you consider the speeches of these men, they cannot give this cause except in fevers that pass from thick matters to thin, or vice versa. ¶ But now sense shows certain tertians quite pure, and from matter quite uniform in substance, to have proceeded always by anticipating: and in some always by postponing: and the intake of the most prepared things is quite similar. But we will say here that there can be many causes, of which we will say some are on the part of the body, and some on the part of the matter: on the part of the body indeed it happens that a man is quite sensitive in his members, but close to bloodiness and moist in them: and in the beginning the choleric matter meets them covered: but as the paroxysms proceed it is always more uncovered, and they are bitten more and more quickly by the new matter: wherefore the paroxysms hasten. In some other body, however, the matter which is moved paroxysmally could gradually add an obstruction to the sense, wherefore the paroxysms will postpone. On the part of the matter: for because of many contingent causes the fever can begin in the first paroxysm with some quantity of matter, and it is possible for all the second matter to be more ample or more biting than the first, even if it is digested or is in decline, and thus the paroxysms will always anticipate. But it is said: according to this, in the decline a paroxysm could be made from more ample matter than in the peak. It must be answered in two ways: first, that that last paroxysm was the peak and the decline. Or it must be said: that it is not inconvenient, but although the matter be more ample, it will be more obedient to the power [of nature]. And you should consider that many causes can accelerate the paroxysmal motion, even if the fever is in decline.

### ...then when that is multiplied and appears, or its contrary, then it is the **peak**. And again when digestion appears or its contrary quickly from the sputum or a cloud [in the urine]: then know that the peak is near: if it is delayed, then know that the peak is far off. The knowledge of the particular hours is taken thus: for the hour of the beginning of the paroxysm is the hour in which the **pulse** is constricted: and you already knew his intention: and the color of the extremities becomes leaden: and the extremes are chilled: and specifically the tip of the ear and the nose, until the hour in which the spreading of heat is felt.

<margin>b</margin> ¶ *And similarly.* It should be considered that the symptoms here are understood as pains, heats, thirst, wakefulness, etc. And what he says is clear.

<margin>c</margin> ¶ *And perhaps.* As if he says: in fevers of heterogeneous members there is no order of onset: and therefore from these signs of anticipation and postponement and length and shortness we cannot know the times of the disease. And perhaps he wants that diversification, insofar as it signifies diverse matters, to signify a long disease.

<margin>d</margin> ¶ *And sometimes.* He teaches to know from another sign, that is, **evacuation**: and consider that such an illness is long if it is to be healthy: because there can be a short mortal fever. And in this is included that aphorism of the fourth part: *In fevers a great sweat coming on, the fever not failing...* etc.

<margin>e</margin> ¶ *And sometimes.* Here, as they say, he teaches to know the times of the disease from signs appearing afterward: although it could begin there: and sometimes it is known from the disposition of the evacuation. Note that the signs appearing afterward are signs of the **crisis** <note>the turning point of a disease</note> of which evacuation is one: and he has already spoken of this and the signs of digestion and its contrary. And he says these signs are of health and death, although they are appearing afterward; they cannot [be used] to know the times of the disease, because signs of death, since they are common to the three times, are understood from what was said before. And consider that he took the signs of digestion in the sputum and urine, meaning others as well: and he taught to know the times of the disease, when they are and when they will be, as appears in the text. And the knowledge from these signs is certain: but from the signs said above it is not so certain, as first concerning the crisis.

### ¶ And sometimes there are associated with the beginning an alteration of color and sluggishness and distress: and slowness of movements: and depth of sleep: and softening of the eyelids and heaviness of speech: and a shivering between the shoulder blades and in the back, and perhaps a strong rigor happens in it: and sometimes there happens a flow of saliva and a leaping of the temples and a ringing of the ears: and sneezing and a commotion of the members of the body.

<margin>f</margin> ¶ *Knowledge of the hours.* Here he teaches to know the particular times. And first he does this according to the given common [rule] of the lengthening and shortening of the paroxysms. There: *and know*. The first part in two: first he does this. Second he puts an example. There: *and if it seems to you*. The first in four according to which he notifies the four times. It must be considered that the symptoms of the beginning of a paroxysm all follow the motion of the suffocations of natural heat under the matter: and therefore the pulse is constricted. And you knew this in the first book, second [part], fourth [chapter] below, in the chapter on the signs of putrid fevers. And from this, because the heat flees, the color becomes leaden: and the extremes are chilled. These symptoms are then diversified according to the diversity of matters: and according to the diversity of bodies: and the parts in which the impression appears: whence the extremes are chilled specifically, etc., because they are cold with little flesh, *Prognostics* I. And therefore sometimes the color is much altered. ¶ He said this: *alteration of color*, as in a fainting fit. *Sluggishness* in a phlegmatic one: *distress* when the matter moves around the part of the stomach and the respiratory organs. *Slowness* in a phlegmatic one and also *depth of sleep*, etc. Whence certain phlegmatic ones begin paroxysms with a stupor. *Shivering* when the matter is somewhat choleric. *Flow of saliva*: the matter being moved in the stomach or head. *Commotion of the members*, that is, a shaking: as in the tremor of a type and of other symptoms. The cause is clear.

### And the power [of the body] is indeed more vehemently weakened because of the weakness: which is in the beginning or the peak.

<margin>g</margin> ¶ *And more vehemently.* It must be considered that weakness happens by way of suffocation: and this happens more in the beginning of a paroxysm. And by way of strong inflammation: and this happens more in the peak of a paroxysm: and therefore death happens more in the beginning than the peak of a paroxysm: but in the increase or decline: as below in the second section, the chapter on the hours of death. And thirdly concerning the crisis, fourth chapter. In the decline indeed death and weakness happen by way of resolution <note>the "dissolving" or wasting away of the body's strength</note>. And this is rarer, as Saliceto says there. ¶ This is what Avicenna wanted here when he says *and more vehemently*, etc. That is, it happens more that the power weakens in the beginning and peak than in the decline of a paroxysm: in the increase, if the power fails, it is because of the addition of heat, just as in the peak: and this happens more in the peak. In the decline indeed it happens more frequently that with the febrile heat thinned out, a man is more relieved than his natural heat is simultaneously resolved by accident.

### ¶ And the first half of the hour of increase is the hour in which the pulse begins to appear and to become large and to become fast. And the heat is spread in the whole body according to equality.

<margin>h</margin> ¶ *And the hour of increase.* It must be considered that there is an hour of symptoms having an affinity to the beginning in which there is suffocation, and an affinity to the peak in which there is now an eruption made and a venting of heat: therefore he divided the increase into two parts: and he did not do this because it is longer in proportion, as **Albertus** <note>Albertus Magnus</note> says, because it is common for the increase to be short and the beginning and peak to be long, as will appear later. But he said this because of its deformity <note>variation</note>.

# Book IV

<margin>a</margin> **And the time of the peak.** This is called the "status" <note>Latin: *status*, meaning the peak or plateau of a disease</note> because the accidents <note>symptoms or secondary conditions</note> remain in their ultimate state; therefore, with heat added to its limit, the pulse becomes faster and more frequent. But you might say: it does not seem to be larger, because the strength <note>vital faculty</note> is more weakened in the peak than in the growth stage. It must be said that the added necessity forces the strength to enlarge the pulse more than at other times; and elsewhere we have touched upon it being stronger. Nevertheless, natural strengths, assuming some necessity, work vehemently, as Galen <note>Galen of Pergamon, the influential Greek physician</note> says in his commentary on the pulse, treatise 4, first commentary.

<margin>b</margin> **And the time of decline.** He doubts because he says that sweat occurs. Then, indeed, the decline happens as they sweat. It must be said first that the word "then" does not denote a strict sequence, but indicates two modes of decline: one without manifest evacuation, and another with manifest evacuation. Secondly, when the matter begins to be propelled toward the skin, even if sweat does not yet appear, the hidden heat can begin to be alleviated.

<margin>c</margin> **And many times.** This is the discourse of the third book on the crisis, chapter four. Therefore, return to the pulse to distinguish a true decline from the peak; for in a true one it proceeds toward improvement, but in a false one <unclear>it fluctuates</unclear>.

<margin>d</margin> **And if you wish.** He doubts how he attributes "shuddering" <note>Latin: *horripilatio*, goosebumps or shivering</note> to tertian fever <note>a fever that recurs every third day, typically malaria</note> when everyone says it happens with "rigor" <note>intense cold/shaking</note>, as Galen says in the second book on crises. It must be said that by "shuddering," he understands a gradual process from a small chill to a great rigor. For first, in a tertian fever, a certain shuddering of the skin begins, and some cold follows, because the heat begins to intensify; but when the bile is carried strongly over the sensitive members, then there will be manifest rigor. These terms—shuddering, cold, and rigor—are used with much variety among authors, as will be said below in its own chapter.

<margin>e</margin> **In many dispositions.** Because a non-true tertian is governed in which rigor does not occur, as the author will set down below.

<margin>f</margin> **And know that the sickness is sometimes prolonged.** Namely, a tertian or a paroxysm <note>a sudden attack or increase in symptoms</note> or whatever material disease it may be. He doubts because there are other causes of prolongation besides those he sets down below in the first chapter on putrefaction: namely, the rest of the matter and the weakness of sensation; and errors in the regimen <note>lifestyle/dietary rules</note>; and in the second chapter on the signs of prolongation, he places the cause as "thickness in the viscera." It must be said that he summarized it, and you should complete this knowledge from what is to be said. For not everything can be written in one place; and if you consider the rest of the matter and the thickness in the interior parts, it is reduced to the thickness and coldness of the matter. Malice of the regimen is reduced to extrinsic causes, which he puts here as an example, such as cold weather; weakness of sensation is reduced to weakness of the strength. But you might ask: why does he say the weakness of strength or innate heat "helps" that, when it is actually the principal cause? It must be said that every cause which is outside the nature of the disease and the nature of its cause can be called "extrinsic" in prolongation and shortening. However, causes taken from the disease itself or its cause are more proper and intrinsic; and these can be called "co-helpers," while those are "principals." He doubts whether the method of distinguishing particular times (namely of paroxysms) and universal times of the whole disease is the same. It seems so, because just as universal times are distinguished and identified by the fact that the pulse is submerged, oppressed by heat, or expanded (as appeared above), so too are the particular times of paroxysms. Here the Author <note>Avicenna</note> follows Hippocrates, who teaches that the same signs used to know universal times also teach one to know paroxysms, as appears in the aphorism on paroxysms and consistencies. On the contrary, Avicenna speaks distinctly about the distinction between particular and universal times and brings forward distinct signs. It must be said that by a certain general and proportional distinction, these times are distinguished in a similar way. For just as in the universal beginning of a disease the heat is suffocated because the matter is raw, then it is manifested more fully in the matter, then it reaches its peak, then it declines; so it happens in the beginning, growth, peak, and decline of a paroxysm. However, by a special and appropriated distinction, they are distinguished in a different way, as everyone commonly says. For the universal times of a disease are distinguished either by the alteration of the matter or by the accidents, as appears in the first book on crises. But the particular times of paroxysms are distinguished neither by the alteration of the matter nor by the accidents, but by the way the heat behaves in expansion or enclosure. That the times of paroxysms are not distinguished by the alteration of the matter and the action of nature is apparent because there can be a time in which the action of nature on the matter has not yet occurred, and yet the paroxysm will have all its stages, just like a paroxysm at the beginning. And it is possible that a paroxysm has its decline, and yet there will be no decline of the disease; indeed, a person could die in the decline of a paroxysm. This does not happen in the decline of the whole disease once the action of nature on the matter has been completed. For when the strength overcomes and expels it, the sick person cannot die, as proven in the third book on crises, chapter five. But in the decline of a paroxysm, it can happen that the natural heat is resolved <note>dissipated</note> along with the matter, and the sick person dies. Nor are the times of a paroxysm distinguished by accidents like the universal times of a disease. And this is proven by Dionysius: first, that the times of a disease are distinguished by the accidents of "foreign heat" <note>feverish heat</note> insofar as it is foreign and against nature, and thus the distinction is by the accidents of the disease. But the distinction of paroxysm times is by the movement of the heat as heat—I mean movement inward and outward—and by its addition or resolution. Second, because the peak of a paroxysm is not a peak regarding the accidents such that the accidents are most intense in it; for it could be that the growth and peak of a paroxysm are mild, and the beginning of the paroxysm will have more difficult accidents, so that in such a beginning the sick person will die if it is a mortal illness, as taken from Galen. And because of this, Dionysius infers that the distinction of paroxysm times by accidents is accidental to them, but by the movement of heat it is essential. For the beginning of a paroxysm is when the heat is constricted and the extremities are cold; the growth is when it expands; the peak is when it is fully expanded; the decline is when it is resolved. It is not so with universal times regarding accidents, because then it will be the peak when the accidents of the disease are intense, and the decline when they are remitted. And therefore, the peak of the disease regarding accidents can coincide with the beginning and growth of a paroxysm, and the decline of the whole disease regarding accidents can coincide with the peak of a paroxysm. Dionysius adds, however, that it must be understood that there is some agreement between the times of a paroxysm and the times of a disease distinguished by accidents: because both in the decline of the disease regarding accidents and in the decline of a paroxysm, a person can die. For it is possible that the accidents of the disease appear to be remitted because the natural strength is overcome and conquered, not resisting or fighting the disease; and because of this, the accidents of the disease appear remitted, and yet the sick person tends toward death. Although this is a decline of the disease regarding accidents, it is not a decline regarding the essence of the disease. And as it is something outside of nature compared to the strength and its action. And therefore Galen’s reasons, by which he proves in the third book on crises that in the decline a person does not die, prove it regarding the decline in terms of the operation of nature, but not regarding the decline in terms of accidents. And this is also what Avicenna meant when he said: "and many times indeed." Although Avicenna’s discourse refers to the decline of a paroxysm, it can nevertheless refer to the decline of the disease regarding accidents, because the decline

First, that every disease having four universal times (if healthy) or three (if mortal) depends on some matter through whose varying disposition its times are distinguished. This proposition is apparent because one distinction of the universal times of a disease is on the part of the matter. The second proposition is that any paroxysm has its own matter on which it depends, and this is a known proposition. The third is that the matter through whose disposition the universal times of the disease vary is one from the beginning of the disease until the end of the decline in a simple disease; but in a composite one, there are two from the beginning to the end. The reason: because authors distinguish universal times by this: that which in the beginning is raw now begins to be digested (i.e., in the growth stage); but in the peak it is digested or turns toward digestion; in the decline, this is called the time when the raw matter is expelled. So says Galen in his booklet on the universal times of disease, and in the first and second books on crises, and Avicenna. But this would not be the case if the matter were different and changing. On the contrary, in an intermittent tertian fever, there cannot be one single matter, because on the day of rest it is not there. Furthermore, how could there be one single matter from beginning to end in a quartan fever <note>a fever recurring every fourth day</note> that lasts four years? It must be said that in intermittent fevers, the times of the disease are not properly distinguished by the alteration of the matter in the same way as in continuous fevers; for continuous ones are one continuity in their parts. And if the times are distinguished by the alteration of the matter, it will be by the alteration of the antecedent matter standing in the veins. Therefore, the sign of digestion that you consider on the day of rest is not compared to the continuous cause, since there is none, but to the antecedent cause; not that it must be one and the same from the beginning of the whole fever until the end. Since therefore the antecedent cause moves toward a paroxysm while it is raw, then those paroxysms are the "beginning" paroxysms; when it is softer, they are the "growth" paroxysms. And from this you may infer corollaries. First, that the universal times of a disease are properly distinguished in continuous diseases, in which one matter runs from the beginning until the peak, reserved for the crisis. And therefore, in continuous fevers, prolongation—in which many crises occur—greatly confuses the times of the disease; so that after signs of digestion and the mode of crisis have already appeared regarding that matter, the signs of digestion are lost again, and reappear again, and crises happen again. And all this is because of the precipitation of many new matters to the site of putrefaction, so that it is not called one matter, but can be called many having a beginning, growth, and peak. And this led Galen to posit these acute "convertible" diseases after he saw that an acute fever absolutely lasts 14 days; that one which lasts 14 days will be fairly uniform in its exacerbations if it is of the exacerbating type, or entirely uniform if it is a *synochus* <note>a continuous non-intermittent fever</note>; and that which terminates before this time (namely on the 11th, 6th, 7th, or 5th day) is a true continuous fever having one manifest distinction of the times of the disease. Or they are prolonged further, properly to the 40th day, by "conversion" or a kind of relapse, as Galen indicates in his commentary on acute illnesses. The second corollary is that in modern times, because of the [dietary] habits of men and the multiplication of matters that are frequently precipitated toward putrefaction besides the primary matter that starts the fever, the times of the disease are greatly confused regarding the alteration of the matter; and now digestion will appear in the urine for some days, and afterward rawness will return again, as if another disease were starting over. And according to this, we have seen a continuous fever lasting 30 days, even though it was enough for our ancestors when they said it lasted 40. The fourth proposition is that any matter that causes a paroxysm or exacerbation is distinct in number from another that causes another paroxysm. The reason is that whatever matter causes a paroxysm causes the patient to be ill and is afterward resolved and finished, so that it can no longer carry the foreign heat. Therefore, if the paroxysm returns again, it must be that the matter is different; and so it is understood by Galen in the second book on the differences of fevers and by Avicenna below. The fifth proposition is that the matter which is disposed through distinct universal times from the beginning of the disease until the end is not that which causes the paroxysm on the day of the paroxysm or the fourth day; but that which paroxysms is accumulated with it. The reason is that one remains (namely the matter by which the times are distinguished) and the other is expelled, either every day or every third or fourth day. The sixth proposition is that if there is a continuous fever from one matter from the universal beginning until the end, to the decline, and this fever has exacerbations, the matter that causes the exacerbation is not the same in number as that which runs through all the times of the disease; but it is another, and there are as many in number as there are exacerbations. And although many of these propositions can be reduced to one, we have divided them into many so that we may speak more clearly. From these propositions, I first infer that continuous fever is of three modes: one in which from its beginning a single matter is moved to putrefaction, or is entirely occupied by unnatural heat, and that whole matter remains from the beginning until the end of the fever (namely to the beginning of the decline) with no addition of other matter made to it; and such is a true putrid or non-putrid *synochus*. On the contrary: in a putrid *synochus*, for example, there would be no succession in heat if some part of the blood were not continually added to the prior part which was previously putrefied. Furthermore, Galen in the third book, chapter one, distinguishes the species of *synochus* by the fact that what evaporates from the blood is equal to what is ignited, or more or less. Therefore, a new accession occurs in the blood, namely of newly ignited blood, which was not ignited at the beginning of the fever; otherwise, that variety would not occur. Furthermore, a continuous resolution occurs from the blood in a fever; therefore, if new blood does not approach the fever, all the blood would always be declining. Furthermore, suppose a blood-fever in which there is evaporation only in a measure that would not cause a fever; if therefore in the first evaporation of blood there were no accession of other blood succeeding in that evaporation, the fever would cease in the first evaporation; and thus some blood-fever would cease in less than a sixth part of an hour. Furthermore: every day blood is generated from the food that is eaten, and that is mixed with the heated blood; therefore it will be heated.
