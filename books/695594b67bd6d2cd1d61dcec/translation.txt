In our current age, given the interest that mystical theology and the research conducted regarding it excite in various ways, it cannot be seen as surprising that a translation of the Areopagite writings <note>The works attributed to Dionysius the Areopagite, a foundational figure in Christian mysticism</note> is appearing, and that explanations of these documents—so significant for mystical theology—are being attempted. Yet the translator must not evade the duty of facilitating the appreciation of his work by indicating the connection in which these writings seem to him to stand regarding the entire history of mystical theology in general. The concept of mysticism itself has been understood in such diverse ways, so often misinterpreted by opponents—where good and bad have been thrown together—and so boundlessly recommended and defended by friends, that it seems necessary to at least suggest something on this matter in this very place. —

The words mystery, mystical, mysticism, and <term>mystics</term><note>The systematic study or practice of mystical experiences</note> generally point toward something secret, not known to everyone, and conscious only to a few. However, linguistic usage has always applied these words particularly to religious knowledge, to insights and perspectives on the relationship of man to God and everything connected with this—

The **Mysteries** <note>The "Mysteries" refers to ancient Greek religious rites, such as the Eleusinian Mysteries, which were closed to the uninitiated.</note> of the Greeks taught the initiates the truths hidden under images and symbols regarding the one eternal God, the world, and the destiny of man. To every non-initiate, these teachings were a secret, and the initiates recognized one another by them as if by secret signs. Christianity itself emerged during the persecutions of the first centuries as a secret doctrine—not for its own confessors, but for the pagans and non-Christians in general who took no part in the new teaching. 

However, as the Christian faith became dominant in the fourth century, a separation soon appeared within the Christian communities themselves. The external Church grew on all sides; the acceptance of a creed <note>original: "Glaubensbekenntniß"</note> enabled admission into the great community. Given the multitude of confessors, it was inevitable that many confessed merely with their lips, while others attained only a general, superficial concept of it. Only a few penetrated into the spirit and sense of the Founder's teaching and experienced its healing effects through the transformation of their entire inner being. 

Such was the case among the laity; a similar situation, albeit with different modifications, occurred among the teachers. These teachers had to know the doctrine they presented and understand the content of the Scriptures on which it was based; the path by which they arrived at this understanding depended on their spiritual individuality. If **intellect** was predominant in them, they took from the biblical writings especially that which appealed to it and combined it into a whole; if it was **imagination** or **feeling**, they inclined toward that which suited this prevailing quality of their spirit.
