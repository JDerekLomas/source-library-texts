<note>original: "植物名實圖考" (Zhìwù Míng Shí Tú Kǎo). This monumental work, published posthumously in 1848, is celebrated as one of the most comprehensive and scientifically accurate botanical treatises of the Qing Dynasty. It contains descriptions and illustrations of 1,714 types of plants.</note>

## Sweet Potato

The **Sweet Potato** is detailed in the *Flora of the Southern Regions* <note>original: "Nanfang Caomu Zhuang," a 4th-century botanical work</note>. It is also known as the **"Foreign Tuber"** <note>original: "Fan Shu" (番藷). The prefix "Fan" indicates its introduction from overseas, likely the Philippines.</note>. It was first included in the "Vegetables" section of the *Compendium of Materia Medica* <note>original: "Bencao Gangmu" by Li Shizhen, 1596</note>. In recent times, it has been planted very extensively. People living in the mountains rely on it as a staple grain, though occasionally it is prepared as a side vegetable.

In Nan'an <note>A region in Fujian Province</note>, some plants flower in the middle of the tenth month. The flowers are shaped like those of the **bindweed** <note>original: "Xuanhua," referring to the morning glory family, *Convolvulaceae*</note>.

Furthermore, the *Gazetteer of Zunyi Prefecture* records a wild variety locally known as the **"Thatch Dog Tuber."** There are those who process this variety to deceptively pass it off as **Chinese Yam** <note>term: "Shanyao" (Dioscorea polystachya), a more expensive medicinal tuber</note>. During years of famine, people harvest these tubers to make dried provisions.

According to the *Flora of the Southern Regions*, the sweet potato originated in Wuping, Jiaozhi, and ancient Jiuzhen <note>Regions in modern-day Northern Vietnam and Southern China</note>. This would suggest it has been a product of the Chinese regions for a long time. However, the *Book of Fujian* <note>original: "Min Shu"</note> claims it originated from **Luzon** <note>The Philippines</note> in the Western Ocean, and that Chinese travelers took cuttings of the vine to bring back to Fujian. Why is there such a discrepancy?

<column-break/>

The *Gazetteer of Haicheng County* records that when **Yu Yinggui** served as Magistrate, he was particularly fond of the "foreign tuber." He sometimes ate them without even removing the skin; because of this, they acquired the name **"Foreign Tuber"** <note>a play on the Magistrate's status and the plant's exotic origins</note>.

Today, there are two varieties, red and white, both of which are sweet and delicious in flavor. They are particularly abundant in the high, dry lands near **Dongting Lake** in Hunan. Displaced people <note>original: "Liumin," refugees or migrants fleeing disaster</note> dig up the leftover tubers; because of this, they suffer no famine during the winter months. **Xu Guangqi** <note>A famous Ming Dynasty scholar and scientist</note> wrote the *Treatise on the Sweet Potato* with the earnest and heartfelt words of a benevolent man; it is a pity that... <note>The text likely laments that Xu's promotion of the crop was not even more widely adopted earlier.</note>

# Water Clover

<note>original: 萍菜 (Pingcai); Scientifically known as *Marsilea quadrifolia*, an aquatic fern that resembles a four-leaf clover. It is distinct from common duckweed.</note>

**Water Clover** is recorded in the **Compendium of Materia Medica**; <note>term: *Bencao Gangmu*, the definitive pharmaceutical encyclopedia of the Ming Dynasty, compiled by Li Shizhen.</note> it is vulgarly called **"Spicy Rice Seed."** <note>original: 辣米子 (Lami zi).</note> It is found frequently in fields and wild areas. No one ever plants or cultivates it, for it is entirely a wild vegetable.

The **Gazetteer of Jiangxi** <note>A regional record of the history and products of Jiangxi Province.</note> records that **Master Zhu** <note>Referring to Zhu Xi (1130–1200), the influential Neo-Confucian philosopher who often lived in Jiangxi.</note> used this plant as a vegetable. Consequently, the people there boast of it as a rare and wondrous delicacy, claiming it only grows in the cleanest headwaters and is not commonly found. This, however, is merely **hearsay.** <note>original: 耳食之論 (ershi zhi lun), literally "the theory of eating with one's ears," meaning to believe rumors without personal investigation.</note>

In my own hometown, people pick this plant and pickle it to make **fermented preserves.** <note>term: 菹 (Zu), an ancient term for salted or fermented vegetables.</note> It is remarkably clean, has a sharp, spicy bite, and is pleasantly chewy. Though it is but a **lonely, tiny herb,** <note>original: 伶仃小草 (lingding xiaocao).</note> when compared to **Shepherd's Purse,** <note>original: 薺 (Ji); the plant described on the previous page.</note> they each hold their own ground—one for its spiciness and the other for its sweetness. 

However, while Shepherd's Purse is not picky about where it grows, this plant grows only in the **open wilderness.** It delights in purity and detests turbidity; in this regard, the claims about its nature are indeed true.

# Carrot

The carrot was first recorded in the "Vegetables" section of the *Compendium of Materia Medica* <note>original: "Bencao Gangmu," the definitive 16th-century pharmaceutical text by Li Shizhen</note>. In the southern regions, it is eaten only during the autumn and winter, but in the North, it is served as food year-round.

Some say that it first entered China during the Yuan Dynasty <note>1271–1368 AD</note>. When the Yuan forces expanded toward the east, they first acquired the region of Yunnan <note>term: "Dian" (滇), an ancient name for Yunnan Province</note>; for this reason, this vegetable is particularly abundant and grows to a great size in Yunnan. 

There are two varieties, red and yellow. However, its flavor is similar to that of "stinking celery" <note>term: "Xiehao" (邪蒿), likely referring to *Seseli libanotis*, an aromatic herb in the parsley family</note>. Those who are fond of eating "fat-tailed sheep" <note>a breed of sheep prized for its succulent fat, common in Central Asian and Mongol cuisine</note> always cook the meat together with this root. Is this perhaps a continuation of the culinary traditions of the Yuan Dynasty? <note>term: "Shi Xianzhang" (食憲章), referring to dietary codes or established culinary customs of that era</note>

# Pumpkin

The pumpkin was first included in the "Vegetables" section of the *Compendium of Materia Medica* <note>original: "Bencao Gangmu," the definitive 16th-century pharmaceutical text by Li Shizhen</note>. It is suspected to be the "Shade Gourd" mentioned in older agricultural treatises. It is now grown everywhere, though it is said to have the potential to trigger various illnesses <note>In traditional Chinese medicine, certain foods were considered "stimulating" (fa), meaning they might aggravate existing conditions or sensitivities</note>.

Gazetteers from the northern provinces list four gourds: East, West, South, and North. The "East Gourd" is likely a transcription error for the "Winter Gourd" <note>In Chinese, "East" (dong) and "Winter" (dong) are homophones, leading to frequent confusion in texts</note>. The "North Gourd" comes in two varieties—"watery" and "mealy"—each differing in shape and color. Originally, these were not produced in the South.

### Sponge Gourd

The <term>Sponge Gourd</term> <note>original: 絲瓜 (sigua), the Luffa</note> was first included in the <term>Vegetable Category</term> of the *Compendium of Materia Medica* <note>original: *Bencao Gangmu*, the definitive 16th-century pharmaceutical text by Li Shizhen</note>. It is cultivated everywhere. Its pulp contains a network of fibers, which is why it is commonly called the "Silk Gourd." It is used as a substitute for a washcloth. The *Compendium* records various medicinal formulas using this plant, which have proven to be quite effective. This gourd does not possess a strong flavor and is not particularly suited to everyone's palate. However, it is easy for common folk to plant and is very hardy; it can be grown in any vacant patch of land. In the regions of the rivers and lakes, some gourds grow as long as five or six feet.

A poem by <term>Zhu Beishan</term> <note>A poet of the Yuan or Ming era</note> reads:

*After several days of rain, the sky clears and autumn grass grows tall;*
*The sponge gourd vines extend, climbing up the tiled walls.*

The image of an old garden’s autumn vines is brought vividly to life by these lines. Another poem by <term>Zhao Meiyin</term> says:

*Yellow flowers fade away as the green body grows long;*
*Wrapped in a hundred knots of silk, it endures the morning frost.*
*It thins until it can be held in a single pinch;*
*Then it brushes a person’s face, stained with the scent of rouge.*

Reflecting on the final line, it likely refers to the gourd’s use as a washcloth <note>specifically for removing makeup or cleansing the skin</note>.

Thus, what began as a common substitute for <term>kuai</term> <note>original: 蒯 (kuai), a type of coarse sedge or grass used for scrubbing</note> has eventually earned a place at the tables of the refined and elegant.

<note>original: 攪絲瓜 (jiaosigua), literally "Stir-silk Gourd." This is the Spaghetti Squash (*Cucurbita pepo*), distinct from the fibrous sponge gourd.</note>

## Water Gourd
<note>original: 水壺廬 (shui hulu). This refers to a variety of the bottle gourd (*Lagenaria siceraria*). In the context of "famine herbals," these plants were studied for their ability to provide sustenance when primary crops failed.</note>

Water gourds are found throughout the Shanxi and the capital regions. In general, they resemble the pumpkin <note>The original term *nangua* (literally "southern melon") refers to various squashes and pumpkins which had been introduced to China and were becoming common by the Ming dynasty.</note>, though their leaves have significantly more forked lobes; the flowers, however, are no different from those of other gourds. There are several varieties of the fruit, including those with green patterns and those that are purely white. If planted early in the season, they reach maturity quickly.

The flesh of the gourd is fibrous and holds much juice, yet farmers and gardeners do not plant them extensively. This is because if they are cooked with rich, fatty meats, they acquire a "flavor beyond flavor" <note>An idiomatic way to describe an exquisite or transcendent taste that arises from the gourd absorbing the fats and juices of the meat.</note>. However, if they are simply boiled with other vegetables and salt, the result is like "adding water to water" <note>original: 如水濟水 (ru shui ji shui). A classical idiom used here to describe a dish that is bland, redundant, and lacks any nutritional or flavor-based "punch."</note>. To those accustomed to a rich diet <term>Gaoliang; literally "fatty meat and fine grain," a traditional term for the lifestyle of the elite</term>, the gourd is a refreshing and savory delicacy; for those with the sallow complexions of the famished, it is merely a grass that scours the intestines <note>The author points out a harsh reality of famine: high-fiber foods that the wealthy find "cleansing" or "refreshing" can be nutritionally empty and physically harsh on the digestive systems of the starving.</note>.

# Paicai (Row Greens)

Paicai is produced in Changsha <note>A city in Hunan province known for its fertile land.</note>. It belongs to the mustard family. Its flowers and leaves are slender, and it grows in clusters of fine stems, with dozens of stems forming a single group. In spring, it sends up a stalk <term>Ting: a flowering stalk or scape emerging from the center of the plant.</term> shaped like a fan; its "cockscomb" <note>Referring to a flattened, crest-like growth at the top of the stalk.</note> is nearly two inches wide. On this stalk, fine stems are interspersed with flowers, blooming much like those of a common mustard plant. The flower heads are heavy, causing the stems to bend like hooks.

When raw, it is not fit for eating. Local people soak and pickle it to make a relish <note>original: 齏 (ji), a term for finely chopped pickled vegetables or condiments</note>; its acidity is quite effective at stimulating the Spleen <term>The Spleen in Traditional Chinese Medicine is the primary organ for digestion and the extraction of energy from food.</term>. Vegetable sellers always blanch the greens before bringing them to market, which turns them a color as yellow as gold. Whether served in thick stews or flash-fried in oil, it is a staple dish that must be provided at every meal.

The *Gazetteer of Shanghai County* <note>A local historical record of the Shanghai region.</note> states: "There is a variety of mustard with fine stems and a flat center known as 'Silver Thread Mustard,' which may perhaps be this very plant." 

In terms of vegetable flavors, sour and pungent are considered superior. Mustard varieties are most abundant in the South, where many people have a fondness for pungent flavors. If a mustard is not pungent, it is allowed to ferment until it becomes sour, at which point it becomes truly delicious. 

The artist Shen Shitian <note>Shen Zhou (1427–1509), a master painter and scholar of the Ming Dynasty.</note> once jokingly wrote *The Biography of Mr. Vegetable-Mustard*, in which he said: "Throughout his life, his mouth was sharp and biting, picking apart the rights and wrongs of others without the slightest mercy. Those who were struck by his words might even find themselves moved to tears, with runny noses and breaking into a sweat." 

Every time I eat mustard, I am reminded of these words and cannot help but burst into laughter <note>original: 噴飯 (penfan), literally "to spray out one's rice," a common idiom for laughing uncontrollably while eating.</note>. Indeed, that it can make people weep and sweat, and yet people...

# Huozhou Oilseed Rape

In the second month of the lunar year <note>This marks the beginning of spring, usually late February or March in the Gregorian calendar.</note>, the Huozhou oilseed rape begins to sprout. Its leaves resemble those of the **broad bean** <note>original: 蠶豆 (candou); also known as the fava bean.</note>, though they are more delicate and soft. A single branchlet typically bears three leaves. The stalks are a vibrant green and grow quite plump, reaching a thickness comparable to one’s little finger. It produces flower spikes that are remarkably thick and dense. When it blooms, the flowers are shaped like those of the **sword bean** <note>original: 刀豆 (daodou).</note> but are yellow in color. As the plant matures, it develops seed pods; these seeds are harvested and pressed to extract oil.

The stalks and the <term>flowering shoots</term> <note>original: 薹 (tai). These are the tender, bolt-like stems of the brassica family, which are highly prized as a seasonal delicacy in Chinese cuisine.</note> share a similar flavor, which carries a hint of bitterness. In years when spring arrives late and the wild grasses are still sparse, this vegetable is among the first to be presented at the table. In local inns, it is frequently served as an accompaniment to wine; it is wonderfully succulent, filling the mouth with a clean, rich taste. ## Gai Lan

**Gai Lan** <note>Also known as Chinese Broccoli or Chinese Kale.</note> is widely cultivated in the <term>Lingnan</term> <note>A geographical area covering modern Guangdong, Guangxi, and Hainan.</note> and <term>Ningdu</term> <note>A county in Jiangxi province.</note> regions; it is also written as **Gai Lan** <note>original: 芥蘭, using a different character for "lan".</note>. The *Notes on the Southern Regions* <note>original: Nanyue Bijie (南越筆記).</note> states that its leaves contain lead and should not be consumed in large quantities. Note: This refers to cooking and eating the leaves; the thick and fleshy parts are plucked for consumption. It grows in winter, and the local people are very fond of it.

Its roots are small and thin, which is quite different from the <term>Pie Lan</term> <note>original: 撇藍 (pienan); Kohlrabi.</note> found in the North. Historically, chroniclers have often conflated the two as a single species. This is likely because Northerners are familiar with Kohlrabi but never see Gai Lan, while those in Fujian and Guangdong know Gai Lan but never see Kohlrabi; they simply assumed they were the same because the names sound similar.

The *Miscellaneous Records of Lingnan* <note>original: Lingnan Zajie (嶺南雜記).</note> states: "Gai Lan is sweet and pungent like mustard; its leaves are a deep indigo-blue, and it is said that if refined, they can produce lead." It is also called **Ge Lan** <note>original: 隔藍 (gelan); literally "Separated Blue/Kale".</note>. Monks say that before the <term>Sixth Patriarch</term> <note>Huineng (638–713), a pivotal figure in Chan (Zen) Buddhism.</note> entered the monastery, he lived as a hunter. Because he refused to eat meat or blood, he would place this vegetable in the same pot as the wild game but kept them separated by a barrier while they boiled so he could eat the vegetable alone; hence the name "Separated Kale."

The *Book of Fujian* <note>original: Min Shu (閩書).</note> states: "The leaves of the Gai Lan vegetable are like those of the indigo plant but thicker, with a verdant blue-green color." It is extremely similar to the <term>Wannianqing</term> <note>original: 萬年青; literally "Ten-thousand-year-green" or Evergreen (Rohdea japonica), though here used to describe a similar-looking vegetable.</note> found in <term>Sichuan</term>, but Gai Lan is a crop planted annually, whereas the Evergreen persists for many years without change and has a slightly more bitter taste. Thus, Gai Lan is produced in Sichuan and other regions as well.

## Zhuge’s Vegetable

Zhuge’s vegetable <note>original: 諸葛菜 (Zhuge cai). Scientifically known as *Orychophragmus violaceus*, or Chinese violet cress. It is a member of the mustard family.</note> is extremely abundant in the northern regions, though it is occasionally found in the Hunan area as well. When the leaves first emerge, they resemble those of the small mallow <note>original: 葵 (kui); likely *Malva verticillata*.</note>. As the stalks sprout, the leaves look like those of rapeseed; the leaves on the stem are slightly broader with rounded teeth and grow in a way that clasps the stem.

In early spring, it produces four-petaled purple flowers that are quite delicate, though some varieties bear white flowers. The plant is resistant to frost and thrives in cold weather; in the capital, the flower buds already begin to open by the second lunar month. When eaten, it has a very smooth and slippery texture. Its roots are slender, unlike the thick, fleshy roots of the turnip <note>original: 蔓菁 (manjing); *Brassica rapa*.</note>.

One of its names is "Zhuge’s Vegetable." According to the *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, a foundational text for identifying plants and animals.</note>, it is listed as the *fei* <note>original: 菲</note> or *xi* vegetable. Guo Pu’s commentary <note>Guo Pu (276–324 CE) was a renowned scholar who provided essential botanical and zoological notes on ancient texts.</note> states: “The *fei* grass grows in low-lying, damp areas. It resembles the turnip, has purple-red flowers, and is edible.”

Lu Ji’s *Commentary on the Classic of Poetry* <note>A 3rd-century work explaining the natural world described in China's oldest collection of poems.</note> notes: “The *fei* resembles the bindweed; its stalks are coarse, and the leaves are thick, long, and covered in fine hairs. In the third lunar month, it is steamed or boiled to be eaten as a vegetable. It is smooth and delicious and can be made into a savory soup.”

The people of Youzhou <note>An ancient region covering parts of modern Beijing, Hebei, and Liaoning.</note> call it *wu* <note>original: 芴</note>, while today the people of Henei <note>A region in modern-day Henan province.</note> call it “overwintering vegetable” <term>su-cai <gloss>literally "lodged vegetable," referring to a plant that survives through the winter to grow again</gloss></term>. Judging by its physical characteristics, it is precisely this plant. It is most common in the north, where it grows wild in abandoned gardens without being intentionally planted; because it grows from roots that have survived the winter, it is called *su-cai*. It is unclear at what point it began to be mistakenly called “Zhuge.” In Jiangxi, there is a type of climbing vine-vegetable that is similar to this.

The **Red Lily** grows in the mountains of **Yunnan** <note>A province in southwestern China known for its diverse flora and high altitude</note>. In its general appearance, it resembles the **Tiger Lily** <note>original: 卷丹 (Juandan), likely *Lilium lancifolium*</note>, though its leaves are shorter and its flowers are more succulent.

From the center of the flower, a single long filament <note>the pistil</note> emerges, ending in a rounded protrusion shaped like a nipple <note>the stigma</note>. Compared to the common Tiger Lily, this variety is considered more refined and elegant.

The **Gaohe Vegetable** grows on **Diancang Mountain** <note>Also known as Cangshan, a famous mountain range in Dali, Yunnan Province</note> in Dali. The *Travels in Yunnan and Guizhou* <note>original: *Dian Qian Ji You* (滇黔紀遊)</note> states that it grows in the seventh or eighth lunar months, with red stems and deep green leaves, tasting as pungent as mustard.

Gui Fu’s *Assorted Notes* <note>original: *Zhapu* (札璞); Gui Fu was a Qing dynasty scholar</note> records that on Cang Mountain there is a herb similar to celery with purple stems. It is pungent, fragrant, and edible, called the "Gaohe Vegetable," following an old name from the **Nanzhao Kingdom** <note>A powerful kingdom that existed in the Yunnan region from the 8th to 9th centuries</note>.

The *Complete Classics and Collection of Illustrations of Ancient and Modern Times* <note>original: *Gujin Tushu Jicheng* (古今圖書集成); the largest encyclopedia ever printed in China (1726)</note> quotes old local gazetteers saying that if one speaks in a loud voice near where it grows, clouds and mist will suddenly rise, and wind and rain will arrive at once. This is because **Gaohe** <note>Literally "High River"</note> is actually a **Dragon Pool** <note>original: *Longqiu* (龍湫); a mountain spring or pond believed to be the home of a dragon spirit</note>.

I sent someone to bring back a **dried specimen** <note>original: *la* (腊); refers to plants preserved by drying</note>. Upon examining its leaves, I found they have many intricate patterns and grow in an alternating, staggered pattern. They slightly resemble chrysanthemum leaves but lack stalks. The taste was not actually pungent, but instead possessed a clear, refined fragrance.

When soaked in water, the liquid turns green. For use in vegetarian meals, it is ranked higher than bok choy or mustard greens. When cooked with meat, it is remarkably similar to the **dried spinach** found in Northern China, yet with a more elegant and fresh quality. It is truly a delicacy among wild vegetables.

However, because Cang Mountain is so high and steep, common reports suggest it is difficult to obtain. Is it because this vegetable is prepared like a common garden variety that the legends of "startling the dragon" make it seem more difficult to fetch? Or has an experienced gardener perhaps transplanted and cultivated it in a garden? Regardless, its color and flavor are both excellent.

### Technical Terms:
- **Dali** <term>Dali (大理): A city in Yunnan, historically the center of the Nanzhao and Dali Kingdoms, known for its unique flora and marble.</term>
- **Dragon Pool** <term>Longqiu (龍湫): In Chinese folklore, high-altitude pools were often considered sacred. The "shouting for rain" phenomenon mentioned here is a common motif in Chinese mountain lore, often linked to sensitive local microclimates.</term>
- **Mustard Greens** <term>Jie (芥): Refers to various species of *Brassica*, known for their sharp, biting flavor which the Gaohe Vegetable was said to share.</term>

Every time I chew this plant, I exclaim that I have never before obtained the true **Gaohe Vegetable**. The *Gazetteer of Langyan Well* <note>original: *Langyanjing Zhi* (琅鹽井志); Langyan Well was a significant salt-producing center in Yunnan</note> records a plant called the **Lazy Woman’s Vegetable** <note>original: *Lanpocai* (懶婆菜)</note>. In my own hometown, any vegetable that is not transplanted <note>In traditional Chinese agriculture, transplanting was a key step to ensure plants grew large and tender</note> is called a "Lazy Woman's Vegetable." This is because, without the effort of cultivation and tending <note>original: *peishi* (培蒔)</note>, the plant’s life force is spent quickly and it ages prematurely; the body of the plant remains stunted and soon becomes tough and dry. 

For this reason, the old botanical illustrations and elders consider it a lowly, cheap thing. Yet, the vegetable from the Langyan Salt Well specifically earns its fame through this "laziness." If a person can achieve a high and noble character through "laziness" <note>This refers to the philosophical ideal of non-striving or *wu wei*</note>, is that not like the Gaohe vegetable? It stands alone in pure, cold isolation, causing people to look up in admiration as it "lies in the snow and drinks the clouds," making it difficult to obtain.

Meanwhile, the vegetable of the Langyan Well requires no selective effort <note>original: *tijue* (剔抉); the act of picking or choosing with great care</note> to thrive, and thus preserves its natural essence <note>original: *tianzhen* (天眞); literally "heavenly truth," referring to a state of uncorrupted nature</note>. **Zhai Tang** <note>A famous hermit of the Jin Dynasty known for his integrity</note> once said to the statesman **Yu Liang**: "Your Lordship respects your own 'withered wood and rotten stumps' <note>A famous historical anecdote where Zhai Tang used this metaphor to describe his own humble, retired life which Yu Liang nevertheless respected</note>." 

Therefore, when one faces this humble vegetable, one should also push aside their meal and rise to show respect.

## Adamant Spike

The **Adamant Spike** grows deep within the mountains of **Yunnan**. It possesses a single main stem with many fine, delicate branches; each branch bears five leaves. In appearance, it resembles the "Single Broom" plant <note>original: *Duzhou* (獨帚), typically referring to the Burningbush or *Kochia scoparia*</note>, though its foliage is significantly more pointed and elongated.

The mountain dwellers gather this plant to use as a vegetable. In the region of **Kunming**, the people harvest the tender leaves and boil them <term>to make a soup</term> <note>original: *mao* (芼); an ancient culinary term specifically referring to the preparation of greens or herbs in a broth</note>. The resulting dish is refreshing and carries a subtle bitterness that is quite flavorful. Locally, it is known by the name **Wawangtou**.

### Zhiteng Arugula
<note>original: *Zhiteng cai* (芝藤菜). While the previous page used the standard name *Zhima cai*, this text uses a regional variant from Yunnan. The description of the yellow flowers with "black veins" confirms its identity as *Eruca vesicaria*.</note>

**Zhiteng Arugula** grows in **Yunnan**. When it first sprouts, it resembles **young cabbage** <note>original: *songcai* (菘菜); a broad term in historical Chinese botany for leafy greens in the *Brassica* family, such as bok choy or napa cabbage</note>. It puts forth stalks and blooms with **four-petaled yellow flowers** that have **black veins**. The plant grows to about a **foot** in height. When eaten raw, its taste is similar to **white lettuce** <note>original: *baiju* (白苣); likely referring to a type of chicory or endive</note> but with a slightly bitter aroma. According to the **Herbal of Southern Yunnan** <note>original: *Dian Nan Ben Cao* (滇南本草); a significant pharmaceutical work by Lan Mao (1397–1476) focused on the flora of Southwest China</note>, its medicinal nature is **slightly cold**. It is used to treat syndromes of **wind-stroke** <term>Zhongfeng (中風): a traditional category for sudden-onset conditions like paralysis, facial drooping, or loss of consciousness, often corresponding to modern stroke</term> and **summer-heat** <term>Shure (暑熱): a traditional diagnostic category for illnesses caused by extreme heat and humidity, such as heat exhaustion or seasonal fevers</term>.
