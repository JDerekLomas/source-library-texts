### Chapter 3: Astronomy of the Earth

If an external force constrains it, it can shrink from a large size to a small one; yet its physical substance also possesses an inherent force. If that external constraint is removed, it can expand once more and return to its larger state. 

For example, if a leather <term>balloon; literally: "air-ball" (氣球)</term> is filled with air and its opening is sealed tight, should a person press it with their hand, the air will necessarily contract and compress. Once the hand is removed, the air will expand and return to its original state. 

While the hand is pressing down, one feels the power of the air surging upward and resisting the hand. If one were to throw the balloon against the ground, it would inevitably bounce. If air did not possess the <term>elasticity; literally: "power of hardness and softness" (剛柔之力)</term>, how could it behave in such a way? 

Furthermore, there are variations in the degree of this elasticity. If the pressing force is small, the upward surging force is also small; if the pressing force is large, the upward surging force is likewise large. 

Additionally, three properties of its nature can be observed: 
First, no matter how many years it is held under pressure, its force remains constant from beginning to end without any loss or diminution. 
Second, the more it is heated, the greater its force becomes. <note>This is an early description of the relationship between pressure, volume, and temperature, known in modern science as the Ideal Gas Law.</note>

# Theory of Wind

It is said that while air is a substance of constant movement and circulation, its original nature is actually quiet and motionless. Up and down, and toward all four sides, it remains unbiased and balanced, consistently maintaining a state of <term>equilibrium; literally: "even balance" (均平)</term>. If the state of equilibrium is lost in even a single location, the surrounding air will all move because of this shift. This movement is what we call "wind."

The causes for the formation of wind are twofold: one is heat, and the other is cold. Heat causes the air to expand and disperse, making the air in that specific location lighter and thinner than in other areas. Cold, conversely, causes the air to condense and gather together, making the air there heavier and denser than in other areas. Because there are variations in the speed at which air moves, the winds that arise are distinguished by whether they are gentle or rapid.
