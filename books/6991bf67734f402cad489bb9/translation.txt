# The Inner Binding and Endpapers

The interior of this volume reflects the same high standard of craftsmanship as its exterior, greeting the reader with a vibrant display of color and texture. 

-> **Decorative Panel** <-

The central feature of the inner cover is a panel of **<term>marbled paper</term>** <note>A decorative paper created by floating pigments on a liquid surface and "combing" them into patterns before transferring them to paper</note>. This specific example uses a **<term>stone pattern</term>** <note>A marbling design intended to mimic the natural veins and splotches found in marble or granite</note>, rendered in earthy shades of brown, ochre, and black.

Surrounding the marbled paper is a border of fine brown leather. This border is adorned with **<term>gold tooling</term>** <note>The artistic process of pressing gold leaf into the leather surface using heated brass stamps or rollers</note>. The decoration consists of triple **<term>fillets</term>** <note>Straight, parallel decorative lines impressed into the binding</note> that frame the inner panel with precision.

These elements together form the **<term>endpaper</term>** <note>The sheet of paper pasted onto the inner cover to hide the structural elements of the binding and protect the text block</note>. The use of such sophisticated materials and techniques is typical of high-quality **<term>bookbinding</term>** from the 18th or 19th centuries, marking this as a luxury edition intended for a significant private or institutional collection.

<summary>This page describes the decorative interior of a luxury book binding, featuring a hand-marbled paper panel framed by gold-tooled leather. These details highlight the meticulous craftsmanship and high social status associated with the volume's production.</summary>

# ALSO THE LETTER OF THE AFORESAID DIONYSIUS TO BONIFACE
## AND TO SECUNDINUS

**T**o the good men, most venerable to me, Boniface, chief of the notaries <term>Primicerius: The head of a department, in this case, the papal chancery.</term> and Secundinus, second chief of the notaries <term>Secundicerius: The second-in-command of the chancery.</term>, **DIONYSIUS EXIGUUS**.
Having long been compelled by the prompting of the holy and venerable Bishop Petronius to finally commit the rule of the Paschal reverence to the pen, I believed that every subsequent opposition of diversity would be removed; especially because I had taken care to instill the authority of the 318 holy bishops who met at Nicaea <note>The Council of Nicaea in 325 AD, which attempted to standardize the date of Easter for all Christians.</note> into these volumes. These bishops, in that venerable council, properly establishing the nineteen-year cycle <term>Decennovenal cycle: A 19-year period after which the phases of the moon recur on the same days of the solar year.</term>, noted that the 14th moons of Easter <note>The "Paschal Full Moon" used to determine the date of Easter Sunday.</note> remained immobile throughout all times by the law of its revolution. 
But since your holinesses ask a question regarding this matter from the archives of the Roman church concerning Bishop Paschasinus—who it is established presided over the holy Council of Chalcedon in the person of the most blessed [Pope] Leo—addressed to that same Pope at that time, and which is now being scrutinized; which things clearly agree with the holy fathers in evident reason. We declare these present things to be best, so that we might also rely on your testimony, which confirmed the Paschal decrees of the venerable pontiffs by a manifest miracle. Although the account of common and embolismic years <term>Embolismic: An intercalary or "leap" month added to the lunar calendar to keep it synchronized with the solar seasons.</term> has been made in those writings, and this account—which is said to descend from the tradition of the Hebrews—is greatly sought after by those wishing to know whether it seems to harmonize with this rule. We have deemed it necessary to digest this information also, lest it be shown to differ in any way, through a forced brevity. 
Thus, we should know that this same 19-year circle always revolves the ogdoad <term>Ogdoad: A group of eight years (from the Greek 'octo').</term> and the endecad <term>Endecad: A group of eleven years (from the Greek 'hendeka').</term> within itself. For that number [19] is explained as eight and eleven. The ogdoad is that which begins from the first [year] of the 19-year cycle, which proceeds in this manner by the lunar 17 <note>This refers to the "Epact" or age of the moon at the start of the cycle.</note>. So that it has the first and second years as "common"—that is, the smaller years; the third as "embolismic"—that is, the larger year; the fourth and fifth likewise as common; the sixth as embolismic; the seventh as common; and the eighth as embolismic. And through this, five common years and three embolismic years are continually assigned to the ogdoad. A common year gathers twelve lunar months, which make 354 days. An embolismic year, however, is shown to have 13 moons and 384 days. 
Likewise, the endecad runs by this law: it begins in the ninth year of the 19-year cycle, which runs by lunar six. The first and second years are common; the third is embolismic; the fourth and fifth are common; the sixth is embolismic; the seventh and eighth are common; the ninth is embolismic; the tenth is common; and the eleventh is embolismic. Thus the endecad is concluded with seven common years and four embolismic years. 
The logic of the embolisms is proved to exist for this reason: that it is seen to supply the "losses" of the common years, so that the lunar course may be made equal to the solar time. For although the moon circles the annual cycle of the sun through each month, yet it is not able to fulfill its perfection in its own twelve months. Finally, in common years, eleven days of the moon are seen to be lacking compared to the account of the solar year. But in embolismic years, the lunar year is seen to exceed the solar year by eighteen days. Therefore, let us bring forward the years of the ogdoad and the endecad into the open according to the order of the aforementioned circle, and we shall clearly prove that through 8 years and 11 years the moon contends with the sun, when the moon collects just as many days as the sun has run. In the ogdoad, we said there are five common years and three embolismic years. Five times 354 days becomes 1,770; and three times 384 becomes 1,152.

### EXPLANATORY NOTES ON THE FIGURED POEM
<note>The text above is a translation of the phrases embedded in the letter-grid. In the original Latin, this grid is a perfect 35x35 square.</note>

**The Dedication:**
The first line addresses **Haistulf** and **Otgar**.
<note>Haistulf was the Archbishop of Mainz (813–825 AD) and Otgar was his successor. Hrabanus Maurus served under them as a monk and later as Abbot of Fulda. This version of the poem was likely a presentation copy for these powerful church leaders.</note>

**The Visual Symbols:**
*   **The Cross:** Lines of text intersect to form a giant cross. The horizontal and vertical bars of the cross contain a separate, hidden poem praising the sacrifice of Jesus.
*   **The Diamond:** A diamond shape (rhombus) is often superimposed over the cross, representing the "four corners of the world" or the "enclosure of the Church."
*   **Repetitive Phrases:** You will notice phrases like "And with love" or "Upwards and downwards" repeat. These are used by Hrabanus to fill the geometric shapes so that the letters line up perfectly to form the grid.

**The Meaning of the Final Line:**
The poem concludes with a reference to the **Ocean** and the **Altars**.
<note>original: "Oceani... resonat laribus." This suggests that the praise of the Cross resounds from the ends of the earth (the Ocean) to the very hearths and altars of the faithful.</note>
