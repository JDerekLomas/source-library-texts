Probability is relative, partly to this ignorance and partly to our knowledge. We know that out of three or a greater number of events, only one must happen; but nothing leads us to believe that one of them will happen rather than the others. In this state of indecision, it is impossible for us to pronounce with certainty upon their arrival. It is, however, probable that one of these events, taken at will, will not happen; because we see several equally possible cases which exclude its arrival, while only one favors it.

The theory of chance <note>original: "théorie des hasards," now commonly referred to as probability theory.</note> consists in reducing all events of the same kind to a certain number of equally possible cases, that is to say, such that we are equally undecided about their existence; and in determining the number of cases favorable to the event whose probability is sought. The ratio of this number to that of all possible cases is the measure of this probability, which is thus only a fraction whose numerator is the number of favorable cases, and whose denominator is the number of all possible cases.

One can imagine the two black balls of the first urn attached to a thread that breaks at the moment one of them is seized, and the four white balls forming two similar systems. All the chances that lead to seizing one of the balls of the black system will bring out a black ball. If one now conceives that the threads uniting the balls do not break at all, it is clear that the number of possible chances will not change, nor will the number of chances favorable to the extraction of black balls; only, one will draw two balls from the urn at once. The probability of extracting a black ball from the urn will therefore be the same as before. But then, we evidently have the case of urn B, with the only difference being that the three balls of this latter urn are replaced by three systems of two balls invariably united. Here, the equally possible cases are not the extractions of the balls themselves; they are the chances that bring them about, the sum of which—assumed to be the same for each urn—is distributed over six balls in the first, and over three in the second.

The first of these principles is the very definition of probability which, as has been seen, is the ratio of the number of favorable cases to that of all possible cases.

But this supposes the various cases to be equally possible. <margin>6th Principle.</margin>
Each of the causes to which an observed event may be attributed is indicated with more likelihood the more probable it is that, assuming this cause exists, the event will take place; the probability of the existence of any one of these causes is therefore a fraction whose numerator is the probability of the event resulting from that cause, and whose denominator is the sum of similar probabilities relative to all the causes: if these various causes, considered *a priori*, are unequally probable, one must, instead of the probability of the event resulting from each cause, employ the product of that probability by the probability of the cause itself. ### *On Expectation.*

The probability of events serves to determine the hope or the fear of persons interested in their existence. The word *expectation* <note>original: "espérance"</note> has various meanings: it generally expresses the advantage of one who awaits a certain good, under suppositions that are only probable. This advantage, in the theory of chance <term>theory of chance: the mathematical study of random phenomena</term>, is the product of the sum expected by the probability of obtaining it: it is the partial sum that should be returned when one does not wish to run the risks of the event, assuming that the distribution is made proportionally to the probabilities. This distribution is the only equitable one when all extraneous circumstances are set aside; because with an equal degree of probability, one has an equal right to the sum expected. We shall call this advantage *mathematical expectation*.

<margin>**VIIIth Principle.**</margin>
When it depends on several events, it is obtained by taking the sum of the products of the probability of each event by the benefit attached to its occurrence.

This is the sum he must give in advance to the one who provides him this advantage; for in a fair game, the stake must be equal to the advantage it procures.

### *On profits depending on the probability of events.*

Let us recall here what we said when speaking of expectation <note>The author refers to "mathematical expectation," a fundamental concept in probability where one calculates the average outcome of a random variable.</note>. We have seen that to obtain the advantage resulting from several simple events, some of which produce a gain and others a loss, one must add the products of the probability of each favorable event by the benefit it procures, and subtract from this sum the products of the probability of each unfavorable event by the loss attached to it. But whatever the advantage expressed by the difference of these sums may be, a single event composed of these simple events does not guarantee against the fear of experiencing a real loss. One understands that this fear must diminish when the composite event is multiplied. The analysis of probabilities leads to this general theorem.

Through the repetition of an advantageous event, whether simple or composite, the real profit becomes more and more probable and increases constantly. It becomes certain under the hypothesis of an infinite number of repetitions; and by dividing it by their number, the quotient—or the average profit of each event—is the mathematical expectation itself, or the advantage relative to the event. It is the same for loss, which becomes certain in the long run, however slightly disadvantageous the event may be.

This theorem on profits and losses is analogous to those we have previously given regarding the ratios indicated by the indefinite repetition of simple or composite events; and like them, it proves that regularity ends up establishing itself even in those things most subordinated to what we call *chance* <note>original: "hasard"</note>.

### *On illusions in the estimation of probabilities.*

The mind has its illusions, just as the sense of sight does; and just as the sense of touch rectifies the latter, reflection and calculation likewise correct the former. Probability based on daily experience, or exaggerated by fear and hope, strikes us more than a superior probability that is merely a simple result of calculation. Thus, for slight advantages, we do not fear exposing our lives to dangers much less improbable than drawing a <term>quine</term> <note>A "quine" refers to successfully drawing five specific numbers in a lottery, an event with extremely low odds.</note> in the lottery of France; and yet no one would wish to secure those same advantages with the certainty of losing their life if that quine were drawn.

The events we witness have an influence on our judgments that often deceives us in the assessment of the causes upon which they depend. The vivid impression we receive from them hardly allows us to notice the contrary events that others have observed. One cannot take too much care to guard against this illusion, which is one of the principal sources of our errors.

The coincidence of certain remarkable events with the predictions of astrologers, soothsayers, and <term>augurs</term> <note>In ancient Rome, an augur was a religious official who observed natural signs, such as the flight of birds, to interpret the will of the gods.</note>, with dreams, or with numbers and days reputed to be lucky or unlucky, etc., has given rise to a multitude of prejudices that are still very widespread. We do not reflect on the great number of non-coincidences which made no impression or of which we are unaware. However, it is only the ratio of one to the other that can provide the probability of the causes to which these coincidences are attributed. If this ratio were known, experience would undoubtedly confirm what common sense and reason dictate to us regarding these prejudices. Thus, the philosopher of antiquity <note>Likely a reference to Diagoras of Melos or Cicero’s account of a similar story.</note>, to whom were shown in a temple—in order to exalt the power of the god worshiped there—all the <term>ex-voto</term> <note>original: "ex-voto"; these are offerings or tablets left in a temple to fulfill a vow or show gratitude for a miracle.</note> of those who, after having invoked the god, had been saved from shipwreck, asked a question consistent with the calculus of probabilities by asking how many people, despite that invocation, had perished.
