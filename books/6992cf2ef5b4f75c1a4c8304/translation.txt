## A Study of the Three Guards of Duoyan

The <term>Three Guards</term> <note>original: "三衛" (Sanwei) - These were administrative-military units established by the Ming Dynasty to manage and ally with Mongol tribes on the northeastern border.</note> are located to the north of the <term>Daning Regional Military Commission</term> <note>original: "大寧都司" - A strategic military outpost in modern-day Inner Mongolia that served as a headquarters for frontier defense.</note>. They are named **Duoyan**, **Fuyu**, and **Taining**. 

In terms of history, this land belonged to the **Mountain Barbarians** <note>original: "山戎" (Shanrong) - A nomadic people known for raiding northern Chinese states during the early first millennium BCE.</note> during the <term>Spring and Autumn period</term> <note>770–476 BCE.</note>. During the Qin Dynasty, it formed the northern border of **Liaoxi Prefecture**. Later, it was occupied by the **Xi** and **Khitan** peoples. 

The **Eastern Han Dynasty** launched a campaign and defeated them; consequently, their chieftains fled and went into hiding within the **Songmo** <note>original: "松漠" - Literally the "Pine Desert," a region located in the upper reaches of the Liao River.</note> region. The **Kumo Xi** <note>An ancient nomadic people of Northeast China, closely related to the Khitan.</note> were later subordinates of the **Khitan** and were known as the **Uriankhai** <note>original: "兀良哈" (Wuliangha) - A Mongol ethnic group; the name was often used by the Ming to refer to the Three Guards.</note>; they still retain this name today. During the **Yuan Dynasty**, the **Daning Thousand Households Office** was established on the site of the former capital of the Xi.

At the beginning of our dynasty <note>The Ming Dynasty, starting 1368.</note>, the Regional Military Commission and the **Yingzhou Guard** were established. In the fourteenth year of the **Hongwu** era <note>1381 CE.</note>, the Emperor enfeoffed his son, **Zhu Quan**, as the **Prince of Ning** to guard the northern frontier. At that time, **Feng Sheng**, the **Duke of Song**, was conducting a campaign against **Nahachu** <note>A prominent Mongol leader in the northeast who commanded a large force before surrendering to the Ming.</note>. The Emperor issued an edict ordering him to occupy the **Daning** passes and distribute troops to establish outposts in order to maintain control.

The government even released funds from the internal treasury <note>original: "内帑鈔" (neitang chao) - Paper currency from the Emperor's private funds, used here to incentivize the difficult task of northern logistics.</note> to hire civilians to transport grain and supply the army. Feng Sheng subsequently constructed four cities: **Daning**, **Kuanhe**, **Huizhou**, and **Fuyu**. Whenever the army set out on a campaign, heavy reinforcements were always left behind to defend these positions. Ultimately, they succeeded in defeating and securing the surrender of Nahachu.

In the twenty-first year [of Hongwu, 1388], **Alishili**, a member of the former Yuan imperial clan and **Prince of Liao**, along with various chieftains of the **Duoyan** tribe, petitioned to submit to the Ming. An imperial decree was issued to establish the **Three Guards** as their place of residence. The territory stretching from **Jinzhou** and **Yizhou**, passing through **Guangning**, and reaching the **Tao River** became the **Taining Guard**. Alishili was appointed as the Commandant, and **Dabin Temür**— <note>The text concludes mid-sentence; Dabin Temür was another Mongol leader appointed to a high post within the Taining Guard.</note>

In the fourth year [of the Zhengtong era, 1439], the **Fuyu** and **Taining** tribes formed an alliance with **Esen** <note>The powerful leader of the Oirat Mongols.</note> to launch an invasion. The **Doyan** tribe alone held a strategic pass and refused to follow them. When the invaders failed to gain an advantage elsewhere, they turned to plunder the people and livestock of the other two Guards before departing. Consequently, the **Fuyu** and **Taining** grew increasingly weak, while the **Doyan** remained strong and prosperous. Eventually, however, even the **Doyan** joined with the invaders. It was because of this shifting alliance that we suffered the **Tumu Crisis** <term>Tumu zhi bian</term> <note>The 1449 battle where the Ming army was decimated and the Zhengtong Emperor was captured by Oirat forces.</note>.

From that time on, when the **Three Guards** came to present tribute, they would frequently mingle their own people among the Northern invaders' envoys to spy on our defenses. When they saw us treating the Northern envoys with greater generosity, they became resentful. Furthermore, because they knew we lived in fear of the Oirats, they constantly used their ties to the North to increase their own importance, forming marriage alliances and maintaining communications with the Northern tribes to intimidate us.

# Essentials of the Harbors

The country has three major ports; all are gathering places for merchant ships and are river-mouths leading to the sea. Along the West Sea Circuit <term>Saikaido</term> are Bo-no-tsu <gloss>Satsuma Province</gloss>, Hakozaki <gloss>Chikuzen Province</gloss>, and Anotsu <gloss>Ise Province</gloss>. <note>Historically, Bo-no-tsu, Hakozaki, and Anotsu were known as the "Three Great Ports of Japan." Note that Ise is geographically on the eastern side of the country, though grouped here for its importance.</note> Among these three, Bo-no-tsu serves as the primary route; visiting ships must pass through it on their journeys to and fro.

Hakozaki is considered the central port. The territory is vast and the population is dense <term>min’en</term>; <note>Literally "people’s smoke," a poetic way to describe a bustling, populated area.</note> there are no Chinese maritime merchants who do not gather there. In this place, there is a pine forest stretching ten miles long <gloss>called the Ten-Mile Pines</gloss>. The local name is Hakozaki <gloss>sa-ki</gloss>, which refers to the area in front of the shrine. <note>original: "法哥煞機" (Fa-ge-sha-ji). This is a phonetic transcription of Hakozaki, likely referring to the famous Hakozaki Hachiman-gu Shrine.</note>

There is a street named "Greater Tang Street" <term>Daitogai</term>. Chinese people remained there, and it is said that today they have all become Japanese. <note>This refers to the historical "Tōjin-machi" (Chinese towns) where merchants from the Ming Dynasty settled and eventually assimilated.</note>

Anotsu is the final port. The location is distant and sits close to Yamashiro <note>The province containing the imperial capital, Kyoto.</note>; its supply of goods is inconsistent, sometimes well-stocked and sometimes lacking. Only the central port <note>Hakozaki</note> has everything available. Trade is conducted using silver, gold, and copper coins, relying on agents called *naika*. <note>original: "乃隔" (Naika/Naige). These were specialized brokers or wholesalers who acted as intermediaries for foreign trade.</note>
