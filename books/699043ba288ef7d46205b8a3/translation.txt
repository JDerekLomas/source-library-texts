But even suppose that someone has expressly threatened that he wishes to harm another through magic; how will the other requirement of this indication be established—namely, that harm was actually done to the other PER MAGIC <note>original: "PER MAGIAM"</note>, since no such Magic exists? There are many hidden things in nature through which harm can be given to another even without the cooperation of the devil, on account of the magnetism of nature <note>A reference to "animal magnetism" or sympathetic magic, which some early modern thinkers viewed as a natural physical force rather than a demonic one</note>, the reason for which neither the Aristotelians nor the Cartesians <note>Followers of Aristotle or René Descartes; the author is critiquing the two dominant philosophical schools of his time</note> can provide. But this is the ancient asylum of Academic ignorance: whatever effects cannot be demonstrated from the textbooks of Academic Physics, and yet cannot conveniently be ascribed to God, must be ascribed to the Devil.

§. LIV. The *third indication*, association with magicians and witches <note>original: "magis & sagis"</note>, begs the question. For since there are no magicians and witches, there is also no association with them. Furthermore, even if we grant that witches exist, nevertheless association with them would be a most deceptive indication, since there could be many other causes—for example, kinship, neighborhood, upbringing, financial interest, equality of status, and infinite others—why someone might have associated with such a magician. Shall we think those people are adulterers, forgers, or gluttons who associate even familiarly with such men? To be sure, the little verse is well known: *He is known by his companion who is not known by himself.* But it is also well known that verses of that kind are not sufficient for torture; otherwise, by a similar proverb—that a man alone with a woman is not presumed to be praying the Our Father <note>original: "Pater noster"</note>—anyone found alone with a woman would also have to be tortured. §. LVI. What cautions of judgment, therefore, must be applied in trials against witches <note>original: "sagas"</note> so that the innocent are not punished? The author of the *Criminal Caution* <note>original: "Cautionis Criminalis," the famous 1631 work by Friedrich Spee which criticized the torture used in witch trials</note> lists several in Question 16 and following, because he acts as if he believes in the existence of the crime of magic; however, every single one of these is subject to infinite abuses, to be examined elsewhere. For us, there is but a single caution, because we consider the crime of magic to be a myth: that the Sovereign should not permit any inquiry into the "crime of magic"—that is, concerning a pact between humans and the devil (for we are not speaking here of harm caused to people through hidden magic, whether natural or artificial)—and that a lower judge should never initiate such an inquiry. And although it is well known to us that intermediate magistrates are the executors of the supreme power in the State and cannot themselves correct received laws or customs, we are simultaneously certain that sufficient evidence for an inquiry will never be present. Therefore, a lower judge can defend his own method of proceeding by following the laws themselves and what they dictate regarding evidence of crimes, provided he grants the accused a defense to avert the inquiry.
