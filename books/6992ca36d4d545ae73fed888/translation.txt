->Haicheng — Qiu Weiling, Shuyuan<-

Emperor Wu of the Han Dynasty <note>Emperor Wu (r. 141–87 BCE) famously spent vast fortunes seeking the elixir of immortality, only to express regret in his later years.</note> once said: "How could there be divine immortals in heaven?" This was a statement born of realization after much experience, and it has stood as a definitive argument for a thousand years. However, the literary mind and the artist's intent are often vast and unconstrained; writers excel at inventing far-fetched associations. These stories are passed down through popular tradition, and as they are retold, errors are gradually accepted as truth. If one begins to believe that a realm of immortals truly exists, one is deluded. 

Take, for instance, the ancient claims that Lady Shangyuan <note>A high-ranking goddess in the Daoist pantheon.</note> desired to marry Feng Zhi, or that the Primordial Lady of Purple Simplicity <note>Zisu Yuanjun, another celestial figure.</note> wished to follow Ren Yuanzhi. In their own time, there must have been noblewomen or close relatives of the imperial family involved in such affairs—much like the story of Emperor Guangwu’s sister <note>Princess Huyang, who wished to marry the official Song Hong; Song Hong famously refused, stating that "the friend of one's humble days must not be forgotten."</note> setting her heart on Song Hong. Because the writers of those days found it difficult to speak of these scandals directly, they composed these absurd fables to allude to actual events. Consequently, even divine immortals were covered in a shroud of impurity. This completely contradicts the ancient descriptions of the "Supreme Being who has forgotten worldly passions" <note>A concept from the philosopher Zhuangzi, suggesting that the highest state of being is to be beyond the reach of human emotions.</note> or those who exist transcendently beyond the dusty world. Recognizing this, one can realize that stories of immortals have no basis in fact; furthermore, one can see that all novels that use borrowed names to commit slander are not even worth the effort of an argument.

In the village operas of the countryside, there are plays with titles such as *Baili Xi Refuses to Recognize His Wife* and *Cai Bojie is Unfilial to His Parents*. <note>Baili Xi and Cai Yong (Bojie) were historical figures of high integrity who were frequently slandered in popular plays to create dramatic tension.</note> When the common people watch these, they feel such a sense of injustice on behalf of the protagonists that their eyes nearly burst with rage. I personally wonder: what grudge did the playwrights hold against these two gentlemen that they felt the need to—

Ultimately, the reputation of the sages of old rests upon the "straight path" of truth; there are things between heaven and earth that are naturally indestructible. Could a few actors like You Meng <note>You Meng was a famous court jester of the State of Chu (c. 6th century BCE), renowned for his ability to impersonate others so convincingly—using makeup and costumes—that he could influence the King's perspective on history and politics.</note>, with their face paint and blurred ink, truly overturn the judgment of an entire era? I have always disliked the way mediocre artists paint dragons, invariably exposing the tail. A divine dragon’s transformations are meant to be a fleeting glimpse; it is far better to "see the head but not the tail." Consider the idiom of "drawing a snake and adding feet"—this is merely an attempt to exhaust every detail of form, a wasted effort in the craft of describing objects <term>Ti wu: a literary term referring to the evocative description of the physical world</term>.

If a single brushstroke is repeated, even an occasional bit of humor can achieve a classical and refined transformation; what is truly insignificant is when the writing style becomes overly ornate and cluttered. Since Wang Tao <note>Wang Tao (1828–1897) was a pioneering Chinese journalist and author. His *Later Tales from the Liao Zhai Studio* attempted to emulate Pu Songling’s famous ghost stories.</note> wrote his *Later Tales*, every chapter has been identical in tone, which is naturally flavorless. 

Besides that, the five collections of *Notes from the Thatched Abode of Close Observation* <note>original: *Yuewei Caotang Biji* (閱微草堂筆記), a collection of supernatural anecdotes and moral reflections by Ji Yun.</note> follow a rigorous and austere style. It is brief in its storytelling, yet the breadth and grounded nature of its discourse creates a school of its own; it is indeed a leader among novels. 

I once remarked that if Pu Songling <note>The author of *Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio* (*Liaozhai Zhiyi*).</note> had not written his *Strange Tales*, he would have naturally faded into obscurity without a legacy. However, even if Ji Yun <note>Ji Yun (1724–1805), also known as Ji Xiaolan, was a high official and the chief editor of the *Siku Quanshu*.</note> had not written his *Notes*, his lifelong energy spent on the *Annotated Catalog of the Imperial Library* <note>original: *Siku Tiyao* (四庫提要), the massive descriptive catalog of the Emperor's library.</note> would certainly remain an indestructible monument.

I once considered the book *The Golden Lotus* <note>original: *Jin Ping Mei* (金瓶梅), a famous Ming dynasty novel known for its realistic depiction of domestic life and its explicit sexual content.</note>, whose fame fills the world. I suspected that although its lewdness and obscenity might disturb one’s will and violate prohibitions, its literary style must be elegant and its spirit so hearty that it surely stands shoulder-to-shoulder with "secret histories" <note>term: *mixin* (秘辛) — a genre of writing focused on scandalous or private details of famous people.</note> or surpasses "unauthorized biographies." 

I went through several channels to borrow a copy from a friend. I opened the box and read it, finishing in three days. In the end, I could not find a single wonderful stroke of the brush within it. Suspecting I had been too reckless and skipped over parts, I opened it and read it again and again, but I still could not see its "wonder." 

Furthermore, the prose is dragging and sluggish; it lacks the ethereal transformations of *The Dream of the Red Chamber* and falls short of the vivid, dripping characterizations found in *The Mirror of Flowery Elegance* <note>original: *Pinhua Baojian* (品花寶鑑), a 19th-century novel focused on the lives of male opera performers and their patrons.</note>. I do not know how it sustains such a heavy reputation. 

Is it perhaps because the book has been banned and destroyed in so many places that few copies circulate? People’s hearts are easily swayed; they take what they hear as what they see. Thus, they end up "treasuring their own broken brooms" <note>An idiom meaning to overvalue something of little worth simply because it is one's own.</note> or treating "spoiled meat" <note>original: *loufu* (漏脯) — meat contaminated by rainwater dripping from a roof, considered toxic or foul in classical literature.</note> as a delicacy at the table.

Whether a work is good or not, one must first set down a draft, for one can wait for a later time to revise or prune it. If, after such revisions, it still does not satisfy, only then should it be discarded. In the end, I am simply unwilling to let the current opportunity pass by.

The Hermit of the Independent Mountain <gloss>The sobriquet of Pan Lanshi</gloss> has, over many years spent in Hong Kong <note>original: *Xianggang*; literally "Fragrant Harbor"</note>, collected the poems he composed into a volume titled *Fragrant Sea Collection* <note>original: *Xianghai ji*; a poetic reference to Hong Kong</note> for publication. 

He sent a letter requesting a preface. In my reply, I wrote: Having received your letter and learned that your great work is to be carved onto immortal wood <note>original: *shoumu*; a traditional metaphor for printing blocks, suggesting the work will "live" forever</note> in the Fragrant River <note>original: *Xiangjiang*; another name for Hong Kong</note>, my joy is boundless. I have long harbored thoughts I wished to express but have lacked the proper occasion; now, I dare to voice them for a true friend. 

Now, Hong Kong is but a small corner. It possesses no famous mountains or great rivers sufficient to broaden one's spirit, nor the legacy of former sages and past worthies to stir one's aspirations. In the past, it was a vast expanse of reeds that crowds of people passed by and forgot. Today, it is a bustling, noisy marketplace of dust, low and lacking in any lofty discourse. And yet, the "sounds of nature" <note>original: *tianlai*; referring to spontaneous, unforced poetic inspiration</note> ring out here on their own. Like the songs of Ying <note>a reference to a classical story where a singer’s music was so refined that very few people—the "few to harmonize"—could sing along</note>, your verses find few who can match them. Is this not something that simply cannot be stopped?

However, I have personally passed through this place. Situated at the crossroads of Fujian and Guangdong <note>original: *Min-Yue*</note>, where people from the five continents gather, it is indeed a major stronghold in the southern heavens. When examining its history, the events of the past are not far off. It sits close to our "inner heart" <note>referring to the Chinese mainland</note>, yet the external situation is worrisome; it has become a frontier land <term>Otuo|甌脫; originally referring to a border shelter or no-man's-land between states</term> that acts like a festering ulcer.

One’s reputation is always at the mercy of the swaying waves of public opinion. When can the "lumps in one's chest" <note>An idiom referring to pent-up indignation or frustration</note> finally be dissolved by pouring a cup of wine? Your use of "fragrant herbs and beautiful people" <note>A classic literary trope used to symbolize virtuous characters and political integrity</note> is surely nothing more than a way to express your deep feelings. I suspect that the creation of this collection stems from the same sentiments as Wang Can’s "Climbing the Tower" <note>A famous poem by Wang Can (177–217 AD) expressing the sorrow of exile and longing for home</note> or Du Fu’s "Lament in the Empty Valley" <note>Du Fu (also known as Du Ling) wrote of scholars whose virtues went unrecognized in desolate places</note>. 

If this were merely a matter of developing a barren, rocky mountain, or trying to wash away the "lewd sounds" of the street with the common "Songs of the Lower Ba" <note>A metaphor for low-brow or popular culture</note>, then your ambitions, my friend, would surely lie in that direction rather than this one.

I reflect that my current situation and location are quite similar to yours. I have been to Singapore <note>original: *Xingzhou*; literally "Star Island"</note> three times—a remote and crude place. Fleeing into this wilderness, the vulgarity and the "dusty faces" of the crowds are such that there is nothing worth speaking of. 

Erroneously, my fellow expatriate comrades entrusted me to expand our archives of books and history and to establish a literary society. My hope was to break through the narrow-mindedness <note>original: *juxu*; a reference to a frog in a well that cannot imagine the vastness of the sea</note> of the Chinese descendants who have moved to the East. 

In the two years since, I have exhausted my meager abilities. Yet, the local customs remain coarse and simple, tending toward the martial, while talk of utility and profit borders on boasting. Pacing at midnight, I am often filled with worry, not knowing how to find a way forward. 

Compared to you, sitting with arms around your knees, chanting long poems in serene self-contentment—and now spreading floral beauty <note>original: *chipazhaiyan*; a metaphor for composing exquisite literature</note> to enlighten the people—I should feel ashamed of my own failure in "neglecting my own field to weed another's" <note>An idiom meaning to criticize or manage others' affairs while failing at one's own tasks</note>. 

Moreover, critics have repeatedly raised a clamor behind my back, arguing that my approach is too indirect and that achieving results will be difficult. Such criticisms are indeed correct. 

However, if there were a grand and extraordinary talent like yourself, you would surely be able to rouse the spirit of the people and transform the prevailing social atmosphere. Although we are not currently working together in the world of writing <note>original: *qianqian*; refers to the lead pens and wooden tablets used for writing in ancient times</note>, the feelings we harbor in our hearts must be the same. This is my humble opinion; I leave it to you...

News arrived this winter that the **Min-Zhang Guild Hall** <note>A community center for people from Fujian (Min) and Zhangzhou living in Guangdong</note> has been completed. I sent a long couplet to be inscribed on the pillars of the main hall, which says:

> I still remember the carriages and horses of previous times at **Huadi** and the **Pearl River** <note>Huadi (Flower Land) was a famous scenic garden area in Guangzhou near the Pearl River</note>, where I met old friends. If one speaks of the scenery here—the yellow oranges and green tangerines—it feels just like our home mountains.

These words are all a record of actual events.

**Master Jianyuan**, the respected father of **Zicong** <note>Cai Zicong</note>, was a close friend of my late father’s generation. Although he has lived in Guangdong for thirty years, he has not lost his native accent. Zicong cares for him with the utmost filial devotion. Aside from his books and tea bowls, Zicong has no worldly indulgences or habits. Dressed simply in blue shoes and cloth socks, he has a solitary and transcendent air, much like a man of the **Dao**. He is gentle and approachable toward his juniors; even if someone stands before him speaking clumsily, he listens patiently until they have fully expressed their thoughts. Though I am a crude and unrefined person, I spent several days in his company. Every time we spoke, my words flowed like a torrential flood, and I could not stop myself. The Master listened without any sign of weariness; this is a quality of character that others cannot match.

He once spoke to me about my **late father, Lord Qinzhi** <gloss>Taboo name: Junxin; Courtesy name: Zhengzhong; Literary name: Qinzhi. He earned merit in the Fujian Naval Administration and was awarded the fifth-rank button and the title of State Department Associate. He later purchased the rank of Salt Distribution Commissioner. This biography was written by Zeng Zongyan of Min County.</gloss> and anecdotes from his years traveling in Guangdong during his prime. Hearing of the hardships of "the bow and the forge" <note>original: *gongye*; a classical allusion to a son following in his father's professional footsteps and the difficulty of maintaining a family legacy</note> felt as though I were hearing my father’s own instructions once more. Unknowingly, I was overcome with emotion and wept. It is a pity that my travel schedule was so rushed that I could not stay longer to benefit from his seasoned guidance and character. Alas, what can be done?

During my stay in the **Fragrant Sea** <note>A poetic name for Hong Kong</note>, there was a Cantonese merchant named **Ou Shuzhi**. He is naturally inclined toward refined culture and was very attentive, repeatedly asking me for a commemorative inscription.

*Yan* and *Chi* <note>original: "妍媸"</note> are names of insects, one beautiful and the other stinging <note>The word *yan* (妍) represents beauty, while *chi* (媸) represents a species of worm or insect associated with ugliness or stinging</note>. Thus, they are used as metaphors for the beauty or ugliness of things. This is found in the *Fengsu Tong* <note>The *Fengsu Tongyi*, or "Comprehensive Meaning of Customs," is a 2nd-century work by Ying Shao that explains social customs and myths</note>.

The anecdotal records used to claim that the Lingnan region <note>The "Linghai" or coastal southern provinces of Guangdong and Guangxi</note> produced bright pearls, which the locals valued immensely. They would name a son "Pearl Boy" and a daughter "Pearl Lady." However, upon investigating this today, it appears not to be the case. The names likely arise because the word "Pearl" <note>original: "珠" (zhu)</note> sounds identical to the word "Pig" <note>original: "猪" (zhu)</note>. <note>In traditional folk culture, giving a child a "lowly" name like "Pig" was thought to ward off evil spirits and ensure the child’s survival</note>

The pairing of "Yu and Liang" <note>Referring to the rivals Zhou Yu and Zhuge Liang from the Three Kingdoms period</note> is frequently seen in the *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*, but it does not appear in the official historical gazetteers. Master Wang Yuyang <note>Wang Shizhen (1634–1711), a famous Qing dynasty poet and scholar</note> in his *Xiangzu Biji* <note>"Notes of the Fragrant Ancestor"</note> used this pairing to describe Chen Zilong <gloss>style name Womei</gloss> and Wu Weiye <gloss>style name Meicun</gloss>. It seems the term has been in common use since the commentaries of the Ming Dynasty "eight-legged" essay collections <term>eight-legged essays: a rigid style of prose required for the imperial examinations</term>.

In the *Analects*, it is written that "Zilu followed [the Master] and fell behind, meeting an elderly man" <note>original: *zhangren* (丈人)</note>. In the *Spring and Autumn Annals of Wu and Yue*, when Wu Zixu met a fisherman, he said, "My life belongs to Heaven, but now it belongs to you, elder" <note>original: *zhangren*</note>. In the letters of Liu Zongyuan to Yang Jingzhao and his reply to Zhou Junchao, the term *zhangren* is also used. From this, we see that in ancient times, *zhangren* was a respectful term for any elderly man, whereas today it is specifically used to refer to a wife’s father <note>the modern English equivalent of "father-in-law"</note>. The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary</note> is a book falsely attributed to the Han dynasty; in its chapter on kinship, it refers to the wife’s father as "External Uncle" <note>original: *waijiu* (外舅)</note> and the wife’s mother as "External Aunt" <note>original: *waigu* (外姑)</note>, yet even there the term *zhangren* is not used. Only in the *Records of the Three Kingdoms*, under the entry for the Emperor’s uncle, General of Chariots and Cavalry Dong Cheng, does the commentator Pei Songzhi note: "In ancient times, there was no such address as *zhangren*, thus he was called 'Uncle'." Based on this, the change from "Uncle" to *zhangren* must have begun during the Northern and Southern Dynasties.

Chen Zhongqing, a *Mingjing* <term>a scholar who mastered the classic texts for the imperial examinations</term> from Panyu, <insert>highly</insert> admired the character of his local predecessor Kuang Zhanruo <note>Kuang Lu (1604–1650), a famous Ming loyalist, poet, and traveler</note>. Because of this, he named his studio after Kuang.

### ### *Jianhu Sanji*, Volume 3, Tianzhitang Hall

The volumes of the *Yongzhai Zhaji* <note>original: "鄘齋雜記"; "Miscellaneous Records of the Yong Studio"</note> are not many, but its discussion of the titles and forms of address for relatives is quite insightful. I have summarized it here: 

To call the younger brother of one’s mother a *Jiu* <note>Maternal Uncle</note> and the sister of one’s father a *Gu* <note>Paternal Aunt</note> is in accordance with the *Rites*. Modern people have expanded these to "Uncle-Father" <note>original: "舅父" (Jiufu)</note> and "Aunt-Mother" <note>original: "姑母" (Gumu)</note>, which has already lost the ancient meaning. In the *Book of the Later Han*, Guo Kuang’s elder cousin-sister was the consort of Emperor Guangwu’s grandfather. When Kuang went to see Guangwu, the Emperor was greatly pleased and said: "Only now have I gained a Great Uncle!" This is what common custom now calls a *Jiu Zu* <note>Grand-Uncle; specifically the mother's uncle</note>. This term "Jiu Zu" has been used for a long time. Ji Wenda <note>The famous Qing dynasty scholar Ji Yun (1724–1805)</note> noted this in his writings, stating that such terms should be corrected immediately. 

However, ancient terms were also sometimes flawed. In the *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, dating to the 3rd century BCE</note>, the section "Explaining Kinship" defines the son of a paternal aunt as a *Sheng* <note>original: "甥"; today this usually means a sister's son (nephew), but anciently it was more broad</note>; it also calls the son of a maternal uncle a *Sheng*, the younger brother of a wife a *Sheng*, and the husband of a sister a *Sheng*. 

While we do not suffer from these specific confusions today, bringing colloquial terms into formal writing still requires careful consideration. For instance, if one calls a wife’s brother *Jiu* <note>Uncle</note>, does that not conflict with the title for one’s mother’s brother? Or if one simply calls them "Inner Brothers" <note>original: "内兄弟" (Nei Xiongdi)</note>, it remains unclear. An old saying from the Six Dynasties period <note>220–589 CE</note> suggests that the son of a paternal aunt should call the son of a maternal uncle an "Inner Brother," while the uncle’s son calls the aunt’s son an "Outer Brother." It seems more appropriate for cousins to be called directly by their relationship—"Aunt’s Son" or "Uncle’s Son"—and for brothers-in-law to be addressed directly as "Sister’s Husband" or "Wife’s Brother."
