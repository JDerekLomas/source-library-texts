Learning and First <note>original: 學而第一 (Xue Er Di Yi)</note>
The Master <gloss>Teacher</gloss> said: "To learn and in due time to practice it, is this not a pleasure <gloss>pronounced 'yue'</gloss>?"
"To have friends come from distant places, is this not a joy?"

### CHAPTER I. 
1. The Master said, "Is it not pleasant to learn with a constant perseverance and application?"
2. "Is it not delightful to have friends coming from distant quarters?"

---

**TITLE OF THE WORK.**—*Discussed Sayings* <note>original: 論語 (Lunyu)</note>, "Discourses and Dialogues"; that is, The discourses or discussions of Confucius with his disciples and others on various topics, and his replies to their inquiries. Many chapters, however, and one whole book, are the sayings, not of the sage himself, but of some of his disciples. The characters may also be rendered "Digested Conversations," and this appears to be the more ancient signification attached to them, the account being that, after the death of Confucius, his disciples collected together and compared the memoranda of his conversations which they had severally preserved, digesting them into the twenty books which compose the work. Hence the title—*Discussed Sayings*, "Discussed Sayings," or "Digested Conversations." See *Preface to the Correct Meanings of the Annotated Analects* <note>original: 論語註疏解經序</note>. I have styled the work "Confucian Analects," as being more descriptive of its character than any other name I could think of.

**HEADING OF THIS BOOK.**—*Learning and... Number One* <note>original: 學而第一</note>. The two first characters in the book, after the introductory—"The Master said," are adopted as its heading. This is similar to the custom of the Jews, who name many books in the Bible from the first word in them. *The first* <note>original: 第一</note>, "The first"; that is, of the twenty books composing the whole work. In some of the books we find a unity or analogy of subjects, which evidently guided the compilers in grouping the chapters together. Others seem devoid of any such principle of combination. The sixteen chapters of this book are occupied, it is said, with the fundamental subjects which ought to engage the attention of the learner, and the great matters of human practice. The word *learn* <note>original: 學 (xue)</note>, "learn," rightly occupies the forefront in the studies of a nation, of which its educational system has so long been the distinction and glory.

We find it, in conversations, used in the same way as our "Sir." <note>The following commentary explains the specific Chinese characters used in the text above.</note>

The use of the term "Master" <note>original: 子 (zǐ)</note> indicates that the person spoken of was the teacher of the writer, as in the phrase "My master, the philosopher Shěn" <note>original: 子沈子</note>. Standing single and alone, as in the text, it denotes Confucius, *the philosopher*, or, rather, *the master*. If we render the term by Confucius, as all preceding translators have done, we miss the indication which it gives of the handiwork of his disciples, and the reverence which it bespeaks for him. "To learn" <note>original: 學 (xué)</note>, in the old commentators, is explained by "to read chantingly" or "to discuss" <note>original: 誦 (sòng)</note>. Chû Hsî <note>A highly influential 12th-century Neo-Confucian scholar</note> interprets it as "to imitate" <note>original: 效 (xiào)</note>, and makes its results to be "the understanding of all excellence, and the bringing back of original goodness" <note>original: 明善而復初</note>. Subsequent scholars profess, for the most part, great admiration of this explanation. It is an illustration, to my mind, of the way in which Chû Hsî and his followers are continually being wise above what is written in the classical books. "To practice" <note>original: 習 (xí)</note> refers to the rapid and frequent motion of the wings of a bird in flying, used here for "to repeat" or "to practice." The character "it" <note>original: 之 (zhī)</note> is the object of the third person pronoun, referring back to the deep meaning of what is learned. The phrase "Is it not...?" <note>original: 不亦 ... 乎</note> is explained as "Is it not indeed?" <note>original: 豈不</note>. See the *Supplement to the Four Books with Collected Explanations* <note>original: 四書補註備旨</note>. To bring out the force of "also" in the word *yì* <note>亦</note>, some say: "The occasions for pleasure are many, is this not also one?" But it is better to consider it as merely redundant; see Wang Yin-chih's masterly *Treatise on the Particles*, chapter iii; it forms chapters 1208 to 1217 of the *Explanations of the Classics under the Imperial Qing Dynasty* <note>original: 皇清經解</note>. The word for "pleasure" <note>original: 說 (yuè)</note>, read *yuë* as always when it has the entering tone marked, stands for the character meaning "delight" <note>original: 悅</note>. What is learned becomes by practice and application one's own, and hence arises complacent pleasure in the mastering mind. This "delight" <note>original: 悅</note>, as distinguished from "joy" <note>original: 樂 (lè)</note> in the next paragraph, is the internal, individual feeling of pleasure, while the other is its external manifestation, implying also companionship. 

2. The term for "friend" <note>original: 朋 (péng)</note> properly means "fellow students"; but, generally, individuals of the same class and character, like-minded. 

3. "A man of complete virtue" <note>original: 君子 (jūnzǐ)</note>, I translate here as such. Literally, it is "a princely man." See the note on "Master" above. It is a <term>technical term</term> in Chinese moral writers for which there is no exact equivalent in English, and which cannot be rendered always in the same way. See Morrison's Dictionary, under the character for "son/master" <note>子</note>. Its opposite is the "small, mean man" <note>original: 小人 (xiǎorén)</note>. "Men do not know him" <note>original: 人不知</note> was anciently explained by some as "men do not know," meaning they are too stupid to understand his teaching. The interpretation in the main text is, doubtless, the correct one.

2. “The superior man <term>Junzi: traditionally 'the son of a ruler,' but used by Confucius to mean a person of high moral character</term> bends his attention to what is radical <note>meaning the 'root' or fundamental basis</note>. That being established, all practical courses naturally grow up. ---

<note>The following is a scholarly commentary on the Chinese text above.</note>

The philosopher named Ruo <note>original: 若</note>, also known by the titles Ziyou and Ziruo, was a native of the State of Lu. He was famed among the other disciples of Confucius for his strong memory and love for the doctrines of antiquity. In his physical appearance or manner, he resembled the Sage himself. See the Book of Mencius, Book III, Part I, chapter iv, section 13. The title "Master Yu" <note>original: 有子, Yu Zi</note> is used here; he and Zeng Shen are the only two of Confucius’s disciples who are mentioned with this honorific style in the *Analects* <note>original: Lun Yu</note>. This has led some to believe that the work was compiled by their own disciples. While this theory may not be fully proven, I have not found this specific naming convention explained satisfactorily elsewhere. 

The spirit tablet of Yu is now housed in the same hall of the Confucian temples as the Sage himself, occupying the sixth place in the eastern row of “the wise ones.” It was promoted to this position in the third year of the Qianlong Emperor <note>1738 AD</note> of the Manchu <insert>Qing</insert> dynasty. 

A degree of activity enters into the meaning of the word "acting" <note>original: 爲, wei</note> in the phrase "acting as a man" <note>original: 爲人, wei ren</note>, implying "as a human being, showing themselves filial," etc. The character for "brotherly" <note>original: 弟, di</note> here serves as a substitute for "fraternal submission" <note>original: 悌, ti</note>; it is pronounced in the fourth tone. In its standard meaning of "younger brother," it was anciently pronounced in the third tone. The conjunction "and yet" <note>original: 而, er</note> is used here differently from its simple use as "and" in the preceding chapter. The word "to love" <note>original: 好, hao</note> is a verb in the fourth tone, differing from the third-tone adjective meaning "good." The word for "few" or "rare" <note>original: 鮮, xian</note> is in the third tone. On the grammatical structure "never has there been such a thing" <note>original: 未之有, wei zhi you</note>, see Prémare’s Grammar, page 156. 

2. The term "Superior Man" <note>original: 君子, Junzi</note> has a less intense or specific meaning here than in the previous chapter. I translate it as “The superior man” for want of a better English term. "The root" <note>original: 本, ben</note>, or "what is radical," refers here to filial and fraternal duties, while "the Way" <note>original: 道, Dao</note>, meaning "ways" or "courses," refers to all that is intended by "practicing benevolence" <note>original: 行仁, xing ren</note> below. The grammatical particles <note>original: 也者, ye zhe</note> restart the discussion about filial piety and fraternal submission to introduce further description. See Prémare, page 158. The final particle <note>original: 與, yu</note>, in the second tone, is a half-interrogative, implying that the answer is "yes." 

"Benevolence" <note>original: 仁, Ren</note> is explained here as “the principle of love” or “the virtue of the heart.” Mencius says: “Benevolence is man” <note>original: 仁也者人也, Ren ye zhe ren ye</note>, in accordance with which the scholar Stanislas Julien translates it using the Latin term *humanitas* <note>humanity/kindness</note>. "Benevolence" often comes close to the meaning, but as noted with the term "Superior Man," we cannot give one single, uniform English rendering for this complex concept.

### 3. FAIR APPEARANCES ARE SUSPICIOUS.
**Fine words and an insinuating appearance** <note>original: 巧言令色</note> — see the *Book of Documents* <note>original: Shû-ching</note>, II, iii, 2. The character **skillful** <note>original: 巧</note> refers to "skill in workmanship"; then, "skill" or "cleverness" generally, and sometimes with a negative connotation, as used here, meaning "artful" or "hypocritical." The character for **order** <note>original: 令</note> means "a law" or "an order," but also "good"; here it is used like the previous term with a negative meaning, signifying "pretending to be good." **Color** <note>original: 色</note> refers to "the manifestation of the feelings made in the color of the countenance," and is used here to describe one's general outward appearance.

### 4. HOW THE PHILOSOPHER TSĂNG DAILY EXAMINED HIMSELF, TO GUARD AGAINST HIS BEING GUILTY OF ANY IMPOSITION.
Tsăng, whose given name was **Shăn** <note>original: 參</note> and whose courtesy name was **Ziyú** <note>original: 子輿</note>, was one of the primary disciples of Confucius. A follower of the sage from the age of sixteen, he was considered inferior in natural talent to some other students. However, through his deep **filial piety** <term>xiao: the virtue of respect for one's parents and ancestors</term> and moral discipline, he won the Master’s high esteem and mastered his doctrines through perseverance. It is said that Confucius employed him to compose the **Classic of Filial Piety** <note>original: 孝經</note>. The authorship of **The Great Learning** <note>original: 大學</note> is also attributed to him, though likely incorrectly. Parts of his writings are preserved in the **Book of Rites** <note>original: Li Chî</note>. His ancestral tablet is placed among the "Four Assessors" of Confucius, holding the place of highest honor on the west, even above Mencius.

The term **examine** <note>original: 省</note> is read *hsing*. The phrase **three examinations** <note>original: 三省</note> is literally "three times," but context and the consensus of scholars suggest it means "on three points." **Body** <note>original: 身</note> refers to "one's personality" or "myself." The word **for** <note>original: 爲</note> is used here in the fourth tone. **Loyalty** <note>original: 忠</note> is derived from the characters for **middle** <note>original: 中</note> and **heart** <note>original: 心</note>, signifying "action from the center of the heart" or faithfulness. Regarding **friends** <note>original: 朋友</note>, the second character <note>original: 友</note> depicts "two hands joined," symbolizing union.

The phrase **to pass on but not practice** <note>original: 傳不習</note> is quite mysterious. This translation follows the famous commentator **Zhu Xi** <note>original: Chû Hsî</note>. However, the scholar **He Yan** <note>original: 何晏</note> explained it differently: "Whether I have given instruction in what I myself had not studied and practiced?" It seems more accurate to interpret "pass on" <note>original: 傳</note> actively as "giving instruction" rather than passively "receiving instruction." See **Mao Xihe's** <note>original: Mao Hsî-ho</note> *Revised Errors in the Four Books* <note>original: 四書改錯</note>, Volume XV, Article 17.
