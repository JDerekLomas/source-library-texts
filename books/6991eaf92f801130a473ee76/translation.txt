# THE ANATOMY OF LEONARDO DA VINCI

## THE ORGANS OF RESPIRATION AND THE HEART

->**Observations on the Movement of the Air**<-

The windpipe <note>original: "aspre arteria" or trachea</note> does not change its position during the act of breathing, but rather it is the lung that expands and contracts against the ribs of the chest. <margin>Note how the ribs must expand to allow for the fullness of the breath.</margin> 

It is observed that the heart is a muscle of great strength, much more powerful than the other muscles. <insert>The heat of the heart is generated by the rapid motion of the blood.</insert> I have found that the heart is not the source of the nerves, as many have previously claimed, but rather it is a vessel made of dense muscle, nourished by arteries and veins like other muscles.

### On the Valves of the Heart

| Part of the Heart | Function | Observation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Right Ventricle | Receives blood from the liver | Thick-walled and robust |
| Left Ventricle | Contains the vital spirit <note>Leonardo refers here to the oxygenated blood, which ancient medicine termed "vital spirits."</note> | Thinner than the right |
| The Valves | To prevent the reflux of blood | They close with marvelous precision |

<column-break/>

The percussion of the blood against the walls of the heart creates a sound that can be felt. <gloss>This is the pulse of life itself.</gloss> When the heart contracts, it expels the blood into the large artery <note>The aorta</note>, and this movement is what gives life to the entire body. 

<margin>Compare this to the flow of water in a riverbed.</margin>

One must consider how the <term>diaphragm</term> <note>The muscular partition separating the chest from the abdomen</note> assists in the expulsion of waste from the intestines by its downward pressure, while simultaneously drawing air into the lungs. It is a dual instrument of nature.

# ## Anatomical study of the heart and the pulmonary vessels

<note>This page contains a detailed anatomical study of the heart and the branching network of the pulmonary vessels (the bronchial tree). Leonardo investigates the physiological relationship between respiration and circulation. The text is written in his characteristic mirror script.</note>

On the movements of air through the vessels of the lung.

<margin>
How the air moves
through the vessels of the lung
for the cooling of the
blood.
</margin>

The lung is the instrument of the voice, and its function is to renew and refresh the air in the heart, and to expel the tainted air and receive new air, which refreshes the blood that passes through its vessels—which blood has been heated by the continuous motion of the heart. 

The air that exits the lungs is accompanied by the voice, and this voice is produced by the percussion made by the air in the vessels of the lung; this air is pushed by the diaphragm, which constricts the lung and the spirit <note>original: "lo sspirito"; here referring to the vital breath or pneuma</note> and that which constricts the lung by way of a <unclear>breathing hole</unclear> that opens and closes according to the will of the animal.

<margin>A</margin>

<note>Drawing of the heart at center, with a dense, tree-like network of vessels extending to the left, representing the vascularization of the lungs.</note>

<margin>
Note if the heart
moves in 4 beats
or in two.
</margin>

These vessels are those that carry the vital spirit to the members of the lung, and through these vessels of the spirits the air is infused into the blood, which blood is that which nourishes the lung itself and refreshes the heart.

<note>Small diagrams to the right of the heart: a pair of dividers (compasses) and a cylinder with spiral lines, possibly representing a mechanical model of vessel tension or flow.</note>

Regarding the tightening of the heart, it is said that it is a muscle, and like a muscle it shortens and thickens in its parts, and by this means it drives the blood out of itself through the vessels that depart from it, and receives the blood through the vessels that return to it. The motion of the blood is generated by the tightening of the heart, which tightening is muscular, and this muscle opens and closes by the spirit that is infused through the nerves—which nerves are those that give motion to all the muscles of the body.

The vortex <note>term: "vortex" (original: "moto retrosorso") refers to the circular, swirling motion of fluid</note> of the blood is generated in the upper part of the vessel <note>the aorta</note> at the moment the heart contracts and pushes the blood out. <insert>This motion is necessary so that the thin membranes of the valve are not torn by a sudden weight of blood.</insert>

If the blood were to flow back in a straight line, it would strike the valve with such force that it would break the delicate tissues. But because nature has designed these three pouches <note>the sinuses of the aortic valve</note>, the blood is forced to swirl. This swirling motion allows the valve to close gently and perfectly, ensuring that no blood leaks back into the heart.

<margin>
<note>Text at the top right, adjacent to the second drawing.</note>
The more the water is of a faster motion, the more it spreads out and the less it strikes with impact.

This is proved by the 4th [proposition] of the 3rd [book] on waters, which says: water of the same width will be of a velocity so much less as it is of a greater depth.

---

And this arises because it regards the body of water that is moved together with the percussion; when the motion is slow, the body of water is of a thickness equal to that of the channel. But if the motion is fast, the water does not have time to spread out between the channel and the place of impact, and for this reason, it is thinner than the channel. Because of this thinness, the percussion will be of less value than it would be if it were as thick as the channel.

Water that pours from a narrow channel into a wide place is of a velocity so much less as the wide space is of a greater width than the said channel.

Water that pours from a narrow channel into a lake of greater depth: the slower its motion in that channel, the deeper it plunges into that lake, because it is of a body of greater weight, being of the same thickness as the said channel.

"That ball will be of greater force which is moved by a faster motion."
To this, it is answered that water will not behave in that same way, because the ball does not become thinner the faster it moves, as water does. <note>Leonardo distinguishes between solid projectiles and fluid dynamics; water "stretches" or thins when accelerating, reducing its concentrated mass at the point of impact.</note>

That thing is of greater weight which is of greater quantity.
Therefore, if it is true—as proved by the 4th [proposition]—that water moving with slow motion will be of equal thickness to the mouth of its channel, and that which moves with fast motion will be thinner than that mouth, it follows that water moved slowly will be of a larger body than that which moves quickly, both being discharged from the same channel and, consequently, in the same amount of time.

# On the Nature of Sight and the Pupil

The pupil of the eye <term>luce dell'occhio; literally 'the light of the eye,' referring to the pupil as the aperture for light</term> changes to as many different sizes as there are degrees of brightness or darkness of the objects that appear before it.

In this case, nature has provided for the visual faculty <note>original: "virtù visiva"</note> when it is offended by excessive light, by contracting the large circle of the pupil, and similarly, when offended by darkness, by expanding it, much like the closing of a purse string.

### On the Movement of the Eyelid

The eyelid, which is the cover of the eye, moves from top to bottom and from bottom to top. <margin>The closing of the eye</margin> This occurs so that the eye may be cleansed of the continuous dust that flies through the air, and so that the moisture of the eye does not dry out.

# Along an oblique line <note>original: "per linia obliqvā"</note>

A weight which descends along an oblique line becomes lighter the more that obliquity deviates from the perpendicular line that points toward the center of the world <note>Leonardo uses "center of the world" to refer to the center of the Earth, the source of gravity in the Aristotelian worldview</note>. The reason is this: let **a. b.** be the line of motion of the weight, and within this line let there be weight **a.** which descends along line **a. b.** I say that if such a line is perpendicular, it is found to possess the entirety of its gravity <term>gravity: here meaning "effective weight" or downward force</term>. But if it should be oblique, such as **a. c.**, then I say that such a weight is as much less heavy as the line **a. c.** is longer than the line **a. b.** This gravity is found in this manner: draw a line from the end of the obliquity **c.** perpendicular to the line of gravity **a. b.**, which shall be **c. b.**; and hold it as a rule that as much as line **c. b.** is shorter than line **a. c.**, so much lighter will weight **a.** be than that which was upon line **a. b.**

If a weight descends along a line of equal obliquity, it will always maintain the same gravity; within itself it will maintain the same velocity of motion. But if such a line should be of unequal obliquity, then the weight will be of unequal gravity. But hold it as a rule that as much as a weight becomes lighter, so much slower will its motion become.

But if the obliquity should be like that of a circle <note>referring to a curved or circular path</note>, then the weight descending along such a line will maintain as much more gravity as it is closer to the perpendicular of gravity <gloss>the center of the world</gloss>.

If a weight descends along an oblique line of unequal obliquity, it will produce within itself unequal gravity <unclear>and</unclear> unequal velocity of motion. And let there be the line of a circle along which weight **a.** descends lengthwise toward **b.**; I say that as weight **a.** becomes closer to point **b.**, it will be so much heavier and of faster motion than when it was at point **a.**

Water that moves along a line of equal obliquity will always maintain the same gravity and velocity of motion. But if the obliquity is unequal, then the water will be of unequal gravity and velocity of motion.

# On the Motion of Weights and the Nature of the Circle

...gravity <note>Continuing from the previous page: "as it is closer to the perpendicular of gravity."</note>. Therefore, the weight that moves along the arc of a circle will constantly change its gravity, because at every moment the obliquity of its path changes. It follows that the motion of such a weight will be of a varied velocity, being faster where the arc is more vertical and slower where the arc approaches the horizontal.

### On the Balance of Forces

<margin>The weight is to the power as the length of the path is to the height of the rise.</margin>

If you wish to know what force <note>original: "potentia"</note> is required to sustain a weight upon an oblique path, observe the proportion that the length of the oblique line bears to the vertical height of its starting point. For example, if the line **a. c.** is double the length of the vertical line **a. b.**, then a force of half the weight of **a.** will suffice to prevent it from sliding down the slope.

->**Of Water and its Weight**<-

Water has no weight within itself <note>Leonardo refers to the principle of buoyancy and hydrostatic pressure; water does not "weigh" anything when surrounded by more water</note>, but it acquires weight when it is supported by a body that is lighter than it. 

*   **First:** Water is by its nature fluid and seeks the lowest point.
*   **Second:** It exerts pressure equally in all directions.
*   **Third:** The surface of all stagnant water is equidistant from the center of the world.

<insert>Note that the air also has weight, though it seems to us to have none.</insert>

If a vessel is filled with water and has a hole at the bottom, the water will flow out with more or less impetus <term>impetus: the force or energy with which a body moves</term> according to the height of the water above the hole. This is because the weight of the water column presses upon the exit, and as the height of the column diminishes, so too does the pressure and the speed of the flow.

# On the Motion of Water

The nature of water is such that it always seeks the lowest point, yet its movement is governed by the weight of the fluid above it and the resistance of the bed over which it flows. <note>Leonardo often describes water as a living force, comparing its "veins" to the circulatory system of the earth.</note>

When a stream encounters an obstacle, the force of the impact is distributed according to the angle of the strike. If the bank is curved, the water will erode the concave section with greater violence, while depositing silt and sand upon the convex bank. <margin>The curvature of the river dictates the depth of the channel.</margin>

### Observations on Turbulence

1.  The primary current moves with the greatest velocity at the center of the surface.
2.  The lateral currents, hindered by the friction of the banks, move more slowly.
3.  Eddies <term>vortices: circular movements of water caused by opposing currents</term> are formed where the swift water meets the stagnant.

# On the Motion of Water (Continued)

The movement of the waves is not a movement of the water itself, but rather a movement of the power that travels through the water. <note>Leonardo is describing the principle of wave energy—where the water stays relatively in place while the energy pulse moves forward.</note> Just as the wind passing over a field of grain creates a wave that travels across the stalks while the roots remain fixed in the earth, so too does the wind act upon the surface of the great lakes.

### On the Percussion of Waves

When a wave strikes a vertical wall, it is reflected back with a force equal to its impact. <margin>The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection.</margin> If, however, the wall is sloped, the wave will expend its energy by climbing the incline, losing its power as it turns into foam and spray.

1.  The height of a wave is proportional to the strength of the wind.
2.  The depth of the wave's influence does not reach the bottom of deep seas.
3.  The intersection of two wave patterns creates a complex lattice of water. <term>interference: the phenomenon where two waves overlap to form a new pattern</term>

->**Observations on Submerged Bodies**<-

| Object Type | Resistance | Effect on Current |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Spherical stone | Low | Creates minor eddies |
| Flat timber | High | Causes significant turbulence |
| Pointed piling | Minimal | Divides the flow cleanly |

<column-break/>

One must also note that water, being a heavy fluid, possesses a natural desire to maintain its level. <note>original: "appetito di tornare al suo centro"</note> When this level is disturbed by the wind or by the fall of a heavy object, the resulting circular ripples expand outward until their force is consumed by the resistance of the surrounding water. <insert>This expansion is perfectly circular if the water is still.</insert>

The air that is trapped beneath a falling cataract creates bubbles which, upon rising, break the surface tension. This interaction between the two elements—water and air—is the cause of the white foam seen at the base of waterfalls. <note>Leonardo frequently studied the "accidents" or various forms water takes when mixed with air.</note>
