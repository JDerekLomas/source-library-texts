**P**ERCEIVING it to be most unjust, *Most Excellent Prince*, that this treasure—which I have unearthed from my printing presses <note>original: "typographicis meis torcularibus," literally "my typographic wine-presses." A common Renaissance metaphor for the printing process.</note> under the guidance of your favorable star—should be diverted elsewhere: I would be too greedy were I to keep it for myself, or failing to realize how deeply I am in your debt, were I to neglect the Imperial precedent of venerable sanctions. It is a treasure in which the collected fragments of the rites of ancient <term>Church discipline</term> <note>The rules, customs, and practices governing the early Christian Church.</note>—as many as have survived—rise up like Indian pearls in the mind of **Tertullian** <note>An early Christian author (c. 155–220 AD) from Carthage.</note>, surpassing all precious stones in value. And not without reason; for, as **Isidore** <note>St. Isidore of Seville (c. 560–636 AD), famous for his encyclopedia of knowledge.</note> says, the knowledge of things perishes if their names are unknown. Indeed, the Catholic faith would be driven to ruin if the obscure vocabulary of the first Doctors <note>The "Doctors of the Church" were early influential theologians.</note>—whether corrupted by the deceits of heretics or even tainted by deadly poisons—were twisted into a wicked meaning. It is, therefore, a treasure which, hammered out under new auspices, sealed with a new image, and struck with new ornament, was not received by Indian midwives or the cradles of the distant **Tagus** <note>The river Tagus in Spain/Portugal; a reference to the gold and wealth coming from the Spanish Empire and the Americas.</note>. Instead, it is a treasure recalled from a humble <term>printing house</term> <note>original: "chalcographio," referring to a place of copper-plate or metal engraving/printing.</note> into the light of the learned, and is here presented to You, and dedicated to You.

The **Hierolexicon** <note>literally "Sacred Dictionary"</note> is precious for this one title alone; namely, because by examining the most valued gems of ecclesiastical expression, it shines forth with true doctrine and sparkles with true erudition. It was unearthed from the rich gold mine of **Domenico Magri** <note>Domenico Magri (1604–1672) was a Maltese theologian and renowned scholar of ecclesiastical history.</note>, a man of the keenest judgment and great literacy, and further refined by the final hammer-blows of his brother **Carlo**; it is precious for these names alone. But now, it is made most precious by your name as well.

It is presented, I say, to you, *Most Noble Prince*, who, having devoted the first part of your life to the more pleasant study of literature, sought a more solid and sublime doctrine from the sacred books: fortunate beginnings of letters, by which you, a true Hero, chose to be born no less than to be made! What wonder is it, then, that while yet a boy you acted with the gravity of **Cato** <note>Marcus Porcius Cato (the Elder or Younger), Roman statesmen famous for their strict morality and wisdom.</note>, and while scarcely an adult you were listened to by your elders? We were struck with wonder at seeing the High Noon in the Dawn, and Maturity in the Spring! 

But what shall I say of your more steadfast age, so akin to prudence? Lest I disturb the noble impulses of your modesty, I command silence to my own unskilled talkativeness: we admire the Day from the Morning Star, and the "lion from its claw" <note>original: "ex vngue Leonem." A proverbial expression meaning one can judge the quality of a whole work or person from a small sample.</note>. You seek out and attain from your character such great clarity of Virtue—noted by wise men everywhere—as if you had no nobility from your lineage at all. Yet Fame recounts as many most precious gifts of your soul—gathered from so many men into one—as there were Orators for Princes, Kings, and Popes; as many most brave and intrepid <term>Polemarchs</term> <note>From the Greek *polemarchos*, meaning a high-ranking military commander or "war-leader."</note> decorated with the virtue, authority, and success of military affairs; as many <term>Viceroys</term> <note>original: "Proreges," officials who run a country, colony, or city-state in the name of and as the representative of the monarch.</note>; and as many Emperors as the ages have looked up to with high esteem in your most ancient family.

Indeed, I do not dare to even lightly touch upon the magnitude of your Most Noble House, filled and enlarged by so many Leaders, so many Heroes, and so many Princes of both "Togas" <note>A reference to "cedant arma togae," meaning the peace of civil administration (the toga) as opposed to the war of the military (arms).</note>. If I were to undertake to describe in even a few words the military virtue of the **Gaetani**—whose excellence sounds forth in every province, every kingdom, and every corner of Europe—a century would fly by. He who recounts **Anechinus** coming forth from Spain; **Goffredus** <note>Godfrey</note>, a most vigorous commander of a vast army; **Stephanus** <note>Stephen</note> rushing against the Africans; **Ioannes** <note>John</note> and **Petrus** <note>Peter</note> gifted with so many honors by the Emperor; **Christophorus** <note>Christopher</note> and so many other Gaetani Leaders, proves that Italy has had its own Alexanders. He who relates the illustrious deeds of your ancestors represents forests most fertile in palms of victory. 

Truly, lest I seem to attempt the impossible, I withdraw from the enumeration of infinite things. Light must be denied to the one who is blind, and the noble **Eagles of the Gaetani** always pant for an inaccessible flight. Only with the most reverent awe do I venerate from afar the most ancient branch of the Gaetani blood, derived from the most ancient source of the **Julii** and the **Anicii** <note>The Julii were the clan of Julius Caesar; the Anicii were one of the most powerful noble families of the later Roman Empire, to which St. Gregory the Great belonged.</note>; and so many Knights of the Sacred Assembly of **Jerusalem** <note>The Knights Hospitaller or Knights of Malta.</note>, of the other of **St. James** <note>The Order of Santiago.</note>, and of that of...
