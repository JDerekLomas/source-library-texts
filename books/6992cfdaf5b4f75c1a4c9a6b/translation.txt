->The Birthday occurs on the Fifteenth Day of the Ninth Lunar Month<-

The Ultimate Sage, the King of Culture and Manifestation <note>original: "至聖文宣王" (Zhìshèng Wénxuān Wáng). This is the grand posthumous title bestowed upon Confucius by the Yuan dynasty in 1307, cementing his role as the supreme patron of the scholar-official class</note>, was born in Queli, Changping District, in the state of Lu <note>modern-day Qufu, Shandong Province</note>. His ancestors were originally people of the state of Song.

The Sage’s great-grandfather was named Kong Fangshu. To escape the political turmoil caused by Hua Du in the state of Song, he moved to the state of Lu. He fathered Boxia, and Boxia fathered Shuliang He. Shuliang He's eldest son was named Meng Pi (with the courtesy name Boni), but because he suffered from a physical deformity <note>original: "瘕" (jiǎ). In historical records, this is often interpreted as a deformity of the foot or a chronic ailment that disqualified him from being the primary ritual heir</note>, he was unable to succeed the family line. The second son was the Sage himself.

In the winter of the twenty-first year of the reign of Duke Xiang of Lu, on the *Gengzi* day of the tenth month, the Sage was born. On that evening, two dragons circled the room and the Five Elders descended into the courtyard. These Five Elders were the spirits of the Five Planets <note>referring to the visible planets: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn</note>. His mother, of the Yan family, heard the "Music of the Highest Heaven" <term>鈞天之樂 (Jūntiān zhī yuè): The celestial music of the dwelling of the Emperor of Heaven</term> echoing in her room. When he was born, the top of his head was sunken in the middle and raised on the sides <note>the text uses "圩頂" (wéidǐng), describing a head shaped like a basin or a valley surrounded by hills</note>; because of this, he was given the name Qiu <note>meaning "Hill"</note> and the courtesy name Zhongni. He lost his father when he was young, and his father was buried at Mount Fang. The Sage grew to a height of nine feet and two inches <note>in ancient measurements, this would make him exceptionally tall, emphasizing his "larger than life" status</note> and had a waist ten spans wide. He possessed forty-nine auspicious physical marks, including the "inverted crown" <note>the text cuts off here, referring to a list of physiognomic signs believed to denote a person of supreme wisdom and virtue</note>...

### The Devotion of the Disciples

In the twenty-first year of Duke Ai, a temple was established at the Master's former residence. One hundred households were assigned to guard and maintain the tomb and the temple. 

His disciples all observed a "mourning of the heart" <term>Mourning of the heart: A period of internal grieving and simple living observed by students for a teacher, reflecting the three-year mourning period usually required for a parent</term>. While others left, Zi Gong alone built a hut beside the tomb and remained there for a total of six years before finally taking his leave. 

Within the temple, the disciples preserved the Former Sage’s robes, his ceremonial cap, his zither and lute <note>original: "琴瑟" (qín sè). Stringed instruments used in ritual music</note>, his carriage, and his books. Disciples and people of the State of Lu moved to the area around the tomb and built their homes there, forming more than a hundred households. Consequently, the place was named "Confucius Village." In the State of Lu, this tradition was passed down through the generations, and seasonal sacrifices have been offered at the tomb of the Master's descendants...

# The Central Peak

->**A Ritual Performance at the Central Shrine**<-

This woodblock illustration depicts a solemn ritual gathering at the shrine of the Central Peak. In the center, a high official or scholarly figure sits in formal robes, signifying the imperial or local authority overseeing the ceremony. He is attended by three subordinates, one of whom is actively reading from a scroll or ledger—likely reciting a prayer, an inventory of offerings, or a commemorative text.

In the background, the essential instruments of state ritual music are displayed on ornate, towering frames:

*   **The Bronze Bells** <note>original: 鐘 (Zhong); specifically the *bianzhong*, a set of bronze bells used in ancient Chinese court music to symbolize harmony and order.</note>
*   **The Stone Chimes** <note>original: 磬 (Qing); specifically the *bianqing*, an instrument consisting of L-shaped stone tablets that produce clear, high-pitched tones.</note>

The presence of these instruments indicates that the scene portrays a high-level "Sacrifice to the Mountains and Rivers," a ceremony intended to ensure the stability of the realm and the favor of the <term>Central Peak</term> <note>Mount Song is historically considered the "center of heaven and earth," making its shrine particularly significant for imperial legitimacy.</note>.

### Key Ritual Terms

*   **Central Peak** <note>original: 中嶽 (Zhongyue)</note>: The sacred mountain of the center, Mount Song.
*   **Shrine** <note>original: 祠 (Ci)</note>: The temple or ancestral hall dedicated to the deity of the mountain.
*   **Ritual Bell** <note>original: 鐘 (Zhong)</note>: A large bronze bell used to signal the start of ceremonies.
*   **Lithophone** <note>original: 磬 (Qing)</note>: A resonant stone chime used in classical ritual ensembles.
