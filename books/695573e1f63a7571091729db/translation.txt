->the most invincible Duke of the Milanese. Franchino Gaffurio, professor of music,<-

<image-desc>A decorative square woodcut initial 'Q' containing a circular medallion with a classical profile portrait of a man's head facing left.</image-desc>How great the profession of the musical art was among the ancients, most illustrious Prince—not only in authority but even in veneration—we are easily taught. This is shown by the example of the greatest philosophers who, as very old men, applied themselves to this discipline as if to place the final touch <note>original: "summam manum," literally "the highest hand" or finishing stroke</note> upon their studies. It is also shown by the institutions of the strictest republics which, though they took great care to excise whatever might publicly harm morals, nevertheless not only did not cast out this art, but even cultivated it with the greatest zeal as the parent and nourisher of character. To conclude, it is confirmed by the steady and consenting judgment of all peoples and nations, among whom nothing was ever celebrated with greater care. For what other discipline has been received with such great assent by mortals and such great harmony of every age and sex, that no person of any condition has yet been found who does not strive to console his troubles with at least some rude melody? Indeed, nothing else is so capable of leading minds to the most diverse emotions in a brief moment; this is proven not only by the testimony of the best men, but by "hidden faith" <note>original: "occultata fide," a common legal or philosophical expression for self-evident truth that doesn't require external proof</note> as they say, so much so that antiquity did not hesitate to call its professors not only Musicians, but also prophets and wise men. To this is added the authority of the poets, who feigned that Linus and Orpheus, born of gods, led forests and rocks because they reduced the hard and rustic life of certain men to a milder culture. But lest perhaps false faith be given to the words of poets—who, in their familiar style, extol everything to the highest degree—there have also been found professors of wisdom who did not hesitate to assert that not only sensible things, but even those which live without sense in any way, are soothed by musical modulations. And for this reason, they have judged that the most eminent author Virgil did not speak with poetic hyperbole in his *Bucolics* when he said that the peaks of the hard oaks moved to the song of Silenus. Indeed, if we believe Plato, who said that the soul of the world consists of musical modulation, I truly do not see why it can be doubted that other things living with any kind of soul—which it is clear was given to them from heaven—are affected and gladdened by things congruent to their nature, since it is already clearly established that "likeness is a friend to itself." For these reasons, most illustrious Prince, and also being impelled by nature (which we do not easily resist), I applied my mind to this discipline from my early youth, especially since it contributes to morals (as pleases the most skilled) and did not abhor my profession. Following the long-accepted opinion of religious men that this is the most important duty—that my studies should not benefit me alone—I published a book following the principles of the most ancient writers concerning the speculation of this art, which the Greeks call <term>Theorica</term> <note>The theoretical or contemplative branch of music, as opposed to practical performance.</note>, so that the reasoning of my studies might be established and the fruit of my labors more widely scattered. This work, renowned Prince, I dedicated to your name—steadfastly yet timidly, lest I should interrupt you with untimely trifles while you are occupied with the gravest cares and the weight of the greatest affairs. However, after I perceived with what favor and kindness you followed our little work (otherwise rude and uncultured), that liberality added a "spur to the running horse" <note>A classical idiom meaning to encourage someone who is already working hard.</note>, and my zeal was ignited in no small measure to approach the other part of this discipline, now secure in your favor. For Music, unlike the other disciplines of <term>Mathesis</term> <note>The mathematical sciences (Arithmetic, Geometry, Music, and Astronomy).</note>, does not only occupy itself with speculation, but issues into action and is joined to morality, as was said before. Nor did I seem to have sufficiently fulfilled my duty if I remained only in inquiry—judging that to look to the utility of only a few—unless our industry also toiled for the public benefit. For this part of music is not only useful for its own knowledge, but having pushed its roots further, it also assists the rest of [one's] studies; which is proved by the testimony of the most serious men, who [claimed] they [learned] nothing else from Music than lit...

# PREFACE

<margin>Fabius Quintilianus <note>A celebrated Roman educator and rhetorician (c. 35–100 AD).</note></margin>
...they have professed to have learned letters. For Fabius Quintilianus, on the authority of Timagenes <note>A Greek historian and teacher of rhetoric in Rome.</note>, confirms that this art <note>Music</note> is the most ancient of all literary studies. However, when I speak of Music, I do not mean this theatrical and effeminate kind which corrupts public morals rather than shaping them; but rather that modest and manly music celebrated by the ancient heroes. This was the music brought to the tables of kings and the feasts of the wealthy, where, as the guests reclined, the lyre was passed around and sang of the extraordinary deeds of the most famous men. This was surely the greatest incentive toward the pursuit of virtue. Indeed, this art is raised even higher: it inserts itself into the very heavens, and by the testimony of the most celebrated poets, it describes the labors of the sun, the wandering Moon, and the Titanic stars. As if not content to have filled the spaces of the earth with its merits, it penetrates the heavens and joins with the mysteries of divine things.

Therefore, most clear-sighted Prince, I have dedicated this work—born near me under your leadership and auspices, and now printed in your name—with the veneration I owe you, to use the words of Pliny the Elder <note>original: "Plinii secundi"</note>. For to whom could I more suitably refer my labors than to him to whom not only all Italy, but even the most remote nations, are accustomed to refer the judgment and arbitration not only of their plans but also of their deeds, as if to an honorary judge? Nor did I fear that this little gift of ours would come as a burden to you while you are occupied with such great affairs; for I know you are accustomed to managing the greatest matters in such a way that you do not despise even the smallest, and by the felicity of your genius, you arrange all things so that they are carried out in an undisturbed course. Although, those things which look toward the advancement of studies should not be considered among the smallest matters to you. Witness to this are the illustrious gymnasiums of disciplines of every kind, but especially of Greek and Latin eloquence, which have been established by your great care throughout the cities of your empire. For who else of your strength and fortune encourages, protects, and cherishes the talents of his people with a greater command toward virtue?

As for what concerns me, I do not demand that my writings be taken in comparison with great authors, nor that they be granted the same authority as established writers; if there is anything of good in our work, I most willingly credit it to my learning. It will be enough for me if I merely strive. I wish for our industry to be commended only so far as I may be said to have provided for the progress of students with a harmonious and concise brevity; so that those things which would have to be sought scattered through the volumes of many authors may be found here collected in one work in a convenient order of subjects. For we have labored so that all matters, beginning from their first principles, might be led through a series to the conclusion, lest the reader be led around in confusion in an otherwise difficult art, and suffer more from the ineptitude of the writer than from the obscurity of the subject. We take only this for ourselves: we believe our industry should seek commendation from this point.

Therefore, most humane Prince, receive our little gift with the clemency you usually show in other matters, and allow it to be supported at least by the preface of your name. Thus it will deserve to be received; it cannot provide commendation to itself by its own merit, but it will follow your dedication. Since we otherwise credit all the goods of our fortune or genius to you, so whatever we have achieved through study and industry, we wish to be dedicated and consecrated to your name for all time.

Although many have pursued the science of harmony <note>original: "harmoniam scientiam"</note> through theory alone—which is the concern of the theorist—more extensively than those who have practiced it, it is believed that no science could have reached such a great use of harmony without actual practice. For why should I mention those most excellent ancients who first wrote on theory? For posterity has celebrated Orpheus, Amphion, Linus of Thebes, Arion, and Timotheus, and the rest, because by their singing (by their practice, I say) one charmed wild beasts, another rocks and forests, another the creatures of the water, and others soothed rustic and unrefined minds. It is well known that these men became most famous both through the principles of the discipline itself and through its actual performance. Nor would I rashly bring forward the Pythagoreans, the Platonists, and the Peripatetics, by whose command the use of both the natural and the artistic voice is greatly commended for the instruction of youth. This was asserted for the following reason: since the musician and philosopher Aristoxenus—as Marcus Tullius <note>Cicero</note> testifies in the first book of the *Tusculan Disputations*—affirmed that just as a certain tension of the body exists in song and on strings, which is called harmony, so from the nature and shape of the whole body various modes are produced like sounds in song. There are also those who have posited that powers are useless unless they are reduced to acts; for which reason they feel that the exercise of the melodic voice has contributed most to the consideration of harmony—not that it adds a varied multitude to it, but rather brings it to its very perfection. Therefore, the practice of music is the movement of sounds producing consonances and melody. Indeed, we gather these sounds in vain through reason and science unless they have been grasped by practice itself. Hence, it is necessary for their tensions and relaxations <note>pitch height and depth</note> and consonances to become known not only to the mind and through ratios, but through the habit of hearing and vocalizing.
