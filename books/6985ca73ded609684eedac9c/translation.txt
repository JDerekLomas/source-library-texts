# TO THE READER, SALUTATIONS FROM A. DU VERDIER.

<image-desc>A large decorative woodcut initial 'L' set against a background of intricate floral and leaf motifs.</image-desc>

*The great Scipio, surnamed Africanus after the nation he subdued, when looking upon the images of the ancient heroes <term>Heroes: in this context, the term refers to both semi-divine figures of myth and the exceptionally virtuous men of antiquity</term>, was accustomed to say that he was greatly spurred toward magnanimous deeds by the reading of their achievements. By this, he meant to show that the principal goal of writing history is to place before one's eyes, as if in a high and eminent place, the examples of the acts of men.*

*Emperor GAIUS <note>likely referring to Gaius Caesar, or Julius Caesar, as the following story is traditionally attributed to him</note>, having seen a statue of Alexander the Great in Spain, began to weep, as if suddenly seized by a shame of himself for having not yet performed any deed worthy of memory at the age in which the other had already subdued and conquered a great part of the world. However, Plutarch says that this happened to Julius Caesar while contemplating some history of the same Alexander painted on a wall of the temple of Hercules. For the ancients had the custom of painting the walls of frequently visited temples with the illustrious deeds of great personages, with a summary of them written below, in order to incite readers to acquire glory by imitating such examples. We read that this happened to the aforementioned Caesar, whose deeds—so numerous and memorable—shall not be erased by any age, but remaining in full force shall be recited for all time. Seeing then that history has such efficacy that it can move the spirits of men to do good, and can better incite them when that which is written is presented and expressed in painting, engraving, high-relief sculpture <note>original: "taille esleuee en bosse"</note>, or otherwise: I have made a collection of several figures of distinguished persons, other than those which have been made heretofore and which are seen in the books of medals <term>books of medals: a popular 16th-century genre of books containing portraits of famous people based on ancient coins and medallions</term>: at the foot of which*
