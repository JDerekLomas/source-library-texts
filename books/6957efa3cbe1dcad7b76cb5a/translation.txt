Although we possess a multitude of plants, far exceeding in number those which were either described by the ancients or merely indicated by name—and celebrated in medicines or other uses—it is nevertheless difficult to judge which of our modern specimens correspond to each of theirs. For those that were indicated only by name have seen their names change over the long passage of time, and any conjecture regarding them has perished entirely. As for those that were described, unless they possess very evident and unique characteristics, they remain hidden among many similar species. From this follows a significant loss of the knowledge discovered with great zeal by the ancients and handed down to us. Thus, it is rightly to be feared that in this changing tide of time, the discovery of new things may be far surpassed by the loss of the old. It is as if this were the fate of the human race: to be constantly occupied in the restoration of things that perish, and for sciences—like flowing waters—to exist in a state of continuous renewal.

Furthermore, the knowledge of plants <note>original: "stirpium cognitio"</note> which was handed down by Greek, Latin, and Arabic authors was in danger of being completely erased due to the damage caused by the "barbarians" <note>A common Renaissance term for the perceived decline in scholarship during the Middle Ages</note>, had it not been recently revived by Jean Ruel <note>original: "Ruellius"</note>, Ermolao Barbaro <note>original: "Hermolaus"</note>, Antonio Musa Brassavola <note>original: "Brasauolus"</note>, and other foreigners. In our own times, it has been enriched by Pietro Andrea Mattioli of Siena <note>original: "Mattiolus Senensis"</note>. Through the work of Luca Ghini—my teacher and a leader in this faculty, who, while professing the study of "Simples" <term>Simples: medicinal plants used on their own, rather than in complex mixtures</term> at the University of Pisa <note>original: "Academia Pisana"</note>, shared all the most beautiful and noteworthy findings with him—and through the diligence of other scholars, Mattioli composed an outstanding work on Dioscorides <note>Pedanius Dioscorides, the 1st-century author of the foundational pharmacological text 'De Materia Medica'</note>, such that it seems almost nothing more could be desired. There are also the opinions of Luigi Anguillara <note>original: "Aloysij Anguillarę"</note>, written concisely in this field but with the highest judgment. ...treated matters that were almost obscure among the ancients. Finally, to all these things was added the knowledge of those items which come forth in the **West Indies** <note>original: "India Occidentali"; referring to the Americas</note>, the New World having been discovered by the Spaniards. The most learned **Monardes**, a **Physician of Castile** <note>Nicolás Monardes (c. 1493–1588), whose work on American plants revolutionized European medicine</note>, governed this history. Thus, this science rightfully seems not only to have been revived, but to have increased so much that it must be judged to flourish far more brightly in our times than ever before.

In this immense multitude of plants, however, I see that one thing is lacking, which is usually most desired in any other disordered troop. For unless they are reduced to orders, and distributed into their own <term>classes</term> <note>categories of classification</note> like the battle lines of a camp, it is inevitable that everything will be thrown into confusion by tumult and fluctuation. We see this happening even now especially in the examination of plants: for when the intellect is overwhelmed by a disordered multitude, inextricable errors and fretful altercations arise for the most part. Indeed, if the proper <term>genus</term> <note>the general "family" or group</note> is unknown, no description, however accurately delivered, provides a certain proof, but instead usually deceives; for when the genera are confused, everything must necessarily be confused.

Since, therefore, all science consists in the collection of similar things and the distinction of dissimilar things—and this distribution into genera and <term>species</term> <note>specific types</note> is like classes according to differences indicating the nature of the thing—I have endeavored to provide this in the universal history of plants. My hope is that if I have made any progress in this kind of study, despite the meager nature of my talent, I may bring it forth for the common good. Among the ancients, **Theophrastus** <note>Greek philosopher and the "father of botany"</note> indicated this method of treatment, but pursued it in only a few instances. Among our own people, **Ruellius** <note>Jean Ruel (1474–1537), a French physician and botanist</note> certainly attempted it, but he progressed no further than those things which he excerpted from **Theophrastus** regarding the common method.
