### The Cuckoo

The Cuckoo is also known by the name **Zigui** <note>original: "子規" (*Zigui*). A common poetic name for the cuckoo in Chinese literature.</note> and is also called the **Resentful Bird** <note>original: "怨鳥" (*Yuanniao*).</note>. Its bitter cry is said to continue until it spits blood. Whenever it begins to sing, it always faces toward the north; when its crying becomes truly agonizing, it will even hang upside down from the trees. 

The *Record of Strange Matters from Linhai* <note>original: "臨海異物志" (*Linhai Yiwu Zhi*). A 3rd-century work by Shen Ying describing the geography and local customs of the coastal Linhai region.</note> states: The "Juti" <note>original: "鵙鶗" (*Juti*), an ancient name for a bird of the cuckoo or shrike family.</note> is another name for the Cuckoo. By the third month of the year, it cries day and night without ceasing. 

The *Shuowen Jiezi* <note>original: "說文" (*Shuowen*). The first Chinese dictionary to analyze the structure of characters, compiled during the Han Dynasty.</note> claims that the Cuckoo is the transformation of **Emperor Wang**, the King of Shu <note>original: "蜀王望帝" (*Shuwang Wang Di*). Legend says that King Du Yu of Shu abdicated his throne and, after his death, his soul turned into a cuckoo whose cry reminds people of his longing for his home.</note>. To this day, it leaves its eggs in the nests of other birds to be hatched. All the other birds then feed its chicks as if they were subjects serving their lord. The *Erya* <note>original: "爾雅" (*Erya*). The oldest surviving Chinese encyclopedia and dictionary.</note> notes that the "Tijue" <note>original: "鶗鴂" (*Tijue*).</note> is indeed this same bird.

## The Silver Pheasant

The preface to the *Poetic Exposition on the Silver Pheasant* <note>original: "白鷴賦" (*Báixián Fù*). A 'Fu' is a form of Chinese classical literature that combines prose and rhyming verse to describe a subject in exhaustive detail.</note> states: The Silver Pheasant is a mysterious and extraordinary creature among the feathered tribes. It possesses a white essence with black markings; its claws and beak are a pure cinnabar red. Its body is slender, while its crest and spurs are quite similar to those of the chicken or the long-tailed pheasant. Its spirit and appearance are tranquil and refined, and it does not mingle with the common flock of birds. 

Additionally, the *General Treatise on the Investigation of Things* <note>original: "格物總論" (*Géwù Zǒnglùn*). An influential encyclopedic work from the Ming Dynasty that categorized natural phenomena, plants, and animals.</note> notes: The form of the Silver Pheasant is similar to the common pheasant or the long-tailed pheasant <note>original: "雉翟" (*Zhì Dí*). These terms distinguish between the shorter-tailed common pheasant and the long-tailed varieties often used in ritual dances.</note>. It has a white base with black patterns, and its tail can reach four or five feet in length. It is a bird most worthy of appreciation.

# The Hwamei

The Hwamei resembles an <term>oriole<gloss>likely referring to the Black-naped Oriole common in China</gloss></term> but is smaller. Its plumage is a yellowish-black. Its "eyebrows" look as though they have been meticulously painted, which is why it is called the Hwamei <note>original: 畫眉 (huàméi), literally "painted eyebrow." This refers to the Chinese Laughingthrush, which has a distinctive white eye-ring extending into a long stripe.</note>. It is exceptionally skillful at singing and vocalization, much like the <term>Hundred-Tongues bird<gloss>A traditional name for birds known for mimicry, such as the Black Thrush or certain Shrikes</gloss></term>. 

A poem by <term>Master Ouyang<gloss>Ouyang Xiu (1007–1072), a renowned statesman, historian, and poet of the Song Dynasty</gloss></term> says: 

->A hundred warbles and a thousand notes, shifting at its whim,<-
->Amidst mountain flowers of red and purple, and trees both high and low.<-
->Only now do I realize that listening to them locked within a golden cage,<-
->Can never match the song of one crying freely in the garden woods.<-

<note>Ouyang Xiu’s poem is a classic of Chinese literature, often used to contrast the constraints of official political life with the freedom of nature.</note>

# The Zhui Dove
<note>original: 鵻 (zhuī). This refers specifically to a type of turtledove or pigeon mentioned in the *Classic of Poetry*. In ancient texts, it is often associated with the Wood Pigeon or the Oriental Turtle Dove.</note>

What we now call the <term>Zhui-jiu<gloss>Zhui Dove</gloss></term> is known by many names: the Jing Dove, the Chu Dove, the Zhui Dove, the Suckling Dove, the Yan Dove, the Virtuous Dove, and the Chicken Dove.

The *Fangyan* <note>original: 方言 (*The Dialect Dictionary*), a Han Dynasty text by Yang Xiong that records regional variations in the Chinese language</note> states: Regarding the <term>Jiu<gloss>a general category for pigeons, doves, and sometimes cuckoos</gloss></term>, in the regions between <term>Qin and Han<gloss>The ancient heartland of China, primarily modern-day Shaanxi</gloss></term> west of the Pass, it is called the <term>Yan-jiu<gloss>the "crested" or "soaring" dove</gloss></term>. The larger ones are called the <term>Jiu-jiu</term>, while the smaller ones are called the <term>Ge-jiu<gloss>Pigeon-dove</gloss></term>. Some call them the <term>Zhui-jiu</term> and others the <term>Yan-jiu<gloss>Quail-dove</gloss></term>. In the regions between Liang and Song <note>referring to parts of modern Henan and Anhui provinces</note>, they are simply called the **Zhui**.

The nature of the **Zhui** is compassionate, filial, sincere, and cautious. It is also called the <term>Zhu-jiu<gloss>Invoker or Blessing Dove</gloss></term>. Some say that the **Zhui** and the <term>Shi-jiu<gloss>variously identified as the Cuckoo or "Corpse" Dove</gloss></term> are actually the same bird. The nature of the **Shi-jiu** is singular and compassionate, while the nature of the **Zhu-jiu** is singular and filial. Therefore, one name is **Shi** and the other is **Zhu**.

<note>The virtues of "compassion" and "filial piety" attributed to these birds stem from classical observations of how they care for their young and the constancy of their mating habits, often cited as moral examples in Chinese literature.</note>

### The Owl
<note>original: 梟 (Xiāo). In this period of Chinese natural history, the owl was often portrayed through a moral lens, emphasizing its perceived "unfilial" nature in contrast to birds like the crow, which were thought to feed their parents.</note>

The owl is an unfilial bird. The mother broods over her young for a hundred days until their wings and feathers are fully grown; once they have matured, they eat their mother and fly away. 

In ancient times, the <term>Son of Heaven<gloss>The Emperor of China</gloss></term> performed the Spring Rites of Release. The <term>Yellow Emperor<gloss>Huangdi; the legendary ancestral sovereign of Chinese civilization</gloss></term> utilized the owl and the <term>Pojing<gloss>original: 破獍; a mythical beast said to resemble a tiger or leopard that committed the ultimate sin of eating its own father</gloss></term> in these ceremonies. Just as the owl is said to eat its mother, the *Pojing* is said to eat its father. The Yellow Emperor, wishing to exterminate such species, commanded all of his officials to use them in sacrifices. 

By the time of <term>Emperor Wu of Han<gloss>Reigned 141–87 BCE; a pivotal figure who promoted Confucianism and expanded the empire</gloss></term>, this practice was still followed according to the ancient methods. Every year, the <term>Eastern Prefecture<gloss>original: 東郡 (Dōngjùn); a historical administrative division</gloss></term> would deliver owls as tribute. On the fifth day of the fifth month <note>The Dragon Boat Festival, a day traditionally associated with warding off summer plagues and "poisonous" influences</note>, they were made into <term>Owl Soup<gloss>original: 梟羹 (Xiāogēng)</gloss></term> to be bestowed as a gift upon the various government officials.

# The Pelican

The **Pelican** <term>Tí<gloss>The formal classical name for the bird.</gloss></term> is also known as the **River-Sifter** <note>original: 淘河 (*táohé*); so named because it appears to "sift" the water for fish.</note> and the **Marsh-Pelican** <note>original: 鵜澤 (*tízé*)</note>. This is the bird known today as the **Pelican** <note>original: 鵜鶘 (*tíhú*)</note>. 

They enjoy flying in large flocks and diving deep into the water to feed on fish. In form, the pelican resembles an owl but is much larger; it possesses large, fleshy feet <note>original: 人足 (*rénzú*), literally "human feet," likely describing the large, sturdy nature of their webbed feet</note> and a beak that exceeds a foot in length. Its cry sounds as if it is calling out its own name. Beneath its chin, it has a throat pouch <note>original: 胡 (*hú*)</note> as large as a bag capable of holding several liters.

> "Fish do not fear nets, but they fear the pelican."

A fishing weir (梁) is a low dam used to trap fish.</note> refers to this very bird.

# The Falcon-Chicken
<note>original: 鶻雞 (*hújī*). This name literally translates to "Falcon-Chicken." Historically, this entry is often associated with the Northern Lapwing or similar migratory birds known for their distinct crests and loud, collective calls during migration.</note>

# The Zheng <note>Original: 正 (*zhèng*). While this character usually means "correct" or "upright," in the context of archery it refers to the center of the target.</note>

The **Zheng** is actually the **wagtail bird**. <note>Original: 鴒鳥 (*líng niǎo*). A small, long-tailed bird known for its rapid, undulating flight and constant movement.</note> It is **small and flies swiftly**, making it the **most difficult target to hit** with an arrow. For this reason, it is **painted in the center of the cloth target** <note>Original: 布侯 (*bù hóu*). These were large cloth targets used in ancient Chinese archery rituals, which were essential components of education and state ceremonies.</note> to serve as the **bullseye**. It possesses **eyes clear enough to see for a thousand miles**, and it is the creature that **best perceives the subtle signs of change**. <note>Original: 幾 (*jī*). A profound term in Chinese philosophy referring to the incipient, almost invisible moments when an event is just beginning to unfold.</note>

---

[Illustration: A woodcut illustration depicting a small bird, likely a **wagtail** (Zheng), in flight above a landscape of rugged mountains and bare trees. The bird is shown in a dynamic, diving posture, emphasizing its speed and agility as described in the text.]

---

<term>Zheng (正)</term>: In ancient Chinese archery, the target was called a *hou* (侯). The center of a cloth target was specifically called the *zheng*, while the center of a hide target was called the *gu* (鵠). # The Kite <note>Original: 鳶 (*yuān*). This refers to the Black Kite (*Milvus migrans*), a bird of prey common across Asia, known for its fork-shaped tail and effortless gliding.</note>

### Birds of the Kite and Owl Families

The **Kite and Owl** <note>Original: 鳶鴞 (*yuān xiāo*).</note> species **rub against the wind**, **circling and soaring** high above. The *Book of Rites* <note>Original: 曲禮 (*Qǔ lǐ*). The *Classic of Birds* <note>Original: 禽經 (*Qín Jīng*). The earliest Chinese work dedicated to the study of birds, blending early biological observation with folklore and omen-reading.</note> states:
*   When **doves cry in the evening**, there will be **light rain**.
*   When **kites cry in the morning**, there will be **heavy rain**.

<column-break/>

<term>Kite (鳶)</term>: In the Chinese tradition, the kite is a symbol of the heights of the sky. Its ability to stay aloft with minimal wing-beating was viewed as "rubbing the wind."
<term>Book of Rites (曲禮)</term>: An ancient ritual text. The section cited here relates animal behavior to environmental changes, reflecting the belief that the natural world provides signs for human preparation.
<term>Classic of Birds (禽經)</term>: A foundational text for Chinese ornithology. It often uses rhyming couplets to teach readers how to predict the weather by observing bird calls.
<term>Weather Prediction</term>: In agricultural societies, observing the "morning kite" or "evening dove" was a practical method for farmers to anticipate the arrival of storms or showers.
