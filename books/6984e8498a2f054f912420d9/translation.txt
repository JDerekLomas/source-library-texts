# LEO SUAVIUS
<note>Leo Suavius was the pseudonym of Jacques Gohory (1520–1576), a French scholar, alchemist, and physician who was instrumental in introducing Paracelsian thought to France.</note>

## TO THE NOBLE MAN
### RENÉ PERROT
### OF LE MANS,
<note>René Perrot (Renatus Perotus) was a contemporary of Gohory and a fellow enthusiast of the "New Philosophy" or alchemy.</note>

### GREETINGS.

To whose trust should I sooner commit these books of commentaries on Paracelsus’s *On Long Life* than to yours? They are like children recently born to me; yet, by whatever right they are mine, they are also yours by right of ownership and bond, my sweetest friend, since among friends all things are held in common. Because these writings are imbued with Philosophy, they are rightly dedicated to you, who are most devoted to its study, so that you may be chosen as the judge, as it were, of their initiation into the world.

Let us leave those haughty dedications to Princes to the ambitious writers of our age, who use such names to decorate the frontispieces of their works as if they were the facades of their own houses. For us—who delight in the leisure of literature and have long since withdrawn from the company of the Great—it is more fitting to offer our works to those endowed with virtue than to chase after empty little glory through a prideful dedication.

There are many who are devoid of all learning—

## LETTER.

—ing: with what boldness do you hope they will receive a gift when they are utterly ignorant of what it even is? Why do you offer pearls to a sow <note>original: *sui*; a reference to the biblical proverb "neither cast ye your pearls before swine."</note>, which would prefer a grain of barley to the great pearl of Cleopatra <note>A famous ancient anecdote recounts Cleopatra dissolving a priceless pearl (an *unio*) in vinegar to win a bet about the most expensive meal; here, it represents the highest value which the ignorant cannot perceive.</note>? Wretched me, who spent so many sleepless nights polishing these pages! Even though I knew these people were unskilled in the sciences, I still trusted that they would honor the virtue they saw in others, even if they lacked it themselves.

Indeed, this treatment of such hidden subjects—matters to which very few turn their attention today—is completely foreign to those who have not gained knowledge through the common arts. But it is worthy of you, Perrot. Although your fortune and wealth would allow you to spend your life in luxury and ease, you hold nothing more precious, nothing more vital <note>original: *antiquius*, literally "more ancient," but used here to imply a thing of higher priority or more venerable status.</note>, than the knowledge of the finest things.

As for this author <note>Referring to the Swiss physician and alchemist Paracelsus (1493–1541), whose work *On Long Life* Gohory is editing.</note>, to tell you what I truly think: in our age, I have seen no physician more serious, nor any philosopher more profound <note>original: *subtiliorem*, meaning more subtle, keen-witted, or precise.</note>. Since I saw that he was so pleasing to you, and because I measure the dedication of all good men by your judgment, I decided to explain a few of his more obscure passages: sometimes through notes <term>scholia (explanatory comments written in the margins or between lines)</term>, sometimes through paraphrase, occasionally with formal arguments, and at other times with a simple list of words—

## LETTER.

—by the interpretation of his new works. For diligent men, and those studious of hidden things (for whom alone this work is forged), will, as the Poet says <note>original: *vt Poeta dicit*; referring to Virgil, *Aeneid* II, 65: *ab uno disce omnes*—"from one learn all."</note>, learn all things from one; and the light that is brought into one place will be transmitted to other things likewise surrounded by the same darkness. 

As for the abundance of the books of Paracelsus, I hear from the Germans that nearly three hundred are counted. <margin>Paracelsus wrote three hundred books.</margin> O, fertility of genius! Regarding his medical and surgical work, beyond his treatments of daily illnesses, he wished to give a specimen of his most excellent art at Nuremberg: twelve lepers <margin>He restored twelve lepers.</margin> presented from the public were restored to their former health. This is a matter indeed beyond the belief of many, yet not incredible to those who consider his rare and unheard-of remedies made from the flower of Wallflower <note>original: *Cheiri*; likely *Cheiranthus cheiri*, used in Paracelsian medicine.</note> and Rosemary <note>original: *Anthos*; from the Greek for "flower," often specifically used by Paracelsus to refer to the essence of rosemary.</note>, the secret of coral <note>original: *arcano corallino*; a chemical preparation of coral believed to have cardiotonic or cooling properties.</note>, the essence of gold, the mystery of Antimony <margin>Wonders concerning Antimony and others.</margin>, and very many of this kind, which are not even known by name to common physicians. 

If only these arts were pleasing to our Kings—for, as Cicero says, "honor nourishes the arts" <note>original: *honos alit artes*; a classic defense of patronage from Cicero's *Tusculan Disputations*.</note>, and we are all kindled to our studies by glory—just as they were to the great King Francis <note>Francis I of France (reigned 1515–1547), renowned as a "Renaissance Prince" and patron of learning.</note>, for nature does not...

## LETTER.

—brought forth, nor is our region so barren of talent that there was no hope that knowledge of the greatest and most useful things (which the ancient Magi and Poets wove in such great veils <note>original: *involucris*; referring to the allegorical or "wrapped" nature of ancient wisdom and myths.</note>) and truth itself, as if unearthed from the well of Democritus <note>The Greek philosopher Democritus famously stated that "truth lies at the bottom of a well," meaning it is difficult to find and deeply hidden.</note>, would be revealed in our times as well. Meanwhile, as you yourself know, after long travels undertaken for the sake of knowledge—having consumed my wealth following the example of that philosopher <note>referring again to Democritus, who was said to have spent his inheritance on travel to learn from foreign sages.</note>—I finally rest in the suburban gardens of my city, entirely absorbed in the contemplation of the most beautiful things in nature. In this, I am like the Virgilian Iapyx <note>A character from Virgil's *Aeneid* (Book XII) who, though offered the gifts of prophecy and music by Apollo, chose instead to learn the "silent arts" of medicine to save his father.</note>, who indeed:

> *Preferred to know the powers of herbs and the practice of healing,*
> *And to ply the silent arts without glory:*

or like Menetes, for whom:

> *His art was his home, a poor one, nor did he know the gifts of the powerful;*
> *He sowed his herbs on rented land.* <note>These verses are adapted from Virgil, describing a life of humble, honest labor in nature rather than the pursuit of political power.</note>

For although I might have had some assistance from talent and fortune for conducting public affairs, yet mindful of the human condition, I have willingly renounced the pursuit and service of princes.

Farewell. From <term>Lutetia</term> <note>The Latin name for Paris.</note>, August 6th, in the year [15]66.
