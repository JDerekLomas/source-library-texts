# TO THE MOST ILLUSTRIOUS
## AND MOST REVEREND
### LORD, LORD LÆLIO RUINO OF BAGNOLO
<note>Lælio Ruini (died 1621) was the Bishop of Reggio Emilia and a patron of the arts and sciences.</note>
### BISHOP OF REGGIO, ETC.
### BERNARDINO BALDI
### SENDS MANY GREETINGS.

The fields of Architecture are most fertile, filled with an abundance of the most beautiful things. For this reason, it is greatly to be lamented that in this, our unhappy age, there are so few who would devote useful—indeed, entirely necessary—labor to their most noble cultivation. Whether this happens through the fault of the era, or for some other cause, we shall not debate. This much we certainly know: that the good arts are nourished and spread by honors and rewards, and that many most brilliant minds perish everywhere through the greed or neglect of great men.

Toward such excellent and beautiful studies, beyond any controversy, I have been inclined since my very youth; and these I have not only ardently loved, but also diligently cultivated, as far as was permitted by most troublesome cares and constant occupations. Witnesses to this are those "little nighttime works" <note>original: "lucubratiunculæ." A modest term scholars used for works written by lamplight.</note> of mine, some of which the famous man Marcus Welser <note>Marcus Welser (1558–1614) was a German banker, historian, and patron of the sciences who helped publish Baldi's work.</note>—to whom I had dedicated them—most kindly saw to it that they were published elegantly, lest they remain hidden forever, overwhelmed by darkness. 

Among my other late-night labors, however, I held in my hands the *Manufacture of Artillery* <note>original: "Belopoeeca"</note> of Hero of Alexandria, written in Greek, concerning the construction of ballistae, scorpions, and catapults. These works had been, as they say, "interdicted from water and fire" <note>original: "aqua & igni... interdicta." A Roman legal phrase for exile, here used metaphorically to mean the text was inaccessible or neglected by other scholars.</note> by even the most learned and famous men...

...and having been avoided until now, we have not only rendered these works into Latin, but have also restored them nearly to their original luster—augmented with diagrams and commentaries—for the surviving copies were most corrupt, mutilated, and covered in a great deal of neglect. 

Therefore, although several of my friends not only encouraged me but truly urged and impelled me to release this labor, I could scarcely bring myself to listen to them. This was because I knew that the very subject at hand is considered empty today; following the invention of those bronze engines <note>original: "æneorum tormentorum." Baldi is referring to cannons and gunpowder artillery.</note>—should I call them monsters or thunderbolts?—these ancient devices bring little utility or importance to military affairs. Yet, while I was turning this over in my mind, I realized that the work of those who strive with their whole heart to bring light to ancient and noble learning, and to rescue from darkness the best inventions of the ancients—whatever they may be—is undoubtedly to be praised and promoted. 

For even if the writings of the illustrious ancients in this field are no longer entirely useful or practical, no sane person would deny that these old things can be, or at least become, inspirations for great thoughts for modern <note>original: "neotericis"</note> minds. Today, all the arts thrive and have nearly recovered their former beauty—not only the noble arts, but even the manual ones. Therefore, it was not right that the knowledge of this most beautiful art, so highly praised in ancient times, should utterly perish through the negligence of men in our own time.

You, therefore, most illustrious and Reverend Lælio, if in the meantime you find some leisure amidst the greatest and weightiest occupations by which you are distracted, and if you should occasionally return to the study of those things in which you have taken great delight since your youth, do not despise these works. For in reading this little book, if you do not find practical utility, you will certainly find a noble pleasure, not unworthy of a free man. 

But if, meanwhile, with the talent and knowledge of things in which you excel, you find something in it either incorrectly understood or poorly translated, please forgive me. I would wish that you judge my effort not by its ultimate success, but by my spirit and my desire to be of service, in some small way, to you and to all others who take pleasure in these studies. Farewell, from Urbino.

# ON THE MAKING OF ENGINES

For the earlier engineers, having only grasped the general form and arrangement of the machines in their minds, achieved little fame, specifically because they used fixed proportions when launching the missile. But those who followed them—partly by removing and partly by adding elements—rendered these machines harmonious and efficient. These machines are composed, both as a whole and in their individual parts, based on the diameter of the opening that receives the **tension-spring** <term>tonus; the bundle of twisted hair or sinew that provides the engine's power</term>. Indeed, the principle and foundation of the whole matter is the tension-spring itself. 

Therefore, one must determine the opening for a **stone-throwing engine** <term>Petraria; a heavy catapult designed to launch rocks</term> as follows: The number of **minae** <term>mina; an ancient unit of weight, roughly 430 grams or 1 pound</term> of the stone that is to be launched is multiplied by one hundred. Then, the **cube root** <term>cubic side; the number which, when multiplied by itself twice more, equals the total</term> of the resulting number is found. To this found value, a tenth part of itself is added; it is then necessary to make the diameter of the opening that many digits wide. 

For example, let the stone be eighty minae. The cube root of the resulting number <note>80 x 100 = 8,000</note> will be twenty. If a tenth part <note>2</note> is added to this, it becomes twenty-two; this will be the size of the diameter of the opening. However, where the resulting number does not have a precise cube root, one must find the value that comes nearest to it, and likewise add a tenth part to it. **Therefore in use...**] The Greek manuscripts have almost no certain distinction between sections or parts. This is known not only from the force of the word itself—namely, that a "theorem" pertains to speculation and theory—but from Euclid's own division into Theorems and Problems. Regarding this, the philosopher Proclus writes in Book 1, Commentary 3 on the *Elements* that Euclid called **Problems** those things in which he proposes to construct, demonstrate, or bring into being things that do not yet exist; but **Theorems** are those in which he establishes the means to see, know, and demonstrate the properties of that which already exists. Therefore, Heron would have more aptly divided this little work of his into **Problems** <note>practical constructions</note> rather than Theorems. The eighty [minas] which Hero uses as an example, if you double it, will be 160. I find it noted by no one that this "Greek Mina" is in a double ratio to the Roman weight. The Attic Mina was 100 drachmas. However, the Roman weight was 96 drachmas; therefore, the Alexandrian Mina was not double the weight but nearly equal, according to **Galen**. **Georgius Agricola** <note>A scholar of ancient weights and mining</note>, in the fifth book of *On Weights*, records it was 160 drachmas; therefore, that was not double the weight either. The Hebrew Mina of sixty shekels <note>original Latin: *siclorum*</note> was 240 drachmas, and this is far more than double the Roman weight. # LIFE OF HERON
## OF ALEXANDRIA

->*By the Author Bernardino Baldi of Urbino*<-

**H**eron was highly praised for his knowledge of Geometry and Mechanics, as evidenced by the testimony of those most celebrated men, <term>Pappus</term> <note>Pappus of Alexandria (c. 290–350 CE), a major Greek mathematician whose "Collection" preserved much of ancient geometry.</note> and <term>Eutocius</term> <note>Eutocius of Ascalon (c. 480–540 CE), a commentator on Archimedes.</note>, and indeed by the evidence of his own works. He seems to have flourished in the city of Alexandria—which in that age was the most flourishing center for mathematical studies in Egypt—around the time of the second <term>Ptolemy Euergetes</term> <note>While Baldi places Heron in the 3rd century BCE (reign of Ptolemy III Euergetes, 246–222 BCE), modern historians typically place Heron later, in the 1st century CE.</note>. It is clearly evident that our Heron was a disciple of Ctesibius. This Ctesibius was either a barber staying in Aspendia (as Athenaeus reports in the *Deipnosophists*) or certainly the son of an Alexandrian barber, as <term>Vitruvius</term> <note>The famous Roman architect and engineer (1st century BCE).</note> prefers. If we trust Athenaeus, a most serious writer, this Ctesibius lived during the reign of the aforementioned Ptolemy. Heron was a man of subtle and keen intellect, and according to the same Vitruvius, he was the first inventor of "spirits" <note>original: "spiritualium"; referring to devices powered by air, steam, or water pressure.</note>, which are called <term>Pneumatics</term> by the Greeks, and of wonderful machines. Therefore, our Heron, aided by the excellence of his master and the sharpness and skill of his own intellect, made the greatest progress in Mechanics. Nor is this surprising, for he did not apply himself to these studies while being ignorant of Geometry—as some, indeed almost all, do today, who rashly approach the building itself while neglecting the foundations. Instead, from his early youth he was imbued with the most noble doctrines and, equipped as if with armor, he noblely climbed as if by steps to the highest peak. Thus, he excellently taught others what he had experienced in himself. For he wrote (as Pappus reports in his *Collections*) that one part of Mechanics is rational, while the other requires the work of the hands. He held that the rational part consists of Geometrical, Arithmetical, Astronomical, and Physical principles.

It is clear from this, therefore, that our Heron was a follower of Epicurus in his *Physics*; and also in those matters pertaining to ethics. For he seems to place happiness—as can be seen in the *Preface to the Belopoeeca* <note>Heron's treatise on the construction of artillery and projectile-throwing machines</note>—in that same confidence and security which he, along with that philosopher, calls <term>ataraxia</term> <note>original: "ἀταραξία" — a state of serene calmness and freedom from emotional disturbance, the goal of Epicurean philosophy</note> in Greek. Heron flourished around the one hundred and sixty-fifth Olympiad; that is, in the one hundred and twentieth year before the Christian era. He was, in fact, younger than Archimedes by almost an entire century.
