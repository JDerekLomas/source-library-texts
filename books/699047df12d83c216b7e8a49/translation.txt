### *Meaning of the term ‘Polarization.’*

When a particle of a body possesses properties related to a certain line or direction in the body, and when the body—retaining these properties—is turned so that this direction is reversed, then if these properties of the particle are reversed in relation to other bodies, the particle is said to be **polarized** in reference to those properties. Those properties are then said to constitute a particular kind of polarization.

Thus, we may say that the rotation of a body about an axis constitutes a kind of polarization. This is because if the direction of the axis is turned end-for-end while the rotation continues, the body will be rotating in the opposite direction as far as external space is concerned.

A conducting particle through which an electric current is flowing may be said to be polarized. This is because if the particle were turned around, and if the current continued to flow in the same direction relative to the particle, its direction in space would be reversed.

In short, if any mathematical or physical quantity is of the nature of a <term>vector</term> <note>A quantity having both magnitude and direction, such as velocity or force.</note>, as defined in <term>Article 11</term>, then any body or particle to which this directed quantity or <term>vector</term> belongs may be said to be **Polarized***, because it has opposite properties in the two opposite directions or poles of the directed quantity.

The poles of the earth, for example, have reference to its rotation and have accordingly different names <note>i.e., North and South poles.</note>.

---

* The word <term>Polarization</term> has been used in a sense not consistent with this in <term>Optics</term>, where a ray of light is said to be polarized when it has properties relating to its sides which are identical on opposite sides of the ray. This kind of polarization refers to another kind of <term>Directed Quantity</term>, which may be called a <term>Dipolar Quantity</term>, in opposition to the former kind, which may be called <term>Unipolar</term>.

When a dipolar quantity is turned end-for-end, it remains the same as before. Tensions and pressures in solid bodies, extensions, compressions, distortions, and most of the optical, electrical, and magnetic properties of crystallized bodies are dipolar quantities.

The property produced by magnetism in transparent bodies—that of twisting the plane of polarization of incident light—is, like magnetism itself, a unipolar property. The rotatory property referred to in <term>Article 303</term> is also unipolar.

### *Meaning of the term ‘Magnetic Polarization.’*

382.] In speaking of the state of the particles of a magnet as **magnetic polarization**, we imply that each of the smallest parts into which a magnet may be divided has certain properties related to a definite direction through the particle, called its **Axis of Magnetization**. We also imply that the properties related to one end of this axis are opposite to the properties related to the other end.

$$P = \iiint (Bz + Cy) dx dy dz, \quad Q = \iiint (Cx + Az) dx dy dz,$$
$$R = \iiint (Ay + Bx) dx dy dz. \text{ (8)}$$

We thus find for the value of the potential energy of the magnet placed in the presence of a unit pole <note>A theoretical point source of magnetic charge with a value of one.</note> at the point ($\xi, \eta, \zeta$):

$$W = K \frac{l\xi + m\eta + n\zeta}{r^3}$$
$$+ \frac{\xi^2(2L - M - N) + \eta^2(2M - N - L) + \zeta^2(2N - L - M) + 3(P\eta\zeta + Q\zeta\xi + R\xi\eta)}{r^5} .$$

This expression may also be regarded as the potential energy of the unit pole in the presence of the magnet, or more simply as the magnetic potential at the point $\xi, \eta, \zeta$ produced by the magnet.

### *On the Center of a Magnet and its Primary and Secondary Axes.*

392.] This expression may be simplified by altering the directions of the coordinate axes and the position of the origin. In the first place, we shall make the direction of the $x$-axis parallel to the axis of the magnet. This is equivalent to setting:
$$l = 1, \quad m = 0, \quad n = 0. \text{ (10)}$$

If we change the origin of coordinates to the point ($x', y', z'$), while the directions of the axes remain unchanged, the volume integrals $lK, mK$, and $nK$ will remain the same, but the others will be altered as follows:
$$L' = L - lKx', \quad M' = M - mKy', \quad N' = N - nKz'; \text{ (11)}$$
$$P' = P - K(mz' + ny'), \quad Q' = Q - K(nx' + lz'), \quad R' = R - K(ly' + mx'). \text{ (12)}$$

If we now align the direction of the $x$-axis to be parallel to the axis of the magnet, and set:
$$x' = \frac{2L - M - N}{2K}, \quad y' = \frac{R}{K}, \quad z' = \frac{Q}{K}, \text{ (13)}$$
then for the new axes, $M$ and $N$ retain their values, and the value of $L'$ becomes $\frac{1}{2}(M + N)$. $P$ remains unchanged, while $Q$ and $R$ vanish <note>original: "vanish"; meaning they become zero.</note>. We may therefore write the potential as:
$$K \frac{\xi}{r^3} + \frac{\frac{3}{2}(\eta^2 - \zeta^2)(M - N) + 3P\eta\zeta}{r^5} . \text{ (14)}$$

### 393.] Convention Respecting Signs.

At all parts of the earth’s surface, except some parts of the Polar regions, one end of a magnet points toward the north, or at least in a northerly direction, and the other in a southerly direction. Therefore, when we speak of the "north" and "south" ends of a magnet, we are not comparing the magnet’s internal fluid to the earth’s poles; we are merely expressing the position the magnet tries to take when free to move. On the other hand, when we wish to compare the distribution of imaginary magnetic fluid in the magnet with that in the earth, we shall use the more formal words Boreal and Austral magnetism.

# 405.] LINES OF MAGNETIC INDUCTION.

Since the distribution of magnetic induction is solenoidal <note>A solenoidal distribution means the flux is continuous and has no sources or sinks within the medium; what flows in must flow out.</note>, the induction through any surface bounded by a closed curve depends only on the form and position of the closed curve, and not on the shape of the surface itself.

404.] Surfaces at every point of which
-> $la + mb + nc = 0$ (19) <-
are called Surfaces of no induction, and the intersection of two such surfaces is called a Line of induction. The conditions that a curve, $s$, may be a line of induction are
-> $\frac{1}{a} \frac{dx}{ds} = \frac{1}{b} \frac{dy}{ds} = \frac{1}{c} \frac{dz}{ds}$ (20) <-
A system of lines of induction drawn through every point of a closed curve forms a tubular surface called a Tube of induction.

The induction across any section of such a tube is the same. If the induction is unity, the tube is called a Unit tube of induction.

All that Faraday <margin>* *Experimental Researches*, series 28.</margin> says about lines of magnetic force and magnetic sphondyloids <note>original: "sphondyloids"; an archaic term used by Faraday to describe the spindle-shaped volumes or "fields of force" surrounding a magnet.</note> is mathematically true, if understood as referring to the lines and tubes of magnetic induction.

The magnetic force and the magnetic induction are identical outside the magnet, but within the substance of the magnet they must be carefully distinguished. In a straight, uniformly magnetized bar, the magnetic force due to the magnet itself is directed from the end which points north (which we call the positive pole) towards the south end (or negative pole), both within the magnet and in the space outside it.

The magnetic induction, on the other hand, is directed from the positive pole to the negative outside the magnet, but from the negative pole to the positive within the magnet. Thus, the lines and tubes of induction are re-entering or cyclic figures <note>That is, they form continuous closed loops.</note>.

The importance of magnetic induction as a physical quantity will be more clearly seen when we study electromagnetic phenomena. When the magnetic field is explored by a moving wire, as in Faraday’s *Experimental Researches* 3076, it is the magnetic induction and not the magnetic force which is directly measured.

Since the magnetic induction through a closed curve depends only on the curve itself and not on the shape of the surface it encloses, it must be possible to determine the induction through that curve using a method that depends only on the nature of the curve. This method should not require the construction of a surface acting as a diaphragm <note>A "diaphragm" here refers to a surface stretched across the opening of the closed curve, like a drumhead.</note> across the curve.

This can be achieved by finding a vector $\mathfrak{A}$ related to $\mathfrak{B}$ (the magnetic induction) in such a way that the line-integral of $\mathfrak{A}$, calculated around the closed curve, is equal to the surface-integral of $\mathfrak{B}$, calculated over the surface bounded by that curve.

If, in Article 24, we use $F, G, H$ to represent the components of $\mathfrak{A}$, and $a, b, c$ for the components of $\mathfrak{B}$, we find the following relationship between these components:
-> $a = \frac{dH}{dy} - \frac{dG}{dz}, \quad b = \frac{dF}{dz} - \frac{dH}{dx}, \quad c = \frac{dG}{dx} - \frac{dF}{dy}.$ &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; (21) <-

The vector-potential at a specific point, caused by a magnetized particle located at the origin, is numerically equal to the magnetic moment of the particle divided by the square of the radius vector <note>The "radius vector" is the distance from the particle to the point being measured.</note> and multiplied by the sine of the angle between the axis of magnetization and the radius vector. The direction of the vector-potential is perpendicular to the plane formed by the axis of magnetization and the radius vector. To an observer looking in the positive direction along the axis of magnetization, the vector-potential is drawn in the direction of the clockwise rotation of a watch's hands.

Therefore, for a magnet of any shape where $A, B, C$ are the components of magnetization at the point $(x, y, z)$, the components of the vector-potential at the point $(\xi, \eta, \zeta)$ are:
-> $F = \iiint (B \frac{dp}{dz} - C \frac{dp}{dy}) dx \, dy \, dz,$ &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; \ <-
-> $G = \iiint (C \frac{dp}{dx} - A \frac{dp}{dz}) dx \, dy \, dz, $ &nbsp; &nbsp; } &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; (22) <-
-> $H = \iiint (A \frac{dp}{dy} - B \frac{dp}{dx}) dx \, dy \, dz; $ &nbsp; &nbsp; / <-

In these equations, $p$ is used for brevity to represent the reciprocal of the distance between the points $(\xi, \eta, \zeta)$ and $(x, y, z)$. The integrations are performed over the entire space occupied by the magnet.

407.] If a long, narrow filament of magnetic matter (such as a wire) is magnetized everywhere in a longitudinal direction, then the product of any transverse section of the filament and the mean intensity of the magnetization across it is called the strength of the magnet at that section. If the filament were cut in two at that section without altering the magnetization, the two surfaces, when separated, would be found to have equal and opposite quantities of surface magnetization. Each of these is numerically equal to the strength of the magnet at that section.

A filament of magnetic matter, magnetized so that its strength is the same at every section—regardless of where along its length the section is made—is called a <term>Magnetic Solenoid</term> <note>original: "Solenoid" comes from the Greek word for "pipe-shaped." In this context, it refers to a theoretical magnetic filament of constant strength.</note>.

If *m* is the strength of the solenoid, *ds* an element of its length, *r* the distance of that element from a given point, and ε the angle which *r* makes with the axis of magnetization of the element, the potential at the given point due to the element is:

->*m ds cos ε / r² = m / r² * dr / ds * ds.*<-

By integrating this expression with respect to *s* <note>Integrating: a mathematical process used to calculate the total effect of all the small parts of the filament combined.</note> to account for all the elements of the solenoid, the potential is found to be:

->*V = m (1 / r₁ - 1 / r₂),*<-

where *r₁* is the distance from the positive end of the solenoid to the point where the potential *V* exists, and *r₂* is the distance from the negative end to that same point.

Consequently, the potential due to a solenoid—and all its resulting magnetic effects—depends only on its strength and the position of its ends.

### 418.] SOLID ANGLES. 37

The potential due to a magnetic shell <note>A theoretical thin sheet where one side has north magnetic polarity and the other has south polarity.</note> is equal to the solid angle subtended by the edge of the shell multiplied by the strength of the shell. As we shall have occasion to refer to solid angles in the theory of electric currents, we shall now explain how they may be measured.

*Definition.* The solid angle subtended at a given point by a closed curve is measured by the area of a spherical surface whose center is the given point and whose radius is unity (one), the outline of which is traced by the intersection of the radius vector <note>A line segment connecting the origin/center to a moving point.</note> with the sphere as it traces the closed curve. This area is to be reckoned positive or negative according to whether it lies on the left or the right hand of the path of the radius vector as seen from the given point.

Let $(\xi, \eta, \zeta)$ <note>Greek letters xi, eta, and zeta, representing the coordinates of the fixed point.</note> be the given point, and let $(x, y, z)$ be a point on the closed curve. The coordinates $x, y, z$ are functions of $s$, the length of the curve measured from a given point. They are periodic functions of $s$, recurring whenever $s$ is increased by the whole length of the closed curve.

We may calculate the solid angle $\omega$ <note>The Greek letter omega, commonly used to represent a solid angle.</note> directly from the definition in this way. Using spherical coordinates with the center at $(\xi, \eta, \zeta)$, and setting:

->$x - \xi = r \sin \theta \cos \phi, \quad y - \eta = r \sin \theta \sin \phi, \quad z - \zeta = r \cos \theta,$<-

->$\omega = \int(1 - \cos \theta) d\phi,$<-

or, using rectangular coordinates:

->$\omega = \int d\phi - \int_0^s \frac{z - \zeta}{r^3} \left[ (x - \xi) \frac{dy}{ds} - (y - \eta) \frac{dx}{ds} \right] ds,$<-

the integration being extended around the curve $s$.

If the $z$-axis passes once through the closed curve, the first term is $2\pi$. If the $z$-axis does not pass through it, this term is zero.

If a point moves along a closed curve, the end of a perpendicular line drawn from it will trace a second closed curve. Let the length of this second closed curve be σ; then the solid angle subtended by the first closed curve is:

->ω = 2π - σ.<-

This follows from the well-known theorem that the area of a closed curve on a sphere with a radius of one unit, when added to the circumference of its polar curve <note>In spherical geometry, a polar curve is a secondary curve where every point is 90 degrees away from the original curve.</note>, is numerically equal to the circumference of a great circle of that sphere <note>The circumference of a great circle on a unit sphere is 2π.</note>.

This construction is sometimes convenient for calculating the solid angle subtended by a rectilinear figure <note>A shape consisting of straight lines.</note>. For our own purpose, which is to form clear ideas of physical phenomena, the following method is preferred, as it uses no constructions that do not arise naturally from the physical data of the problem.

419.] A closed curve *s* is given in space, and we must find the solid angle subtended by *s* at a specific point *P*.

If we consider the solid angle as the potential of a magnetic shell <note>term: magnetic shell; a theoretical thin sheet where one side acts as a north pole and the other as a south pole.</note> of unit strength whose edge coincides with the closed curve, we must define it as the work done by a unit magnetic pole against the magnetic force while it moves from an infinite distance to the point *P*. Therefore, if σ is the path of the pole as it approaches point *P*, the potential must be the result of a line-integration <note>term: line-integration; the process of calculating the total effect of a field along a specific path.</note> along this path. It must also be the result of a line-integration along the closed curve *s*. The proper form of the expression for the solid angle must, therefore, be a double integration with respect to the two curves *s* and σ.

When *P* is at an infinite distance, the solid angle is clearly zero. As the point *P* approaches, the closed curve—as seen from the moving point—appears to open up. The entire solid angle can be imagined as being generated by the apparent motion of the different segments of the closed curve as the moving point approaches.

As the point *P* moves from *P* to *P'* over a small segment *dσ*, the segment *QQ'* of the closed curve (which we call *ds*) will change its position relative to *P*. The line on the unit sphere corresponding to *QQ'* will sweep over an area on the spherical surface, which we may write as:

->*dω* = Π *ds dσ*. (1)<-

To find Π, let us suppose *P* is fixed while the closed curve is moved parallel to itself through a distance *dσ* (equal to *PP'* but in the opposite direction). The relative motion of the point *P* will be the same as in the actual case.

# 425.] SOFT AND HARD STEEL.

Soft iron does not retain its magnetic state as readily as hard iron. The process of hammering, or any other kind of vibration, allows hard iron—when under the influence of magnetic force—to assume a magnetic state more easily, and to lose it more quickly once the magnetizing force is removed. Iron that is "magnetically hard" is also physically stiffer to bend and more likely to break.

The processes of hammering, rolling, wire-drawing, and sudden cooling tend to harden iron, while the process of annealing <note>Annealing: a heat treatment where metal is heated and then cooled slowly to remove internal stresses and soften it.</note> tends to soften it.

The magnetic and mechanical differences between steel of hard and soft temper are much greater than the differences between hard and soft iron. Soft steel is almost as easily magnetized and demagnetized as iron, whereas the hardest steel is the best material for magnets intended to be permanent.

Cast iron, although it contains more carbon than steel, does not retain magnetization as well.

If a magnet could be constructed so that the distribution of its magnetization remained unaltered by any external magnetic force, it might be called a **rigidly magnetized body**. The only known entity that fulfills this condition is a conducting circuit through which a constant electric current is made to flow.

Such a circuit exhibits magnetic properties and may therefore be called an electromagnet; however, these magnetic properties are not affected by other magnetic forces in the field. We shall return to this subject in Part IV.

All actual magnets, whether made of hardened steel or of lodestone <note>original: "load-stone"; a naturally magnetized piece of the mineral magnetite.</note>, are found to be affected by any magnetic force brought to bear upon them.

For scientific purposes, it is convenient to distinguish between **permanent** and **temporary** magnetization. We define permanent magnetization as that which exists independently of external magnetic force, and temporary magnetization as that which depends on such force. We must observe, however, that this distinction is not based on a deep understanding of the inner nature of magnetizable substances; it is merely an expression of a hypothesis introduced to allow mathematical calculations to be applied to these phenomena. We shall return to the physical theory of magnetization in Chapter VI.

A body magnetized in this way by the action of magnetic force is said to be **magnetized by induction**, and the magnetization is said to be **induced** by the magnetizing force.

The magnetization induced by a given magnetizing force differs across different substances. It is greatest in the purest and softest iron, in which the ratio of the magnetization to the magnetic force may reach a value of 32, or even 45. <note>original: Thalén, *Nova Acta, Reg. Soc. Other substances, such as the metals nickel and cobalt, are capable of a lower degree of magnetization. However, all substances, when subjected to a sufficiently strong magnetic force, are found to show signs of polarity.

When the magnetization is in the same direction as the magnetic force—as seen in iron, nickel, cobalt, and similar materials—the substance is called <term>Paramagnetic</term>, <term>Ferromagnetic</term>, or more simply <term>Magnetic</term>. When the induced magnetization is in the direction opposite to the magnetic force—as seen in bismuth and similar materials—the substance is said to be <term>Diamagnetic</term>.

In all these <note>diamagnetic</note> substances, the ratio of the magnetization to the magnetic force which produces it is exceedingly small, being only about negative one-four-hundred-thousandth ($-\frac{1}{400,000}$) in the case of bismuth, which is the most highly diamagnetic substance known.

In crystallized, strained, and organized substances, the direction of the magnetization does not always align with the direction of the magnetic force producing it. The relation between the components of magnetization (relative to axes fixed within the body) and the components of the magnetic force can be expressed by a system of three linear equations. Of the nine coefficients involved in these equations, we shall show <note>original: "shew"</note> that only six are independent. The phenomena of bodies of this kind are categorized under the name <term>Magnecrystallic phenomena</term>.

When placed in a field of magnetic force, crystals tend to align themselves so that the axis of greatest paramagnetic induction (or least diamagnetic induction) is parallel to the lines of magnetic force. (See Article 435).

In soft iron, the direction of the magnetization coincides with the direction of the magnetic force at that point; for small values of magnetic force, the magnetization is nearly proportional to it.

->$\frac{dV}{dv} + \frac{dV'}{dv'} + 4 \pi \sigma = 0.$<-

These small magnetic elements of the substance each contain precisely equal quantities of the two fluids. Within each element, the fluids move with perfect freedom, but the fluids can never pass from one magnetic element to another.

The problem, therefore, is of the same kind as that relating to a number of small electrical conductors scattered through a dielectric <note>dielectric: an insulating material that can be polarized by an electric field</note> insulating medium. 435.] Let *α*, *β*, *γ* <note>alpha, beta, gamma</note> be the components of magnetic force, and *A*, *B*, *C* those of the magnetization at any point. Then the most general linear relation between these quantities is given by the equations:

$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
A &= r_1 \alpha + p_3 \beta + q_2 \gamma, \\
B &= q_3 \alpha + r_2 \beta + p_1 \gamma, \\
C &= p_2 \alpha + q_1 \beta + r_3 \gamma,
\end{aligned}
\right\}
\qquad (1)
$$

where the coefficients *r*, *p*, and *q* are the nine coefficients of magnetization <note>These coefficients describe how easily a material becomes magnetized along different axes.</note>.

Let us now suppose that these are the conditions of magnetization within a sphere of radius *a*, and that the magnetization at every point of the substance is uniform and in the same direction, having the components *A*, *B*, and *C*.

Let us also suppose that the external magnetizing force is also uniform and parallel to one direction, and has for its components *X*, *Y*, and *Z*.

The value of *V* <note>the potential of the external magnetizing force</note> is therefore:
$$
V = -(Xx + Yy + Zz), \qquad (2)
$$
and that of Ω' <note>Omega prime</note>, the potential of the magnetization outside the sphere, is:
$$
\Omega' = (Ax + By + Cz) \frac{4 \pi a^3}{3 r^3}. \qquad (3)
$$
The value of Ω <note>Omega</note>, the potential of the magnetization within the sphere, is:
$$
\Omega = \frac{4 \pi}{3} (Ax + By + Cz). \qquad (4)
$$
The actual potential within the sphere is *V* + Ω, so that we shall have for the components of the magnetic force within the sphere:
$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
\alpha &= X - \frac{4}{3} \pi A, \\
\beta &= Y - \frac{4}{3} \pi B, \\
\gamma &= Z - \frac{4}{3} \pi C.
\end{aligned}
\right\}
\qquad (5)
$$
Hence:
$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
(1 + \frac{4}{3} \pi r_1) A + \quad \frac{4}{3} \pi p_3 B + \quad \frac{4}{3} \pi q_2 C &= r_1 X + p_3 Y + q_2 Z, \\
\frac{4}{3} \pi q_3 A + (1 + \frac{4}{3} \pi r_2) B + \quad \frac{4}{3} \pi p_1 C &= q_3 X + r_2 Y + p_1 Z, \\
\frac{4}{3} \pi p_2 A + \quad \frac{4}{3} \pi q_1 B + (1 + \frac{4}{3} \pi r_3) C &= p_2 X + q_1 Y + r_3 Z.
\end{aligned}
\right\}
\qquad (6)
$$
Solving these equations, we find:
$$
\left.
\begin{aligned}
A &= r'_1 X + p'_3 Y + q'_2 Z, \\
B &= q'_3 X + r'_2 Y + p'_1 Z, \\
C &= p'_2 X + q'_1 Y + r'_3 Z,
\end{aligned}
\right\}
\qquad (7)
$$

Consider an element of the shifted body whose quantity is $-ρδv$ at a distance $-δx$. The effect of these two elements is equivalent to that of a magnet of strength $ρδv$ and length $δx$. The intensity of magnetization is found by dividing the magnetic moment of an element by its volume. The result is $ρδx$.

Hence, $-\frac{dV}{dx} δx$ is the magnetic potential of the body magnetized with the intensity $ρδx$ in the direction of $x$, and $-\frac{dV}{dx}$ is the potential of the body magnetized with intensity $ρ$.

This potential may also be considered in another light. The body was shifted through the distance $-δx$ and assigned a density of $-ρ$. Throughout that part of space common to the body in its two positions, the density is zero; this is because, as far as attraction is concerned, the two equal and opposite densities cancel each other out. There remains, therefore, a shell of positive matter on one side and of negative matter on the other, and we may regard the resulting potential as being caused by these. The thickness of the shell at a point where the normal <note>A line perpendicular to the surface.</note> drawn outwards makes an angle $ε$ with the x-axis is $δx \cos ε$, and its density is $ρ$. The surface-density is therefore $ρδx \cos ε$, and, in the case where the potential is $-\frac{dV}{dx}$, the surface-density is $ρ \cos ε$.

In this way, we can find the magnetic potential of any body uniformly magnetized parallel to a given direction. This force consists of two parts: one due to external causes, and the other due to the magnetization of the body itself. If, therefore, the external magnetic force is uniform and parallel, the magnetic force resulting from the magnetization must also be uniform and parallel for all points within the body.
