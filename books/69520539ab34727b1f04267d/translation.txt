Indeed, how many reefs of difficulty had to be overcome; how many uncultivated, trackless, and remote deserts had to be traversed; and with how many many-shaped monsters of Nature <note>term: "pantamorphæ Naturæ" — referring to the various and strange symbolic forms found in Egyptian art</note> I had to struggle! I believe only those who have attempted similar things can know this—those who, soon terrified by the insurmountable labor of overcoming such difficulties and driven sideways as if by despair, immediately turned back from the path they had set for themselves. They had learned by experience how difficult, arduous, and full of risk it is to enter the horrifying and confused recesses of such forests without a preceding light, and without a guide or companion for the intricate ways; to commit oneself to such tangled Labyrinths without the thread of Ariadne <note>In Greek mythology, Ariadne provided a thread to help Theseus navigate the Labyrinth; Kircher means he had no existing guide to help him decipher the symbols</note>.

->**Preface to the Reader.**<-

...to attempt, so that if it were not granted to fully penetrate the hidden recesses of the inner sanctuaries <note>original: "adytorum"; the most sacred, inner parts of a temple, used here as a metaphor for deep, secret knowledge.</note>, at least to look inside somewhat through a crack; if not even this, I at least thought it honorable to set down the very attempt itself as a merit to Divine praise. While I remained stuck, perplexed in this turmoil of mind, I know not whether by Divine or human providence it happened that many learned men, having been informed of my **Efforts** published through the *Coptic Forerunner* <note>Refers to *Prodromus Coptus* (1636), Kircher's groundbreaking work establishing the link between Coptic and ancient Egyptian.</note>, acted with such vigor that they not only encouraged me with excellent book resources, but the letters of Great Men and Princes also compelled me to continue the weaving of the conceived work. From this, it finally seemed right to me to apply my mind seriously and constantly to promoting the project. Therefore, while I carefully compare each item with others through tireless labor from the monuments of ancient authors, written in almost every kind of language, and explore everything more deeply with all and each other by the precise rule of the **combinatory art** <note>A method of logic and mathematical arrangement used to find hidden connections between different symbols or ideas.</note>, it finally happened that—sensing the tricks <note>original: "τεχνάσματα" (technasmata)</note> and machines of the playful Sphinx, and having been admitted a little deeper into its hidden and hitherto inaccessible sanctuaries—the Sphinx being now extinguished and the proposed riddles <note>original: "gryphis"; referring to the complex, griffin-like enigmas of the past.</note> dissolved, I fully understood the secrets, rules, canons, and institutes of the entire Hieroglyphic art, by the breathing grace of the Divine Spirit (which, as it works in its own to will, to be able, and to perfect: so also without it we can not only do no good, but cannot even think it).

Meanwhile, since the *Oedipus* <note>Refers to Kircher's *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–54), his massive attempt to "solve" the riddle of the hieroglyphs.</note> has now been brought to light, there have been those who, by constantly supplying new monuments of Hieroglyphic literature, strove earnestly that these might be explained by me with equal success; besides those new additions to Hieroglyphic literature which were supplied from **Spain, Italy, Germany, Egypt,** and **Constantinople.** To these, finally, in the course of this present year 1673, this **Mummy-related Monument** was added, which the most noble and illustrious Mr. de Four sent to me from Lyon, for the purpose of unravelling it, out of the greatest kindness of which he was capable. It was the custom of the ancient Egyptian <term>Hierogrammateôn<note>sacred scribes or priests responsible for holy texts and records</note></term> to embalm the bodies of those who had been most celebrated and conspicuous in this life—whether through their religion and worship of the Gods, or through their benevolence and the contribution of other things serving the utility of the fatherland—treating them as if they were the bodies of certain earthly Gods. ->**On the Preparation of Mummies:**<-
->**Why the Egyptians strove so much for Incorruption.**<-

<margin>The word Mummy is Persian, meaning a dried and preserved body.</margin> **M**ummy or [Arabic: مومي] <note>original: "mumiya," a Persian/Arabic word for bitumen or wax.</note> is a Persian word, and it denotes the same thing as a dried Corpse preserved by a certain method, free from corruption. Although many Physicians have described its virtues <note>In Kircher's time, "mummy" (ground-up ancient remains) was frequently used as a medicinal ingredient to treat various ailments.</note> at great length, there have been none who have—I will not say handed down—but even touched upon the method and system of preserving them. Most people think that the corpses which they call Mummies are prepared by no certain preservative, but reach this state of incorruption by the pure and simple artifice of nature alone, in the manner that follows. These were protective spirits or minor deities assigned to guard specific places or secrets.</note> with the most profound arrangement of symbols, as I explained a little earlier regarding the two statues marked B and B.

<margin>Why they are seen with the veil of the Deity, a belt, and in a kneeling posture.</margin> They veiled the head with the sacred veil of Isis, so that no part of the bare head or hair would appear—for hair, according to the testimony of Porphyry, is nothing but the superfluous waste of the human body, and they therefore considered it unworthy of the sight of the Deity. They bound them tightly with the bandage with which they believed the body of Osiris was tied. For the rest, they are seen in a **kneeling** <note>original: *ingeniculationis*</note> posture, with the whole body naked, or at least (as we said above) clothed in a wrinkle-free garment woven from the finest linen. They imagined that in this mystical habit, nothing could be more pleasing for acquiring what they sought, or more effective for "binding" themselves to God.

<margin>The meaning of the Altar figure they carry on their head.</margin> They carry the symbol of an **Altar** on their head, by which they secretly foreshadowed the worship of the Divine Power; nothing is more common than this in the hieroglyphics. They placed it upon the head to suggest that, according to the intellectual notions of the Divine Power, sacred rites ought to be performed. With the hand raised and one finger extended—the sign of **silence**—they noted that neither the sacred rites nor the Name of God ought to be made public. 

<margin>In what the purification of the soul consists.</margin> Indeed, Pythagoras himself, a diligent follower of the Egyptians, and Plato in his second letter to Dionysius, expressly warn the worshippers of divine things not to popularize the sacred mysteries; concerning this, I will soon provide Plato’s own words. 

<margin>Melissus. Pythagoras.</margin> This purification of souls consists not only in the expulsion of vices from the mind, but also in the appropriate silence regarding the mysteries. For the secrets of philosophy are not to be broadcast to everyone. As Melissus used to say, the eyes of ordinary souls cannot endure the rays of Divinity. For this reason, Pythagoras thought those people were equally impious and unjust who made the gifts of Wisdom common to those whose souls had not been cleansed and purified, even in their dreams.

Thus he wrote. For this reason, Pythagoras (whom Plato venerates in all things) began the sacred mysteries of his teachings with the most exacting purification of the mind. Plato similarly commands that secrets not be brought out to the common people, lest the common folk either perversely despise what they have received or fall into errors. 

In that same place, he judges that the secrets of the Deity are more safely committed to words than to letters, for letters make things common to anyone. In these words, Plato seems to have followed the ancient custom of the Jews, who assert that the mystical sense of the Law was not so much handed down in writing by God through Moses, but rather entrusted to minds. The Pythagoreans certainly observed this, and Pythagoras himself publicly forbade "philosophizing" <note>In this context, to "philosophize" means to publicly debate or explain the inner core of his doctrines.</note>, as the author Lysis says in his letter to Hipparchus. ### CHAPTER VI.
### Concerning the Sepulchral Bundles, and those Averting Idols which were placed within the Wrappings.

<margin>The hieroglyphic monument was a sort of universal [record].</margin>

**M**any years ago, I was sent a statue from Egypt, one foot in height, made of rot-resistant wood—either cedar or wild fig. They said it had been found within the body of a mummy, and I still preserve it in my Museum as something rare and curious. Small statues half a palm's width in size, made of baked clay, are also found inserted into mummies: these signify nothing other than the *Lares* <note>Lares: Kircher uses the Roman term for household protective spirits to describe Egyptian 'ushabti' figures.</note> of which we spoke above. Thus, it is evident from this chapter that hieroglyphic inscriptions do not contain histories, the lives of kings, or their power and wealth, but rather the dictates of a higher doctrine, which we have discussed thus far. Indeed, they recorded the deeds of kings and things worthy of remembrance for posterity in the common language, which we call Coptic or Pharaonic.

### EPILOGUE.

Let the reader know that those things which we have brought forward—whether in this small work or elsewhere—concerning the Holy Trinity, the Soul and Spirit of the World, the origin of Gods, Demons, and Souls, the influences of Divine Ideas into the lower world, the Transmigration of souls, magical sacrifices and their methods, and similar matters, must all be read with caution. 

For most of these things are entirely contrary to the Orthodox faith and provided the first heresiarchs <note>Heresiarchs: the founders or leaders of early heretical sects.</note> with the material and opportunity to establish their most foul dogmas, as is evident from the refutations by the Holy Fathers: Augustine, Justin, Lactantius, Arnobius, and others. 

Therefore, if any light of truth shines forth in these things, we shall consider it not so much a true exposition as a certain foreshadowing of Orthodox doctrine, produced by the sublimity of the Natural Light with which they were endowed and the excellence of their genius. Or, if they occasionally reason correctly about the nature of God, we shall judge that they uttered it by a supernatural intervention of the Divine Power rather than having understood it themselves—just as the Holy Fathers feel regarding the Sibyls <note>Sibyls: ancient prophetesses believed by early Christians to have foretold the coming of Christ.</note>. 

Therefore, I would wish these matters to be discussed and explained only insofar as they serve to teach the foundations of hieroglyphic doctrine: as is commonly done in the mythology of the Gods, where we show not so much what they *did* teach, but what they *should* have taught.
