# ¶ The Book on Pleasure, by Marsilio Ficino the Florentine while still a young man, addressed to Antonio Canisiano, a Florentine citizen. Preface.

## THREE TYPES OF DISCOURSE.

I remember when I was together with you at your estate in Campoliano <note>An area in the Tuscan countryside</note>, and we were led along both by the exceptional beauty of that region and by the sweetness of the various fruits in which that villa especially abounds, that we fell into a discussion about pleasure. You yourself asked me to commit this discussion to writing as soon as I had the leisure. Therefore, as soon as I returned to Figline <note>original: Figinum; Ficino's birthplace near Florence</note>, I composed this little work on pleasure for you with the greatest pleasure myself.

¶ There are, to begin from the most important point, three types of discourse among philosophers. One by which we defend and approve a certain side of a question through argument, as the Peripatetics <note>Followers of Aristotle, known for walking while they taught</note> and Stoics <note>A school of philosophy emphasizing logic and emotional fortitude</note> have done. The second, where once a question is proposed, we bring forward diverse opinions and reasons concerning what is being sought, so that from the many views presented and compared with one another, we may choose which of them appears more probable and likely; this was used by the Academics <note>Followers of the later Platonic school who favored questioning over dogma</note> and nearly all the Socratics. The third type is most characteristic of the Sceptics, who, because they think all things are indifferent and hold nothing certain or even probable to follow, confuse and mix together those things which are separated and distinct by the order of nature; for this reason, they are rejected by all the more excellent philosophers.

¶ I have therefore decided to adopt the method of the Academics and Socratics in this discourse from among all of them. For indeed Plato, whom we follow and venerate as a god among philosophers, forbids those who are still in their tender years to offer their own opinion on a matter, lest—as often happens with younger people—what we most approved in our early years must later be retracted in more mature age and with graver judgment, and turned into a contrary opinion. Therefore, I judged it better to run through the opinions of other philosophers on pleasure in a brief discourse, rather than to express my own, so that I might not shamelessly assert anything against what we are taught by our teacher Plato, and so that I might satisfy your will, which I know to be most ardent in the study of philosophy. But enough of this; let us now come to the opinions of the philosophers.

## ¶ Chapter One. Concerning the parts of the soul.
## ¶ Likewise concerning gladness, joy, and pleasure, according to Plato.

<margin>P</margin>Plato, therefore (to begin with him as the leader of these philosophers), when he had divided the soul into two parts—namely, the mind and the senses—attributed **gladness** and **joy** to the mind, and **pleasure** to the senses. However, he thinks these first two differ from one another, because all joy is worthy of praise, whereas gladness is partly to be praised and partly to be blamed. For he defines gladness <note>original: "laetitia"; often used for more external or excitable mirth</note> as a certain elevation of the mind in the possession of some good things, which is able to both exceed and preserve moderation. Joy <note>original: "gaudium"; a more internal, spiritual delight</note>, however, is that very delight which is received from contemplation or some other practice of the virtues. And so, in the *Phaedrus* <note>Plato’s dialogue on the soul, beauty, and love</note>, when he spoke of that first and true life which the soul enjoys in the heavenly seats, he says that it is "nourished by the contemplation of truth, and it rejoices." For this reason, he frequently calls joy the "nourishment of the mind." For he wants truth itself to be like a field, and the contemplation of that truth to be like the fruits of that field. Then, the nourishment which is taken from these two is the joy by which the mind thoroughly enjoys the contemplation of truth. 

For this reason, in the same *Phaedrus*, when he was disputing about those shadows into which the soul has been pressed down and submerged from the highest and true light, he says: "There is much effort to see where the field of truth is; for the suitable food of the soul comes from there, and the nature of the wings by which the soul is raised up is nourished by this." Plato calls these "wings" those by which the soul flies back to the things above, from where it had descended on account of the thought of earthly things. Since this can be achieved through two virtues—namely, the contemplative and the moral—Plato understands these same virtues (as I interpret him) to be the "wings" of the soul itself. These the soul recovers by the contemplation of truth and divine things, just as it had lost them through the desire for these earthly things. When, indeed, having recovered its wings through the practice of both natural and moral philosophy, it has flown back to the heavens, he thinks two rewards are given to the returning soul: one is the contemplation of divinity. The other is a certain perfect and absolute joy, which the soul enjoys in that very knowledge of God. Plato calls the former "ambrosia" <note>The food of the gods</note> and the latter "nectar" <note>The drink of the gods</note> in the *Phaedrus* with these words: "The soul which has truly beheld and been nourished by these things, entering again within the heaven, returns home. But when the charioteer has returned, standing the horses at the manger, he sets before them ambrosia, and over it nectar to drink." Following the manner of Pythagoras <note>A pre-Socratic philosopher who influenced Plato's views on the soul and reincarnation</note>, he calls the "charioteer" reason. The "horses," however, are the other parts and natures of the soul which reason leads. the rest

### ¶ Chapter II. What pleasure is, and why it is not a good in itself, for many reasons, according to Plato.

<margin>u</margin>Plato maintains that pleasure is a certain sweet motion in the senses, which arises from the fulfillment of a lack <note>original: "indigentia"; a state of being in need or empty</note>. For lack and its fulfillment are, in a certain way, opposing forces. From lack arise desire and pain, while from fulfillment, pleasure comes to our senses. He does not think this pleasure of which we are now speaking should be numbered among "the goods." Although he demonstrated this with many very true arguments, this one is the first and most powerful of all: ¶ Just as the presence of heat makes the things it touches hot, so too by the presence of "The Good," all things to which goodness is added become good. However, no one becomes better through the possession of pleasure. Indeed, pleasure exists in beasts even more than in humans, yet animals are not better than humans—they are not even "good" in a moral sense. Furthermore, women, children, and the foolish—as well as all who are cowardly—pursue pleasure and embrace it with all their might, much more so than men who are good and excellent in character. Yet it would be absurd if a "good" were more suited to the wicked and the cowardly than to honest and excellent men.

¶ Nor can opposites be mixed together, as he believes. Yet pleasure is undoubtedly found where there is evil, since it is not lacking in the wicked and the unjust. Therefore, pleasure can in no way be "The Good," because it combines with that which is evil. Since "The Good" and "The Bad" are opposites, they can never truly join together. ¶ Add to this what is said by Socrates in the *Gorgias* <note>One of Plato's dialogues focusing on ethics and rhetoric</note>: that good and evil are never found in the same man at the same time. Yet the same person, at the very same time, is frequently affected by pain and pleasure alike. For he calls every lack "painful" and every fulfillment of that lack "pleasant" to the senses. For instance, he calls thirst a lack of nature, and drinking a fulfillment. Therefore, the former is troublesome, while the latter is agreeable; to drink...

...furthermore, it happens that one can experience both [pleasure and pain] at once. From this, he <note>Plato</note> wishes to show that it is possible for someone to possess pain and pleasure simultaneously. Indeed, desire, anxiety, suspicion, envy, and pity—all of which contain pain within themselves—are companions of pleasure. For this reason, Timaeus <note>original: "timæns"; likely referring to the speaker in Plato’s *Timaeus*</note> says that desire, permitted by the senses, proceeds from pain and pleasure at the same time; thus, it happens that pleasure is coupled and confused with pain. And since good is not mixed with evil, he denies that pain (which is an evil) and pleasure can be "the good."

¶ Furthermore, since every good thing ought to be stable and permanent, who would say that pleasure—which has no stability—is a good? Yet, this cannot be denied if one considers its definition: pleasure is unstable and uncertain. For in its definition (as we recalled above), it was called a "motion" <term>motion: *motio* or *kinesis*; in Platonic philosophy, things that are constantly changing or moving are considered less "real" or "good" than things that are eternal and still</term>. Nothing is more contrary to stability than motion. Nor is there anyone who has ever tasted any pleasure of the body who does not know how fragile it is, how brief, with what speed it slips and flows away, and how quickly it is turned into its opposite. 

Moreover, that which is good can never be contrary to another good. Yet, since pleasure hinders many of the greatest goods, who could doubt that it is contrary to the good? For pleasures hinder **prudence**, they pour darkness into the mind, they corrupt judgment, and with their charms and enticements—like certain little harlots—they allure the minds of men and ensnare them. They cast the entire nature of man down into the mud and filth of vices and crimes. For the divine Plato, in his letter to Dionysius <note>Referring to the *Epistles* attributed to Plato, specifically addressed to the tyrant Dionysius of Syracuse</note>, says that "pleasure brings detriment to men, giving birth to ignorance, restlessness, foolishness, and insolence." 

¶ Furthermore, there is no good that is not worthy of either praise or veneration. Yet no one of sound mind praises pleasure, nor is anyone judged worthy of honor and glory simply because they enjoy the greatest pleasures. Rather, he is honored who is of such a character that he scorns and neglects them with a high and unconquered spirit. 

¶ Added to this is the fact that no good can rightly be called "ours" unless it is either inherent in our very nature or has proceeded from ourselves. And we are nothing other than **mind and reason**. The body, however, is a **prison** <note>original: "carcer"</note> and a **tomb** <note>original: "sepulchrum"; this reflects the famous Orphic/Platonic pun *soma sema*—"the body is a tomb"</note> for us. The senses, indeed, are like certain guards or ambushers, who seem to care for and strive for this above all: to plunge us deeper into the darkness of this prison and to bind us tight, so that the souls may not fly back to their own place and heavenly homeland...

But who would say that pleasure proceeds from choice? Is it not true that for things which fall under choice, counsel and deliberation must first have taken place? For nothing is chosen unless it has been previously consulted upon and judged. But no one deliberates in order to have pleasure within himself; for it is innate to us. Wherefore Socrates thinks there are two guiding principles <note>original: "ideas"</note> in each of us which we follow wherever they lead. One is the opinion that aspires to the best; the other is the desire for pleasures. The former he calls an acquired principle, while the latter he calls innate. Therefore, pleasure follows neither art, nor reason, nor any order at all. Rather, it is stirred up within us by a sudden and reckless impulse of nature and the senses, rather than by any certain judgment or counsel. It flatters the senses, soothes and tickles the body with a certain ruinous sweetness, makes the soul effeminate <note>meaning "soft" or "weakened," a common moral critique of the time</note>, and moves the mind and reason out of their proper state.

At one moment the lustful soul rejoices and hopes, and is, as it were, lifted upward; at another, it grieves, despairs, fears, is troubled, and falls downward. Now it wants this, then it does not want it; indeed, at the very same time, reason may want one thing while the senses <unclear>affect</unclear> another. Thus it happens that a soul of this kind not only stretches itself now toward this and now toward that, but even at one and the same time is pulled apart by contrary emotions and differing opinions. 

Therefore, Socrates used to say he would rather his lyre be out of tune, or the choir he led, or that many other men should contradict him, than that he, being one man, should be at odds and out of harmony with himself. ...should desire. Let them tend toward a single point and not be separated from each other by any means, but join together in a kind of fusion; whoever possesses any one of them cannot feel the lack of any other good. Let the virtues follow one another with such a connection that whoever has attained one already enjoys the possession of all virtues. For this reason, God seems to have offset the labors and difficulties of attaining goods with their very union and coupling. 

Yet, on the contrary, evils disagree and dissent among themselves, and can in no way join together or be fused with one another. Correcty does Aristotle <note>original: *Aristoteles*</note> say that "evil destroys itself," <note>A reference to Aristotle’s *Nicomachean Ethics* (Book IV), where he argues that vice is inherently unstable and self-destructive</note> for if it were complete in every respect, it would be intolerable. Never do prodigality <note>wasteful extravagance</note> and avarice <note>extreme greed</note>, or boldness and fearfulness, meet in the same person; in the same way, no other extremes of vice are linked together, since they are fundamentally opposed. Therefore, it seems that nature has applied the best remedy against the inclination toward wrongdoing: that he who is more prone to a fall cannot fall into every part of the precipice at once. Yet, he who ascends with the greatest difficulty necessarily reaches the whole and complete summit afterward. 

Therefore, since the condition of evils and goods is of this kind, it is concluded by necessary reasoning that those things which do not follow one another cannot be "goods." Pleasures, however, far from coming together, seem in a way to fight against each other and more often bring about an impediment. ### Caput .vi. Opinions of the Peripatetics concerning pleasure.

<margin>a</margin> Aristotle, however, seems to call none of these things "pleasure." Instead, he views it as a certain perfection of apprehension itself, such that there is first a perception of sweetness, and from this, another thing is born as its companion and annexed perfection, and this is pleasure. But what this kind of perfection is, which he names pleasure, he never (it seems) sufficiently explained. The Peripatetics <note>Followers of Aristotle’s philosophy, named after the "peripatos" or covered walkway where they studied.</note>, who descended from Aristotle, were divided into different opinions while they wished to explain and bring to light what had been obscurely stated by their leader. For some placed pleasure in the senses, while others placed it in the appetite that follows the senses. Even those who placed it in the senses interpret Aristotle in different ways. Indeed, some think there are two perceptions in the senses: the first seems to proceed and flow into the mind from those things offered from the outside; the second, however, having proceeded more from the mind, extends toward those things which are outside. For first, the mind perceives sweetness (for example), which first perception seems to arise from the sweetness of the object itself. Then, it considers that it has attained that sweetness—that it perceives, possesses, and enjoys it. They believed that this second perception of the mind—whether it be called thought, meditation, or observation—was called "pleasure" by Aristotle. In this view, pleasure is that very consideration by which one thinks about the fact that they are enjoying the presence of a good.

¶ Some do not think it is a sense, but rather that when the mind notices it has attained sweetness, it is offered up and, as it were, uplifted, conceiving and displaying a certain cheerfulness; and they believe this was called "pleasure" by Aristotle. Thus, pleasure is the elevation or cheerfulness of the mind having already attained the good it desired.

¶ But those who placed pleasure in that part of the soul by which we feel appetite <note>The "appetitive" faculty refers to the part of the soul that drives us toward what we perceive as good and away from what is bad.</note> believe there are two properties of appetite. They call the first "desire" and the second "rest." They claim that the former is full of trouble, worry, care, and anxiety, but the latter is infused with an ineffable sweetness; they believe this is what the leader of the Peripatetics called pleasure. Thus, pleasure is said to be nothing other than a certain limit or rest of the appetite in a good that has already been attained. ¶ **Aristotle**, however, seems to disagree most strongly with **Cicero** and the **Stoics**, as he does not wish for any joy or pleasure to be called "gladness." For when he was enumerating the **affections** <note>original: *affectus*; these are the emotions or states of being "affected" by external things.</note> in the second book of his work on ethics, he included gladness among them. He then added that these are the affections which pleasure or distress follow. From this, it follows that for Aristotle, pleasure cannot be an affection; otherwise, what he said just before would necessarily be false. Since pleasure cannot follow itself, anyone who calls pleasure an "affection" would seem to deny Aristotle's premise that pleasure follows all affections. Furthermore, since in that same second book he called affections "movements of the soul," yet in the tenth book of that same work he demonstrates that pleasure is by no means to be called a "movement," who would not understand that Aristotle utterly denied that pleasure is an affection? Since, therefore, according to this opinion, pleasure is neither a movement nor an affection, while he maintained that gladness *is* an affection, it is concluded by necessary reasoning that pleasure and gladness differ greatly from one another. 

Nor, indeed, is gladness understood among the **Peripatetics** <note>The followers of Aristotle, named for the "colonnades" (peripatoi) where they walked and talked.</note> as something present in the soul while it actually enjoys a present good. For no one among the Peripatetics doubts that gladness is a movement of the soul. And indeed, since affections—which are certain passions of the **appetite** <note>The faculty of the soul that desires or seeks things.</note>—are defined by them, and "passion" in their opinion consists of a flux and movement, then it follows they are motions. Therefore, gladness, being an existing affection, must necessarily be seen as a movement. However, once the soul has attained what it desired, it is no longer moved; rather, all the agitation of seeking ceases, and it rests most sweetly in the good it has acquired. Because of this, while the soul is enjoying the good, one must remove all the affections of the appetite that were concerned with reaching that very thing. Therefore, we shall also remove "gladness" from the soul when it is enjoying some good.

A disturbance (as the Stoics <note>A school of ancient Greek philosophy that taught that virtue is the only good and that we should remain indifferent to external circumstances.</note> define it) is a commotion of the soul turned away from right reason and contrary to nature; they believe it is born from an opinion. For as soon as an image of something presents itself, the soul judges it to be either a destructive evil or a good. From this judgment, four disturbances of the soul proceed. For those things presented to us, whether they are good or evil, appear to be either in the future or in the present.

If the soul contemplates some future evil, it immediately attempts to turn away from it. If this turning away happens according to reason, it is called **caution**, <note>original: *cautio*</note> and this is neither a disturbance nor foreign to the soul of a wise person. However, that which happens without reason and with a lowly, broken loss of spirit is called **fear**. <note>original: *metus*</note> Fear is a turning away from reason, or an anxious expectation of a future evil. But when that which is perceived as an evil is imminent and affects the soul in the present, that disturbance arises which they call either **distress** <note>original: *ægritudinem*</note> or **pain**. <note>original: *dolorem*</note> Distress is a distraction of the soul that opposes reason; indeed, the wise person has no such affection for a present evil.

But when the image of something appears as a future good, the very opinion of it (to speak in the Stoic manner) impels one to obtain it. When this is done with constancy and prudence, we call such an appetite the **will**; <note>original: *uoluntatem*</note> they believe this exists only in the wise person and should in no way be called a disturbance. They define it thus: the will is that which desires something according to reason. But that which is incited in opposition to reason, and more violently, is **lust** <note>original: *libido*</note> or **unbridled desire**, <note>original: *cupiditas*</note> which is found in all fools.

Furthermore, when we are moved in such a way that we enjoy some good, it happens in two ways: for either the soul is moved seriously and calmly, which they call **joy**; <note>original: *gaudium*</note> or, when it exults lightly and excessively, they then call it **exultant gladness** <note>original: *lætitiam gestientem*</note> and **pleasure**. <note>original: *uoluptatem*</note> They define this as an irrational and exultant elation of the soul when one thinks they are enjoying a great good. In this, they agreed with the **Peripatetics** <note>Followers of Aristotle's school of philosophy.</note> (whose opinion we reported in the second place above when speaking of Aristotle), for both schools agree that pleasure is the very elation of a soul thinking it has attained some greatest good.

¶ Moreover, the Stoics divide pleasure into several parts. They held that there are five parts in the senses and four in the mind, while the mind agrees with the senses. Those things which move the senses do not have distinct or specific names among the Stoics, except for that which is perceived by hearing; this they call **delight**, <note>original: *delectationem*</note> and they define it así: Delight is a pleasure soothing the soul through the sweetness of hearing, while the others charm the remaining senses.

### Chapter 11. The Cyrenaics on Pleasure, that it is the Goal.

<margin>h</margin> Let these things said regarding the opinion of the Stoics suffice. Let us now hear the **Cyrenaic** philosophers, <note>A school of philosophy founded by Aristippus which taught that immediate, sensory pleasure is the highest good.</note> who followed from **Aristippus**, a disciple of **Socrates**. The Cyrenaics posited two disturbances: **pain** and **pleasure**. They called pleasure a "smooth motion" and pain a "rough motion." They believe that no pleasure differs from another, and one does not soothe more than another. Furthermore, they claim that all living creatures seek out pleasure and reject pain. 

However, they understand pleasure to be of the body, and they say this is the "end" <note>original: *finem*; the ultimate purpose or goal of human life.</note>—not that pleasure which consists of a stable state or the mere absence of pain, or which acts as a rest from disturbance (which **Epicurus** <note>A later philosopher who redefined pleasure as "ataraxia" or the absence of pain and mental distress, rather than active sensory indulgence.</note> accepted and established as the goal). They believe it is proven to us that pleasure is the goal because nature has reconciled us to it from childhood, even without the judgment of reason. When the soul's judgment has neither been corrupted by custom nor shaped by opinion, it follows that we seem to consider pleasure the highest good by the instinct of nature alone. And when we enjoy it, we seek nothing else besides it, as if it were the ultimate object of desire.
