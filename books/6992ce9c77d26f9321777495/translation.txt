**Method for Harvesting Melon Seeds: Every year, one should first select from the primary parent plant.**

When harvesting seeds from a melon, cut off both ends and take only the seeds from the center. <gloss>The melon fruit naturally grows opposite the leaves.</gloss> This ensures the fruit sets well and the seeds mature early. If you use seeds from the "middle generation" <note>This refers to fruits that develop later on the vine</note>, the vines will grow excessively long, and fruit will only set after the second or third leaf node; these also mature late. Planting "early" seeds results in fast maturation but smaller melons, while planting "late" seeds results in slow maturation but larger melons. Regarding the instruction to remove the two ends: seeds near the stem produce melons that are curved and thin; seeds near the blossom end produce melons that are short and bitter. Furthermore, when harvesting seeds, take them from the most delicious melons while they are being eaten. Mix them immediately with fine chaff, sun-dry them until they are nearly dry, and then rub them clean.

Good quality fields, or land where small beans or millet were previously grown, are the best choices for planting.

# Taro

## **Essential Techniques for the Peasantry**

The *Book of Fan Shengzhi* <note>original: "氾勝之書" (Fan Shengzhi Shu). Fan Shengzhi was a 1st-century BCE agriculturalist whose work is one of the earliest known Chinese farming manuals.</note> states: To plant taro, the cultivation pits should be three feet square and three feet deep. Take bean stalks <gloss>pronounced *qi*; five stalks</gloss> and place them inside the pit. Tread on them firmly with your feet until they reach a thickness of one foot and five inches. Take the moist topsoil from the pit, mix it with manure, and place it back inside the pit. This upper layer should be one foot and two inches thick. Pour water over it and tread on it again to ensure the soil retains its moisture <note>The treading and watering create a compact, moist environment that mimics the swampy conditions taro naturally prefers.</note>.

Take five taro seed-tubers and place them in the four corners and the center of the pit. Tread them into the soil. In the event of drought, water them frequently. Once the bean stalks have rotted <note>The rotting organic matter provides high-nitrogen fertilizer and warmth to the developing tubers.</note>, the taro produced will all be three feet in length. A single pit can yield a harvest of three <term>shi</term> <note>A traditional unit of volume. In this period, one *shi* was roughly 20 to 30 liters.</note>.

There is also another method for planting taro: one should select fertile, loose soil near a water source. Mix the soil with manure to make it soft and rich. In the third month <note>Roughly April in the Gregorian calendar.</note>, when the heavy rains fall, the taro can be planted. As a general rule, plant one seedling every two feet.

In the sixth month <note>the height of summer, providing the necessary heat for fermentation</note>, prepare fermented black soybeans. The quantity of black soybeans used is not restricted; two or three *dou* <term>dou</term> <note>a traditional unit of volume; at the time, one *dou* was roughly 6 to 10 liters</note> is a suitable amount. 

Wash the beans thoroughly and soak them overnight. Drain them and let the moisture drip away until they are dry to the touch, then steam them. Cook them until they are tender. Spread the beans out on a bamboo mat and wait until they have cooled to the temperature of a human body.

The mixture must not be too wet, nor should it be too dry. The ideal consistency is reached when you squeeze the beans in your hand and the juice just begins to seep out between your fingers.

# Plum and Apricot

## Essential Techniques for the Peasant People
The methods for planting and cultivation are the same as those used for peaches and plums.

### ○ Making "White Plums" <note>original: "白梅" (báiméi); these are salted and sun-dried plums, different from the fresh fruit.</note>
When the plum fruit is first formed and the pit is still sour, pluck them. At night, soak them in brine; during the day, expose them to the sun. After ten nights of soaking and ten days of sun-drying, they are complete. These are used for seasoning dishes. <gloss>They are frequently added to minced meat or ginger-based condiments.</gloss>

### ○ Making "Dark Plums" <note>original: "烏梅" (wūméi); these are smoked plums used in traditional medicine.</note>
As with the previous method, pluck the plums when the pits have just formed. Place them in a basket and suspend them over a hearth to smoke them until they are dry. <gloss>These plums are used for medicinal purposes and are not suitable for seasoning food.</gloss>

### ○ Method for Making Apricot and Plum Paste <note>original: "麩" (fū); literally "bran," referring here to a dried, powdery fruit concentrate or its mixture with rice bran.</note>
When apricots or plums are fully ripe, collect a large quantity and grind them in a basin. Use a piece of raw cloth to wring out the thick juice and spread this juice onto a tray. Dry it in the sun, then scrape it off by hand. This can be mixed with water to create a syrup, or ground together with rice bran as you see fit.

# Jujube
<gloss>Supplement: Stem Jujube</gloss>

**Essential Techniques for the Peasant People** <note>original: "齊民要術" (Qímín Yàoshù); a seminal 6th-century Chinese agricultural manual by Jia Sixie.</note> Always select those with a pleasant flavor to keep for planting. Wait until the jujube leaves first begin to sprout, then transplant them. <gloss>The nature of the jujube is hard and woody, which is why it sprouts late and the transplanting occurs later in the season than other trees.</gloss> Space the trees one every three paces, ensuring the rows are properly aligned. <gloss>This allows for the plowing of the land between them.</gloss> One should allow cattle and horses to trample the ground beneath them to keep it clear. <gloss>The jujube is a hardy and strong tree and does not thrive when intercropped with grain seedlings; thus, if the ground becomes overgrown with weeds, pests will breed.</gloss>
On the first day of the first lunar month at sunrise, use the back of an axe to strike the trunk in a mottled pattern and pound it; this is known as "marrying the jujube" <note>original: "嫁棗" (jià zǎo); a traditional technique of bruising the bark to stress the tree, which restricts nutrient flow to the roots and encourages a heavier fruit set.</note>. <gloss>If the tree is not pounded in this way, it will flower but produce no fruit.</gloss> Wait until the large silkworms begin to enter their spinning frames <note>A phenological marker indicating late spring or early summer.</note>, then use a staff to strike between the branches to shake off the "crazy flowers" <note>original: "狂花" (kuánghuā); excessive or sterile blossoms that drain the tree's energy.</note>. <gloss>If they are not struck, the flowers will be too dense and the fruit will fail to develop.</gloss> Harvest the fruit as soon as they are completely red.

〇 **Harvesting Method:** Shaking the tree daily to let the fruit fall is the superior method. <gloss>Those harvested when only half-red will wither on the inside.</gloss>
