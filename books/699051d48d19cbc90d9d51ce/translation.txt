# Apparatus of editions and various readings of Martianus Capella, communicated to me by the Most Distinguished Cornelis Valerius Vonck

1. The Basel edition of 1532, in folio format.

3. The Grotian edition <note>The 1599 edition edited by Hugo Grotius</note> containing a variety of readings in the hand of **Nicolaus Heinsius** from the Windian Museum, which **Timmermann**, an advocate at Middelburg, prepared for Vonck. I have returned this to Geronotto.
4. The same edition, annotated by the hand of **Pieter Burman**, containing the readings of three Manuscripts. / I ought to return this to the heirs of Burman.
5. The same edition formerly from the Wielingian Museum, annotated by the hand of **Johann Friedrich Gronovius**—as Wielingius believed—where the variations of two Manuscripts are found, though they do not proceed beyond the beginning of the seventh book.
6. **Nicolaus Heinsius** also, as is illustrious, had attempted many emendations of Martianus at the end of his own copy; I am surprised that none of these appear in his *Adversaria* <note>A collection of miscellaneous notes or critiques</note> published by **Pieter Burman the Younger**, where Martianus is cited only once, as if in passing. In those same unpublished observations, that learned man sometimes mentions <unclear>our</unclear> manuscript—that one, no doubt, though it almost agrees with the Grotian one. 8. A variety of readings from the Wolfenbüttel Manuscripts excerpted by **Korte**, <unclear>Manuscript A</unclear>, which I myself had formerly shared with the Most Distinguished Vonck.

Moreover, my parts are four: letters, literature, the learned man, and learnedly. ## On the nature of letters.

BUT regarding letters, a twofold question arises. For they are either natural or artificial. By the suggestion of Nature, their names were brought forth into the substance of speech. However, their artistic formation designated <margin>alternative reading: deformed</margin> the lines which we write. This was done so that the spoken word and the written word might serve one another, the one being used when the other is absent. And so, they were called "mutes" <term>mutae: consonants or written signs that do not sound on their own</term> in the part that is written, and "vowels" <margin>alternative reading: voices</margin> in the part that is read. Indeed, the latter can be understood only by the ears, the former only by the eyes. Therefore, some letters are those which are able to produce a whole sound by themselves, while others can do nothing alone.

For as for the vowels—of which the Greeks record seven, Romulus <note>original: "Romulus"; likely referring to the early Roman tradition or the legendary founder as a proxy for the Latin language</note> six, and later usage five, with the Greek 'Y' having been rejected—these can in the Latin territory be now lengthened, now shortened, now given an acute accent, now a grave, or even a circumflex. They can be joined together or separated without losing their name. Sometimes they form syllables alone, and sometimes they receive consonants on either side. Furthermore, several vowels sometimes change into one another, or gracefully succeed one another; for instance, 'A' is frequently turned into 'E'. For *capio* <note>I take</note> becomes *cepi* <note>I took</note>; sometimes it turns into 'I', as *salio* <note>I leap</note> becomes *insilio* <note>I leap upon</note>; sometimes into 'O', as *plaustro* <note>wagon</note> becomes *plostro*; or into 'U', as *arca* <note>chest</note> becomes *arcula* <note>small casket</note>. Likewise, the letter 'E' is reformed into the first letter 'A', as *sero* <note>I sow</note> becomes *satum* <note>sown</note>; or into 'I', as *moneo* <note>I warn</note> becomes *monitus* <note>warned</note>; or into 'O', as from *tegendo* <note>covering</note> comes *toga* <note>garment</note>; or into 'U', as from *tego* <note>I cover</note> comes *tugurium* <note>hut</note>. Similarly, the vowel 'I' is also converted into 'A', as *liquis* becomes *liqua*. Into 'E', as *fortis* <note>strong</note> becomes *forte* <note>strongly/by chance</note>. Into 'O', as *qui* <note>who</note> becomes *quo* <note>by whom</note>. Into 'U', as *ibi* <note>there</note> becomes *ubi* <note>where</note>. In the same way, the letter 'O' passes into 'A', as *creo* <note>I create</note> becomes *creavi* <note>I created</note>; or into 'E', as *tutor* <note>guardian</note> becomes *tutela* <note>guardianship</note>; or into 'I', as *virgo* <note>maiden</note> becomes *virginis* <note>of the maiden</note>; or into 'U', as *volo* <note>I want</note> becomes *volui* <note>I wanted</note>. ## On the Nature of Syllables.

I believe I have sufficiently recounted what the peaks of syllables are and how they are turned. Now I will make known their lengths.

For every single syllable is either short, long, or common.

A syllable is **short** when the vowel is shortened and is not aided by any following elements, or when it ends with a shortened form or letter.

A **long** syllable, however, is produced in two ways: by Nature and by Position.

By **Nature**, when a vowel is lengthened, or when a syllable holds a circumflex accent, or an acute accent on the penultimate <note>the second to last syllable</note>; or when every single vowel is a monosyllable, or when it is found with a diphthong <note>two vowels blended into one sound</note>. Or when the first syllable, composed under another form of any word, changes neither its vowel nor its sharp accent.

By **Position**, when two consonants follow a short vowel, whether in the same syllable, or in the following one, or divided between both; or three in the other; or one double consonant.

The **double consonants** are X and Z; sometimes I and V can become double, as when I is between vowels, such as in *aio* or *Troia*. Likewise V, as in: "Was it for this, nourishing parent..." <note>original: "Hoc erat alma parens," quoting Virgil's Aeneid</note>.

However, these forms of consonants or double consonants which aid short vowels can only do so if they are enclosed within the same word. Otherwise, the consonants of the next word do not aid the preceding one, although Virgil is criticized for saying: "I sing of arms and the man, of Troy..." <note>original: "Arma virumque cano, Troiae," another reference to the Aeneid</note>.

### Concerning Common Syllables

*There are eight ways in which **COMMON** syllables <note>A "common" syllable is one that can be treated as either long or short in poetry depending on the requirements of the meter.</note> are formed. The first is when a short vowel is followed by a "liquid" consonant <note>Liquid consonants are 'l' and 'r', which allow for a smooth flow of sound.</note> attached to other consonants: for it is long, as in:* In the vast cave of the Cyclopes. <note>original: "Vasto Cyclopes in antro."</note> *Short, as in:* And the vast Cyclopes from the cliff. <note>original: "Vastosque ab rupe Cyclopas."</note>

*The second way is when a short vowel ends in a single consonant followed by the letter H: for it is long, as in:* We weary our backs with the spear. <note>original: "Terga fatigamus hasta."</note> *Short:* Whatever honor the tomb may have. <note>original: "Quisquis honos tumuli."</note>

*The third is when two consonants follow a short vowel, the first of which is the letter S: for it is long, as in:* Thick hair. <note>original: "Spissa coma."</note> *Short:* Let each man lay aside his hopes. <note>original: "Ponite spes quisque suas."</note>

*The fourth instance is when a short syllable ends a part of speech <note>i.e., the end of a word.</note>: for it is long in a comma <note>A "comma" here refers to a short phrase or a pause in a line of verse.</note>, as in:* For to you, Thymbrus, the head of Evander. <note>original: "Nam tibi Tymbre caput Euandrius."</note> *Short:* This head, O citizens. <note>original: "Hoc caput ô ciues."</note>

*Likewise, another instance occurs when a common syllable is made from a diphthong: long, as in:* The Aonian Muses. <note>original: "Musæ Aonides."</note> *Short:* In the great Ionian islands. <note>original: "Insulæ Ionio in magno."</note> *For when a vowel follows, a diphthong can become short, as in:* Or with stakes burnt at the end. <note>original: "Sudibúsve præustis."</note>

*Another instance is when another vowel follows a long vowel: for it is long:* O unhappy me, whom do you flee? <note>original: "O ego infelix, quem fugis."</note> *Short:* Under high Ilium <note>Troy</note>. <note>original: "Sub Ilio alto."</note>

*Another instance is when a pronoun ends with the letter C and is followed by a vowel: for it is long:* This was it, nourishing parent. <note>original: "Hoc erat alma parens."</note> *Short:* This man alone has turned my feelings. <note>original: "Solus hic inflexit sensus."</note>

*Another instance is when the letter Z follows a short vowel: for it is long, as in:* The spoils of the leader Mezentius. <note>original: "Mezenti ducis exuuias."</note> *Short:* Wooded Zacynthos. <note>original: "Nemorosa Zacynthos."</note>

*The nature of the three types of syllables has been discussed. It remains for the final syllables to be addressed, in which the authority of the art and the canonical form consist; but first, the noun must be treated again.*
