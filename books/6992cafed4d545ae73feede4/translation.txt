In the art of painting, there are the **Six Principles** <note>original: 六法 (*liùfǎ*). # Introductory Remarks 2

The use of <term>shading and washes</term> <note>original: 渲染 (*xuànrǎn*). The technique of applying layers of water and pigment to create depth and atmosphere.</note> in painting is incredibly subtle. It depends entirely on a quality of being light, clear, thin, and distant. When one captures this divine mystery, what can be applied to paper with a brush cannot easily be carved into a wooden block with a knife. Conversely, what is carved with a knife cannot necessarily achieve that same light, clear, and distant quality of a wash on paper. Because of this, I hesitated, sighing that my hands were tied and my skills exhausted. Could it be that Heaven and Earth intended to keep these profound principles secret and did not wish them to be passed down?

It is essential that the engraver be able to use the knife as a substitute for the brush, capturing the lively, "flying" style of the brushwork. Similarly, the printer must be able to use the <term>printing brush</term> <note>original: 帚 (*zhǒu*). Literally "broom," but here referring to the wide, flat brushes used in woodblock printing to apply ink and color washes to the blocks.</note> to create washes, capturing the light and clear method of shading. Only then can the secrets of brush and ink be transmitted to posterity.

Consequently, I searched far and wide for skilled hands. After eighteen years, I finally found the right people. We applied ourselves with great care to the engraving, and put our whole hearts into the shading and washing. At times, where the painter's brush failed to reach, the engraver’s knife succeeded. Where the engraver’s knife failed, the printer’s brush succeeded.

As a result, to produce the colors of a single image, we distinguished between what came first and what came last across dozens of separate blocks, sometimes stacking them until the pile was over a foot high. The labor for a single block involved dozens of gradations of pressure and weight; some prints took a considerable amount of time to complete. Whenever a picture was finished, not only did art lovers praise it enthusiastically upon seeing it, but even master painters marveled at the results and gave their full approval.

From the Five Dynasties period through the Song and Yuan dynasties, artists began to clearly sign their names and surnames; the Ming dynasty followed this tradition. In this edition, which gathers famous paintings, there are instances where famous individuals were asked to contribute inscribed poems, leaving no room to re-insert the artist's name. Historically, the art of painting was either passed down through family generations <note>original: "箕裘" (jīqiú); literally "sieve and fur-coat," an idiom for inheriting a father's professional legacy.</note> or transmitted through the direct bond of master and disciple <note>original: "靑藍" (qīnglán); "blue from indigo," referring to the transmission of knowledge from teacher to student.</note>. Since the first collection of this manual was published, everyone within the realm has come to understand how to paint landscapes; it has become a craft that anyone can learn and master. Next are mnemonic songs <note>original: "歌訣" (gējué); rhyming verses used to help students memorize complex painting rules.</note> so they know the principles to follow. This is followed by various "starting styles" to teach the proper way to hold the brush and begin the work <note>original: "握管" (wòguǎn); literally "grasping the tube."</note>. The lower section contains complete imitations of famous paintings from both ancient and modern times to provide models for study. A person of insight will understand these at a glance. We shall soon see women in their quarters wielding brushes and young children excelling at painting. This serves as a small contribution to our civilized age and the refinement of this peaceful era <note>original: "黼黻太平" (fǔfú tàipíng); a metaphor comparing cultural achievements to the beautiful embroidery on ritual robes.</note>.

The art of painting is not considered masterful without the technique of shading and washing <note>original: "渲染" (xuànrǎn); the application of watery washes to create depth and tone.</note>. This collection covers dozens of processes, such as collecting mineral greens, grinding cinnabar, washing blues, and applying white powder. This collection captures two types of beauty <note>Likely referring to the "refined" beauty of the scholar-official and the "popular" beauty accessible to the common person.</note>, achieving a work that can be appreciated by both the sophisticated and the common reader.

# Simple Explanations of Painting Orchids from the Qingzai Hall

Master Micao <note>original: "Micao-shi." This is a pseudonym for Wang Gai (c. 1645–1710), the primary compiler and illustrator of the *Mustard Seed Garden Manual of Painting*.</note> says:

Before every complete illustration of a species, I have researched the works of the ancients and combined them with my own ideas. One must first establish the various methods, followed by the mnemonic verses <note>original: "ge-jue." These are rhymed instructions intended to be memorized by students to help them remember complex rules of composition and brushwork.</note>, and finally the <term>starting formulas</term> <note>original: "qi-shou-shi." These are the foundational "models" or "patterns" of brushstrokes that a student must master before attempting a full composition.</note>. This structure allows the student to seek mastery in a systematic order. 

It is just like when one first begins to learn calligraphy: one must first master the basic strokes, moving from the simple to the complex, and from a single stroke or two to more than a dozen. 

Therefore, in these starting formulas—whether for flowers, leaves, or stalks—we move from few petals to many, from small leaves to large ones, and from single stalks to dense clusters. Each is organized by its category. This ensures that the beginner holds these forms in their mind and before their eyes. It is much like mastering the <term>Eight Principles of the Character 'Yong'</term> <note>original: "Yong-zi ba-fa." A fundamental theory in Chinese calligraphy stating that the eight different strokes found in the character *yong* (永, meaning "eternity") are the building blocks for all other characters.</note>; though there are thousands of characters, they do not deviate from these basic forms. In this way, the student may move from these simple explanations to a more profound pursuit, and thus progress in their craft.

# The Origins and Lineage of Painting Methods

Ink orchid painting <note>original: "Mo-lan." A style of painting orchids using only black ink, emphasizing brushwork and scholar-amateur expression over decorative color.</note> began with Zheng Suonan, Zhao Yizhai, and Guan Zhongji. In the generations that followed, there has been no shortage of masters. Generally, they are divided into two schools: the **literati**, who use the orchid to express their inner spirit, allowing a sense of untamed freedom to flow from the tip of the brush; and the **refined ladies**, who capture the plant's essence, letting a quiet and elegant grace float upon the paper. Each school achieves its own unique brilliance.

Zhao Chungu <note>original: Zhao Mengfu (1254–1322), one of the most influential painters and calligraphers in Chinese history.</note> and his son Zhongmu <note>original: Zhao Yong.</note> passed down their family's methods. Yang Buzhi and Tang Shuya were uncle and nephew, both known for their graceful style. Yang Weigan and Zhao Yizhai were contemporaries; both used the name "Zigu" <note>original: "Yang Zigu" and "Zhao Zigu."</note> and were equally skilled in orchid painting, neither being superior to the other.

Into the Ming dynasty, masters appeared in great numbers—Zhang Jingzhi, Xiang Zijing, Wu Qiulin, Zhou Gongxia, Cai Jingming, Chen Gubai, Du Zijing, Jiang Lengsheng, Lu Baoshan, and He Zhongya. It was truly a time when "ink spat forth a hundred fragrances," and "the ink-stone nourished nine hundred acres of orchids" <note>This is a literary allusion to Qu Yuan’s *Songs of Chu*, where the orchid symbolizes the purity of the scholar.</note>—a peak of artistic prosperity.

Following Guan Zhongji <note>original: Guan Daosheng (1262–1319), the most famous female orchid painter of the Yuan dynasty.</note>, many women attempted to imitate her style. By the end of the Ming dynasty, women like Ma Xianglan, Xue Susu, Xu Pianpian, and Yang Wanruo emerged. Although they were "flowers of the mist and ripples" <note>original: "yan-hua." A polite euphemism for courtesans of the pleasure quarters.</note> who applied their beauty to painting these "hidden fragrances," and some might say they "brought shame to the orchid fields," their work was nonetheless extraordinary and transcendent. They did not merely belong with the common weeds.

## The Sequential Method for Painting Leaves

The art of the orchid depends entirely on its leaves. Therefore, one must master the leaves first. A leaf must begin with a single starting stroke, following the techniques of the **nail head**, the **rat tail**, and the **mantis belly**.

*   **First Stroke:** Employs the <term>nail head</term> (a firm start), the <term>mantis belly</term> (a slightly thickened middle), and the <term>rat tail</term> (a tapering, elegant finish).
*   **Second Stroke:** Should cross the first to form a <term>phoenix eye</term> <note>original: "feng-yan." The oval-shaped negative space created by two intersecting brushstrokes.</note>.
*   **Third Stroke:** Should "break" the <term>elephant eye</term> <note>original: "po xiang-yan." A third stroke that cuts through the space of the 'eye' to prevent the composition from looking too symmetrical or static.</note>.
*   **Fourth and Fifth Strokes:** These should be added with varying bends and folds.

The base of the leaves should be wrapped at the root, using a <term>bract style</term> <note>original: "tuo-shi." Refers to the sheath-like leaves at the base of the plant.</note> that resembles a "fish head." When painting a dense cluster with many leaves, some should look down and others should look up to create a sense of life and movement. They should interweave without simply overlapping. One must recognize the difference between the **Orchid** <note>original: "Lan." Typically refers to the *Cymbidium goeringii*, which has one flower per stalk.</note> and the **Cymbidium** <note>original: "Hui." Typically refers to *Cymbidium faberi*, which has multiple flowers on a single stalk.</note>: the former is fine and supple, while the latter is coarse and strong. The fundamental methods for beginning your practice are briefly provided here.

# The Left and Right Method for Drawing Leaves

There are specific forms for drawing leaves from the left and the right. We do not say "drawing" leaves, but rather "sweeping" the leaves <note>original: "撇" (piě). This term refers to a specific calligraphic stroke that tapers to a point, emphasizing the shared technique between painting and writing.</note>, which is the same as the method used in calligraphy. When the hand moves from left to right, it is a "natural" stroke <note>original: "順" (shùn); literally "favorable" or "with the grain."</note>; when moving from right to left, it is an "inverse" stroke <note>original: "逆" (nì); moving against the natural direction of the wrist.</note>. A beginner must first master the natural stroke to gain ease in brush movement, but should gradually practice the inverse stroke until they are proficient in both. Only then is the technique considered refined and marvelous. If one is restricted to the natural stroke, the leaves will only lean to one side, which is an incomplete method.

# The Method of Density and Sparseness for Drawing Leaves

Though the leaves may consist of only a few brushstrokes, their charm should be ethereal and fluttering, like "garments of rosy clouds and pendants of moonlight" <note>original: "霞裾月珮" (xiájū yuèpèi). A poetic allusion to the flowing robes of celestial beings or immortals, suggesting the orchids have a divine, unearthly grace.</note>. They should be elegant and free, without a single hint of common or "dusty" air. In a cluster of orchids, the leaves must partially hide the flowers, and behind the flowers, more leaves must be inserted. Although they appear to grow from the same root, they must not look cluttered or chaotic. To be a master, one must achieve the state where "the intent arrives even where the brush does not" <note>original: "意到筆不到" (yì dào bǐ bù dào). A core principle in Chinese aesthetics where the viewer's mind completes a line or connection that the artist has only suggested.</note>. This requires a delicate approach.

---

The ancients could paint anywhere from three or five leaves to several dozen. A few leaves did not look pathetic or cold, and many leaves did not look like a tangled mess; they naturally achieved the proper balance between complexity and simplicity.

# The Method of Drawing Flowers

The flowers must capture various poses: bowing, looking up, facing forward, and facing away, as well as being in bud or in full bloom. The stems are inserted among the leaves, while the flowers emerge from the leaves, possessing a sense of direction and varying heights so they do not overlap or look like a string of beads. If one places leaves behind the flowers, the flowers appear nestled within the foliage; however, sometimes flowers emerge far beyond the leaves. One must not be too rigid in following rules. 

The *Hui* orchid <note>original: "蕙" (huì); refers to *Cymbidium faberi*, which typically has multiple flowers on a single tall stalk, whereas the *Lan* (Cymbidium goeringii) usually has one flower per stalk.</note> is similar to the standard orchid, but its charm is somewhat less refined. It has a single, upright stalk with flowers branching out on all four sides, blooming at different times. The stem stands straight like a person, while the heavy flowers seem to droop; each part finds its own natural posture. When painting the flowers of both the *Lan* and the *Hui*, one must avoid making the five petals look like the fingers of an open palm; instead, they must overlap and fold with a sense of bending and stretching. The petals should be light and airy, turning toward each other to reflect a mutual radiance. With long practice...

## The Method for Dotting the Heart

Dotting the "heart" <note>original: "點心" (diǎnxīn); in this context, it refers to the dark spots or the center of the orchid flower, not the food item.</note> of an orchid is like giving eyes to a beautiful woman. ## The Method for Using Brush and Ink

The Yuan Dynasty monk Jueyin <note>original: "覺隱" (Juéyǐn); the monastic name of the famous orchid painter Puming, also known as Xuechuang, active in the 14th century.</note> said: "I often paint orchids in a joyful mood, and bamboo in an angry mood." This is because orchid leaves soar and the flower buds open as if exhaling, capturing the spirit of joy. 

All beginners must first temper their brushwork. The brush should be used with a <term>suspended elbow</term> <note>original: "懸肘" (xuán zhǒu); a fundamental calligraphic technique where the arm is held entirely off the table, providing the painter with a greater range of motion and fluid, concentrated power.</note> so that the movements are naturally light and appropriate, vigorous yet rounded and lively. The use of ink must harmonize dark and light. The leaves should be dark, the flowers light. The dots of the "heart" should be dark, and the stems and buds should be light. This is the established rule. If painting from life in color, one must further know that the front of the leaves should be dark, the back light; the leaves in front dark, and those behind light. One should progress by seeking this level of detail.

This is also a valid method for painting orchids. However, if one tries to mimic exact colors and adds greens and blues, it conversely loses the natural innocence and lacks elegant charm. Nevertheless, among the various styles, it cannot be omitted. Therefore, its method is attached below.

### Formulas for Painting Orchids in Five-Character Verse

To paint an orchid, start with the <term>flicking stroke</term> <note>original: "撇" (piě); a fundamental brushstroke that starts thick and tapers to a fine point, perfectly mimicking the shape of an orchid leaf.</note>. Capture the folding and drooping of the leaves. Use positions of reclining and looking upward to naturally create "emotion" or spirit. To distinguish shapes, you must separate foreground and background with the depth of the ink.

Add flowers to complete the scene. Gather the leaves in clusters and ensure they wrap around the root. Use pale ink for the flowers; first, draw the supple stems to support them.

The petals should be oriented in different directions; capture a sense of lightness in their posture. Inside the stem, use delicate wrappings. Distinguish clearly between leaves and flowers. Use <term>dark ink</term> <note>original: "濃墨" (nóng mò); highly concentrated ink used for emphasis.</note> for the heart of the flower.

Petals face upward when fully open; they must tilt at an angle when just beginning to bloom. In clear weather, they seem to compete for the light; in the wind, they seem to smile and welcome it.

Drooping branches are like ribbons. Buds cradled in dew seem to contain a hidden fragrance. The five petals should not look like an open palm; they must resemble fingers, some curving and some extending.

Volume One, Page Fifteen

->**Outlined Flower Clusters and Stems**<-
<note>original: "鉤勒花簇莖" (gōulè huā cù jīng). This refers to the technique of using fine, uniform lines to define the silhouette of the plant before adding any color or wash.</note>

<term>Goulue: A foundational technique in Chinese painting where the artist uses a fine-tipped brush to "hook" (gou) and "trace" (le) the outlines of the subject.</term>

<column-break/>

The illustration on this page depicts the structural arrangement of orchid flowers as they gather in clusters. In the "outline" style, the artist must maintain a steady hand to ensure the stems appear both delicate and strong, supporting the weight of the blossoms.

**Flower Clusters**
<note>original: "花簇" (huā cù). This refers to the natural grouping of blossoms on a single stalk.</note>
When several flowers appear together, the brushstrokes for the stems must connect them logically to the central stalk. Each stroke should reflect the organic growth of the plant.

**Stems**
<note>original: "莖" (jīng). In orchid painting, the stem is often called the "bone" of the flower.</note>
The stems are drawn with a "centered tip" brush technique to ensure they appear round and three-dimensional, even without the use of shading or ink washes.

<term>Centered Tip (Zhongfeng): A brush technique where the tip of the brush stays in the middle of the stroke, creating a balanced and firm line.</term>
<term>Mustard Seed Garden Manual: A famous 17th-century instructional manual that standardized these botanical techniques for generations of artists.</term>

<margin>The artist should observe how the flowers overlap. The outlines must be clear so that the front and back of the cluster are easily distinguished.</margin>
