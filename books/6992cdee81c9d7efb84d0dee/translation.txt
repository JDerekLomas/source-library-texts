## General Preface to the Foot-strained Crossbow

The word <term>crossbow</term> <note>Original: *Nu* (弩).</note> means "fury" <note>Original: *Nu* (怒). In traditional Chinese philology, this is a "paronomastic gloss"—a pun used to explain the essence of a thing. The weapon's mechanical release is compared to a sudden outburst of anger.</note>. Its force is fixed and does not increase <note>Unlike a traditional bow, where the archer can vary the draw strength, a crossbow's tension is determined by its mechanical construction.</note>. For example, a common crossbow with a strength of 150 or 160 catties uses an arrow weighing no more than two <term>mace</term> <note>Original: *Qian* (錢). A small unit of weight; two *qian* is approximately 7.5 grams.</note> and is effective at a distance of fifty paces.

When released, it is accurate and powerful. However, if one greedily seeks only to shoot at great distances, it is difficult to guarantee a hit; furthermore, the force will be so weak that it cannot penetrate hard targets. As the ancients said: **"The force of a powerful crossbow at the end of its flight cannot even pierce thin silk from the state of Lu."** <note>Original: *Qiangnu zhi mo, bu neng chuan Lu gao* (強弩之末不能穿魯縞). This famous idiom from the *Records of the Grand Historian* describes a force that has exhausted its momentum and become harmless.</note> This is exactly the point.

The word <term>bow</term> <note>Original: *Gong* (弓).</note> means "exhaustion" or "reaching the limit" <note>Original: *Qiong* (窮). Like the previous page's explanation of "crossbow" as "fury," this is a play on words suggesting that the weapon represents the absolute limit of tension and energy.</note>. This is because the strength of the bow and the strength of the man are both pushed to their absolute limits. 

For example, a bow with a pull of forty <term>catties</term> <note>The text reads *xun* (勛), likely a transcription error for *jin* (斤/catties), or a specific local measure of tension. A 40-catty bow is a standard weight for a trained soldier.</note> can be used by most men. Why is it that it can fire an arrow weighing five <term>mace</term>? <note>Original: *Qian* (錢). A unit of weight; five *qian* is approximately 19 grams.</note> The arrow is two feet and three inches long. When it is notched upon the <term>bowstring</term> <note>Original: *Gongxian* (弓弦).</note>, the string is five inches away from the back of the bow. If you subtract those five inches, there remains one foot and eight inches of travel when the string is <term>pulled to the full draw</term> <note>Original: *Cheman zhi gou* (扯滿至彀). *Gou* refers specifically to the maximum arc of a drawn bow.</note>. 

Because the bow's return stroke is deep and its energy is fully spent, and because the archer adds the <term>skill of the release</term> <note>Original: *Safang zhi qiaoli* (撒放之巧力). This refers to the "snap" or "follow-through" a skilled archer provides, which a mechanical trigger cannot replicate.</note>, the arrow can still kill an enemy at a hundred paces. In contrast, when a <term>crossbow</term> is strung, there are only five inches of distance remaining until the string reaches the trigger mechanism. Thus, when the arrow is released, it only benefits from five inches of power. The <term>crossbow arms</term> <note>Original: *Nudan* (弩擔). Literally the "shoulders" or the stave of the crossbow.</note> cannot "close" as deeply as the bow does, and the operator cannot apply the "skillful force" of a manual release. Therefore, even though the static power of a crossbow may be great, the arrow it uses is actually lighter, and it cannot reach as far as the power of a bow...

### Comparative Terminology and Dimensions of the Trigger Mechanism

What is today called the **Trigger Hook** <note>original: *jigou* (機鉤)</note> was anciently called the **Tooth** <note>original: *ya* (牙)</note>. What is today called the **Rear Sight** <note>original: *zhaomen* (照門)</note> was anciently called the **Compass** <note>original: *gui* (規)</note>. What is today called the **Housing** <note>original: *xia* (匣)</note> was anciently called the **Outer Shell** <note>original: *guo* (郭)</note>. What is today called the **Trigger Lever** <note>original: *boji* (撥機)</note> was anciently called the **Hanging Knife** <note>original: *xuandao* (懸刀)</note>. What is today called the **Intermediate Sear** <note>original: *dianji* (墊機)</note> had no name in ancient times.

The **Mechanism Housing** <note>The bronze box that contains the trigger components.</note> is one <term>cun</term> <note>A traditional unit of length often called a "Chinese inch." During the Ming dynasty, 1 *cun* was approximately 3.2 centimeters.</note> and nine <term>fen</term> <note>A subunit of length equal to 1/10th of a *cun*, roughly 3.2 millimeters.</note> in length, four *fen* and a fraction in width, and five *fen* in height. 

The **Trigger Hook** has a total length of seven *fen*, and the hook itself has a total height of five *fen*. The **Rear Sight** has a total height of nine *fen*. The **String-Notch** <note>The "mouth" or opening in the hook that catches the bowstring.</note> is two *fen* deep and two *fen* wide. 

The **Intermediate Sear** <note>A piece that sits between the hook and the trigger lever to smooth the release.</note> is one *cun* and three *fen* long, four *fen* wide, and one *fen* thick. 

The diameters for the **Housing Pin-hole**, the **Hook Pin-hole**, the **Sear Pin-hole**, and the **Trigger Pin-hole** are all one *fen* and a fraction. 

The **Trigger Lever** is one *cun* and one *fen* long, four *fen* wide, and one *fen* and a fraction thick. 

The **Two Axle Pins** <note>original: *jian* (鍵); the transverse bolts that act as pivot points for the internal parts.</note> are nine *fen* long; their thickness should be sized according to the diameter of the respective holes.

## Instructions for Constructing the Crossbow Stave from Bamboo

The wood of the **Three-lobed Cudrania** <term>original: *Zhe* (柘); a hardwood traditionally prized for bow-making</term> or the **Mulberry** <note>original: *Sang* (桑)</note> is unsuitable for making the stave because it cannot withstand being kept under tension for long periods. Therefore, bamboo is used instead. The quality of bamboo varies depending on where it is grown: some possess a strong recoil while others lack it; some are rigid and do not break, while others are brittle and snap easily; some are prone to boring insects, while others are resistant.

For current purposes, select **Sprout Bamboo** <note>original: *Mengzhu* (萌竹); likely referring to a species of large timber bamboo like *Phyllostachys edulis*</note> that is over three years old. The skin should be covered in a white, powdery frost, similar in color to the powder found on the skin of a **winter melon** <note>original: *Donggua* (東瓜); the wax gourd, known for its protective white powdery coating when ripe</note>. 

The bamboo should be one foot and two inches in circumference. The nodes must be long, straight, and level—aligned as straight as the character for "one" (一)—and must not be crooked or slanted. Carefully inspect the piece from top to bottom to ensure there are no insect holes or physical damage. The thickness should be consistent throughout, without being overly large at the head or narrow in the middle. 

When split open, bamboo with thick walls is of the highest quality. One should discard the three or four dense nodes near the root end. Split the remaining length into four longitudinal pieces. Based on the size of the socket hole in the crossbow stock, calculate the required width and thickness. Finally, determine the specific measurements for the **string-notches** <term>original: *guakou* (挂口); the points where the bowstring is secured</term> and the total length required for the stave.

## On Layering and Offsetting the Bamboo Nodes

For a single stave assembly, the first, second, and third layers should all be of equal length. Select a section so that a bamboo node sits exactly in the middle; this center node is to be fitted into the **stave-socket** <note>the hole in the crossbow stock through which the bow is mounted</note>.

Even with the center node fixed, there will still be nodes toward the two ends of the slat. The first layer serves as the foundation. When preparing the second layer, you must also select a piece with a node in the center, but the nodes at its two ends must not align or overlap with the nodes of the first layer. The third layer is prepared similarly: its middle is centered, but its end nodes must not be allowed to align with those of the first or second layers. 

Follow this same principle for the fourth and fifth layers. It is vital that the nodes at the ends of every layer are spaced apart from one another; the assembly is only considered successful if they do not overlap. If the nodes are allowed to align directly on top of one another, the stave becomes brittle and will break easily at that point.

For the first layer, use bamboo from the middle of the stalk where it is slightly thinner. For the second and third layers, select and adjust the pieces accordingly. Use the thickest bamboo from the **root end** <note>the base of the bamboo culm, which is the densest and strongest part</note> for the sixth layer, which is known as the **Striking Layer** <term>original: *Dadan* (打擔); the outer-most layer of a laminated bow that provides the primary structural tension and power</term>.

Cut the pieces to their proper lengths and match them carefully to form a complete set. ## Explanation of the Sparrowhawk Staves

Historically, crossbow limbs <note>referred to here as *dan* (擔), the staves or "yokes" that form the bow</note> were constructed by joining two pieces. While the center of such a bow remains stiff and powerful, the two tips inevitably remain weak; consequently, the arrows cannot be discharged over long distances. 

In this new system, three pieces are joined at the center. By making the fourth, fifth, and sixth staves progressively longer, the power at the center and the power at the tips are rendered balanced and harmonious. 

The first, second, and third staves utilize the original thickness of the bamboo. In this design, at the center section—spanning approximately six *cun* <note>about 19.2 centimeters</note>—half of the thickness is shaved away while the other half is retained. These staves are then inserted into the limb socket <term>original: *dan yan* (擔眼); literally "stave eye," the mortise in the crossbow stock where the limbs are secured</term>. This allows the center to sit lower while the two tips are angled upward. When the bow is strung, the curvature resembles the shape of a sparrowhawk's wings spread in flight; hence, it is given this name.

## Discussion on Cords for Binding the Bow-Limbs

For binding the <term>bow-limbs</term> <note>original: *nu dan* (弩擔); the horizontal piece of the crossbow that provides the tension</note>, some people use <term>leather cotton-fluffing strings</term> <note>original: *tanhua pixian* (彈花皮弦); heavy-duty leather cords traditionally used in the mechanism of bows that aerate or "fluff" raw cotton</note>. Others use small <term>palm-fiber ropes</term> <note>original: *xiao zongsuo* (小棕索); cordage made from the tough, water-resistant fibers of the windmill palm</note>.

However, leather strings involve the use of glue in their binding. As soon as they encounter rain or dew and become damp, the glue dissolves and the leather softens. This makes the <term>tension</term> <note>original: *songjin* (鬆緊); literally "looseness and tightness"</note> inconsistent. Furthermore, they are susceptible to <term>damage by rats</term> <note>original: *shushang* (鼠傷); leather and animal glue often attracted rodents who would chew through the bindings</note>.

Nowadays, we exclusively use small palm-fiber ropes. They are not affected by rain or rats and are, moreover, quite durable.

**The Craft of Shaving Arrows**

Whenever carving arrows <term>jian (箭); in this context, the term refers specifically to the shafts of crossbow bolts</term>, use old bamboo with long sections between the nodes. If the bamboo is merely dried in the sun, it may appear dry, but after being stored for a long time, it will inevitably bend again.

The sections near the nodes at both ends that are warped <gloss>pronounced *qiao* (蹺)</gloss> should be cut away. In places where the bamboo remains curved, it must be worked further. Take the root end of the bamboo and use a saw to make a light horizontal mark as a guide. This prevents the mistake of using the root end as the arrow's tip. If the orientation is reversed, the arrow will not travel far; it will tumble <term>fan jindou (翻筋斗); literally "perform a somersault"</term> through the air and fall prematurely.

The bamboo should be split into arrow blanks, and the inner "bamboo yellow" <note>The soft, pulpy inner lining of the bamboo stalk.</note> must be shaved away before sun-drying the blanks once more. The shaft must not be thick in one section and thin in another; it is essential that it is uniform and even from the root to the tip.

After the initial shaping with a knife, use a rounding knife <term>yuanguidao (圓規刀); a specialized woodworking tool used to ensure a perfectly cylindrical profile</term> to finely scrape the surface until it is round and clean.

**Weight Ratios and Balance**

A weight of two or three <term>fen (分); a traditional unit of weight, roughly 0.37 grams</term> should be the standard ratio for the shaft. To this, add an iron arrowhead weighing four or five *fen*. Once the poison is added, the total weight will reach approximately two <term>qian (錢); also known as a "mace," equal to 10 fen or roughly 3.73 grams</term>. 

If you exceed these specifications and the **head becomes too heavy while the tail is too light**, the arrow will wobble in flight. It will not travel far, and it will lack the accuracy required to strike and kill the enemy. However, if the crossbow has a draw strength of three hundred <term>jin (斤); a unit of weight used here to measure the "power" or tension of the bow, roughly 600 grams per unit</term>, then the combined weight of the arrow and the head may be increased accordingly.

**The Method of Attaching the Iron Head**

To attach the iron head, take the bamboo tip that has already been carved. Shave the very point of the tip down until it is slightly smaller in diameter. Split this end into four even sections, roughly one inch in length. Shave away the inner core of these sections so that when the edges are pressed back together, they form a hollow socket. 

Insert the iron arrowhead into this socket. Then, use fine silk floss to wrap the head tightly, starting from the very tip and winding downward. Extend this wrapping half an inch past the point where the bamboo was split. 

**The Application of Poison**

Insert the prepared arrow into a "medicine pot" <note>A specialized vessel containing a liquid or paste toxin, often derived from aconite or snake venom.</note> to allow the poison to saturate the silk floss. 

When a person is struck by such an arrow, they will find that only the wooden shaft can be pulled out; the iron arrowhead remains lodged deep within the flesh and cannot be extracted. Once the poison on the silk comes into contact with the blood, it immediately spreads throughout the body, **"seals the throat"** <note>A historical idiom for rapid respiratory failure or paralysis caused by neurotoxic poisons.</note>, and the target dies.

# Procedures for Loading the Crossbow

Once the drawing of the string is complete, hold the head of the crossbow <note>original: "弩頭" (*nǔ tóu*), the front section where the bow limbs meet the stock.</note> with your left hand. Place the butt of the tiller <note>original: "弩稍" (*nǔ shāo*), the rear end of the wooden stock.</note> firmly below your left breast, and with your right hand, take an arrow and fit it into the arrow groove. 

The traditional rule states: **When nocking an arrow, if you do not aim at the sky, you must aim at the ground.** This is taught because if you nock the arrow while the crossbow is held level, an accidental discharge—where the string slips and the arrow flies—could injure anyone standing directly in front of you. 

If you "aim at the ground," the head of the crossbow is pointed downward while nocking; thus, if the string slips and the arrow flies, it merely strikes the earth. However, there is a concern that the arrow may slide down the groove and be lost. 

Therefore, it is better to place the butt of the tiller below the left breast with the head of the crossbow pointing upward. In this position, should the string slip and the arrow fly, it will go high into the air; this is "aiming at the sky."

# Stance and Aiming Technique

Once the **bolt** <note>original: *jiàn* (箭)</note> has been notched, step the left foot forward and brace it firmly. The right knee should be slightly bent. Hold the front of the crossbow tiller tightly with the left hand. Rest the **crossbow shoulder** <term>crossbow shoulder: the horizontal limb or the cross-piece where the bow meets the stock <note>original: *nǔ yán* (弩檐)</note></term> upon the **web of the left hand** <note>original: *zuǒ shǒu jǐng* (左手頸); literally "the neck of the left hand," referring to the fleshy area between the thumb and forefinger.</note> and push it forward.

Press the **tail-hook** <note>original: *wěi gōu* (尾勾); the rear end or butt of the crossbow stock.</note> against the palm of the right hand. Place the index finger inside the **trigger eye** <note>original: *nǔ jī yǎn* (弩機眼); the opening or guard for the trigger mechanism.</note> and wait to pull the **trigger** <term>trigger: the lever or action that releases the string <note>original: *bō jī* (撥機)</note></term>. The thumb should rest against the right side of the tiller's tail; take care not to block your vision or obstruct a careful sighting.

The middle, ring, and pinky fingers should wrap around the tail-hook and pull backward. While the left hand pushes forward and the right hand pulls back, use the opposing strength of both hands to ensure the weapon does not shake. Close the mouth and hold the breath. Squint the left eye and align the sight with the **notched sight** <note>original: *āo zì* (凹字); literally the "concave character," referring to the U or V-shaped aiming notch on the mechanism.</note>. Judge the distance and the elevation, then pull the trigger and release. 

If these steps are followed, there is nothing you cannot hit; with enough skill, you can even pierce the center hole of a copper coin. If this method is practiced until it is second nature, then even in a sudden encounter with an enemy, you will prevail. Even if the enemy wears **heavy armor** <term>heavy armor: protective military plating or mail <note>original: *zhòng kǎi* (重鎧)</note></term>, you should simply aim for the face or the **flanks** <term>flanks: the side of the body between the ribs and hip <note>original: *xié* (脇)</note></term>—those places where there is no protection. If you fire in this manner, you will surely not miss.

The ancients achieved victory over their enemies by having ten thousand crossbows fire simultaneously. Today, let us instead discuss this using a smaller number of troops as an example.

Suppose there are **three hundred crossbowmen**.

First, we employ one hundred men whose **crossbows** <note>original: *nǔ* (弩)</note> are already **spanned** <term>spanned: the act of drawing back and securing the powerful string of the crossbow <note>original: *shàng* (上)</note></term> and whose bolts are already nocked. They are arrayed at the front. This rank is designated as **"Firing the Crossbows"** <note>original: *fā nǔ* (發弩)</note>.

Next, we employ another hundred men whose weapons are also spanned and bolts nocked. They are arrayed in the second position. This rank is designated as **"Advancing the Crossbows"** <note>original: *jìn nǔ* (進弩)</note>.

Finally, we employ the remaining hundred men, arrayed at the rear. These men are currently in the process of spanning their crossbows and nocking their bolts. This rank is designated as **"Spanning the Crossbows"** <note>original: *shàng nǔ* (上弩)</note>.

Once the first hundred men in the "Firing" rank have finished their volley, they retreat to the back. The hundred men who were "Advancing" then move to the front and become the new "Firing" rank. The hundred men at the rear who were "Spanning" move forward and become the new "Advancing" rank. Meanwhile, those who just finished firing and retreated to the back now become the "Spanning" rank.

By **rotating** <note>original: *lúnliú* (輪流)</note> the discharge of bolts in this manner, the fire from the crossbows will never be exhausted.

In ancient times, seven types of timber were used for the frame; **Cudrania wood** <note>original: zhèmù (柘木); also known as Silkworm Thorn, highly prized for its elasticity</note> was considered the best, and **Wild Mulberry wood** was second. However, Cudrania is often marred by insect damage and knots, and its twisted growth makes it difficult to find pieces suitable for construction. Wild Mulberry is the same as "mountain mulberry," of which there are two varieties: the "hollow" and the "solid." 

Today, we select **Oil-mulberry** <note>original: yóusāng (油桑); likely a dense, resinous subspecies</note>; that which possesses a fine grain, a heavy weight, and a reddish-yellow color is considered superior. Its <term>spanning-force|the amount of tension the pole can exert or withstand</term> is eight hundred catties <note>approx. 480 kg or 1,060 lbs</note>, which requires the wood to be exceptionally wide and thick. 

Because the wood is so wide and thick, it is necessarily stiff and rigid rather than supple. If the initial stringing is not done according to the proper method, the pole will frequently snap or break. However, the method for stringing a new spanning-pole is consistent: 

When first stringing it, a strong man must be employed to hook it into place. Using the **waist-strap** <note>original: yāobàn (腰絆); a harness used to leverage the strength of the legs and back</note>, he must intermittently and slowly brace and kick <note>original: chēngchuài (撐踹)</note> against the pole, allowing its natural rigidity to gradually soften. Once it has been bent, use a "long string" to hook and hold it. 

Leave it set for two or three days. Then, shorten the "long string" slightly to increase the tension, re-string it, and leave it for another two or three days. By gradually drawing and bending it—working the stiffness out of the wood through repeated stress—and then tightening the string again, the pole is prepared until the string sits only about one inch away from the pole. At this point, the setting of the pole is complete.
