### *Lecture, or an introduction*
### *to all*
### *sciences.*

Since indeed all men possess an innate natural desire to know and understand the truth in all knowable things—as Aristotle <note>Aristotle (384–322 BCE) was the foundational figure for medieval and Renaissance logic and science.</note> testifies in the first book of his *Metaphysics* <note>original: "primo Metaphysicæ"</note>—it follows that whatever possesses the capacity for wonder as a whole species likewise possesses a natural desire to know and understand the truth in all things that can be known. And since all men possess this capacity for wonder as a whole species, it therefore follows that they desire this knowledge.

Therefore, desiring to be perfected in this task, it is necessary to search for the method of attempting it. Hence, the higher a subject is, the more worthy it is of being known, since it is more true because of its proximity to the most True <note>In Lullian philosophy, "the most True" refers to God, the source of all truth and being.</note>, for...

...on which account we believe truth to be the proper object of the soul. And since all truth presupposes <term>Being</term> <note>original: "esse." In this philosophical context, "Being" refers to the fundamental existence of anything that is real.</note>, it is clear that Being itself is self-evident, so much so that whoever denies Being, denies himself. Indeed, by the very act of denying it, he posits that Being exists. 

Therefore, "Being"—or the <term>Word</term> <note>original: "verbum." This likely refers to the "Logos" or the divine creative principle through which all things were made.</note>—considered as inseparable from all things, is the proper subject of this Kabbalistic wisdom. Since this Being (or Word) is the first principle that regulates all things and is not regulated by anything else, it is obvious that this wisdom is the regulator of all other sciences by a long margin. Otherwise, among those things that regulate and those that are regulated, there would be an infinite regress <note>A logical dilemma where a series of causes or justifications goes on forever without a starting point; medieval scholars argued there must be a "first cause."</note>. 

And because every doctrine and discipline essentially comprises three things—namely, knowing the parts of its subject, knowing the desired goal, and knowing the means to reach that goal—this Kabbalistic wisdom is therefore divided into three parts:

...since the human intellect is an <term>abstract substance</term> <note>original: "substantia abstracta." In medieval scholastic philosophy, this refers to a reality—like the soul or an angel—that exists independently of physical matter.</note>, it is necessary that its object of understanding be abstract, and consequently, its act of understanding must be as well. Therefore, this work is divided into three treatises. In the first of these, the parts of the <term>proper subject</term> <note>original: "subiecti adæquati." This refers to the specific, fitting scope of this particular science.</note> will be explained, along with those things most attributed to it. In the second treatise, the sought-after goal is taught. In the third and last, the means to reach that very goal will be provided.

And because every work of method is established not only so that the human intellect may exercise itself, but so that it may serve as a remedy for forgetfulness, the first treatise comprises three parts. The first part consists of the **Alphabet** and the **figures** that are utilized in this work. The second concerns the <term>essences</term> <note>original: "quiditatum," literally "what-ness." It refers to the fundamental nature or defining characteristics of the subjects being discussed.</note> of the parts of this proper subject. The third concerns the **rules** which this science employs. All of these things wonderfully resist forgetfulness.
