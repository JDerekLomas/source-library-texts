## CHAPTER II.

*That the Transposition of Letters can be either fitting and elegant, or malformed and shapeless. That meaning is often absent. What is* ANAGRAM-MAKING*, what is* Processing. *Foreign or "Barbaric" names, and their power.*

**T**o draw forth many things from a single name is a meticulous branch of Literature: to choose one name out of many, by which you may disguise and cover your own, is always pleasant, often useful, and sometimes necessary. A Name is also transformed into an ornament when it rises from the same elements <note>original: "elementis," referring to the individual letters as the "atoms" of the word.</note>, resulting in either a unique meaning, a timely tribute <note>original: "encomio," a speech or piece of writing that praises someone highly.</note>, or a most elegant expression. 

In this varied turning of words, the arrangement of letters is either Fit or Unfit. A gaping clash of consonants <note>original: "hiulcus Consonantium concursus," referring to an unpronounceable or "ugly" string of letters.</note> makes an arrangement Unfit. You should love the "Fit" kind when it possesses the power of meaning; you should tolerate it even when it does not. But in whatever way it is done, the practice is accepted among the Figures of Speech by the Grammarians, and is called <term>METAGRAMMATISM</term> <note>From the Greek 'meta' (change) and 'gramma' (letter).</note>, or <term>ANAGRAM-MAKING</term>.

## CHAPTER XII

### *The Mode and Method by which all the forms, as many as have already been counted, may be constituted. An example in two-letter and three-letter words.*

**E**nough has been said regarding the Number and the Logic of Number. The Mode and Method by which all forms may be obtained, although it requires great labor, is nonetheless achieved by wit and by certain rules which I subjoin here.

There must be established as many "orders" as there are letters, as if new words descend from each individual letter. Each letter must be placed at the beginning as many times as the remaining letters (which complete the word) vary their positions. Let **Io** <note>In Greek mythology, Io was a priestess whom Zeus turned into a heifer; her name is used here as a simple two-letter example.</note> return and serve as a two-letter <term>dissyllable<note>A word of two syllables.</note></term> example, a single heifer represented by two types of letters. Here, either **I** is placed first, and it will be **IO**; or **O** is placed first, and it will be **OI**. But because there is only one **O** after the **I** in **IO**, nothing can be formed from **IO** except **OI**.

->*The logic of letters applied to syllables, and likewise to words. Proteus. That Anagrammatism exists in military service, at the banquet, and while walking; proven by use and play.*<-
