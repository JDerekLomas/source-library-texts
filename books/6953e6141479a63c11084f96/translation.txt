# TO THE READER.

**T**he writings that have reached us under the name of Hippocrates, the so-called "Father of Medicine," occupy more than a thousand folio <note>A "folio" refers to a large book size made by folding a sheet of paper once; it indicates a very substantial volume.</note> pages in the edition by Fœsius <note>Anutius Fœsius (1528–1595) was a French physician known for his celebrated Latin translation and edition of Hippocrates' works.</note>. Those attributed to Galen are still more voluminous, embracing no less than six or eight immense folios. It may, from this plain statement, be readily conjectured how impossible must be the attempt to convey even a tolerable idea of them in the small compass of a few hundred octavo <note>An "octavo" is a standard book size, smaller than a folio, roughly the size of a modern hardcover novel.</note> pages. A perfect comprehension of the full value of both these literary and scientific works can only be attained by referring to the original writings or to a complete translation.

With the exception of a few of the Hippocratic treatises, an English translation has never appeared. Of the writings of Galen, not one has received that form for the benefit of the English reader. And yet the names of both these great men are familiar to our ears, as though they were the daily companions of our medical researches. Our teachers refer to them *from the chair of authority* <note>original: "ex cathedra"</note>; our books continually quote them; and yet, not

# TO THE READER.

Not one in a hundred members of the medical profession—at least in America—has ever seen these works. If questioned, they could not even say what subjects the books address.

From what has been said, it will be clear to every reflecting mind that the editor's only goal is to provide a brief overview of the subject matter found in the extensive treatises of these venerable writers. This overview is admittedly too slight to provide even a partial idea of a tenth of their merits; yet, he hopes it is enough to encourage further research into their interesting pages. It is not a credit to the medical profession, either in Great Britain or America, that a full translation of both these authors has never yet been provided to the English reader! Furthermore, in America at least, even in the original Greek or the Latin translation, so few copies are to be found. Meanwhile, hundreds of contemptible works are published annually, cluttering our shelves only to be forgotten. 

Few authors today follow the Roman poet Horace’s important rule: "Let it be kept back until the ninth year" <note>original: *Nonum prematur in annum*; a piece of advice from Horace's *Ars Poetica* suggesting that writers should wait years before publishing to ensure their work is polished and mature</note>. Scarcely has a student graduated from his "nourishing mother" <note>original: *alma mater*; the university or college one attended</note> when he considers himself qualified to become an author. He immediately presents the world with a "learned" work, stolen from the *Dictionary of Medical Sciences* <note>original: *Dictionnaire des Sciences Médicales*</note> or some similar publication. He writes on diseases he has never actually seen, yet quotes ancient authorities as if they were as familiar to him as his own household gods!

The pleasure I have experienced in reviewing the writings of these pioneers of medicine has led me to believe that even this imperfect presentation may be welcomed by many. This is especially true since few people are likely to own these works in their complete and perfect form. However, it is necessary to repeat that one should not judge the whole of their work by this incomplete summary—

# TO THE READER.

It would be like someone who judged the character of a building by examining a single brick that formed a tiny fraction of it. I have, therefore, to request all due allowance for this attempt to introduce to my contemporaries a few faint traces of their medical ancestors <note>original: "progenitors"</note> who lived two thousand years before them.

---

To enable the reader to judge the difference of opinion that has existed regarding the writings that have reached us under the name of Hippocrates—amounting to nearly seventy in number—I have provided the arrangements used by three editors: namely, Foës <note>original: "Fœsius," a 16th-century French physician known for his Greek-Latin edition of Hippocrates</note> and Haller in their Latin translations, and Gardeil in a French one. By comparing these, it will be seen that no complete agreement exists between them. One thing alone seems evident: out of these seventy treatises, only about twelve or fourteen are uniformly attributed to this illustrious man. The others are variously ascribed to his son, his son-in-law, or to writers who lived before or after him. 

The arrangement I have followed myself is that of Foës; not from any particular preference, but from the accidental circumstance of his edition being the first that came into my possession, long before I even knew that the others had translated these writings. My copy of Foës bears the date of 1624; that of Haller, 1775; and that of Gardeil, 1801. Since then, an edition has been produced by Kühn in 1825, in which he chiefly follows Foës with few alterations. Foës provides abundant notes on nearly every treatise, besides forty or fifty pages of "**various readings**" <note>alternative interpretations of the original Greek text</note> from numerous commentators; however, these would apply only to a full translation and are therefore altogether unsuitable for the nature of this work.

# TO THE READER.

As this abstract was originally formed for personal convenience, and without the remotest view of ever committing it to the press, I have additional reason for requesting the indulgence of my readers for any errors they may find therein. I am now too far advanced in life to again retrace the immense folios <note>original: "folios"; large books made of sheets of paper folded once, often used for substantial scholarly or legal works</note>, which, thirty years ago, afforded me so much pleasure, but which at present I can merely recur to for occasional reference; and I regard this outline but as a pioneer, to aid perhaps the labors of a younger and more accomplished translator of the entire work.

->JOHN REDMAN COXE.<-

Philadelphia, Sept. 16, 1846.
