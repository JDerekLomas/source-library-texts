# Illustrated Investigation of the Names and Realities of Plants

<note>original: "植物名實圖考" (Zhiwu Mingshi Tukao). This title reflects a deep-rooted Chinese intellectual tradition of "rectifying names"—ensuring that the names found in ancient literature correctly match the physical "realities" of the plants as they exist in nature.</note>

<note>This work was authored by Wu Qijun (1789–1847), a high-ranking government official and scholar-scientist of the Qing Dynasty. It remains one of the most important pre-modern botanical texts, featuring detailed illustrations and descriptions of 1,714 different plants, categorized by their uses and characteristics.</note>

## Barnyard Millet

The *Famine Relief Herbal* <note>original: "救荒本草" (Jiuhuang Bencao), a Ming dynasty work dedicated to plants that can be eaten during times of starvation.</note> states: Water millet grows along the edges of rice paddies, while dry millet grows in fields and the wilderness. The seedlings and leaves resemble <term>finger millet</term> <note>original: "穇子" (shēnzi), another hardy cereal grain.</note>; the leaves are deep green, and the stalks near the base often have a purple tint. At the tips of the stalks, it produces flat ears of grain that bear seeds about the size of broomcorn millet. They are a tea-brown color, slightly bitter in taste, and "mildly warm" in their medicinal nature. One may harvest the seeds and pound them to remove the husks, then cook them as porridge. They are even better when steamed. Alternatively, they can be ground into flour for various foods.

The Farmer of Yulou <note>A pseudonym used by the author to provide expert agricultural commentary.</note> says: Barnyard millet can easily be confused with rice seedlings. There are two varieties: one with round ears like broomcorn millet, and another that is flat with several ears growing together. If it is threshed together with rice, the result is a messy mixture with the husks still attached; however, if it is processed separately with a pestle, the grains are white and fine. When boiled into porridge, it is smooth and delicious. In the North, it is often planted on the ridges between fields. It is not to be looked down upon as a mere weed like <term>foxtail or darnel</term> <note>original: "稂莠" (lángyǒu), a classic metaphor for "weeds" or "useless people" that resemble good grain but produce nothing.</note>. 

The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary and encyclopedia, dating back over two millennia.</note> mentions the terms *tí* and *dié*; the commentary explains these are varieties that resemble barnyard millet but grow low to the ground as a "foul weed." Furthermore, an ancient poem says, "The reeds and the millet lean upon one another." Thus, we can distinguish that the *tí* variety is the barnyard millet of the dry lands, while the standard variety is the millet of the marshes. The *Complete Treatise on Agricultural Administration* <note>original: "農政全書" (Nongzheng Quanshu), a monumental 17th-century agricultural manual by Xu Guangqi.</note> earnestly encourages the planting of barnyard millet as a precaution against unforeseen disasters. Such foresight truly reflects the compassionate heart of a benevolent person!

## Smooth-headed Barnyard Millet

The stems and leaves of the **Smooth-headed Barnyard Millet** <note>original: "光頭稗子" (guāngtóu bàizi). The name refers to the lack of awns or bristles on the grain head.</note> are entirely the same as those of <term>wild rice</term> <note>original: "茭菰" (jiāogū), *Zizania latifolia*. While today this is often grown for its edible gall-infected stem, historically it was also recognized for its grass-like appearance.</note>. It grows on dry land. The flower spikes emerge from within the leaves; they are flat, clean, and hairless, which is why it is given the name "Smooth-headed."

When cooked, it is fragrant and delicious. The variety known as **water barnyard millet** <note>original: "水稗" (shuǐbài)</note> resembles common rice and grows in flooded paddy fields. This is likely what the <term>Huainanzi</term> <note>A 2nd-century BCE philosophical and encyclopedic text.</note> refers to as the "Li" <note>original: "離" (lí)</note> plant, which matures earlier than the rice crop. Those that grow on dry, uncultivated land are known as "Ti" <note>original: "稊" (tí), a term often used in classical literature to describe small, weed-like grasses or tares.</note>. Could it be that this plant is indeed that very same variety?

# Muzi (Finger Millet)

The *Materia Medica for Famine Relief* <note>original: "救荒本草" (Jiùhuāng Běncǎo), a 15th-century guide to edible wild plants for survival during famine</note> states that the Muzi <note>The Finger Millet (*Eleusine coracana*)</note> grows in paddy fields and damp ground. Its seedlings and leaves resemble those of rice, though they are somewhat shorter. At the tip of the stalk, it produces seed heads that look like barnyard millet. The seeds themselves are about the size of broomcorn millet grains and are a brownish-red color with a sweet flavor. To prepare it, one harvests the seeds, pounds them into grain to cook as porridge, or grinds them into flour for steaming into bread. 

In the mountains of Guizhou, many people plant "Eagle-Claw Barnyard Millet," which is also called Muzi. In Yunnan, it is known as "Duck-Palm Barnyard Millet." It is quite similar to what farmers call "Muzi-millet." Upon examining the botanical records, one sees that the seed heads emerge in clusters of varying lengths, like a hand with large and small fingers; perhaps this is why it earned the name *Cancan* <note>original: "穇穇" (cǎncǎn), an onomatopoeic or descriptive name for Finger Millet in various dialects</note>? 

According to the *Enlarged Register of the Fragrant Flowers* <note>original: "廣羣芳譜" (Guǎng Qúnfāng Pǔ)</note>, it is also called "Dragon-Claw Millet" or "Duck-Claw Barnyard Millet." In the northern regions, it is planted on wild slopes. The seedlings and leaves resemble foxtail millet. When it reaches its full height, a three-sided stalk emerges from the neck of the plant, blooming with clusters of tiny flowers. The seed heads then split into several branches shaped exactly like an eagle's claw; the description is exceptionally vivid. 

The *Rizhao County Gazetteer* notes that Muzi is considered the lowliest of the millets. It comes in two varieties, black and white, and is well-suited for wet soil. One picul <note>a traditional dry measure of volume</note> of the unrefined grain yields about two *dou* <note>roughly 20 liters</note> of edible rice. The common people rely on this crop for their very survival. Furthermore, the *Three Gorges Gazetteer* claims the plant originated in Yunnan and calls it the "Cloud..." <note>The text cuts off here, likely referring to "Cloud Millet" or a similar regional name</note>.

### Southern Barnyard Millet (Nanbai)

**Southern Barnyard Millet** <note>original: "南稗" (Nanbai)</note>, also known as **Wild Goose Claw Barnyard Millet**, is also called <gloss>Dragon Claw Millet</gloss>. The price of its grain is competitive [with other staples]. It was once said that this crop did not exist in the Southeast, likely because the stony fields within the mountain gorges were considered too difficult for planting superior seeds. However, when I passed the embankments of the **Zhang and Gong Rivers** <note>The Zhang and Gong rivers meet in Ganzhou, Jiangxi province, in Southeastern China</note>, the harvest was extremely abundant. Golden clouds of grain stretched across the fields, so plentiful they could hardly be gathered in time. <gloss><unclear>It is also found in Guangdong.</unclear></gloss> How then can it be said that the Southeast lacks this plant?

The mountains of **Guizhou** <note>original: "黔" (Qián), the abbreviated name for Guizhou province</note> are treacherous and barren, much like the terrain of the gorges, yet from the banks of the streams to the very peaks of the mountains, this crop is planted almost everywhere. In the autumn, when the seed heads ripen, their reddish-brown and green colors weigh heavily over the paths. Those that grow side-by-side look like the palm of a hand; those that are curved look like a clenched fist. 

Because it requires little labor to cultivate yet yields a harvest that fills many carts, the people rely on it as their very lifeblood. ## Mountain Black Bean

<note>original: 山黑豆 (Shan Hei Dou). This refers to various species of wild legumes, likely *Glycine soja* (wild soybean) or similar vining pulses found in the wild across East Asia.</note>

The *Famine Relief Herbal* <note>original: 救荒本草 (Jiuhuang Bencao). A seminal 15th-century botanical work by Zhu Xiao, a Ming dynasty prince, intended to help people identify edible wild plants during famines.</note> states that the Mountain Black Bean grows in the mountains and wilds of Mi County <note>A county in Henan Province, China.</note>. The sprouts resemble those of the domestic black bean. The leaves grow in clusters of three; the center leaf is large, resembling a common vegetable bean leaf, while the two side leaves are more similar to cultivated black bean leaves and are slightly rounded.

The plant produces small, pinkish flowers. The resulting pods are much thinner and smaller than those of domestic black beans, and the beans themselves are also extremely tiny. The taste is slightly bitter. When the sprouts and leaves are tender, they can be harvested and <term>blanched</term> <note>original: 煠 (zha). In this particular county, there was a minor drought at the time, but it did not become a disaster, nor were there other strange events. # Bitter Horse Bean

The *Famine Relief Herbal* states that the **Bitter Horse Bean** grows in the countryside of Yanjin County and is found widely in various locations. The plant reaches a height of about two feet. Its stems resemble those of the <term>Astragalus (黃芪): a common medicinal herb in the legume family</term>, and the stems are covered in fine, delicate hairs. The leaves are similar to broad bean leaves but slightly smaller, and also resemble puncturevine leaves, though they are larger.

Between the branches and leaves, it produces **reddish-purple flowers**. It bears seed pods roughly the size of a thumb-tip; because the pods are largely hollow and inflated, they are colloquially known as **"Sheep's Urine Bladders."** Inside the pods are seeds the size of mung beans, which are tea-brown in color. Both the seeds and the leaves have a bitter flavor.

**Preparation for consumption:**
To prepare the leaves, boil them until soft, then soak them in frequently changed water to extract the bitter compounds. Once cleaned, they can be tossed with oil and salt for eating. To prepare the seeds, soak and wash them in water to remove the bitterness, then sun-dry them. They can then be ground or pounded into flour to make baked flatbreads or steamed buns.

According to observations in the Pingxi region of Shanxi, the plant is also prevalent there. In those areas, the flowers resemble pea blossoms and are a vivid red. The fruits are hollow and thin, appearing in clusters of ten or more. The seeds within are quite small, and one often finds insects lurking or lying dormant inside the pods. Due to its unpleasant odor, the local people there call it the **"Horse Fart Bladder."** When <term>famine (饑饉): a period of extreme food scarcity</term> strikes, people resort to gathering this plant. However, like a <note>original: "枯魚銜索" (kūyú xián suǒ) — literally "a dried fish hanging on a rope." This classical idiom refers to a person or resource in a desperate, precarious state that is slowly being depleted.</note>, how can such a supply not be completely exhausted?

# Job's Tears

*(Illustration: A detailed woodcut showing the Job's Tears plant, featuring its long, sword-shaped leaves and clusters of hard, bead-like seeds at the tips of the stems.)*

The **Job’s Tears** plant is a tall, grass-like perennial. In traditional botanical study, it is categorized among the **Cereal Crops** due to its edible seeds, though it holds a dual status as a vital medicinal herb. 

**Physical Characteristics:**
The plant is distinguished by its long, tapering leaves and its unique fruit. The seeds are encased in a naturally hard, polished, pearly-white or grayish shell. Because of their shape and natural hole, these seeds have historically been used not only for food but also as beads for necklaces and rosaries.

**Medicinal and Culinary Use:**
In the *Compendium of Materia Medica*, Job's Tears are noted for their "sweet and mild" flavor. They are often simmered into porridges or soups. Medicinally, the grain is used to "leach out dampness," a traditional concept referring to the removal of excess fluids or puffiness in the body, and to strengthen the functions of the spleen and stomach.

# River Cereal
<note>original: 川穀, *Chuāngǔ*; a wild variety of Job's Tears (*Coix lacryma-jobi*)</note>

According to the **Famine Relief Materia Medica** <note>original: *Jiuhuang Bencao*, a famous Ming dynasty text detailing edible wild plants for survival during crop failures</note>, the **River Cereal** grows in the wild fields of **Fanshui County** <note>located in modern-day Henan Province</note>. The seedlings reach a height of three to four feet. The leaves resemble those of young **kudzu** or **sorghum** <note>original: 葛秫, *géshú*</note>, though they are slightly smaller. 

Small clusters of yellow-white flowers bloom between the leaves. The plant bears seeds that resemble **Job’s Tears** <note>original: 草珠兒, *cǎozhū'ér*, literally "grass beads"</note>, though they are quite small. The flavor of the seeds is sweet. 

**Preparation for Consumption:**
To prepare the seeds, harvest them and pound them to remove the husks, turning them into a grain. When using them raw, first rinse them clean with cold water. Afterward, scald them with boiling water three to five times, draining the water each time <note>this process likely removes tannins or bitterness found in the wild variety</note>. They can then be placed in a pot and cooked as either porridge or steamed rice; both are suitable for eating. These seeds are also capable of being used to **brew wine**.

# Mountain Flat Bean

The **Mountain Flat Bean** <note>a wild legume commonly found in grassy slopes and fields</note> grows in the wild fields and wilderness. The stems of the plant trail along the ground, reaching lengths of one to two feet. 

The leaves are very small and grow in pairs, densely arranged along the leaf stalk to resemble the shape of a feather. They are quite similar to the leaves of the **sensitive plant** <note>original: 呼草, *hūcǎo*; a plant known for its leaves closing when touched</note>, though they do not react to touch in the same way. 

The plant produces small, pale yellow flowers. It bears seed pods that are extremely thin and flat, resembling the shape of a domestic **flat bean** <note>original: 扁豆, *biǎndòu*; refers to the common hyacinth bean, *Lablab purpureus*</note>, but significantly smaller in size. 

**Preparation for Consumption:**
To prepare this plant for food during a famine, harvest the **young, tender leaves** and shoots. First, scald them in boiling water. Afterward, soak and rinse them thoroughly in clean water to remove any bitterness or tough textures. Once cleaned, the leaves may be seasoned with oil, salt, and ginger, and then eaten as a cooked vegetable.

## Chickpea
<note>original: 回回豆, *Huíhuí dòu*; literally "Muslim bean," referring to the *Hui* people and the plant's introduction from Central or West Asia via the Silk Road. Scientifically known as *Cicer arietinum*.</note>

It grows in wild fields. The stems are green, and the leaves resemble those of the **puncture vine** <note>original: 蒺藜, *jílí*; *Tribulus terrestris*</note>; they also look like the young, tender leaves of the **honey locust** <note>original: 皁莢, *zàojiá*</note>, but they possess fine serrations along the edges. It opens five-petaled, pale purple flowers that look similar to those of the **goosefoot** plant <note>original: 漢藜, *hànlí*</note>. It produces pods that are shaped like **almonds** <note>original: 杏仁, *xìngrén*</note> but are plumper. Inside are beans that resemble **morning glory seeds** <note>original: 牽牛子, *qiānniúzǐ*</note> but are slightly larger. The taste is sweet. Gather the beans and boil them to eat.

## Chickpea
<note>original: 回回豆, *Huíhuídòu*; literally 'Muslim bean,' referring to the Hui people and the plant's introduction from Central or West Asia. Scientifically known as *Cicer arietinum*.</note>

According to the *Famine Relief Herbal* <note>original: 救荒本草, *Jiùhuāng Běncǎo*; a 15th-century treatise on plants edible during famines, authored by the Ming prince Zhu Xiao</note>, the **Chickpea** is also known as the **Nahe bean** <note>original: 那合豆, *Nàhédòu*; a phonetic transcription of the Persian word for chickpea, *nakhud*</note>. It grows in wild fields. The stems are green, and the leaves resemble those of the **puncture vine** <note>original: 蒺藜, *jílí*; *Tribulus terrestris*</note>; they also look like the young, tender leaves of the **honey locust** <note>original: 皁莢, *zàojiá*; *Gleditsia sinensis*</note>, but they possess fine serrations along the edges. It opens five-petaled, pale purple flowers that look similar to those of the **goosefoot** plant <note>original: 漢藜, *hànlí*; a species of *Chenopodium*</note>. It produces pods that are shaped like **almonds** <note>original: 杏仁, *xìngrén*</note> but are plumper. Inside are beans that resemble **morning glory seeds** <note>original: 牽牛子, *qiānniúzǐ*</note> but are slightly larger. The taste is sweet. Gather the beans and boil them to eat.

# Wild Broomcorn Millet
<note>original: 野黍, *yěshǔ*; likely referring to *Eriochloa villosa*, commonly known as hairy cupgrass, or a wild variety of *Panicum miliaceum*.</note>

Wild Broomcorn Millet grows in the fields and wilderness of the north. The *Famine Relief Herbal* <note>original: 救荒本草, *Jiuhuang Ben Cao*; a 15th-century handbook for surviving famine by identifying edible wild plants.</note> records it. The grains are sparse and the plant develops ears early; once the fruit is ripe, it falls easily.

The Farmer of Yulu <note>original: 雩婁農, *Yúlú Nóng*; the pseudonym of He Gang (何剛), a Ming dynasty scholar known for his agricultural commentaries.</note> says: I have heard from the country folk that all cereal grains have wild counterparts. Their sprouts are short and their grains are thin. However, if one plants them in fertile soil, their husks and seeds become no different from the domesticated crops in the fields. 

In the primordial past, when the world was first opening, these "excellent seeds" <term>嘉種 (jiāzhǒng): high-quality seeds selected for cultivation</term> were merely wild plants growing amidst the mud and thickets, with no distinction between them. When the Sages appeared, they tasted these plants and recognized their benefits to humanity. Thus, they began the work of clearing the land, tilling the soil, and cultivating the crops. Through the labor of moving, scattering, moistening, and warming the earth, they created the furrows of the fields <note>original: 畎畝, *quǎnmǔ*</note>. They dug ditches and channels to provide irrigation <note>original: 灌漑, *guàngài*</note>, and built dams and dikes to protect against flooding. The arduous task of providing food for the people was surely not achieved by the strength of a single person! 

In later generations, when faced with the calamities of floods or droughts <note>original: 祲, *jìn*; referring to ominous weather or bad auras leading to disaster.</note>, people anxiously search for these wild grains to save the survivors <note>original: 孑遺, *jiéyí*</note> of the famine. Alas! When the mountains and rivers are parched and no green grass remains in the wild, then even these would be seen as a bounty.

How can it only be the virtue of <gloss>nourishing</gloss> the world? Perhaps it is born as a blessing to answer the prayers of those struggling for breath, or it sprouts branched ears to supplement the needs of those in desperate poverty. This <gloss>bestowed</gloss> benevolence has never paused for a single moment. Even when the calamities of fate arrive, the Creator can do nothing to stop it. 

The <gloss>drought</gloss>-resistant benevolence of these plants provides relief without fail. The prognostications found in the *Farmer’s Five Elements* <note>original: 田家五行, *Tiánjiā Wǔxíng*; a famous Ming-era collection of folk wisdom and weather lore for farmers.</note> are often verified here. If one diligently observes the seasons and applies effort, the manifestations will be clear and without <gloss>error</gloss>. Even the ancient arts of tortoise shell and yarrow stalk divination <term>龜筮 (guīshì): traditional methods of divination used to predict the future</term> cannot match the clarity of these agricultural signs. 

When I lived in the countryside, the spring rains were sufficient, but the summer rains were frequently delayed and <gloss>depleted</gloss>. What I harvested with my own eyes was not even double what was originally sown. In the middle of the sweltering heat, I happened to rest at a farmhouse and saw that the threshing floor and gardens were already filled with <gloss>bundled</gloss> stacks of grain. 

When I asked the reason, the farmer said: "Among the rice varieties, there is one called 'Six-Month Ridge' <note>original: 六月稜, *Liùyuèlèng*; an early-ripening rice cultivar known for its ability to mature quickly before the peak of summer droughts.</note>. It is planted early and harvested quickly. Its grains are <gloss>coarse</gloss>, but they are plentiful and store well for years." 

In recent years, many farming families have lost their livelihoods because of the summer droughts. I, however, plowed when there was even a "foot of moisture" <note>original: 尺澤, *chǐzé*; a metaphor for minimal irrigation or the smallest amount of rain sufficient for planting.</note>, and the crops matured by the end of summer. While those praying for rain beat their drums loudly on the ridges with their straw dragons <term>芻龍 (chúlóng): ceremonial dragons made of straw used in folk rituals to summon rain</term> and willow wreaths, I heard the sighs of the people as they harvested my grain in the scorching sun. The wages for hired laborers were low, and the stalks and straw suffered no damage. Although the harvest was modest, at least the markets were not "red" <note>original: 市無赤, *shì wú chì*; a reference to the 'red' parched earth of famine; meaning the markets were not empty of food.</note>.

# Notes, Volume 14

The price of rice is currently soaring with no end in sight. Compared to times when grain was so abundant it lay scattered and wasted <note>original: 粒米狼戾, *lìmǐ lánglì*; an idiom describing grain spilled everywhere like wolf-droppings, signifying an extreme surplus that leads to waste.</note> because no one would buy it, the current profit margin for sellers has increased nearly five-fold. 

Alas! It is remarkable how the labor of a single skilled farmer can rival the natural cycles of abundance and scarcity dictated by the Creator. If this is so, then those who rule the people should seek out those with a profound understanding of farming to serve as agricultural supervisors <term>田畯 (tiánjùn): an ancient title for local officials who managed farming affairs and encouraged the peasantry</term>. 

They should also search widely for diverse seed varieties, observing how they respond to the inconsistencies of environmental forces <gloss>Yin and Yang</gloss> and the fluctuations of cold and heat, and then adjust their cultivation accordingly. This would cause the people to pursue their seasonal tasks with the same urgency as if they were "fighting a fire or chasing a fugitive," effectively uprooting their indolent and shiftless <note>original: 呰窳, *zǐyǔ*; a classical term for laziness, specifically referring to those who are careless in their work and merely survive day to day.</note> habits. Would this not be sufficient to prepare for the one-in-ten-thousand chance of a crop failure or disaster during the harvest <term>穡 (sè): a formal term for the act of reaping or harvesting grain</term>?

Xu Yuanhu <note>original: 徐元扈, *Xú Yuánhù*; the courtesy name of Xu Guangqi (1562–1633), a renowned Ming dynasty scientist and author of the *Complete Treatise on Agricultural Administration*.</note> said:

> Barnyard grass <note>original: 稗, *bài*; often considered a weed in rice paddies, but here praised as a hardy survival crop.</note> yields a large harvest and is suited to both floods and droughts. One should select the best seeds and plant them in the lower fields. In years of disaster, they can then be planted extensively. This is far better than becoming a displaced refugee, wandering and scavenging for scraps.

I also believe that those who manage the household must seek out grain seeds capable of enduring both flood and drought in advance. One should observe the elevation of the land—whether high or low—and plant several different plots. Do not be negligent or careless simply because a particular yield seems thin. In a good year, all crops will flourish together; in a bad year, surely they will not all fail together. Is this not better than being forced to gather self-sown grain <term>穭 (lǚ): grain that grows wild or "volunteers" from seeds dropped in previous years</term> and weeds because there is no grain to harvest? 

Yet, if someone says that some families are capable of this while others are not, I say: one must first rid themselves of greed.

# Oats

**Oats** <note>original: 燕麥, *yanmai*, literally "swallow wheat"</note> grow mostly in abandoned or fallow lands and are distinct from **sparrow-wheat** <note>雀麥, *quemai*; likely a species of brome grass</note>. The *Materia Medica for Famine Relief* <note>救荒本草, *Jiuhuang Bencao*, a famous 15th-century herbal</note> distinguishes between them very clearly. The *Praises of Wild Vegetables* <note>野菜贊, *Yecai Zan*</note> states that they have small grains which can be made into porridge. Their stalks are slender and long, making them excellent for weaving hats; for this reason, those in the northern regions who make a living from straw-hat making plant them.

**The Farmer of Yulou** <note>雩婁農, *Yulou Nong*; the pen name of the author, Wu Qijun</note> says: 

How great is the suffering of the people living on barren soil! The *Treatise on Curiosities* <note>博物志, *Bowu Zhi*</note> claims that eating oats makes a person’s bones soft. The *Materia Medica for Famine Relief* also records this, suggesting that oats are merely a means to "keep one out of the ditches" <note>An idiom meaning to prevent one from starving to death and being tossed into a mass grave</note>. 

However, the *Gazetteer of Lijiang Prefecture* <note>麗江府志, *Lijiang Fu Zhi*</note> records that oat flour is used to make **dry rations** <term>original: 乾餱, *ganhou*; parched grain or travel rations</term>, which are mixed with water for consumption; this serves as a year-round staple for the local people. The region of **Weixi** <note>Located in modern-day Yunnan province</note> is bitterly cold, and its people perform heavy physical labor, yet have they ever suffered from "diseased feet" <note>Referring back to the claim of "soft bones" or perhaps nutritional deficiencies like beriberi</note>? 

The insects that live on smartweed and the borers that live in cinnamon are born to those plants and find them sweet; how could a bird know the plants' pungency? Those who indulge in fine wine and rich meat, living in a drunken stupor, are perhaps "fortunate" that they were not born in icy caves or under snowy skies, where they would have to fill their "abyss of desire" <note>A metaphor for the stomach or worldly hunger</note> with such humble food. 

However, to live in a daze and die in a dream, becoming as **fat and sleek** <term>original: 肥腯, *feitu*; well-fed livestock</term> as a pig in a latrine or a sheep in a pen—oblivious to the world and inviting calamity through the wasteful destruction of nature's gifts—that too is a form of misfortune.

# Broad Beans
<note>original: 胡豆, *hudou*; literally "foreign bean." This name usually refers to the Broad Bean (*Vicia faba*), also known as the fava bean, which was introduced to China via the Silk Road during the Han dynasty.</note>

**Broad beans** are recorded in the *Materia Medica for the Relief of Famine* <note>original: 救荒本草, *Jiuhuang Bencao*; a 15th-century guide for identifying edible wild plants during crop failures</note>. The beans can be boiled for food or ground into flour. The *Supplement to the Materia Medica* <note>original: 本草拾遺, *Bencao Shiyi*</note> states: "**Broad bean** seeds grow in the wild fields; they are often found among rice grains, though the text does not describe their physical appearance." This likely refers to the plant discussed here.

The author of the agricultural text *Rape-seed* <note>original: 蕓薹, *Yuntai*</note> says: "The **broad beans** of today are a wild variety, not the **broad beans** of antiquity." However, if we examine the *Erya* <note>original: 爾雅; the oldest surviving Chinese dictionary</note>, the commentary on the term **"Great Pulse"** <note>original: 戎菽, *rongshu*</note> identifies it as the modern **broad bean**. Both the *Expanded Dictionary* <note>original: 廣雅, *Guangya*</note> and the *Essential Arts for the People’s Welfare* <note>original: 齊民要術, *Qimin Yaoshu*</note> distinguish the **broad bean** as a separate species from the **soybean**.

Furthermore, the preface to the *Supplementary Records of Famous Physicians* <note>original: 名醫別録, *Mingyi Bielu*</note> notes: "The **broad bean** is currently the **'green-spotted bean.'**" These are beans with green markings found among both **soybeans** and **rice beans**. Generally, in ancient times, many plants like **sesame** <note>original: 胡麻, *huma*; literally "foreign hemp"</note> and **cucumber** <note>original: 胡瓜, *hugua*; literally "foreign melon"</note> were prefixed with the character "foreign" <note>original: 胡, *hu*</note>, but today they are called by different names. Since **sesame** is now specifically identified as a variety from Shanxi, and the **broad bean** grows wild in the fields, it is truly difficult to determine exactly which modern bean corresponds to the **broad bean** of the ancients. 

The *Expanded Dictionary* defines the **broad bean** as the **"lulu"** <note>original: 𧀮𧀮</note>. **Li Shizhen** <note>the primary author of this Compendium</note> uses the double-fingered shape of **cowpea** <note>original: 豇豆, *jiangdou*</note> pods to interpret the term **"lulu."** Finally, the *Study of the Nine Grains* <note>original: 九穀考, *Jiugukao*</note> suggests that based on the notes by **Guo Pu** <note>a famous 3rd-century commentator</note>, the **broad bean** might indeed be the modern variety.

The identification of **peas** <note>original: 豌豆, *wandou*; *Pisum sativum*</note> also follows the theories of Li <note>referring to Li Shizhen, the famous 16th-century pharmacologist and author of the *Bencao Gangmu*</note>. Scholars have often speculated on names like *tichuo* <note>original: 蹄踔; an archaic term for peas, possibly describing the "tripping" or climbing nature of the vine</note> based merely on the sound of the words or the shape of the characters. However, the *Guangya* <note>original: 廣雅; an early 3rd-century Chinese dictionary</note> provides distinct explanations for both the **broad bean** and the **pea**. 

Because different regions use various characters and dialects, determining which historical record is "correct" or "incorrect" is nearly impossible, as the evidence is simply missing. 

According to the *Travelogue of Yunnan and Guizhou* <note>original: 滇黔紀遊, *Dian Qian Jiyou*</note>, the plant known as **"Great Pulse"** <note>original: 戎菽, *rongshu*; an ancient term for large beans</note> in Taihe <note>a region in modern-day Yunnan province</note> is harvested before the new year. The local people there call it the **"Great Wan Bean."** This plant is, in fact, the **silkworm bean** <note>original: 蠶豆, *candou*; the most common modern name for the Broad Bean, *Vicia faba*</note>. 

When literati dip their brushes to write, they instinctively cite ancient texts to prove their points; we need not concern ourselves further with that academic habit here. <note>The author, Wu Qijun, often favored direct observation of plants over the repetitive and sometimes confusing citations of earlier scholars.</note>

The *Treatise on Nourishing Life* <note>original: 養生論, *Yang sheng lun*. A classical text on health and longevity, often attributed to the 3rd-century poet and philosopher Ji Kang.</note> states: "Beans make the heart and belly feel heavy; if they do not, the limbs..." <note>The classical quote suggests that if the belly does not feel heavy immediately, the heaviness will eventually settle in the limbs, implying a general sluggishness caused by the bean.</note>

## Another Variety of the Dragon Claw Bean

The Dragon Claw Bean <note>original: 龍爪豆, *longzhua dou*. Likely a variety of Velvet Bean or similar large-podded legume.</note> is of the same category as the Sword Bean <note>original: 刀豆, *dao dou*; *Canavalia gladiata*. Known for its long, blade-like pods.</note>. The beans are large and flat, about the size of a fingertip. Some have patterns resembling a "purse" shape. There are two varieties: purple and black.

The Farmer of Yulou <note>The pen name of Wu Qijun (1789–1847), the author of the *Illustrated Investigation of the Names and Realities of Plants*. He often used this name to ground his botanical observations in his identity as a man of the land.</note> says: In Guangfeng, Jiangxi, near Fengjin Mountain <note>Literally "Prohibited Mountain," likely referring to a government-restricted forest or nature preserve.</note>, a large bean is produced with pods shaped like claws. The seeds have a white body with red markings. The taste is similar to the hyacinth bean <note>original: 扁豆, *bian dou*; *Lablab purpureus*.</note> but sweeter. Furthermore, even when stored for a long time, they do not develop a medicinal or musty odor. The local people treasure them.

When I attempted to transplant this variety to Nanchang <note>The capital of Jiangxi Province.</note>, the fruit failed to mature and fell off early. I suspect this was because the autumn winds arrive earlier there. However, the "Purse Beans" <note>original: 荷包豆, *hebaodou*. Likely the Runner Bean (*Phaseolus coccineus*), named for its seeds which resemble traditional embroidered purses.</note> planted in my own home district have extremely fine black and white patterns, and their shape is exactly the same as the Dragon Claw variety, though the taste is somewhat thinner. I wonder if they are of the same species, but perhaps the black-patterned variety is uniquely resistant to the cold?

The *Materia Medica of the Tang Dynasty* <note>original: 唐本草, *Tang Bencao*. Compiled in 659 CE, it was the world's first official pharmacopeia.</note> states that people in the north call the hyacinth bean the "Magpie Bean" <note>original: 鵲豆, *quedou*.</note>, because the contrast between its black and white coloring resembles the feathers of a magpie. While this description generally applies to all hyacinth beans, Li Shizhen <note>The most famous Chinese pharmacologist (1518–1593), author of the *Bencao Gangmu*.</note> suggested that those with spotted patterns might actually belong to this Dragon Claw category.

# Compendium of Materia Medica, Volume 25
<note>original: 本草綱目, *Bencao Gangmu*. Compiled by Li Shizhen in the 16th century, this is the most famous and comprehensive pharmaceutical encyclopedia in Chinese history.</note>

### Bird-beaked Bean

The **Bird-beaked bean** <note>original: 鳥嘴豆, *niaozuidou*. Likely referring to the chickpea or a similar legume with a curved protrusion resembling a beak.</note> is found in **Southern Yunnan** <note>original: 滇南, *Diannan*</note>. It is similar to the **tea bean** but possesses a black "halo" or smudge. 

There is also a variety known as the **Taiji bean** <note>original: 太極豆, *taijidou*</note>; it is brown with black patterns that slightly resemble the shape of the **Taiji diagram** <note>The "Supreme Ultimate" or Yin-Yang symbol, representing the flow of opposing forces.</note>. 

Additionally, there is the **Flower-face bean** <note>original: 花臉豆, *hualian-dou*</note>, which is greenish-yellow with black halos and a slightly flattened shape. 

There is also the **Brown-pod bean** <note>original: 棕角豆, *zongjiaodou*</note>; these are round, brown, and wrinkled, though black ones also exist. All of these are considered the <term>preeminent giants</term> <note>original: 巨擘, *jubo*. Literally "great thumb," a metaphor for the most outstanding or largest examples of a category.</note> among the various species of beans.
