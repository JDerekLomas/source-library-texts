# The Pictorial Compendium of the Three Realms
<note>original: "三才圖會" (Sāncái Túhuì). The "Three Realms" or "Three Powers" (Sancai) refers to the traditional Chinese cosmological framework of Heaven, Earth, and Humanity.</note>

->**A Comprehensive Treasury of Knowledge and Illustrations**<-

<image-desc>The cover of this traditional Chinese thread-bound book is a deep indigo blue. A vertical white title strip is pasted on the left side, featuring the four characters of the title written in bold, elegant regular script. The right edge shows the exposed white thread used in the "four-hole" binding technique, a standard method for preserving paper volumes in the 17th century.</image-desc>

This work represents one of the most ambitious attempts in the Ming Dynasty <note>specifically the Wanli period, around 1609</note> to capture the entirety of human knowledge. Compiled by the scholar Wang Qi and his son Wang Siyi, it functions as a <term>Leishu</term> <note>a category of Chinese reference books often translated as "encyclopedia," though organized by category rather than alphabetically</note>. 

The collection is divided into subjects ranging from astronomy and geography to biology, history, and daily life, all unified under the philosophy that Heaven, Earth, and Man are interconnected.

# Preface to the Illustrations of Humanity

The Imperial Censor <note>original: "侍御公" (Shìyù Gōng). This refers to Wang Qi (1529–1612), the primary compiler of the encyclopedia, who held the title of Cending Censor.</note>, having already illustrated what are called the "Patterns of Heaven" <note>Astronomy</note> and the "Principles of the Earth" <note>Geography</note>, reflected once more that since Humanity lives in the space between these two, they cannot be left without their own illustrations.

Consequently, he further illustrated what are called "People and Figures." Once the woodblocks were carved for printing, the scope ranged from Emperors and Kings at the highest level down to the peoples of remote frontiers <note>original: "裔夷" (Yìyí). A historical term for foreign ethnic groups or those outside the central Chinese cultural sphere.</note>. He also included portraits of Daoist immortals, Buddhist figures, ghosts, and spirits—none were not fully depicted.

Now that the printing blocks are finished, he has commanded my unworthy self <note>a humble self-reference common in Chinese prefaces, likely referring to Wang Qi’s son, Wang Siyi</note> to provide a brief preface here at the beginning of this section.

...Humanity likewise possesses its own forms and representations.

The depiction of Buddhist and Daoist deities <note>original: 釋道 (Shì-Dào). A collective term for Buddhism and Daoism.</note> perhaps had its origins in the "Golden Man" used for worshiping Heaven <note>original: 祭天金人 (jìtiān jīnrén). Recently, in the Shaanxi region <note>original: 秦中 (Qínzhōng). Therefore, I have gathered materials from the most distant antiquity down to our present Ming Dynasty <note>original: 明代 (Míng dài).</note>, incorporating those old Shaanxi versions as well, to carefully organize them into this systematic presentation of illustrative style <note>original: 書法 (shūfǎ). Regarding Pangu, who succeeded Heaven and established the ultimate order: some say he was the Lord of Chaos <note>original: "Hundun-shi"</note>, born in the Great Wilderness. No one knows his beginning. He understood the Way of Heaven and Earth and reached the fundamental causes of Yin and Yang. His divine spirit underwent nine transformations in a single day, for he was the master who molded and forged all of creation.

He roamed through the vast, misty voids, which were without end or boundary. The pure, vast energies swirled and thickened, tranquil and overflowing. As all life flourished, the primal chaos opened up. He is thus considered the first sovereign of the **Three Powers** <term>Heaven, Earth, and Humanity</term>. Furthermore, the *Divine Mirror of Geography* states that Pangu possessed a dragon’s head and a human body.

## The Marquis of Lu
The title of **Marquis** <term>Hou: the second of the five noble ranks in ancient China, often translated as Marquis</term> of Lu was first held by **Bo Qin**, the eldest son and heir of **Zhou Gong Dan** (the Duke of Zhou), who was the younger brother of King Wu. ## The Marquis of Jin
The title of **Marquis of Jin** was granted to **Shu Yu**, the younger son of King Wu and the full brother of King Cheng. He was enfeoffed at **Tang**, and the state was also known by the name Jin. The capital was located at **Yi** (present-day Jiang County in Pingyang). Because of the circumstances of his birth <note>The text implies an unusual or breech birth</note>, he was named "Zheng" <note>original: "正", meaning upright or first</note>, which was later changed to "Zheng" <note>original: "政", meaning governance</note>. He was not actually of the Ying bloodline, but was of the Lü family.</gloss>

**The Second Generation** <gloss>Huhai was the youngest son of the First Emperor. The Chancellor Li Si and the eunuch Zhao Gao forged an imperial decree to kill the Crown Prince Fusu and install Huhai instead. He was eventually murdered by Zhao Gao. He reigned for three years.</gloss>

**Ziying** <gloss>He was the son of Fusu, the elder brother of the first ruler <note>Meaning the nephew of the Second Emperor</note>. Zhao Gao installed him on the throne, and he reverted to using the title of King rather than Emperor. He subsequently killed Zhao Gao. He reigned for only forty-six days before surrendering to the Duke of Pei <note>Liu Bang</note>. With this, the state was destroyed and the Annals of Qin are concluded.</gloss>

It also details the short, chaotic reigns of his successors, Huhai and Ziying, leading to the Han conquest.</summary>

# Biographies, Volume 1
## Forty-Eight

->**Xiang Ji**<-

Xiang Ji, whose <term>courtesy name</term> was Yu, was a native of Linhuai. For generations, his family served as generals for the state of Chu. Because they were <term>enfeoffed</term> in the district of Xiang, they took Xiang as their family name. 

He stood over eight feet two inches tall <note>Approximately 189 cm or 6'2" based on the shorter Han dynasty foot-measure</note>. His eyes were notable for having <term>double pupils</term> <note>original: "重瞳" (zhòngtóng); historically considered a mark of a king or a sage in Chinese physiognomy</note>. His strength was such that he could lift a massive bronze tripod, and his talent and spirit far surpassed those of ordinary men.

In the first year of the reign of the Second Emperor of Qin, Yu rose in arms. To satisfy the hopes of the people, he enthroned Xin, the grandson of King Huai of Chu, as <term>Emperor Yi</term> <note>The "Righteous Emperor," a figurehead leader used to legitimize the rebellion against the Qin Dynasty</note>. Yu repeatedly crushed the Qin armies, capturing the general Wang Li and forcing Zhang Han to surrender. However, he buried alive over 200,000 surrendered Qin soldiers <note>The Massacre at Xin'an</note>, slaughtered the inhabitants of the capital Xianyang, and executed Ziying, the surrendered Prince of Qin. 

Because of these actions, the people of Qin lost all hope in him. Furthermore, he broke his agreement with the Duke of Pei <note>Liu Bang, the future founder of the Han Dynasty</note>, exiling him to the remote region of Shu as the King of Han. He also secretly ordered King Bu of Jiujiang to assassinate Emperor Yi.

The King of Han subsequently raised an army to attack Chu, enumerating ten crimes committed by Yu. Eventually, they agreed to divide the empire at the Hong Canal <note>The "Great Ditch" that served as a temporary border between the warring factions</note> for peace and disbanded their forces. 

In the fifth year of the conflict, the King of Han advanced his troops again. Yu was fortified at Gaixia; his soldiers were few and his food supplies were exhausted. He broke through the enemy encirclement and galloped away, but the Han army pursued him to the banks of the Wu River. 

The Station Master of the Wu River prepared a boat and waited for him, saying to Yu, "Though the region East of the River is small, it is still sufficient for you to rule as a king." 

Yu laughed and replied, "It is Heaven that destroys me; why should I cross?" <note>Xiang Yu felt ashamed to return to his homeland after losing all the young men who had followed him into battle</note>. In the end, he took his own life by cutting his throat.
