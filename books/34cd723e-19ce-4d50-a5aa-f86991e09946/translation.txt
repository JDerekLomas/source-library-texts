# The first book of Albert 
## the Great, greatest of philosophers, on minerals begins.
## The first treatise on stones in general.
## Chapter one: concerning the intention,
## and what the division, method, and 
## order of things to be said are.

D<gloss>e</gloss> <note>The large woodcut 'D' depicts a scholar, likely Albert himself, at a desk.</note> The commingling and coagulation, and similarly the freezing and melting and other such properties <note>original: "passionibus", a term in medieval physics referring to how a substance is acted upon or changed</note> of things, have already been discussed in the book on Meteorology. However, the natural things in which these effects first appear are the types of stones and metals, and those things which are intermediate between them, such as marcasite <term>marchassita<gloss>A medieval term for various metallic sulfides, often pyrite or 'fool's gold'.</gloss></term> and alum <term>alumen<gloss>Naturally occurring mineral salts used historically in dyeing and medicine.</gloss></term>, and other such things. And because these are the first among natural compounds formed from the elements—existing as substances before those that have souls <note>i.e., plants and animals, which Albert considers "higher" because they possess a biological 'soul' or life force</note>—it follows that they should be discussed immediately after the science of meteorology; for they seem to be only slightly more than a simple mixture of elements.

Regarding these matters, we have not seen the books of Aristotle, except for excerpts in parts. And the things that Avicenna <note>Ibn Sina (c. 980–1037), a Persian polymath whose works on geology and medicine were central to medieval European science</note> handed down concerning these in the first chapter of his third book on the subject are not sufficient. Therefore, let us first conduct an inquiry into stones, then into metallic things, and finally into the things intermediate between them; for indeed, the generation of stones is easier and more manifest than that of metals.

Now, many things regarding the nature of stones must be said in general, which we will place first; then, we will discuss those specific stones that are more famous. We will, however, shorten our discourse because the causes of many things to be said here have already been determined in the book on Meteorology. While treating stones in general, we will investigate the general matter of stones and their primary efficient principle <note>original: "pꝓziū", likely "principium", the driving force or cause that creates the stone</note>, and the place of their generation. Then, we will look at the manner of the stones' mixing, and the cause of the diversity of their color and other non-essential properties <note>original: "accidentium", meaning qualities like color or smell that aren't part of the object's core essence</note> found in them, such as greater or lesser hardness, volatility and the inability to be shaped <term>indolabilitas<gloss>The quality of being impossible to hew, carve, or work with tools.</gloss></term>, porosity and denseness, weight and lightness, and other such things; in which stones seem to differ not only in species and number, but also in genus...

<margin>Those who wrote about stones.</margin>

They made mention of such things—men like Hermes <note>Hermes Trismegistus, a legendary Hellenistic figure to whom many alchemical and philosophical works were attributed.</note>, Euates <note>original: "euates"; likely referring to King Evax of Arabia, the purported author of a famous medieval lapidary.</note> king of the Arabs, Dioscorides, Aaron, and Joseph—who, by treating only precious <note>original: "sciosis"; likely a transcription error for "pretiosis" (precious).</note> stones, did not treat the general nature of stones. Furthermore, Pliny delivered an insufficient account in his *Natural History*, not wisely assigning the causes of stones in general; nor is it necessary for us to bring in the opinions of all these men, because the knowledge of the matter is not so hidden that we must collect it from the errors of many. For the nature and "complexions" <note>In medieval science, a "complexion" is the specific balance of the four qualities (hot, cold, moist, dry) in a substance.</note> of stones will be known sufficiently enough once their own matter, their immediate efficient cause, their form, and their own accidental properties are investigated according to the method prescribed in the fourth book of the *Meteorology* <note>original: "quarto metheoroꝝ"; referring to Aristotle's work on the elements and their interactions.</note>.

For we do not intend here to show how any of these might be transmuted into another, or how by the antidote of their medicine—which the alchemists call the "elixir"—they cure their "illnesses" <note>Alchemists often spoke of "sick" metals (like lead) being "cured" into "healthy" gold.</note>, or reveal what is hidden, or conversely cover up their manifest traits; but rather to show their mixtures from the elements, and how each is established in its own species. For this reason, we do not care to inquire into the difference between "stone" and "spirit," or "soul" and "body," or "substance" and "accident," about which the alchemists inquire, calling "stone" everything that does not evaporate in fire, and calling the same thing "body" and "substance." However, that which evaporates in the fire, such as sulfur and quicksilver <note>original: "argentum uinū"; literally "living silver" or mercury.</note>, and those things from which various colors are made—these they call "spirits," "soul," and "accident," though they also call them "stones." For it belongs to another science to inquire into these things which rely on very hidden reasons and instruments. 

However, we will maintain here the same method we have used in individual subjects, dividing the whole work into several books, many treatises, and many chapters. Since a treatise is made here concerning many particular things, we must first recognize the natures of these from signs and effects, and from those arrive at their causes and compositions, because signs and effects are more manifest to us. However, in the study of universal nature—of which we made mention in all the preceding books—one had to proceed in reverse: namely, from the cause to the effects, and to the powers and signs, because in such things the common and general aspects are more evident and manifest to us, as determined in the first book of the *Physics*. Indeed, we have sufficiently shown the order of this book in relation to the following books on nature at the end of our book on *Meteorology*, where we stated which things must be discussed first and which later. For since the kinds of stones and metals are "homeomerous" <term>omiomera<gloss>A term from Aristotelian physics meaning "uniform parts." For example, a piece of stone is made of smaller pieces that are also stone, unlike a plant (anomiomera) which has distinct parts like roots and leaves.</gloss></term> more so than plants, in which one finds a diversity of parts such as root, leaf, flower, and fruit—and "homeomerous" things are by nature prior to "anhomeomerous" <note>heterogeneous or non-uniform</note> things—it follows that stones and other minerals must be treated before the bodies of living things.

### Chapter two: on the matter of stones.

<margin>Twofold [matter]</margin>

**I**n beginning, therefore, to treat the nature of stones in general, we say that the matter of every stone is either a species of earth or a species of water. For one of these elements prevails in stones; and even in those in which a certain species of water seems to dominate, there is also something of earth dominating the matter at the same time. A sign of this is that almost all kinds of stones sink under water—the kinds of stones which we said abound in the matter of "earth" in the science of *On the Heavens and the World*. For if the "higher" elements <note>air and fire, which are lighter</note> were dominant in them, they would float a little upon the water. As it is, however, no kind of stone floats unless it be spongy or burnt, and made spongy and porous through burning, like pumice and the stone vomited by hot springs or the fire of a volcano; even if these are reduced to powder, the powder itself sinks under water. 

Furthermore, if there were not an "earthy" moisture mixed in to give a boundary <note>The concept that water needs earth to give it a solid "limit" or shape.</note> even in transparent stones, they would not sink under water—stones like crystal and beryl—just as ice and other things in which the water is pure or overabundant do not sink. Furthermore, all stones that are generated in the kidneys and bladders of animals are generated from a viscous, thick, and earthy moisture. It is necessary, therefore, that such be the matter of stones. 

Specifically speaking of those stones in which the matter is earth, it can easily be seen that in these, earth is not the only matter, for earth alone would not hold together into the solidity of a stone. For we say that the cause of "continuation" <note>cohesion</note> and mixture is moisture; this, by its subtlety, makes one part of the earth flow toward another, and this is the cause of the good mixing of the parts of the matter. If, however, this moisture were not well-infused everywhere into the earthy parts, and if it were not "retentive" <note>able to hold things together</note> and evaporated during the coagulation of the stone, then there would remain an earthy dust without cohesion. For this reason, it is necessary that it be viscous and gluey, so that its parts might entwine with the earthy parts like the links of chains. Thus the dry earth retains the moisture, and the watery moisture existing within the dry matter creates the cohesion. 

And Avicenna <note>Ibn Sina, the Persian polymath whose works on geology and medicine were central to medieval university education.</note> testifies to this when he says that pure earth does not become stone, because earth, by its dryness, does not create cohesion but rather fragmentation; for the prevailing dryness in it does not allow "agglutination" <note>sticking together</note> to occur. The same philosopher gives the reason: that sometimes mud is dried and becomes something halfway between stone and mud, and then in the space of time it becomes stone. He says again that the mud most fit to be transmuted into stone is "unctuous" <note>oily or greasy</note>; for that which is not so is fragmentary or easily crumbled into powder because of the easy separability of moisture from it. A sign of this is that in stones themselves, veins of earth frequently remain, which is hard, dry earth that becomes powder when compressed or struck. The cause of this is nothing other than that its moisture, not being sufficiently unctuous and viscous, evaporated from it during the stone's coagulation.

### Chapter Three: Concerning the transparency of stones
### according to more and less.

<margin>The matter of transparent stones</margin>

**O**f stones that are transparent to a greater or lesser degree, such as those called gems, it can be said in general that their common matter is an impure water. For those stones are a kind of glass produced by the work of nature; because of this, they are even finer in their mixture and clearer in their transparency than the glasses made by art, because although art imitates nature, it cannot fully reach the perfection of nature's work. A sign of what we have said—namely, that water affected by dry heat or cold is the common matter of these stones—is that glasses are made from this kind of moisture which is melted by the strongest fire from various ashes, whether of lead, or fern, or iron, or some other substance. That this moisture is indeed water is shown by the fact that it is coagulated by cold and is melted and liquefied by vehement dry heat. Moreover, that it has been affected by parched, earthy dryness is proved by the fact that it does not liquefy except from ash through a most vehement roasting, just as is shown visibly in the "divine art" <note>A common medieval term for alchemy.</note> and in the art of glassmaking, which is subordinate to alchemy. For a subtle vapor in the earth or in a stone is sometimes contained by the surrounding material, and being rolled up within itself, it becomes moist, just as happens in a covered hot pot in which there is some water. And when the moisture is thus vehemently affected by the dry, the dry and constant power within it coagulates into stone. But here we must not speak of the *manner* of the generation of stones, but rather of the *matter* of stones in general; whether their coagulation occurs by cold or heat will be shown later. That water is the matter of such stone is also shown because in certain places where there is a strong power for generating stones,

For as it descends, it is first affected by the terrestrial dryness from the nature and operation of the place, and thus it is made into the proper matter of stone. This is also shown by the multitude of transparent parts found in such stones. Since the transparency of air and fire are not indestructible, it must necessarily be that this transparency comes from the transparency of water; therefore, their proper matter will be of the nature of water. As for what some of the ancients say when showing the matter of stones—that something descends from running water which resides at the bottom, and this becomes stone—I do not approve of this, because that which descends from water is a terrestrial substance. Therefore, those stones which are generated from this often do not take as their matter water affected by a terrestrial power, but rather earth that has been affected by the powers of water. A sign of this is that such stones are frequently not transparent, but are "terminated" <note>meaning opaque or having definite boundaries that light cannot penetrate</note> and are said to be not "cuttable" but rather "crumbled," and are called flints by the common people. This is especially testified to by crystal and beryl, which have taken on almost the exact form of frozen water; concerning which Aristotle said that they are made from water when the heat is entirely removed. Just as we said that the matter of the stones mentioned in the previous chapter was earth (not simple earth, but affected by an oily, viscous moisture), so now it must be understood of these that simple aqueous moisture cannot be the matter of transparent stones. For as we have shown elsewhere, such moisture does not stand firm through "decoction" <note>long-term heating or boiling</note>, nor is it coagulated by dry heat, nor frozen by cold in any way that would last. Therefore, it is necessary that it be mixed with a very subtle terrestrial influence, and furthermore be most strongly affected by terrestrial dryness, so that its power now, as it were, takes hold of all parts of such moisture, yet without having yet transmuted the substance of such moisture into earth. For in every transmutation of the elements, such an "affection" <note>a change in qualities like heat or moisture</note> precedes the transmutation of the substance; namely, that the power of the transmuting element takes hold of the whole, and the parts of the transmuted element are shaped within it before the transmutation of the substance occurs. And if they are thus mixed into some "elementated" thing <note>a compound substance made of elements</note>, that thing will have the matter of one element and the powers of another element; and this is the highest ingenuity of the alchemists. ### Chapter Four: On the cause of generation according to the various opinions of the philosophers.

<margin>Avicenna</margin>
The "efficient cause" <note>The agent or force that brings something into being</note> of stones is said by almost everyone who has spoken on the subject to be the "mineral power" <term>virtus mineralis: a specific formative force believed to exist in the earth, similar to the "virtue" in a seed that makes it grow into a plant</term>. However, since this power is common to both stones and all metals, it seems to be an insufficiently defined cause for stones, since it has not been confirmed by these authors—neither by distinction nor by specific detail—what this thing is that they call the "mineral power." Nor is anything more found in the writings of Avicenna except that stones are generated from earth and water through this mineral power. 

Hermes, however, in the book he wrote on universal power, seems to say that the generative cause of stones is a certain power which he claims is one and the same in all things; but it acquires different names because of the diversity of the things generated. He offers the example of the light of the sun, which alone is the generator of all things, yet when it is shared—not through a single passive capacity—it performs different operations within them. It pleased him to first attribute this power to Mars as the source of that virtue, but he claimed it varied according to the proportion of the application of the light of the other stars and the material receiving it, as we have said. He believed that from this, different kinds of stones and metals were generated in different places. This statement is entirely "unnatural" <note>Meaning it relies on astrology/metaphysics rather than observable physical physics</note>, because here we are not seeking the primary moving causes—which are perhaps the stars and their powers and dispositions (for that belongs to another branch of science)—but we are seeking the "proximate" efficient causes which, existing within the matter, transform the matter. If the statement of Hermes were sufficient, then once the cause of the 

<column-break/>

generation of all things capable of being generated was established, we would already know the efficient cause. For we know that the motion and power of the heavens is a cause different from nature—namely, the rising and setting of the stars and their rays. Furthermore, these causes act "equivocally" <note>Meaning they act generally rather than specifically; the sun melts wax but hardens clay</note> and have nothing in common with the material of the things being generated. 

We, however, according to the proper method of natural science, seek causes specific to their effects, and especially the material and the unifying force that transforms it. Because of this, Empedocles, who came quite a bit after Hermes, asserted that stones are generated by "glassy heat" <note>original: "calido vitreo"; likely referring to the intense heat that fuses sand into glass</note>, taking his assertion from the ancient fable of Pyrrha and Deucalion, in which stones are called the "bones of the Great Mother" <note>In Greek myth, after a great flood, Deucalion and Pyrrha repopulated the earth by throwing stones (the bones of Mother Earth) behind them, which turned into people</note>. According to Empedocles, bones are composed mostly of the "parts of Vulcan" <note>meaning fire</note>. But this is entirely false, since we know (and it will be shown below) that certain stones are generated from cold. For we have already said in the book *On Meteorology* that those things whose material is primarily water are coagulated by the cold. Furthermore,

Democritus and certain others say that every elemental thing has a soul, and that these souls are the causes of the generation of stones. For this reason, he says a soul is in a stone just as it is in any other "seed" of a thing to be generated, and that this soul moves the heat inherent in the matter during the stone's generation, in the same way that a hammer is moved by a blacksmith toward the

To say that a soul exists in stones solely for the sake of their generation does not hold up, because their generation is not like the generation of living plants and sentient animals. Because of this, a stone seems to have no generative power of its own at all.

### Chapter Five: On the effective cause of stones according to the true opinion, and on its proper instrument.

<margin>The generative cause of stones</margin>

**W**e, however, gathering the true opinion from all of these, say that the most truly generative cause is the **formative mineral power** of the stone. For the mineral power is a certain common power that produces stones, metals, and those things that are intermediate between them. And therefore we add that it is "formative of the stone" so that it may produce a specific stone. Because we do not have proper names for this power, we must clarify what it is through similarities. Let us say, therefore, that just as in the seed of an animal—which is the surplus of nourishment descending from the seminal vessels—there is a formative power which shapes and produces the animal (and is present in the seed in the same way an artisan is present in the object he creates), so too, in matter prepared for stones, there is a power forming and producing stones and leading them to the specific form of this or that stone. This can be seen more clearly in the gums that drip from trees; for we see that these are moisture heavily affected by terrestrial dryness, and therefore they are coagulated by cold. When they remain in the tree without dripping out, the "path" of the tree generates them into wood, leaves, and fruit. In entirely the same way, when dry matter is affected by oily moisture, or moist matter is affected by terrestrial dryness, it is prepared for a stone and generates within itself—from the power of the stars and the location, as will be shown below—the formative power of stones, just as it

is generated in the seed by the testicles when the seed has been drawn to the seminal vessels, providing each matter with its own proper species-power. And this is what **Plato** said: that according to the "merits" of the matter, celestial powers are infused which perform the works of nature. However, as we have shown in the books of physics, every power that forms and makes something according to its species has a **proper instrument** by which it does its work. Therefore, this power existing in the proper matter of stones has two instruments according to the diversity of nature:

1.  One is a **moist heat**, which extracts and digests, inducing coagulation toward the form of stone in terrestrial matter that has been affected by oily moisture. The formative power directs this heat in its work, just as the heat that transforms an animal's seed is directed by the formative power within that seed. For otherwise, without doubt, if that heat were excessive, it would incinerate the matter; or if it were insufficient, it would leave it undigested and unsuitable for the form of a stone.
2.  The other instrument is found in the matter of watery moisture which has been affected by terrestrial dryness, and this is a **moist cold**. This does not just restrict the material (as happens in metals), but actually "expresses" <note>squeezes out</note> the moisture. This hardens and coagulates most vehemently. And because it expresses the moisture so entirely that none remains except for what is needed to hold the substance together, such stones can in no way be melted by dry heat. 

And this is what **Aristotle** says: that crystal is generated from water once the heat has been universally removed. The proof of this is that the operations of alchemists fail in melting stones without adding other moist matter. It is also clear why the operations of alchemists are often difficult and imperfect in the making of stones, even more so than in metals. This is because they do not give a "formative power" to the matter; instead, they use uncertain art in its place. For them, extreme heat is their instrument, and this operates most unreliably. However, the power given from heaven to a specific place and matter—which is called "formative"—is certain. The matter is suited to the instrument, and the instrument to the matter. Therefore, nature is most certain in its operations. There is one other thing to note: although coldness is never the cause of life in living things, it does work to create stones, because minerals are very close to the basic elements, and their qualities remain little altered.

### Chapter four: On the cause of the various degrees of hardness found in stones.

**W**e shall speak consequently of the diversity of hardness found in many varieties of stones. For all kinds of precious stones are so hard that a file removes nothing from them, and when struck hard against one another, as against hardened steel, they emit fire. On the contrary, almost every kind of tufa is found to have so little hardness that it can be carved with a small tool. Furthermore, certain white stones commonly called chalk, and some even softer and whiter, are found to have the least hardness among the genus of stones. Every kind of flint is extremely hard, and after these, marble in its own kind. Freestone is medium in the hardness of stones; nevertheless, greater and lesser hardness is found within it. This commonly happens to the harder stones: if they lie in the air for a long time during cold weather, they afterwards split into many parts when exposed to the sun. On the other hand, those that are less hard—unless they are poorly mixed, like burnt lime—ought to become better and harder the longer they lie in the air in buildings, provided the coagulating power is present; and they do not split from the cold. It is the task of physics to assign the cause of these accidents from the material and the efficient cause, just as was said in other cases. We say, therefore, that the general cause of hardness is dryness.

<margin>Cause of hardness</margin>
Since "hard" is that which has a natural power to resist a touching object, and "soft" is that which by nature lacks such power, hardness cannot be caused except by the "dry" quality, which stands firm in itself without yielding to another. In the nature of stones, dryness is caused by two things, as was shown above. Either it is because heat has drunk up the moisture from earthy matter, and thus the earth remains hardened; or it is because extreme cold has seized the transparent moisture and, by turning it toward its own property, has squeezed out the moisture. By violently compressing the matter, the cold has both hardened and excellently bound it together, as is seen in transparent stones. Because of this, they are extremely hard; when struck, they emit fire and do not endure the file, but must instead be polished by grinding and rubbing with <term>emery</term> <note>original: "amsi," likely a corruption or variant for abrasive powder/emery</note>.

<margin>Another cause of hardness.</margin>
In other stones made of earthy matter, the cause of greater hardness is nothing other than greater aridity. This is caused by a stronger or less strong heat on the part of the efficient cause, and by moisture that is more or less easily separable from the material. For very oily moisture is easily held together, while entirely watery moisture evaporates easily. Because of this, stones that are like chalk, or softer than chalk, are very white and leave a white stain on things they touch; they are certainly mixed with a very evaporable moisture and have been "burnt" by an exceeding heat beyond the measure of coagulation. They have already begun to turn into lime <note>original: "calinari," i.e., calcination</note>, which is why they do not remain in walls; being externally rough like dried-out lime, this roughness recedes into the tenacity of the cement and leaves another part of the stone glued to the cement. Therefore, these stones fall from the walls, and the wall falls from them after a short time while the material is drying out.

### ¶ Chapter five: On the workability and unworkability of stones.

From these points, the cause of workability <note>original: "volabilitatis," referring to how easily a stone can be hewn or split along a plane</note> and unworkability can now be made clear. For stones that are exceedingly hard are not easily worked, but rather crumble into small pieces; and when they do not have ordered pores, they do not split according to a "rule" <note>A straight line or architectural guide</note>. Just as knottiness occurs in wood from the diverse flow of the dry matter from which the body of the wood is generated, so it happens in stones from diverse mixing and the pouring-in of matter. This "knottiness" causes the stone to break inordinately and not according to a rule. Yet the hardest and driest stones, whether they are knotty or not, are more prone to crumbling than to being worked, because their pores are so tightened and constricted by <term>dry-heat</term> <note>original: "optesi," a term derived from Aristotle referring to the process of baking or roasting by dry heat</note> that their cleavage and division is taken away in different directions. However, those stones that are not constricted or hardened beyond a balanced state are easily worked and can be cut according to a rule, although they cannot be cut exactly like wood, but rather by small parts being drawn out while the rest of the body of the stone remains together. This, then, is the manner of a stone’s workability and unworkability. The very instruments of the stonecutters’ art show this: for stonecutters hew workable stones to a rule so that they "fly" <note>split or smooth out</note> across the whole surface; but it is sufficient that several angles of a rough rather than smooth surface reach the line, as they say the <term>Lesbian masons</term> <note>A reference to the "Lesbian Rule," a flexible leaden ruler used in ancient Greece to measure irregular stones</note> do, because on the island of Lesbos they find only stones that crumble.

### Chapter Eight: On certain stones having the images of animals inside and out.

<margin>Avicenna’s opinion on the images of animals in stones</margin>
It seems marvelous to all that some stones are found having the images of animals inside and out. # Here begins the second book, which is about precious stones. Chapter one: on the causes of the powers of precious stones and the refutation of those who say there are no powers in stones.

Making our consideration of precious stones, we will not speak of the cause of their color, nor of their susceptibility to change, nor their hardness or anything of that sort, because all these were dealt with in the previous book as much as suffices for this purpose. But there are three things that must be investigated most especially: the cause of their power <note>original: "virtutis," often translated as "virtue," referring to the inherent medicinal or magical property of a substance</note>, their individual descriptions, and the seals <note>original: "sigilla," referring to engraved images or naturally occurring patterns believed to hold power</note> found depicted in some of them. Having these three, we do not seek to know more of the nature of stones through physics. However, the cause of the power of stones is very hidden, and many natural philosophers seem to have felt differently about it. Many also seem to doubt whether any power is in stones of those things that seem to be in them, such as curing carbuncles <note>original: "antraces," likely referring to burning skin ulcers</note>, driving away poisons, comforting the hearts of men, bringing victories, and such things, asserting that nothing exists in a compound except from the composition and the components. For such an operation as is said to be in stones is not caused by their components. From those components, nothing belongs to them except being hot, moist, hard, susceptible, and such things which are determined in their accidents. Furthermore, since living things are nobler than stones, they ought—as the doubters say—rather to have the powers attributed to stones than the stones themselves. These and similar arguments are used by those who claim that no stones possess powers. On the contrary, one thing has been experienced most wisely: for we see the magnet attract iron, and the diamond <note>original: "adamantem"</note> restrict that power in the magnet. Moreover, it has been experienced that some sapphires drive away carbuncles, and we have seen one such thing with our own eyes. This is also common knowledge among everyone, and it cannot be but that what is commonly said by everyone is true in whole or in part. There were also some who gave special powers to stones and said this was from the "soul" of the stones. These are some of the Pythagoreans; for they say this belongs to the soul alone, and not in one matter only, but it is extended from one into another through animal operations.

A sign of this is that intellectual beings, like humans, do not perceive the changes of the elements as well as brute animals; for example, chicks indicate the differences of hours and seasons better than humans. The same is true of herbs, roots, and woods. Even the very flesh of humans—and what seems less likely, the dry waste excreted by humans and the dung of wolves—have marvelous operations against poison or other plagues. Because of this, it is certain that stones undoubtedly possess operations—either all of them or nearly all of them—even though their operations remain unknown in most cases. And therefore John of Damascus <note>A 7th-8th century Syrian monk and scholar whose writings on natural philosophy and theology were highly influential in the West.</note> says that no thing is destitute of its own proper and substantial operation.

<margin>John of Damascus</margin>

### Chapter Two: On the four opinions of philosophers
->concerning the cause of the virtues of stones<-

It has been asked for a long time by philosophers from what cause fateful <note>original: "fatales," meaning ordained by nature or the stars</note> powers are infused into stones, and to recite all the different opinions would be a waste of effort. Let us therefore touch upon four probable ones, and afterward state our own opinion and confirm it through reason. Some have said that such powers are present in stones due to the elements that compose them. When it is objected that elements only operate through primary qualities (heat, cold, etc.) and that the operations of stones cannot be reduced to these primary qualities, they respond that elements have certain operations by themselves and others when mixed. In a mixture, an elemental quality operates through motion as an instrument, and then it can perform many operations it cannot do on its own. They argue that just as the alteration of food and its elevation and change into flesh is not reduced to any power other than the "digestive heat"—which we know to be the heat of fire that gathers similar things and separates different things (as stated in the second book of *Meteorology*)—so they say these powers should be attributed to no other power but the elemental. This is because a "composite" thing only operates through the mediation of the power of the elements within it. This was the opinion of certain ancient philosophers which Alexander the Greek Peripatetic <note>Alexander of Aphrodisias, a famous commentator on Aristotle who emphasized material and elemental causes.</note> seems to defend.

<margin>Alexander</margin>
For he attributes everything that exists—whether animate or not—to the elements. He even says that the intellect is something following from the "complexion" <note>The specific balance or mixture of qualities in a body</note> of the elements. He claims that when the elements themselves are complexly joined, they perform the most marvelous and highest operations. He says the power that rules and directs the elemental qualities in a mixture is nothing but the resulting "complexion," and he asserts this is marvelous. He proves this through the works of alchemy, in which "simples" <note>Unmixed substances</note> are of little use, but when they are made into complexes, they yield very admirable effects. ## Chapter 4: On the true cause of the power of precious stones.

<margin>Constantine. Albert's opinion on the cause of the power of stones</margin>
**R**efuting, therefore, all these previous opinions, we say with Constantine <note>Constantine the African, an influential 11th-century medical translator</note> and certain others that the power of a stone is caused by the "species" <term>species</term> <note>the essential nature or 'kind' of a thing</note> and the substantial form of the stone itself. Now, some powers of mixed things have the ingredients of the mixture as their cause, but others have the species itself as their cause. This is seen more clearly in those things that are more perfectly "specified" and formed than others, such as a human being. A human, by the very operation that makes him human, possesses the power to understand—an ability that is not caused by any physical temperament. The same is true in brute animals and plants, as is proven in the *Ethics* <note>Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*</note>: the proper operation and the proper good of every thing follow its species, by which it is formed and perfected in its natural being. All tempered mixtures are merely the instruments of such species, because the species contains them all; when the species no longer exists, the mixture is corrupted and dissolved. For form contains matter as its divine and best part; it is not contained by the matter, nor does it "desire" matter by its nature, for it only needs matter for its existence as an individual, not for its divine essence. These matters will be more clearly elucidated in the book *On the Intellect and the Intelligible* and in First Philosophy. This form, therefore, stands between two things here: the celestial powers from which it is granted, and the tempered matter into which it is infused. If it is considered in itself, it is a simple essence capable of only one operation—for whatever is one can only effect one thing, and from a single source comes a single effect, as the whole community of philosophers teaches. But if this form is considered as it is influenced by the celestial powers (multiplied through the higher and lower bodies, and all the images and circles which the twelve signs with their stars distribute over the horizon of that thing into which the form is infused) and, secondly, according to how the elemental powers work upon it—then the form itself will be very "multiple" in its natural powers which surround its simple essence. Thus, it will be capable of producing many effects, even though it perhaps has only one "proper" operation. For it cannot be said that the powers of the causes do not remain in the effects. This is why almost every thing is useful not for just one purpose, but for many, when its operations are understood. When compared to the matter of which it is the form, the form is more powerful—and even "more" or "less" powerful—which is why Hermes rightly says that stones of the same species are found to be more powerful or less powerful in their effects. Perhaps some are even found to have no effect of their species at all because of the "confusion" or defect of the matter—just as a man may have no human operation despite being a man [if his body is too defective]. For although a species, in a logical sense, does not participate in "more or less," we nevertheless see the species present in almost all things according to "more or less" in their actual existence and in the strength of their actions. Therefore, in those things that possess powers following their species, objects are found to be more effective, less effective, or entirely incapable of the operations that naturally follow that species. It should be well remembered what is said in the second book of the *Meteorology* <note>Aristotle's *Meteorologica*</note>: that the species of stones are, in a way, "mortal" like individuals. Just as with men, when stones are kept for a long time away from the places where they were generated, they "die" <note>lose their specific powers</note> and retain the name of their species only equivocally—though their physical shape and color might only be lost over a very long time. And just as in the creation of animals there is sometimes such a disharmony <term>dyscrasia</term> in the temperament that the human soul cannot take hold, leaving only the elements and the human shape; so it is also in the generation of stones, either because of the disorder of the matter or because of the overwhelming powers of the celestial bodies moving in series, as we say in the second book of our *Physics*. These things, then, are what have been said regarding the powers of stones in general, etc.

<margin>Abeston, otherwise called asbestos</margin>
Asbestos <note>original: "abeston"</note> is iron-colored and is found mostly in Arabia. <margin>Adamas, commonly called diamond</margin>
The diamond <note>original: "adamas"</note>, as we mentioned above, is a very hard stone, somewhat darker than crystal, yet of a bright and shining color. This is because such a goat's blood is also effective for breaking the stone in the bladder of those suffering from the stone <term>calculo</term>. This stone also dissolves lead <note>original: "soluit... plumbo"</note>, which seems quite wonderful, due to the large amount of quicksilver <note>mercury</note> that is within it. This stone penetrates iron and all other gems except for the steel in which it is held. It does not, however, attract iron—despite what some have falsely claimed—because that property belongs to its place of origin. The largest quantity of this stone found is the size of a hazelnut. It is born in Arabia and Cyprus for the most part, but the Cyprian kind is softer and darker. And what seems wonderful to many is that when this stone is placed over a magnet, it "binds" the magnet and does not permit it to attract iron.

<margin>Virtues of the diamond</margin>
Its greatest power is manifest when it is set in gold, silver, or steel. The mages say that when tied to the left arm, it is effective against enemies, insanity <note>original: "insantā," likely "insaniam"</note>, wild beasts, fierce men, disputes, and brawls, and against poisons and the attacks of phantoms and incubi <note>demons believed to trouble sleepers</note>. Some call this stone "adamantine," and some even lie by saying it attracts iron.

<margin>Absinthus</margin>
Absinthus is a type of gem, black in color with red streaks and sometimes small spots. Its virtue seems to imitate that of asbestos; for absinthus remains hot for seven days or longer for the same reason given for the asbestos stone.

<margin>Four kinds of agate</margin>
Agate <note>original: "agathes"</note> is a black stone with white veins. Within this species, another type is found that is similar to coral. A third kind is born mostly on the island of Crete, which has yellow veins in black. The fourth kind is Indian, which is variegated as if sprinkled with drops of blood.

<margin>Virtues of the first kind</margin>
The first kind is suitable for the forms carved into stones; for this reason, many stones featuring the carved heads of kings are black. When placed at the head of a sleeper, it is said to show many dream-visions.

<margin>Virtues of the Cretan agate</margin>
The third kind, which is the Cretan one, makes one overcome dangers and confers strength to the body, as Avicenna says. It makes one graceful and pleasing—as Evax, King of the Arabs, said—and persuasive, of a good complexion, and eloquent, and it protects one against adversity. The Indian kind nourishes the sight and is effective against thirst and poison; when it is burned, it is very fragrant.

<margin>Virtues of Alabandine</margin>
Alabandine is so named from the place where it is mostly produced—that is, from Ephesus, which is called Alabandia by another name. It has a shining redness and is a clear stone, almost like the sardonyx <note>original: "sardinus"</note>.

<margin>Alectorius</margin>
Alectorius is a gem called the "cock-stone" <note>original: "lapis galli"</note>. It is a shining white, similar to dark crystal. It is extracted from the stomach of a rooster after it has lived more than four years; some say it is extracted after nine years and is better then, being taken from a decrepit rooster. The largest size found is close to that of a bean.

<margin>Virtues of the alectorius</margin>
Its virtue is to excite passion <note>original: "venerem"</note>, and to make one a pleasing, constant victor and a discreet person. It grants the faculty of oratory, reconciles friends, and when held under the tongue, it extinguishes or represses thirst; this last property has been proven by experience.

<margin>Virtue of the Amandine</margin>
The amandine is a gem of various colors. Evax says that it either extinguishes or represses all poison, makes one overcome adversaries, and grants the understanding of prophecy and the interpretation of dreams, and even of riddles <note>original: "enigmatum"</note>.

<margin>Amethyst</margin>
The amethyst is a very common and well-known gem; it is purple in color and somewhat darkly transparent. Many variations are found in this species, but five are most noted, distinguished by their levels of darkness. This species is born in India. It is the best for carving because it is less hard than others.

<margin>Virtues of the amethyst</margin>
It works against drunkenness, as Aaron says; it makes one vigilant, represses evil thoughts, and confers a good understanding of stable things.

<margin>Virtue of the Androsmanta</margin>

**Cornelian** <note>original: "Corneleus"</note>, or what some call *cornēleu*, is a stone of a flesh-like color—that is, red—and it looks like the washings of meat <note>original: "lotura carnis," referring to the pale pinkish water left after rinsing raw meat</note>. <margin>Three modes of images</margin>
Beginning, therefore, our discussion of images in stones, we say that three modes of images are found in stones. The third mode is when the figure is carved by incision into parts of the stone, with some parts scraped away and others remaining. 

Furthermore, among the images that are "painted," sometimes the image is colored the same as the stone itself, and then it only shows the image by the arrangement of certain lines on the surface of the stone; at other times, the image has a color entirely different from the color of the stone. ### Chapter 2: On the figures of stones made by nature.

**L**et us therefore inquire how they are formed by nature. <margin>Writers on magic</margin>
**N**ow, let us determine the reason why gems were first ordered by wise men to be engraved, and what the benefit is in their seals. There is a principle in this science: that everything whatever that is made by nature or art is first moved by celestial powers; and regarding nature, there is no doubt. In art, it is also superior because something at a certain time, and not before, incites the heart of man to do something; and this cannot be unless it is a celestial power, as the aforementioned wise men say. For there is a double principle of works in man: namely, nature and will. Nature is indeed ruled by the stars; the will is indeed free; but unless it resists, it is drawn by nature and becomes hardened. And since nature is moved by the movements of the stars, the will then begins to be inclined toward the movements and figures of the stars. Plato proves this from the works of children, who, by the liberty of their will, do not yet resist nature and the inclination of the stars. For by the power of the stars, they show in themselves aptitudes for one art or another, in which—if they are exercised—they will be made perfect; and if they resist and practice others, they will never

attain perfection because of the unsuitability of their nature for that art. We do not doubt that everything which is the cause of a cause in some way is also, in some way, the cause of the thing caused. If, therefore, the force and breath of the stars flow a certain causality of art into the artisan, for certain—unless it is hindered—it will flow something of its power into all the works of art. These things being held as a principle, we take from the said philosophers—what must also be proved elsewhere—that the figures of the heavens are the primary figures, and prior to the nature of all generated things. There is no doubt that what is first in genus and in the order of generators flows its causality, in a manner suitable to each, into all things following it. For we do not intend here to speak of figures taken mathematically, but of figures insofar as they induce the diversity of generators and generated things in order and species, and in the nature of their form and matter. Therefore, a celestial figure will have causality in every figure generated by nature, because art is resolved into the principle of nature; for the principle of art, as we say, is nature according as it has come forth from its celestial principle, whose principle is the practical intellect—just as that same intellect is the principle of art, as we have often said in *The Heavens and the World* and the *Physics*. From these things, it is necessarily concluded that if a celestial figure is observed while a figure is being impressed into matter by nature or art, some power of the celestial figure flows into the work of nature or art. It is evident that in the science of geomancy <term>geomantia: a method of divination that interprets markings on the ground or patterns formed by tossed handfuls of soil/rocks</term> the figures of the points are commanded to be reduced to such images, because otherwise they are not useful. Since, therefore, even with great burning, their humidities are not pulled from them, they must be oily. 

A sign of this is that every radical humidity <note>original: "radicalem humiditatem"; the essential moisture that supports the "natural heat" and life of an organism or the integrity of a mineral</note> in which the natural heat of animals is warmed is oily; a wise person would not fail to see that nature provided this because it is difficult to separate and difficult to dry. For nature desires to remain in existence for a long time, both in the individual and perpetually in the species, and therefore she ordained this kind of moisture as the nourishment for vital heat. 

<margin>
The matter of metals and their humidities are joined.
</margin>
Since, therefore, in the heat that melts metals, a humidity of this kind appears inseparable, there is no doubt that the humidity which is the matter of metals is oily. But we still see that the oiliness in oil and in all fat is easily flammable and destructive to those things to which it is related. And we see that fire does not leave them until they are consumed, as we see in the oil of a lamp or in the radical moisture of those suffering from wasting disease. Yet we see nothing of the sort in the moisture of metals. Therefore, it might seem to someone that perhaps an oily humidity is not the matter of metals. 

We, however, answer all such objections by what we said in the fourth book of our *Meteorologica*: namely, that there is a double oiliness in many things. One is, as it were, extrinsic and very subtle, having nothing drossy or combustible mixed in; this is not flammable. 

<margin>
Double oiliness.
</margin>
The other is intrinsic, retained in the roots of the thing, so that it cannot be pulled out or drained by fire. We gave an example of this in the liquor that is extracted from wine, in which one oiliness is floating on top, flammable and easily removed, and as it were accidental; the other is mixed with the whole substance of the liquor itself, not separable from the substance of the liquor except by the destruction of the substance; this is not combustible. The same is true in all things that nature produces. 

A sign of this is what we see happen in the art of alchemy, which of all arts most closely imitates nature. For when it sees that the yellow elixir <note>original: "elixir"; a substance used in alchemy to attempt the transmutation of metals</note> cannot be made in any better way than through sulfur, it notices in sulfur a vice of oiliness so strong that it burns all metals and blackens them by burning.

subtle oily part remains which withstands the fire and is not burned by the fire. It follows, therefore, that a similar oily humidity must abound in the materials of metals which are produced by nature, and this is the cause of their malleability <note>original: "ductibilitas"; the ability to be hammered or drawn out</note> and their ability to melt. 

<margin>
Avicenna and Hermes
</margin>
The authors Avicenna <note>the Persian philosopher Ibn Sina</note> and Hermes <note>Hermes Trismegistus, the legendary founder of alchemy</note> and many others most skilled in the natures of metals expressly say this. Furthermore, in all types of metals, we see that when they are melted, they do not moisten what they are poured over, nor do they rest on one surface, nor do they spread out on it at all. We see this in almost every aqueous or oily liquid, such as water, wine, beer, or oil; for all these, when poured over stone, earth, or wood, whenever they find a flat surface, they moisten it by soaking into the surface and spreading out on it. 

Melted metals do none of these things; they neither adhere to things they touch, nor do they diffuse entirely, but rather they stay together in one way and flow back in another. It must be, therefore, that a subtle oily humidity is not their only matter, but it is perfectly mixed with a subtle earthy matter which does not allow it to adhere to what it touches, nor to flow away entirely, but rather to stand like little spheres <note>referring to the way molten metal beads up due to surface tension</note>. This is because everywhere in it, the subtle earth catches and, as if swallowing it, holds the humidity and bounds it, so that it does not allow it to adhere to anything else. And the humidity is everywhere outside itself, leading out the dry earthy part that flows and runs on a flat surface. For if the dry earthy part were not protected everywhere by the humidity, it would certainly burn in the melting fire and become scaly, just as any dry earthy thing found by fire without being covered by humidity—as in iron—burns and becomes rough. This is almost the same in all metals. 

It is clear, therefore, that the primary matter of metals is a subtle oily humidity that is incorporated into a subtle earthy matter, strongly mixed together, so that the most of each is not only with the most of each, but even within the most of each.

### Chapter Five: On the Efficient Cause and the Generation of Metals in General.

**I**n this way we shall speak of the "efficient cause" <note>the active agent or force that brings a thing into being</note>. ## Chapter eight: On the opinion of Hermes and other philosophers who determine that these metals have multiple forms.

<margin>Anaxagoras</margin>

They have posited that one is hidden and another manifest; one is inside and another outside; one at the bottom and another on the surface. They spoke like those who discussed the "latency of forms" <note>A philosophical theory suggesting that all possible forms already exist hidden within matter and only need to be drawn out.</note> and said that "everything is in everything," as pleased Anaxagoras <note>Anaxagoras (c. 500–428 BC) was a Greek philosopher who taught that matter is composed of infinitely divisible particles containing all qualities.</note>. In the same way, they say copper and silver relate to one another, and almost any metal to any other. This seems to be a strange thing to say. For a homogeneous <note>original: "homogeneū." Furthermore, they say they do not call these "inside" and "outside" according to the physical position of parts in the whole, but according to the properties and natures of what is dominant or not dominant. For that which dominates hides within itself that which it dominates. According to this, they are directly stating the opinion of Anaxagoras: namely, that all metals are in all things, and they are named after whatever is most dominant.

<margin>The opinion of Hermes Trismegistus is refuted</margin>
If what they say were true—since we know gold is not burned by fire, but lead is, especially if sprinkled with sulfur—the lead ought to be burned away when fire is applied, and the gold which is hidden within it should remain. Yet we do not see this happen. Similarly, silver protects lead from being burned; then, once the lead is consumed, the silver that was within the lead (as they say) ought to remain—unless perhaps they say that every metal is infinitely within another, as Anaxagoras said, and therefore none of them can be totally consumed by fire. But we disproved this at the beginning of our *Physics* <note>original: "phisicoꝝ." Albertus refers to his commentary on Aristotle’s Physics.</note>. Furthermore, if this were granted as true, the hidden could never be liberated and made manifest through fire alone, and thus the entire study of alchemy would be in vain. Therefore, this saying does not agree with the physical reasons which we have determined in all our books. Perhaps, however, they say this because of the proximity of these metals in their matter. ### Chapter Two: On the Ductility of Metals.

<margin>The cause of ductility.</margin> **D**uctile <note>Ductility here refers specifically to "malleability"—the ability of a metal to be hammered or drawn out into thin sheets or wires without breaking.</note> things seem to be those metals alone which can be drawn out better and more easily than all others. The cause of ductility in general is that the moisture has been overcome by being enclosed within the dry part, yet it is not entirely bound. When this moisture is released by the repulsion of another substance, it does not "swim" in the earth—for even iron and stones swim in it and do not sink because of the force of the boiling and the thickness of the metallic moisture. For moisture, when it is bound by cold, is nonetheless present. When the metal is subjected to force, the moisture makes it yield to its surroundings, and by thus yielding, it is continuously drawn out. ## Chapter six: That the generation of metals into one another is circular.

**T**o these things must be added a fact common to all metals: that their matter is very closely related. We know from those things determined in the science of *Perigeneos* <note>original: "scientia perigeneos"; likely a reference to Aristotle’s "On Generation and Corruption"</note> that among things having a common symbol <note>i.e., shared characteristics or "identities" in their underlying matter</note> in their matter, virtues, and natural powers, mutual transmutation is easy. For this reason, it is the assertion of many philosophers—whose father is **Hermes Trismegistus**, who is called the "prophet of philosophers"—that the generation of metals is **circular**. That is, they come from one another just as the generation of the elements is circular; this also seems to me to be most true. Since they do not differ in their matter—whether remote or near—and are made from the same prior substances, it is clear that their differences are taken from whether the parts of their matter are purified and digested, or unpurified and undigested. It happens that every impure and undigested thing can be purified and digested if the natural powers of digestion prevail. It also happens that every digested thing can suffer "softening," or a mixing with the undigested, or perhaps a weakening of the defining heat. It happens, then, that the proximate matters of the elements are transmuted into one another; and once transmuted, it is necessary that they themselves be mutually transmutable. In this way, therefore, it happens that the generation of metals from one another is circular.

Both works of nature and the skill of art prove this through experience. For in the works of nature, I have learned by my own sight that from a single source, a flowing vein was pure **gold** in one part and **silver** in another part (having stony lime mixed with it). The miners and metal-refiners told me that this happens very frequently; and for this reason, they rejoice to have found gold, because gold is near the source, though the vein may then fail. I then diligently examined it and found that there was one kind of "vessel" <note>The surrounding rock or matrix in which the metal forms</note> in which the mineral had been turned into gold, and another in which it had been turned into silver. For the vessel that held the gold was of the hardest kind of stone, of the sort from which fire is struck with steel <note>Likely flint or quartz</note>; it held a little gold, not incorporated but enclosed in its cavity, with a small amount of burnt earth between the parts of the stone and the gold. The stone was open, having a hole leading to the silver vein, which passed through a black stone that was not very hard but earthy; it was the kind of stone from which tiles are made for covering houses. This proves that from one place, which was the "vessel" of the mineral matter, both evaporated; and the difference in purification and digestion had been caused by the difference of the stone, resulting in different species of metal.

Alchemical experiment is the proof of art; for alchemists, if they work with nature, reduce one species of metal into another, as has been said. Thus, it is not improbable that the generation of metals from one another is circular; and this property is unique to metals among elements and mixtures. But let it not escape us that in all things which have a circular generation, the transition is easier for those that share more similarities. Because of this, **gold is more easily made from silver** than from any other metal; for in silver, one need only change the color and the weight. And these things are done easily. For a compact substance certainly adheres more when the wateriness is diminished and the weight is increased by good, yellow sulfur; consequently, the color will be changed. This is the method in other cases as well. So much, then, has been said regarding the common properties of metals.

# Chapter Four: On the Nature of Lead.

**I**n the same manner, we determine the nature of **lead** <note>original: "plumbum"</note>, which adds less to the matter of quicksilver than any other metal. For this reason, **Aristotle** and **Avicenna** say that when lead flows [melts], it undoubtedly appears to be quicksilver. Regarding the composition of Lead's substance, it is certainly believed that its quicksilver content is vast compared to its sulfur. Perhaps very little sulfur enters into the composition of lead's substance, but a great deal enters through its quality [influence]. Thus, the sulfur "cooks" it with its heat and converts it to the species of lead, just as a small amount of rennet coagulates a large amount of milk. 

The quicksilver of lead is not of good quality, but is watery and muddy. Therefore, its wateriness easily evaporates in fire and a terrestrial powder remains, like the ash left from the muddy substance of lead. Because the power of sulfur is in lead, as we have said, its vapor dries out quicksilver just as the vapor of sulfur does. For it cannot be that two substances produce the same effect in the same way unless through some single thing that is within them. We have said in the preceding sections why it has a dark color. 

In its effect and virtue, lead is cold and constrictive. It has a special power <margin>The power of lead.</margin> against the lust of the venereal and against nocturnal emissions, if a ring of it, two fingers wide, is placed around the kidneys and camphora is applied; but one must be cautious, lest by its coldness it constricts the matter too much internally and forces it upward to the head, inducing mania or epilepsy. One must also beware lest it induce paralysis or numbness in the lower limbs. This, therefore, is the nature of lead in its mixture and effect. 

**Hermes**, who proved much regarding the transmutation of metals in his alchemical works, taught that if lead plates are suspended over a vessel with much strong vinegar, so that the vapor of the vinegar continually touches those plates, that vapor will condense and corrupt the substance of lead and convert it into a powder which has a white color and is called **white lead** <margin>White lead.</margin> <note>original: "cerusa"</note>. If, however, the vinegar is poured directly upon the plate, it becomes very white and, conversely, destroys the strength of the vinegar. The cause of this is certain: although vinegar is dull in substance because of its coldness, it is sharp in action because it contains the remains of a certain fire that flew out of it, just as ash is the remains of fire in wood. With that sharpness, it penetrates the substance of lead when the lead has been worn down, and it washes the congealed quicksilver within it of its filth, causing it to rise to the surface of the plate like grains of millet; then they become white because of their greater purification. 

Furthermore, Hermes teaches that lead burnt with that which burns it—such as sulfur or **arsenic**—generates a sublimated dark vermilion color (that is, red), which becomes yellow when the fire is more intense. If, however, that substance is calcined with vinegar and dried, it returns to the white color of white lead. The cause of this transformation is that it truly contains sulfur and quicksilver in its composition. When sulfur is burnt and nourished, it turns red; the proof of this is that those who make **red lead** <note>original: "minium"</note> make it from a sublimate of sulfur and quicksilver. The color is dark, however, because of the muddiness of the lead; when the fire is made stronger, that primary muddiness is consumed.

### Chapter four: On the nature and properties of tin.

<margin>Why tin is whiter than lead.</margin>
**D**ealing with **tin** <note>original: "stagnum"</note>, we must determine its nature almost as we did with lead. For these species of metals are very closely related and differ little from one another, except that tin is whiter and purer. There can be no cause for this other than what the philosophers before us assigned: that its quicksilver is cleaner than that of lead. Perhaps it has a little more sulfur, or rather, it was cooked into a metallic species by the power and vapor of sulfur and the admixture of much of the substance of sulfur. It has a very "stuttering" composition; the cause of this can be the "mortification" <note>the chemical "killing" or stabilizing</note> of the quicksilver by some vapor, or a dissolution or washing by "sharp water" <term>aque acute; likely a reference to a mineral acid</term> which separates its parts. I say "vapors" or "water" not as something the quicksilver passed through after it took its metallic form, but rather things that entered into the very substance of the quicksilver. For such water is already broken within itself and has little viscosity, and it makes the

earthy parts mixed with it grow rigid so they do not mix or join well. For that which is rigid and hardened on the surface does not mix well with another and cannot be joined to what is near it. This, then, should be considered the cause of its "stuttering" mixture <note>This refers to the "tin cry," a crackling sound tin makes when bent, which medieval authors described as "stuttering" or "shrieking"</note>. Since it "stutters" within itself, it makes all metals with which it is mixed "stutter" as well, and it takes away their **ductility** <term>ductibilitatem; the ability to be hammered or drawn out without breaking</term>, as Hermes says. When it is drawn out itself, it is quickly and easily split. 

This metal agrees with lead in that neither contracts **rust** <note>original: "rubiginem"</note>; rather, they contract a certain squalor and filth from corrupting influences and from themselves, though lead more so than tin. They also agree in that neither has much sound of its own. The cause of the first is that they do not possess any hot, watery humor—or very little sharp humor—which would corrupt the earth within them and turn it into rust. For the cause of rust is nothing but burnt earthiness. The cause of the second [the lack of sound] is softness and moisture; because when you strike something soft and moist, it yields to the impact. Therefore, it does not expel the air from its whole surface, which is the cause of "sonority," as is shown in the *Science of the Soul* <note>Aristotle’s "De Anima"</note>. Tin, however, has more sonority than lead, even though it has a dull sound. Therefore, for those things which sound sharp—such as copper, silver, and gold—tin tempers and deepens their sounds. For this reason, it is mixed with copper in the casting of **bells** <note>original: "campanarum"</note>.

<margin>Hermes</margin>
As for what Hermes says in alchemical writings—that tin, besides its excessive dryness, breaks the bodies it is mixed with and destroys their ductile matter—he intends it in the way we have said: that from sharp vapor or "washing," the earthy parts within it are dried out. Otherwise, it would be false, since we see that tin is softer than many other metals. They say, moreover, that molten tin putrefies quickly, while lead remains and grows under its "master" <note>The "master" or "lord" may refer to a planetary influence, here Saturn for lead</note> and grows in the earth. I judge it probable that this is seen to happen through experiments. The cause of this is assigned in *The Heavens and the World* <note>Aristotle's "De Caelo"</note>. For the cause of the corruption of the elements is that elements move toward one another; and when the bond of the mixture is not strong, then each of the elements escapes from the other. We have already said that tin is poorly mixed; therefore, when it is weakened by fire and removed from the place of its generation, it corrupts faster than other metals. Lead, however, is very thick in its substance, and from the dews and rains it absorbs, it gradually creates a mineral moisture and converts it to itself. Thus, over a long period of time, it sometimes increases in size. Two kinds of tin are found: namely, a harder and drier kind that comes from **England** or **Britain**, and a somewhat softer kind that is found more abundantly in parts of **Germany**. Let these things we have said about the nature of tin suffice.

<margin>Why silver is resonant.</margin>
**C**oncerning silver, let us speak after this, because in color it seems to agree somewhat with the aforementioned metals. We are already certain through the things said before that **quicksilver** <note>original: "argentum vivum"; literally "living silver," known today as mercury</note> enters into the composition of silver, because it is colored according to its color. It also possesses the activity of quicksilver when it melts; for then it does not stick to the touch, nor does it rest on a single surface, nor does it spread out entirely like water, oil, wine, or any other liquid. We see these three traits primarily in quicksilver. If, therefore, these things occur in melted silver, it must be by grace of the quicksilver that enters into the composition of its substance. Furthermore, since silver is most splendid, shining in whiteness and easily polished, it must possess the character of quicksilver that is highly **digested** <term>digestio; a process of "cooking" or ripening by natural heat within the earth</term>, purified, and most subtly mixed. It also has a certain smell, yet it is not as foul-smelling as the other metals we have already discussed. We know from what was said before that both sulfur and quicksilver—and anything that is moist by its own **complexion** <note>the balance of qualities (hot, cold, wet, dry) in a substance</note> and meltable by fire—possess three humors, just like living plants and animals. One of these is thick and undigested, floating like fat, grease, or oil; this makes things flammable. Another is like **phlegm** <note>original: "flegmaticus"; the watery humor</note>, moistening the parts of things but not generating or increasing them. The third is the **radical** <term>radicalis; the "root" moisture essential to a thing's existence</term> humor, soaked into the essential parts of the thing; the essential parts of things consist of this, and are increased and nourished by it. We find silver to be hard and dry. It must be, therefore, that it has been greatly cleansed of the two superfluous humors, and the refinement of the third humor creates its excellent mixture. When silver is blown upon, a sulfurous odor is sensed; it must therefore have something of the substance and quality of sulfur. From the color of sulfur, it gains its "fermentation" and digestion into the form of a metal, though it possesses very little of sulfur's actual substance. For this reason, the sulfur does not color it [yellow or red], but the silver receives much from the power and quality of sulfur: because the sulfur's heat has consumed the two types of moisture we mentioned, and the third is excellently mixed with refined earthy material. Indeed, the heat and vapor of sulfur—especially sulfur that is well-cleansed and **sublimated** <term>sublimati; purified by heating into a vapor and cooling back into a solid</term>—is most effective; through digestion, it refines and mixes most strongly and excellently. When the two foreign humors are purged from the substance of sulfur by the "artifice" and ingenuity of nature, this natural process is more certain and subtle than any human art, and therefore achieves the most certain effect. Therefore, silver has the nature of quicksilver because it is a metal made from the most purged quicksilver, reduced to a splendid appearance by the color of purified sulfur. Thus it is necessarily white and shining. That it has been dried out, as was said, is shown by the fact that it is very **resonant** <note>original: "tinnulum"; having a clear, ringing sound</note> in tone, which would not be the case if it were infected by superfluous moisture.
