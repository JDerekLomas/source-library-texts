### Section on Waters

Li Shizhen states: Water is the symbolic image of the *Kan* <note>original: 坎 (Kǎn). In the I Ching or "Book of Changes," this hexagram/trigram represents water and the directional north.</note>. In its written form, when horizontal it consists of three strokes; when vertical it is represented as <term>☵</term> <note>The trigram for water, consisting of a solid Yang line between two broken Yin lines, symbolizing light or heat contained within darkness or cold.</note>. Its substance is pure Yin, yet its function is pure Yang. Above, it manifests as rain, dew, frost, and snow; below, it forms the seas, rivers, springs, and wells. Whether flowing or stagnant, cold or warm, the vital energy <note>original: 氣 (qi). The fundamental life force or "breath" that flows through all things.</note> concentrated within it varies. Similarly, the flavors it absorbs—sweet, bland, salty, or bitter—are also diverse. For this reason, the ancients distinguished the waters and soils of the Nine Provinces <note>original: 九州 (Jiuzhou). A traditional name for the ancient divisions of China, implying that different geographical regions produce different physical qualities in people.</note> to understand why people in different regions differ in beauty or ugliness, and why some live long while others die young. Indeed, water is the source of all transformations in the universe, and soil is the mother of all things. We depend on water for our drink and on soil for our food; and it is from this nourishment that human life is sustained.

## Category of Ground Waters (Thirty Varieties)

---

Flowing Water <gloss>from the Supplement to the Materia Medica</gloss>
<note>original: 流水 (Liushui). This refers to running water from rivers or streams. In traditional pharmacology, the speed and direction of the flow (e.g., eastward flowing) changed its perceived therapeutic "energy."</note>

Well and Spring Water <gloss>from the Jiayou Materia Medica</gloss>
<note>original: 井泉水 (Jingquanshui). A staple source for both daily use and decocting medicines, valued for its stability and mineral content.</note>

Solar Term Water <gloss>from the Compendium</gloss>
<note>original: 節氣水 (Jieqishui). This refers to water collected on specific days of the twenty-four solar terms of the traditional calendar, such as the "Beginning of Spring" or "Summer Solstice." It was believed that water captured the unique "Qi" or essence of that specific seasonal transition.</note>

Sweet Spring Water <gloss>from the Supplement to the Materia Medica</gloss>
<note>original: 醴泉 (Liquan). Literally "Liqueur Spring." A legendary or exceptionally pure, sweet-tasting spring water often regarded in ancient texts as a sign of virtuous governance or a miraculous source of health.</note>

Jade Well Water <gloss>from the Supplement to the Materia Medica</gloss>
<note>original: 玉井水 (Yujingshui). Water from wells found in regions rich in jade or quartz. It was believed that the water absorbed the "virtue" and medicinal minerals of the stone over centuries.</note>

Stalactite Cave Water <gloss>from the Supplement to the Materia Medica</gloss>
<note>original: 乳穴水 (Ruxueshui). Literally "Milk Hole Water." This refers to water dripping from stalactites in limestone caves. Because stalactites were known as "stone milk," the water was thought to be highly restorative and rich in concentrated earth essence.</note>

### Explanation of Cross-Referenced Liquids

**Rouge Water** <note>original: 臙漿 (Yanjian). The water produced when washing rouge or cosmetic pigments. It was used in folk medicine for treating certain types of poisoning or skin conditions.</note>

**Chrysanthemum Pool Water** <note>original: 菊潭水 (Jutan shui). Famous water from Nanyang in Henan province, where wild chrysanthemums grew by the banks. Legends claimed those who drank it lived to be over a hundred years old.</note>

**Iron-tempering Water** <note>original: 淬鐵水 (Cuite shui). The water used by blacksmiths to quench red-hot iron. It was believed to contain the "spirit" of the metal and was used to treat conditions of the liver or as a sedative.</note>

**Water inside Yellow Stones** <note>original: 石中黃水 (Shizhong huangshui). A rare liquid found inside hollow "yellow stones" (likely geodes containing limonite or similar minerals). It was highly prized for its purported cooling and detoxifying properties.</note>

**Silk-reeling Water** <note>original: 繰絲湯 (Saosi tang). The hot water used to boil silkworm cocoons to loosen the silk. In medicine, it was sometimes used to treat digestive ailments or externally for skin irritation.</note>

**Hemp-retting Water** <note>original: 漚麻湯 (Ouma tang). The fetid water resulting from soaking hemp stalks to separate the fibers. Despite its smell, it was used in specific topical applications.</note>

**Petroleum** <note>original: 石腦油 (Shinaoyou, literally "stone brain oil"). An early reference to crude oil or mineral oil. Li Shizhen notes its use as a fuel and its medicinal application for skin diseases and "scabies."</note>

# Upstream-Flowing Water: Primary indications include stroke, sudden fainting, chronic headaches, malaria, and various throat ailments; used to induce vomiting of phlegm.

## Elaboration
<gloss>Shizhen</gloss>

Whether stored in jars or vessels, the "Thousand-Mile Water" and "East-Flowing Water" are both capable of washing away evil and filth. They are used for decocting medicinal soups, and in rituals to ward off spirits or cleanse impurities. Even ordinary rainwater collected in road ruts can be effective for such things; how much more so these powerful, flowing waters? The *Classic of Materia Medica* <note>original: "Benjing." This refers to the Shennong Bencao Jing, the foundational text of Chinese pharmacology.</note> states that East-Flowing Water is "feared" by mica stone <note>In traditional alchemy, "fearing" a substance means it can be used to process or "tame" that substance; here, this water is used to refine mica.</note>. Using it to process mica yields results different from other waters—this is proof of its unique efficacy. 

The physician Sun Simiao said: "River water flows from far-off springs, following its natural course to the sea without ever flowing backward. Using it to treat ailments of the head ensures the energy will be directed downward." Thus, it is used to treat the "Five Taxations and Seven Impairments" <note>A classical medical term for chronic physical and emotional exhaustion caused by overwork and stress.</note> and conditions of extreme weakness. When simmering medicine for these conditions, one should use "Agitated Water" <note>original: "Lao Shui." Also known as "Labored Water."</note> and old reeds for fuel, as this ensures the water's nature is not too "aggressive" and the fire is not too intense. If river water is unavailable, East-Flowing Water from a long distance may be substituted, such as that from the Jing or Wei Rivers.

Shizhen says: Agitated Water is the same as "Sweet Rippling Water." Zhang Zhongjing <note>The "Sage of Medicine" (c. 150–219 CE), author of the Treatise on Cold Damage.</note> called it "Sweet Rippling Water." The method of preparation is to place two *dou* <note>A traditional unit of volume, roughly 2 liters.</note> of flowing water into a large basin. Use a ladle to scoop it up and pour it back from a height, repeating this thousands of times until thousands of "boiling pearls" <note>bubbles or foam created by aeration</note> appear. Then it is used to decoct the medicine. 

Water is naturally salty and heavy in character. By "laboring" it through agitation, it becomes sweet and light. This preparation is chosen because it does not overstimulate the Kidney energy <note>In traditional Chinese medicine, salty flavors are associated with the Kidneys.</note> but instead benefits the Spleen and Stomach. Yu Tuan’s *Orthodoxy of Medical Tradition* states: "Sweet Rippling Water is sweet in flavor, warm in nature, and soft in character."
