Whenever performing the <term>Civil Dances<note>original: "Wenwu" (文舞); ritual dances characterized by graceful movements, symbolizing the civil virtues of the ruler.</note></term>—specifically the **Gate of Clouds** <note>original: "Yunmen" (雲門); the dance attributed to the Yellow Emperor.</note> and the **Pool of Harmony** <note>original: "Xianchi" (咸池); the dance attributed to Emperor Yao.</note>—the great stone chimes are positioned to the left of the **Eastern Steps** <note>original: "Zuojie" (阼階); the steps used by the host or the Emperor.</note>. 

The <term>Military Dances<note>original: "Wuwu" (武舞); dances characterized by vigorous, martial movements, symbolizing the conquest of disorder.</note></term>—specifically the **Great Xia** <note>original: "Daxia" (大夏); attributed to Yu the Great</note>, the **Great Harvest** <note>original: "Dahuo" (大濩); attributed to Tang of Shang</note>, and the **Great Martial** <note>original: "Dawu" (大武); attributed to King Wu of Zhou</note>—are performed to the right of the **Guest Steps** <note>original: "Binjie" (賓階); the western steps used by guests.</note>.

The performers are arranged in six rows. When performing these brilliant ancient melodies, each section has four "markers" <note>original: "Biao" (表); physical posts or signs used to guide the dancers' positions.</note>. Each marker is spaced four paces apart to form the **coordinated formation** <note>original: "Duzhui" (都綴)</note>. Each group consists of sixty-four dancers; in total, three hundred and eighty-four people are employed. This is why it is necessary to summon the **sons of the nobility** <note>original: "Zhuzi" (諸子)</note> to provide enough performers.

Music, when perceived by the ear, is **sound**; when perceived by the eye, it is **form**. Only when one listens to the sound and moves the form accordingly will the rhythm be without error. Therefore, when the former kings established the dances, they were careful to harmonize them with the singing. 

One song cycle constitutes one **variation** <note>original: "Bian" (變); a movement or section of the dance.</note> of the dance. Six variations utilize the "Six" dances, while nine variations utilize the "Nine." However, the resonance of the voice can be heard but not seen. Therefore, the <term>feathers and flutes<note>original: "Yu" (羽) and "Yue" (籥); the ritual implements held during Civil Dances.</note></term> and the <term>shields and axes<note>original: "Gan" (干) and "Qi" (戚); the ritual weapons held during Military Dances.</note></term> are used to manifest the external form. 

**Spirited movements and vigorous stamping** <note>original: "Fayang Daoli" (發揚蹈厲); a specific term describing the energetic choreography of the military dances.</note> are used to reveal the inner intent. Essentially, the strength of the dancers' muscles and bones is used to meet the rhythm of the bells, drums, and hand-claps. This all depends on the clarity of the ears and eyes and the sharpness of the mind. By balancing the length of the sounds and matching the speed of the dance, the sound and the form become harmonious. The <term>Eight Sounds<note>original: "Bayin" (八音); the ancient Chinese system of classifying musical instruments by their material: metal, stone, silk, bamboo, gourd, clay, leather, and wood.</note></term> are used to direct the **Eight Winds**. For this reason, the number of dancers in the ritual rows and the number of bronze bells and stone chimes both use eight as their base. Furthermore, those who sing the ritual poems in the hall number four, and those who play the *se* zithers number four; with the arrangement of "one lead singer and three joining in praise," this also employs eight people. Although the singing and the instrumental accompaniment are distinct parts of the ceremony, they are truly interconnected.

The **Feather and Flute** <note>original: "Yuyue" (羽籥); the ritual props held by Civil dancers, symbolizing grace and harmony.</note> performers begin their movements with the pitch of the **Yellow Bell** <note>original: "Huangzhong" (黃鍾); the fundamental pitch of the Chinese gamut.</note>. The **Great Lyre** <note>original: "Dalu" (大呂)</note> responds through the pitches of **Old Purification** <note>original: "Guxi" (姑洗)</note> and the **Southern Lyre** <note>original: "Nanlü" (南呂)</note>; this is referred to as the connection between the **Dragon and the Rooster** <note>original: "Chen-You" (辰酉); referring to the alignment of these positions on the ritual compass.</note>. By using the South to represent the South Wind, the pitches of **Equalizing Rule** <note>original: "Yize" (夷則)</note> and the **Mean Lyre** <note>original: "Zhonglü" (中呂)</note> both become the "Clear Jue" tone. In this way, the **Civil** elements are merged with the **Military**.

The **Shield and Battle-axe** <note>original: "Ganqi" (干戚); the ritual props held by Military dancers, symbolizing strength and the restoration of order.</note> performers begin their movements with the pitch of **Luxuriant Vegetation** <note>original: "Ruibin" (蕤賓)</note>. The **Forest Bell** <note>original: "Hanzhong" (函鍾)</note> responds through the pitches of **Not Echoing** <note>original: "Wuyi" (無射)</note> and the **Pressed Bell** <note>original: "Jiazhong" (夾鍾)</note>; this is referred to as the connection between the **Dog and the Rabbit** <note>original: "Xu-Mao" (戌卯)</note>. By using the North to represent the North Wind, the pitches of the **Great Cluster** <note>original: "Taicu" (太簇)</note> and the **Echoing Bell** <note>original: "Yingzhong" (應鍾)</note> both become the "Flowing Zhi" tone. In this way, the **Military** elements are merged with the **Civil**.

The North enters the South, and the *Yin* enters the *Yang*, in order to join with the harmony of the breath of life. This guides the movement of the **Five Constants** <note>original: "Wuchang" (五常); the five cardinal virtues of benevolence, righteousness, propriety, wisdom, and fidelity, which correspond to the Five Elements.</note>. Its rhythmic system follows the <term>Hidden Sounds<note>original: "Nayin" (納音); a sophisticated method of assigning musical qualities to the sixty-year cycle of the Chinese calendar.</note></term> and the principle of <term>Marrying at the Eighth Interval<note>original: "Geba er qu" (隔八而娶); the mathematical method of generating the twelve pitches by moving seven steps forward in the series to the eighth pipe, alternating between adding and subtracting length.</note></term>, where returning to the opposing positions creates a unified whole.

For instance, the calendar signs of *Jia-Zi* and *Yi-Chou*, *Ren-Shen* and *Gui-You*, and *Geng-Chen* and *Xin-Si* are all associated with the element of **Metal**. The signs of *Jia-Wu*, *Yi-Wei*, *Ren-Yin*, *Gui-Mao*, *Geng-Xu*, and *Xin-Hai* are likewise the same. This is because Metal corresponds to the **Shang Tone**; the **Grand Master of Music** <note>original: "Taishi" (太師)</note> carefully examines the *Shang* tone and divides it accordingly.

<meta>This page continues the theoretical exploration of ritual music, moving from the physical arrangement of dancers to the complex mathematical and cosmological relationships between the musical pitches (the Sixty Sounds) and the five-tone scale. From the cycles of Water—starting with the **Bingwu** <note>The 43rd year of the sexagenary cycle</note>—to the cycles of Wood—ending with **Renzi** and **Renwu**—the way these elements "marry" and align is much like the primary **Jiazi** and **Jiawu** combinations. In this system of opposites and continuous flow, where beginnings and endings follow a strict order, the divisions inevitably harmonize to form the **Sixty Sounds** <note>original: "Liushi Yin" (六十音); a complex system expanding the standard pitches into a full cycle of 60 to match the calendar</note>.

Generally, in the musical sequences of the **Yang** (active) principle, the <term>Jue<note>The third note of the traditional five-tone scale, often associated with Wood and Spring.</note></term> and <term>Yu<note>The fifth note of the scale, associated with Water and Winter.</note></term> tones are found in the preceding pitches. In the musical sequences of the **Yin** (passive) principle, the <term>Shang<note>The second note of the scale, associated with Metal and Autumn.</note></term> and <term>Zhi<note>The fourth note of the scale, associated with Fire and Summer.</note></term> tones are found in the following pitches.

It is necessary for the **Jue** and **Yu** tones to arise together first; only then do the **Shang** and the lower **Zhi** follow. Thus, the <term>Gong<note>The first and most fundamental note of the scale, representing the "Palace" or the center (Earth).</note></term> tone always acts as the master of the **Jue**—though the **Jue** tone may depart, it must always return. Similarly, the **Shang** tone always acts as the master of the **Zhi**—though they may separate, they must eventually reunite.

Although the **Gong** tone governs the **Jue**, there must still exist a "Yu-aspect of Jue" and a "Jue-aspect of Yu" within it. Likewise, although **Shang** governs **Zhi**, these sub-tones of **Jue** and **Yu** are also present. When the sounds correspond, the **Jue** tone functions as the **Zhi**. 

The hierarchy of instruments is governed as follows:
*   The <term>Qin and Se<note>Ancient silk-stringed zithers.</note></term> zithers are led by the **Jue** and **Yu** tones, prioritizing the **Gong** tone.
*   The "Yu-aspect of Jue" is expressed by the **Bells**, which prioritize the **Yu** tone.
*   The "Jue-aspect of Yu" is expressed by the **Stone Chimes**, which prioritize the **Jue** tone.

Through the cycle of "transposing to the Gong tone," with one **Gong**, one **Yu**, and one **Jue**—and through fifteen beats of the drum—the transformations of the **Five Tones** are completed.

In the final month of Spring, the pitch corresponds to <term>Old Purification<note>original: "Guxi" (姑洗); the third of the twelve standard pitches.</note></term>. At this time, the "Dragon" position <note>The earthly branch "Chen"</note> corresponds to the **Jue** tone. The sun resides in the "Great Bridge" constellation <note>original: "Daliang" (大梁)</note>, while the "Rooster" position <note>The earthly branch "You"</note> corresponds to the **Yu** tone. In this moment, the vital energies of the **Yu** and **Jue** tones reach a state of perfect harmony.

<meta>This page continues the technical exposition of ritual music theory. While the previous page focused on the mathematical alignment of the Sixty Sounds and the "marriages" of elements, this section moves into the practical application of these tones within specific ritual dances—the *Da Shao* and *Da Wu*—and their role in the educational and seasonal cycles of the imperial court.</meta>

Because the *South Wind* <note>original: "Nan Feng" (南風); a legendary song attributed to the sage-king Shun, expressing a ruler's care for his people</note> is sung, the Great Harmony of Music is performed, using the *Great Shao* dance <note>original: "Da Shao" (大韶); the ritual music and dance associated with the peaceful reign of Emperor Shun</note> as its primary element. The musical transformation begins with the <term>Jue tone<note>The third note of the five-tone scale, associated with Wood and the East.</note></term>, while the <term>Yu tone<note>The fifth note of the scale, associated with Water and the North.</note></term> follows the <term>Gong tone<note>The first and fundamental "Palace" note, associated with Earth and the Center.</note></term>.

As *Yu* corresponds to Water and <term>Zhi<note>The fourth note of the scale, associated with Fire and the South.</note></term> corresponds to Fire, the "Six Offices" <note>The management of Water, Fire, Wood, Metal, Earth, and Grain</note> and the "Three Affairs" <note>Standardizing the people's livelihood, virtue, and rectitude</note> are together called the *Song of the Nine Virtues*. Through nine transformations of the music, the *Jue-Shao* and *Zhi-Shao* <note>Specific modal variations of the Shao music</note> are completed. This is simply a matter of aligning the physical form of the dance with the harmony of the sounds to capture the symbolic images of the cosmos.

Consequently, in the final month of Autumn, during the Great Feast for the Emperor, the musical pitch corresponds to the *Wuyi* <note>The 11th of the twelve standard pitches</note>, which occupies the *Xu* position <note>The 11th branch of the zodiac, associated with the dog</note>; this is the *Jue* tone of the *Ruibin* pitch <note>The 7th pitch, associated with the summer solstice</note>. It is also said to be in the *Dahuo* <note>the "Great Fire" or Antares constellation</note> position of *Mao* <note>The 4th zodiac branch, associated with the rabbit</note>, which is the *Yu* tone of *Ruibin*. When the *Wuyi* pitch is played and the *Jiazhong* pitch is sung, the performance must center on the *Great Wu* dance <note>original: "Da Wu" (大武); the ritual dance celebrating the military victory of King Wu of Zhou</note>. 

The Grand Minister of Music <note>original: "Dasi Yue" (大司樂)</note> oversees this to instruct the sons of the nobility, and specific officials are appointed to divide the various responsibilities: 

*   The **Music Master** teaches the dance. 
*   The **Flute Master** <note>original: "Yueshi" (籥師)</note> performs the dance with feathers. 
*   The **Master of Shields** <note>original: "Sigan" (司干)</note> provides the ritual weapons. 
*   The **Grand Assistant** <note>original: "Daxu" (大胥)</note> manages the registration tablets of the scholars to await the arrival of the students.

In the Spring, students enter the school, perform the "offering of vegetables" ritual, and harmonize the dance; this is intended to "nourish the elderly" at the **Biyong** <note>The Imperial Academy, traditionally surrounded by a moat</note>. In the Autumn, the curriculum is distributed and the sounds are harmonized; this is intended to feast the Emperor at the **Mingtang** <note>The "Hall of Distinction," a central ritual site for imperial ceremonies</note>. The four cardinal directions—**Kan** (North), **Li** (South), **Zhen** (East), and **Dui** (West)—along with the four corners—**Qian** (Northwest), **Kun** (Southwest), **Gen** (Northeast), and **Xun** (Southeast)—broadcast the **Eight Sounds** <note>The traditional classification of musical instruments based on material: metal, stone, silk, bamboo, gourd, clay, hide, and wood.</note> and set the **Eight Winds** in motion.

As **Water** <note>associated with the North/Kan</note> moistens and **Wood** <note>associated with the East/Zhen</note> rises, the **Yang** (active/light) principle manifests through benevolence; therefore, these elements remain in their constant, natural positions. However, as **Fire** melts and **Metal** is molded, the **Yin** (passive/dark) principle is hidden within its practical application; therefore, they exchange their positions in the diagram. 

Thus it is said: "The **River Map** <note>original: "Hetu" (河圖)</note> and the **Scroll of the River Luo** are the surface and the lining of one another; the **Eight Trigrams** and the **Nine Chapters** are the warp and the woof of the universe." 

This is precisely what the *Classic of Changes* <note>original: "Yi" (易), referring to the I Ching</note> means when it says: 
*   "The Emperor <note>Referring to the creative principle of nature or the Sun.</note> comes forth in the **Stirring** (Zhen/East);
*   He brings all things to harmony in the **Mild** (Xun/Southeast);
*   They perceive one another in the **Clarity** (Li/South);
*   They are put to service in the **Receptive** (Kun/Southwest);
*   They rejoice in the **Joyful** (Dui/West);
*   They contend in the **Creative** (Qian/Northwest);
*   They toil in the **Abysmal** (Kan/North);
*   And they reach completion in the **Stillness** (Gen/Northeast)."

The **River Map** represents the "Pre-Heaven" state <note>original: "Xiantian" (先天); the primordial, abstract realm of energy.</note>, using **Qi** (vital breath) to give shape to sound. The **Scroll of the River Luo** represents the "Post-Heaven" state <note>original: "Houtian" (後天); the physical, manifest world.</note>, using physical matter to complete the forms of objects. 

The scholar **Chen Yang** <note>A famous music theorist of the Song Dynasty, author of the "Book of Music" (Yueshu).</note> claimed that:
*   The **Kan** trigram corresponds to the sound of **Hide**; 
*   **Gen** to **Gourd**; 
*   **Zhen** to **Bamboo**; 
*   **Xun** to **Silk**; 
*   **Kun** to **Clay**; 
*   **Dui** to **Metal**; 
*   **Qian** to **Stone**.

Therefore, we now follow the interpretations found in the *Comprehensive Discussions in the White Tiger Hall* <note>original: "Baihu Tong" (白虎通); a major Han Dynasty text that standardized Confucian cosmology and social etiquette.</note>.

Subtracting one-third yields fifty-four, which becomes **Zhi** <note>The fifth note of the scale</note>. Adding one-third to that yields seventy-two, which becomes **Shang** <note>The second note of the scale</note>. Subtracting one-third yields forty-eight, which becomes **Yu** <note>The sixth note of the scale</note>. This single statement encompasses the entire principle.

# **Principles of Ritual Orchestration**

Music in the **Upper Hall** is centered primarily on **singing**. *The Book of Rites of the Elder Dai* <note>original: "Da Dai Li"</note> states that one should suspend a single set of chime stones and prioritize the **hand-drum** <term>fu: a small, barrel-shaped drum filled with rice hulls, used to keep time</term>. Former high officials stated that suspending a single bell while prioritizing the hand-drum, and utilizing **large bronze bells** and **jade chime stones**, symbolizes the "Sound of the Single Emperor" <note>This signifies the absolute unity and central authority of the sovereign</note>.

The "Positions in the Hall of Distinction" <note>original: "Ming Tang Wei," a chapter in the *Book of Rites*</note> says that the hand-drum and jade chimes are used together. The **hand-drum** <gloss>a small drum placed in the East</gloss> is struck to signal the start, which is the same function as the "Starter." This refers to striking the **Zhu** <term>Zhu: a wooden box struck with a mallet to start a piece of music</term>. <gloss>The "Stopper" is placed in the West to regulate the rhythm and signal the end of the music.</gloss>

Music in the **Lower Hall** is centered primarily on **pipe instruments**. The **Yellow Bell** <term>Huangzhong: the fundamental pitch of the Chinese gamut, roughly equivalent to F or F-sharp</term> faces South to symbolize the Ruler. The **Circular Bell** represents the vital energy (*Qi*) of the Celestial Emperor’s Hall of Distinction. The pitches of **Equalizing Standard** <note>original: "Yize"</note> and **Flowing Zhì** <note>original: "Liuzhi"</note> are harmonized with it. Bronze instruments play and jade instruments respond from above; the arrangement of the **Yellow Bell** in suspended tiers corresponds to the "Way of Heaven."

To the East and West, the **mouth organs** (*sheng*) and **large bells** (*yong*) play in alternating intervals. Together with the **Elegant** and **Hymnal** melodies <note>original: "Ya" and "Song," referring to the classical poetic modes of the *Book of Odes*</note> and the **Large Zithers** <note>original: "Qin" and "Se"</note>, they are all harmonized to a single standard. All these instruments are ultimately governed by the pitch-pipes.

In the four "suspensions" <note>referring to the four sides of the instrument racks</note>, there are sixteen bells or stones each, and each set possesses four **"Clear Sounds"** <term>Qingsheng: higher-pitched or "sharped" notes used for modulation</term>. In the Zhou dynasty, they used seven pitches plus three Clear Sounds, totaling nineteen. For the twelve sets of instrument frames, this totaled 228 individual pieces, which were used to provide music for the "Three Offerings" during the Imperial meal.

At the end of the Tang dynasty, **Yin Yingsun** utilized twenty suspensions; with forty frames, this totaled 240 pieces. In the Song dynasty, **Chen Ci** used twelve suspensions, combining the **ocarina** (*xun*), the **transverse flute** (*chi*), and the **bamboo flute** (*di*), with twelve of each instrument. The scholar **Yang Xiong** said: "When the music is central and correct, it is Elegant."

## The Theory of the Pitch-Pipes and the Supreme Yellow Bell

The *Treatise on Pitch-pipes and the Calendar* in the *Book of Han* <note>original: *Han Qian Zhi*, referring to the Han Shu, the history of the Han Dynasty</note> states that when the **Yellow Bell** <term>Huangzhong: the fundamental pitch of ancient Chinese music, from which all other measurements were derived</term> serves as the Tonic <note>the first note of the scale, known as *Gong*</note>, then the **Great Frame** <term>Taicu</term>, the **Old Purified** <term>Guxi</term>, the **Forest Bell** <term>Linzhong</term>, and the **Southern Pipe** <term>Nanlü</term> all respond with pure tones. In this arrangement, there are no infinitesimal remainders <note>original: *huwei*, referring to tiny mathematical fractions that complicate tuning</note>. The Yellow Bell does not have to serve as a subordinate to any other pitch. This represents the principle of "Unity of Heart and Single Rule." However, if a pitch other than the Yellow Bell is used as the Tonic—even during its own assigned month—the responding pitches will inevitably have gaps or tiny remainders in their mathematical ratios, failing to achieve perfect correctness. This demonstrates that the Yellow Bell is the most transitionally supreme and has no equal.

The previous "Diagram of the Generation of the Pitches" followed the sequence of "generation by eighths" <note>a system similar to the Circle of Fifths, where each new note is found by counting eight steps across the twelve pitches</note>. The current diagram, however, takes those generated pitches and arranges them by their relationships. The six Yang pitches <term>Lü</term> are placed in their proper positions, while the six Yin pitches <term>Lü</term> are placed in their opposing positions <note>original: *chong*, referring to the opposite side of the zodiac circle</note>. For example, the **Yellow Bell** generates the **Forest Bell** at the eighth interval. The Forest Bell normally belongs to the position of *Wei* <note>the 8th Earthly Branch, the Goat</note>, but here it is placed in the position of *Chou* <note>the 2nd Earthly Branch, the Ox, which is directly opposite the Goat</note>. The remaining five Yin pitches follow this same pattern of reversal. This layout specifically illustrates the principle of "Unity of Heart and Single Rule," signifying that the Sovereign <note>the Yang pitch</note> is never made to serve as a minister <note>the Yin pitch</note>.
