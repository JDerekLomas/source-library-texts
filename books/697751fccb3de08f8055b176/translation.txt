Therefore, since such a good Christian purpose or discourse by the holy and godly *Augustine* <note>St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430 AD), a foundational theologian whose works emphasized the importance of language in interpreting Scripture.</note> (which I have just cited) also occurred to my good friends Georg Raben, Sigmund Feyerabend, and the heirs of Weygand Hanen <note>These were prominent 16th-century printers in Frankfurt, known for their high-quality illustrated Bibles.</note>, printers—who for their own part, with serious and unceasing diligence, are at it day and night, or arrange through others to ensure that in their art and printing business they may show all possible service to the common good, and especially to our good-hearted German people, not only in other matters but also in this place, and may invest their talents <note>original: "Pfündlin" – a reference to the Parable of the Talents in Luke 19, implying one's God-given skills or resources.</note> well and fruitfully—they have many times approached me and quite earnestly insisted that I undertake this work for their service and for the great benefit of many people. They asked that I interpret the Hebrew, Chaldean, Syriac, Greek, and Latin proper names or words which occur here and there in the Bible, in both the Old and New Testaments, and which have not yet all been translated or explained in our mother tongue. I was to explain and set them forth in understandable, good German, so that they might be able to attach such a work to the end of the German Bible, which they have now printed several times with a beautiful, legible typeface.

Now, although I would have preferred to refer this work to others more experienced and better-versed in these matters—both because of my own slight understanding and because, as a "half-Netherlander" <note>The author likely spoke a Low German or Dutch dialect, making the "High German" used in formal printing feel like a distinct and challenging idiom.</note>, expressing myself in proper High German and setting it forth according to its due character and power is somewhat difficult and hard for me—the common good nevertheless lay so heavily and firmly in my mind and ears. I am inclined to serve this common good according to the divine grace granted to me, and also out of the good friendship I have had with the aforementioned printers for several years. Thus, I have dared to undertake the task in the name of the Lord and begin the business, subject to the correction of others. In this work, however, nothing has been produced from my own uncertain interpretation, nor—as I hope—will anything absurd be treated or presented.
