->THE CEMETERY<-
->OF AMBOISE.<-

I LOVE to direct my steps to the sanctuary of the dead.
There, dying to falsehood, I require less effort
To understand their language and grasp their thought:
For the dead do not hold that senseless idea
That everything is extinguished within man. In them, everything is alive.
For them, there is no more silence. Near them, one hears
The sobs of the sinner; the rages of the ungodly;
The hymns of the wise; and the sweet harmony
Of those whose friendship, zeal, and virtue
Formed but a single heart while they lived.
O Man, it is here below that it was born,
This nothingness <note>The author is critiquing the materialist philosophy of his era, which suggested that death was a total annihilation of the self.</note> to which they wish to condemn your essence;
And it is your own error that serves as its support.
You know everything! You can do everything! And yet you wish to be nothing!....
To be nothing!.... and yet to grasp and judge the light!....
Leave to the strayed man these dreams of the earth;
We were merely dozing in our dark bodies.
When time <term>tems</term> <note>An archaic French spelling of *temps*.</note> tears us from their muddy remains,
The hour that awakens us is an eternal hour.
Oh, righteous one, what raptures! What new splendor!
You take on another body in the crucible of the tomb;
A vivid radiance, ever more brilliant and more beautiful;
A sharper gaze; a more resonant voice;
Even a purer heart. Just as when I evaporate
These coarse fluids where the salt <note>Saint-Martin often uses alchemical terminology; here, "salt" represents the fundamental essence or "body" of a substance being purified of its "coarse" liquid elements.</note> is captive,
Its fire regains its strength and becomes more active.

<summary>In this opening of the poem, Saint-Martin rejects the materialist idea that death is the end of existence, describing the cemetery as a place of vibrant spiritual life and transformation. He uses alchemical metaphors, such as the crucible and the purification of salt, to illustrate how the soul is refined and empowered after leaving the physical body.</summary>

On this mound, near the place that saw my birth,
I wandered alone. For this rural site, our tombs
Inspired in me a sweet and religious attraction.
Wise Burlamaqui <note>Jean-Jacques Burlamaqui (1694–1748) was a Swiss legal philosopher. His works on natural law, which Saint-Martin studied in his youth, emphasized that law is based on reason and the pursuit of happiness.</note>, it was not far from these places
That you sanctified the dawn of my age;
That a sacred fire, emerging from your profound work,
Shaking my whole body with holy shudders,
Engraved within me the foundations of justice:
Favors, in my springtime, so new, so divine!
But which hid, alas! stinging thorns!
Time <term>tems</term> <note>An archaic spelling of the French word "temps."</note> made them bloom. So I meditated
On our days of sorrow. Pensive, I measured
This long blindness that we call life.
What torments! What disgusts! In my melancholy,
I could distinguish nothing. All around these fields,
I hardly saw those elegant gardens,
Where Choiseul <note>The Duke of Choiseul (1719–1785), a powerful minister under Louis XV, retired to his magnificent estate, the Château de Chanteloup, near Amboise.</note> displayed pomp and opulence;
Those modest rocks inhabited by poverty;
That famous castle <note>The Château d'Amboise, a former royal residence of the Valois dynasty and the site of many historical tragedies, including the 1560 "Amboise Conspiracy."</note> which once saw the birth
Of the too-famed misfortunes of the reign of the Valois.
A mourning even seemed to me—oh! plaintive nature—
To veil all these treasures with which you adorn yourself;
These harvests, these forests, these scattered animals,
This river, this beautiful sky offered to my gaze.
Happy is he who can still, contemplating your works,
Draw from them each day sublime images;
And knowing how to spread a brilliant color upon them,
Soften all hearts by striking the minds!
But, man, dear object of my solicitude,
It is you who forbid me this attractive study;
It is your hand that covered nature with mourning,
And made of her throne a lugubrious coffin;
And when everything is snatched from me in this place of distress,

Your reason, aggravating the grief that oppresses me,
Wishes still to close to me the path to your heart,
And leave my sorrow to isolate itself in mine.
I compared the different caprices of fate,
Successes, setbacks, wealth, and injustices,
Emerging blindly from its blind hands,
Following, blindly, blind human beings.
Sadly, I said to myself: Without a common law,
Which alone might balance these games of fortune,
And which, uniting us by an equal destiny,
Served us as a beacon in our obscurity,
Man would no longer know his origin;
Believing himself separated from the divine source,
He would create Gods for himself, and his imprudent vows
Would offer his incense to the stars, to chance.
But this severe decree that a sovereign law
Pronounces with brilliance to the human family;
This decree that says only to us: *you must die*;
And which we alone know before undergoing it,
Opposes its barrier to such wanderings,
And sheds a vivid light upon our being.
Death, by forcing us into fraternity,
Wishes to paint for our spirit that holy unity,
Where love awaits us; where piety shines;
Where, in a pure abode, the father of the family <note>A mystical reference to God as the head of the universal human family.</note>,
Lavishing treasures that are constantly reborn,
Takes pleasure in merging with all his children;
And has nothing that his heart does not share with them.
Let us take the testimony of nature here.
Every body is the product of concentrated elements,
Which seem to be frustrated of their liberty.
Each of them, in leaving the corporal form,
Goes by degrees to find its original base.

If there exists within us a divine element,
The same law leads it to the same end.
We become Gods when we are decomposed;
And for man, death is an apotheosis <note>From the Greek *apotheosis*, meaning "to make a god." Saint-Martin suggests that death is not an end, but a transformation back into a divine state.</note>.
Thus this unity appears again before our eyes;
And if we can only see it in the heavens,
Here, in this decree, its image is present.
Who would not see in it even a benevolent hand?
Man reads his sentence from his first moments,
So that, like a new Levite <note>The Levites were the members of the Hebrew tribe of Levi who served in the Tabernacle and the Temple, responsible for religious duties and sacrifices.</note>, he may meditate for a long time,
In this sacred book, upon the laws of sacrifice,
And learn at what price they became favorable.
These laws have nothing to revive in the animal;
It is ignorant of its death; it does not know how to love.
What then would this eloquent image be for it,
If it is not permitted to understand its use?
But you, mortal, but you who, under various features,
Have read this unity in man and the universe;
And can touch nothing that does not reveal it to you,
How can you justify your criminal error?
In your vast projects, in your noble efforts,
Your thought is always the idol of your body;
It is always to the spirit that you sacrifice yourself;
You go about everywhere showing Gods and Geniuses,
Consecrating every object; every day; every place;
And deifying everything at last, except God.

I approach at this moment the funerary temple:
Oh! dead ones, console me in my bitter sadness;
I can only entrust my sorrows to you alone.
They would not believe me, the miserable humans,
If I depicted their deep wounds to them.
Wholly given to their disdain, wholly to their murmurs,

What would the tears of a mortal produce upon them!
There, my inclination leads me to take as an altar
One of those tombs with which the enclosure is filled.
The being whose remains sleep buried there
Was meant to serve as an offering: an invisible hand,
No doubt, guided me in this pious design.
My choice did not fall upon those whom birth,
Fortune, or the pride of a vain science,
Had surrounded with a borrowed luster;
I would have feared that in them some deformity,
Some stain might have caused my offering to be rejected.
To have it pure, just as the law demands,
A secret movement caused my choice to lean
Upon the young Alexis, a humble villager,
Who, in piety, labor, and misery,
Had just finished a short career.
This new Jeremiah <note>Jeremiah is the Old Testament prophet known as the "weeping prophet" for his laments over the sins of his people. Saint-Martin compares the humble villager to him to emphasize his holy sorrow for the world.</note> flooded with his tears
These fields where, every day, he poured out his sweat;
These fields where, now, his remains rest.
Our errors, our dangers were the only cause of it:
It was not his own ills; he found himself content.
A wretched day-laborer <term>journalier</term> <note>A manual laborer hired and paid by the day, typically representing the poorest class of agricultural worker.</note>; but active, patient,
Despite his misfortune, it is known in the countryside
If ever a complaint had entered his heart:
Everyone looked upon him as an angel of peace.
The poor, frequently experiencing his benefits,
Received from his hand his own sustenance.
And when we said to him: Alexis, prudence
Would allow you to act less generously;
The sensitive Alexis answered while weeping,
Just like that Indian to the good missionary:
*See how God thereby becomes my debtor.* <note>original: "Voyez que Dieu par là devient mon tributaire." This reflects the biblical proverb that "he who has pity on the poor lends to the Lord."</note>
Such was this lamb who was chosen by me.

In the burning zeal with which my heart is seized—
And what zeal ever appeared more legitimate!—
In spirit, beside me, I picture the victim;
I take it, prepare it, and place it upon the altar;
My hand sprinkles it with oil, and covers it with salt; <note>Saint-Martin uses the imagery of ancient Hebrew sacrifices, where oil and salt were essential elements of an offering to God, symbolizing consecration and the preservation of a covenant.</note>
My desires and my tears serve as my lustral water, <note>Lustral water was used in ancient purification rituals to cleanse a person or space before a sacred act.</note>
And soon from my breast, a long sigh exhales:
"God of love and peace, who in man has sown
The seeds of your glory, and who formed him
Only to cultivate them; by you, I implore you
To grant my prayers, if the victim is pure.
These dead who are here, who, of their sorrowful days,
Under the eye of your justice, have finished the course,
Could they not serve the plans of your tenderness!
To heal your children, oh! profound wisdom,
Is not everything ranked among your powerful means!
Rise up, dead, oh! you, my true fellow citizens;
God permits it, leave the abode of life; <note>A poetic paradox where the "abode of life" refers to the spiritual world or the grave, suggesting that the dead are more "alive" than the living.</note>
See again for a moment your human homeland,
Your friends, your relatives; let everyone, in these regions,
Through you, learn the lessons of wisdom!
The sepulcher, in opening to their fragile remains,
One day, will swallow up their fatal passions.
There they will see sleeping, beside the assassin,
Those whose breasts his fury has pierced;
The starving pauper beside the miser
Who will have rebuffed him in his barbaric disdain;
Beside the ingrate his zealous benefactor,
And the innocent beside his persecutor.
Come and expose to them these prophetic tableaus;
Present to the living these peaceful lessons,
And let them all, from this world, be so many friends."

A voice, which I take for that of Alexis, <note>Alexis is likely a reference to a spiritual guide or a deceased friend. In Saint-Martin's circle, "Alexis" was often a pseudonym for a member of his mystical community or a symbolic figure of spiritual initiation.</note>
From above, upon my altar, suddenly seems to descend;
To the depths of my heart it makes itself heard;
I listened to it speak, filled with a holy dread;
It seemed to say to me: "Friend, be reassured,
Your prayers are pure; the God of love and justice,
With a favorable gaze has seen your sacrifice.
To the highest heavens your incense has risen;
And it will not be to your city alone
That the dead will lend their salutary support.
One day they will traverse all the places of the earth,
To aid its courage in disastrous times.
Iniquity increases; these injurious sounds,
These blasphemies issued from the breast of arrogance,
Soon, from heaven itself, will arm the power.
In those unhappy days, everywhere the air will groan;
The stars will weep; the marble will complain;
By the force of fire the waters will be dried up;
By the force of winds a thousand fires will be born;
All the volcanoes of the globe will erupt at once;
The elements at war will clash with one another;
All will find their voice, and through terrifying signs,
Will teach the wicked what fate they deserve.
Alexis who announces these scourges to you today,
When living, was not alone in weeping for all these ills;
And even now he counts within the walls of your city,
Three brothers of sorrow. <note>original: "Trois frères de douleur." This likely refers to a small group of initiates or fellow "Martinists" living in Amboise who shared Saint-Martin's mystical views.</note> He would count a thousand
Who keep watch in France. There is no nation,
One might say, no place, that does not share in this gift.
God never surprises; and his supreme goodness,
Without rest, paints for mortals their extreme peril."
"You then, who make the dead witnesses to your torments,
Let your tears also be addressed to the living;"
