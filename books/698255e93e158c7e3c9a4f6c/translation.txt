# Saltpeter.

**A**ccording to chemical experience, <term>saltpeter (Salpeter): Also known as potassium nitrate, a vital ingredient in the production of gunpowder and a key subject of early modern chemistry</term>, as it appears visible to our senses, is a <term>neutral salt (Mittelsalz): A substance formed by the perfect balance of an acid and a base, possessing the qualities of neither</term>, which consists of a particular acid, which one calls <term>nitric acid (Salpetersäure): A highly corrosive mineral acid, historically known as aqua fortis</term>, and of a <term>fixed vegetable alkali (feuerbeständiges, vegetabilisches Laugensalz): A salt derived from plant ashes—specifically potash—that remains stable when exposed to heat</term>, which is combined with this acid until it reaches saturation.

The proximate components, perceptible to our senses, are therefore:

1. An acid unique to saltpeter.
2. A fixed vegetable alkali, with which this acid is combined.

It is not enough, however, to know the proximate components; we must also seek the more remote ones, and therefore decompose the nitric acid once again.

# Nitric Acid.

Nitric acid consists of a particular base, which is called <term>saltpeter-matter (Salpeterstoff): An early term for the nitrogenous base of the acid</term>, and which is combined with <term>oxygen (Sauerstoff): Literally "acid-stuff," reflecting the 18th-century belief that oxygen was the principle that made substances acidic</term>.

This saltpeter-matter is a gaseous fluid that forms whenever a certain portion of <term>nitrogen (Stickluft): Literally "suffocating air," so named because it does not support life or combustion</term> combines with <term>oxygen (Lebensluft): Literally "vital air" or "life air"</term>. Consequently, the more remote components in nitric acid are nitrogen and oxygen, which together form <term>nitrous air (nitröse Luft): Known today as nitric oxide</term>, and as they combine further with oxygen, they produce nitric acid.

## Natural Law in the Production of Saltpeter.

This complete analysis and examination of both the immediate and remote components clarifies for us the reasons why saltpeter could, until now, only be developed through vegetable fermentation and animal putrefaction. <note>The author is connecting the "New Chemistry" of gases (like oxygen and nitrogen) to the traditional methods of saltpeter "farming" from decaying organic waste.</note>

In every instance of animal putrefaction, a quantity of <term>nitrogen gas (Stickgas): Literally "suffocating gas"</term> is developed, which rises from the decaying animal substances and stands in exact proportion to the amount of ammonia, or <term>volatile alkali (flüchtiges Alkali): Ammonia gas</term>.

Through the fermentation of vegetable bodies, <term>nitrogen air (Stickluft): Another term for nitrogen gas</term> is also developed, which combines with the <term>vital air (Lebensluft): Oxygen</term> of the atmosphere and forms the "saltpeter-matter," or the nitrous gas. # Conclusions.

1) According to the amount of <term>nitrogen (Stickluft): Literally "suffocating air"</term> and <term>oxygen (Lebensluft): Literally "life air"</term> that combine with one another, the amount of the produced <term>nitrous gas (nitröse Luft)</term> is determined—or, the <term>nitrous principle (Salpeterstoff)</term> is proportional to the quantity of nitrogen and oxygen that combine.

2) The amount of <term>nitric acid (Salpetersäure)</term> corresponds to the amount of <term>nitrous gas</term> that combines with <term>oxygen (Säuernstoff): Literally "acid-matter"</term>. <note>In the chemical nomenclature of the time, oxygen was often called "acid-matter" because it was believed to be the essential component of all acids.</note>

3) And the amount of produced <term>saltpeter (Salpeter)</term> is proportional to the amount of nitric acid combined in specific proportions with the vegetable, fire-resistant <term>alkali</term>. <note>The "vegetable, fire-resistant alkali" refers to potassium carbonate, often obtained from wood ash, which serves as the base for potassium nitrate.</note>

4) The purity of the saltpeter corresponds to the purity of the acid.

5) The purity of the acid corresponds to the purity of the nitrous principle.

6) And the purity of the nitrous principle corresponds to the greater amount of oxygen that has combined with the nitrogen.

Therefore, no one will doubt anymore that it is possible to produce saltpeter by artful means <note>The author uses "art" (Kunst) in the classical sense of "artificial" or "human-made," as opposed to occurring spontaneously in nature.</note>, as the imitation of nature consists merely in this:

## Chemical Law of Deflagration.

Deflagration <note>original: "Verpuffen." The rapid combustion or "puffing off" of a substance.</note> is only to be sought in **nitric acid** <note>original: "Salpetersäure." In this period, saltpeter (potassium nitrate) was viewed as a solid form of this acid.</note>. The types of air present in nitric acid, such as: <term>nitrogen</term> <note>original: "Stickluft." Literally "suffocating air."</note>, <term>oxygen</term> <note>original: "Lebensluft." Literally "life-air."</note>, and <term>hydrogen</term> <note>original: "brennbare Luft." Literally "flammable air."</note> act against one another with violence. At the moment of ignition, a portion of the **oxygen** exerts an effort to produce water by combining with the released <term>caloric</term> <note>original: "Wärmestoff." A historical term for the hypothetical fluid substance of heat.</note>; but since, at the moment of this effort, the **nitrogen gas** <note>original: "Stickgas."</note> volatilizes the **hydrogen** even further, it pulls the **oxygen** back to itself with force and brings it to a state of inflammability, after which the **detonation** occurs.
