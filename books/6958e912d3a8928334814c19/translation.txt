# PREFACE
### TO THE
## READER.

<image-desc>Large ornamental woodcut initial 'C' featuring intricate floral scrollwork and architectural motifs.</image-desc>

WHEN we first applied our mind seriously to botanical studies, under the auspices of the Most Serene Prince Gaston of France <note>Gaston, Duke of Orléans (1608–1660), a major patron of botany and owner of the famous gardens at Blois.</note>, we observed that many—indeed, almost all—authors writing on the subject of botany did so confusedly and without order or method. This was true even though not a few men of exquisite erudition had put great effort into publishing and illustrating their labors. Among the Italians, there were Pietro Andrea Mattioli, Andrea Cesalpino, and Fabio Colonna; among the French, Jean Ruel and Jacques Daléchamps; among the Germans, Otto Brunfels, Valerius and Euricius Cordus (father and son), Hieronymus Bock <note>original: "Tragus"</note>, Leonhart Fuchs, Jacobus Theodorus Tabernaemontanus, and Joachim Camerarius; among the Swiss, both the Bauhin brothers, Caspar and Johann, and Conrad Gessner; among the Flemish, Charles de l'Écluse <note>original: "Clusius"</note>, Rembert Dodoens, and Matthias de L'Obel; among the English, William Turner, John Gerard, Thomas Johnson, and John Parkinson. All these, along with three hundred others of lesser note among various nations, have toiled in the field of botany; yet not all of them, nor any one of them, has succeeded in uncovering a true and genuine method—one that is always and constantly the same, or always maintaining itself in the same way—since the third day of the world's creation <note>A reference to the Biblical creation of plants on the third day.</note>. For this reason, we repeatedly considered that it would be a task of no small value if, after examining all the authors recited above and many other more recent ones, we were to undertake a more general History of Plants, begun some years ago (while we devoted our service for a decade to his aforementioned Highness, the Duke of Orléans). Although both the magnitude of the labor and the difficulty of completing it deterred us from such an undertaking—add to this the immense sum of expenses and the tedious and nauseating crowd of painters, engravers, printers, and others of that sort who practice manual arts—nevertheless, certain friends here encouraged us. These are men famous enough throughout the world, not only highly learned but also devoted to the public good (to whose judgment we have granted much weight since our first acquaintance with them). They promised themselves that this would be performed by us all the more happily and easily because our reputation had spread among botanists throughout all Europe some years ago, and because we had been urged even from afar by certain persons of greater note to undertake this great work. Stimulated, therefore, partly by their counsel and exhortation, and partly by our own zeal and affection toward the botanical commonwealth <term>republicam Botanicam: the international community of botanical scholars</term>, we gird ourselves for this immense labor, not without favorable omens. Let students of botany understand that among the botanists cited above, and others whom we have not yet mentioned, nothing has been found until now but a confused chaos and disorder <note>original: "αταξιαν" (ataxian)</note> in arranging and rightly associating plants. It is of great interest to the botanical commonwealth that a true and genuine method, given by God and nature from the beginning of the world—indeed, one perfect in all its parts—should come forth. I would further wish the Reader to observe that I do not intend to detract from the attempts of so many and such great learned men; if they stumbled in many things, they should be forgiven, since they held out a torch for us who follow. And because we must occasionally point out where they erred, even though they performed their duty strenuously, it remains for us to show those things which they themselves admit escaped them (as seen in the "Hallucinations" <note>A common term in early modern science for errors or oversights in previous works.</note> of theirs which we have cited). By our exhibited Method, we shall arrange plants by genera and species, defining the genera and describing the species, presenting them through Tables of kinship and affinity. I say we shall arrange both genera and species by a "token" or essential mark sought from the ultimate purpose of all the plants contained in these five Sections, maintaining a coherence from Section to Section as much as possible.

Botanists will observe what has been achieved in describing these Tables of ours regarding plants. Although many might perhaps think the plants are described by us with a prolix style, we have nevertheless preferred—indeed, chosen—to defer to the judgment of others skilled in the botanical art and to any fair-minded person, rather than to boast much of our own labors. However, we dare to affirm and profess (and indeed, as we hope, without a mark of arrogance) that we have labored and striven with all zeal so that the histories of individual plants might be set forth clearly, lucidly, and as briefly as could conveniently be done (cutting away the superfluous)

...whether descriptions were being presented, I have set forth an order of classes or sections that is as convenient and efficient as possible, arranging the plants according to **genera** and **species**. I have likewise provided the "essential marks" given by Nature for the broad and intermediate genera, which make plants easy to identify by certain signs. With what labor, care, and industry these have been organized by us will be easily recognized by anyone who chooses to observe and more attentively weigh the multitude of plants shown and described in these Tables of ours, as well as their variety. In the immense multitude of Herbs, I see a lack of that which is usually most desired in any other disordered troop; for unless they are reduced to orders and distributed into their classes like the battle lines of a camp, everything must necessarily be disturbed by tumult and fluctuation. We see this happening even now in the examination of plants: for when the intellect is overwhelmed by a disordered multitude, inextricable errors and peevish altercations for the most part arise. If the proper genus is unknown, no description, however accurately delivered, demonstrates a certain plant, but instead usually deceives; for when the genera are confused, everything (at least the species) must be confused. Since, therefore, all science consists in the collection of similar things and the distinction of dissimilar things, and this distribution into genera and species is like classes according to differences indicating the nature of the thing, I have attempted to provide this in a universal history of plants, so that if I have made any progress in such a study—despite the weakness of my talent—I might bring it forth for the common good. But the order which is assigned according to the "fellowship of natures" <note>The author refers to natural affinities or biological relationships</note> is found to be the easiest, safest, and most useful of all, both for aiding the memory and for contemplating their properties. It is easiest because the almost immense number of plants is enclosed in a compendium under organized genera, so much so that anyone may reduce plants never seen before into their proper class, and, if they are unnamed, call them by the name of their genus. Finally, even the medicinal powers <note>original: "facultates"</note> which Physicians most seek and desire become known as properties from the knowledge of their natures; for those things joined by the fellowship of a genus usually possess similar powers. By pursuing the history of plants according to this order, it follows that a shorter description suffices; for we are not forced to repeat in individual cases those things which commonly belong to the genera, and thus knowledge is obtained from a certain and brief description, such that a picture could not make it more certain: for a picture does not express all differences as speech does.

<margin>In ancient times, Physicians employed and observed useful plants for the cure of various diseases; the remaining parts of the art <note>i.e., other branches of medicine</note> were born later. Although these make a Physician more fit and perfect, they have nevertheless been neglected or even despised by some, no less than plants are today. Yet there was no one among the ancient Physicians who did not strive to acquire for himself a knowledge of both plants and simple medicines—if not of all, at least of those they employed for treatments. And it is from this that Galen <note>A prominent Greek physician in the Roman Empire (c. 129–216 AD)</note> exhorts all Physicians and students of the medical art to the knowledge of plants. "I advise," he says in Book 1 of *On Antidotes*, "that a Physician have skill in all plants (if possible); if not, at least in the many which we frequently use." As for us, while meditating on a universal History of plants, no small anxiety arose concerning method and order. For just as all arts and sciences, if distributed in some methodical order, derive no small ornament and light therefrom, so there is no doubt that much grace and beauty can be added to the history of plants from this source. Although it is easy to add to things already invented, he who finds what was not found before is not easily found. In whose hands lies the right and the rule of correctly arranging discovered plants or truly and properly describing them, except him who, by frequent and repeated inspection, has considered their flowers, seed capsules, and seeds diligently and with the highest attention? A lazy and perhaps stupid Botanist might object to me that this is very easy to do, since the doors have stood open from the third day of creation (according to our assertion cited above) until today; yet no one entered these inner shrines before me, nor has anyone until now uncovered the secrets of the true method, even though they were out in the open. These things may be considered vain by some, but they are true, and I leave them to Posterity for this purpose: so they may know that Christopher Columbus first discovered the West Indies for the Spaniards (of which there was never any mention before him), yet in his own time he was nonetheless reproached by them, claiming the discovery was very easy. Columbus himself, however, to modestly show their ingratitude as well as their ignorance and arrogance, offered them an egg, challenging the wisest among them to make it stand upright or stay on its sharp end by itself. Observing that this surpassed their grasp, he [pressed down] the corner of the egg...</margin>

...by slightly breaking the sharp end, or as it is otherwise told, by using a little salt, he made the egg stand upright; thus he exposed their arrogance and ignorance to posterity. The West Indies <note>original: "India Occidentalis"</note> had existed since the creation of the world, yet no one before Christopher <note>Christopher Columbus</note> ever thought of them. In the same way, the generic characteristics of plants have been fixed since the third day of creation; yet no one before me has reduced them into a method through tables of kinship and affinity based on that generic and essential mark. But enough of these things, let us proceed to more serious matters.

Among the parts of Medicine, which are not few, the knowledge of plants and simples <term>simples: medicinal herbs used on their own, rather than in complex mixtures</term>, as it is neither the least nor the last, has always been held as the chief part, being the most ancient and that which gave birth and origin to the art itself. For when there was no medical art, it took its beginning from the knowledge of certain plants, which those ancient Heroes to whom the invention of the art is ascribed discovered—either by some chance showing the way, or by some other means—and then through frequent experiments applied them to the cure of various diseases and observed them to be useful. Now at last, I fulfill the promise I made to you, the Subscribers and Benefactors, regarding the history of the herbs in these five Sections, later no doubt than many would have wished, and than I myself had intended. But many things often happen beyond expectation, by which our plans are disturbed and delays are cast upon them: thus, occupied by various and long-lasting business and hindered by frustrations, I have not been able to finish the history of these five Sections until now. I am certain that the greatest part of these Sections which I have embraced here, and their arrangement, has been observed by no one (whose labors I have read) until now. I frankly profess, moreover, that this method of mine was not drawn from the books of botanical writers, but was wonderfully and purely taken from the Book of Nature. For the rest, I trust that good men will consult my labors, such as they are—even if they perhaps do not meet the expectations of certain malevolent readers—and will interpret them kindly wherever I have slipped. If they do this, let them know that whatever remains of the life that God, the Best and Greatest, grants me shall be spent in promoting Botanical studies according to my small measure.

Ancient histories testify that the knowledge of plants and other simples was celebrated among the ancients and even among the moderns, and held in the highest honor; indeed, it was most pleasant and delightful, and exceedingly necessary for the preservation of life, not only to philosophers and physicians, but even to kings. Since plants hold the first place in healing, as they are the basis and foundation of Medicine; indeed, by them present health is kept safe and life is extended for many years: by them every kind of disease is driven away, and even those sometimes despaired of, whose hope of safety was placed at the very end, are restored to the light and their native air. Hence many authors have pursued the powers of plants with the greatest zeal, and have displayed their forms and images skillfully engraved, and have explored their healing powers with anxious care, consecrating them to posterity in their writings. And since anyone, according to the excellence of his talent, may publish his opinion for the sake of public utility, I do not think it will be held as a fault in me when I pull apart and disapprove of the opinions of others, even learned men, since I detract nothing from the labors of any man, but rather wish everything brought forth by me to be subject to and even commended to the judgments of honorable, kind, and learned men (but not to the mockers and lazy cavillers). Therefore, it is so far from the case that those who entered this race before us—though they were very learned, diligent, and industrious writers—have deterred us, that they rather seem to have handed us the torch, or rather this lamp, and invited us to the same course: just as this tireless labor of ours was not instituted to turn others away, but rather to sharpen the wit and greater diligence of others. But since most mortals, even the most learned, hesitate regarding most plants, and frequently are not only ignorant of their identity, but have even omitted the way of reducing them to their peculiar, proper, and genuine genera and species. There is no mortal at all who knows all things, nor does the perfect knowledge of all plants at once, and their virtues, belong to any but God alone. Since such a great abundance and variety of plants, without a more certain method than has been handed down by all the aforementioned Botanists until now, hinders and overwhelms the memory, many are deterred from the study of plants: for this study is arduous and difficult, partly because of the great number of plants, partly indeed because of the diversity of names among Authors, but much more because of the lack of a true and infallible method. Hence plants fall more easily from the memory, since so many various names for one and the same plant are proposed by authors, and because of such diversity of names imposed on plants, not only are the genuine names confused, but the entire knowledge and understanding is confounded, which leads students and even professors of Botany away from this study so necessary to Physicians. # PREFACE TO THE READER.

Many have entreated me with the most urgent prayers to share these labors of ours, and to present to them this method of ours—which (boasting aside) is easier and more certain than any proposed thus far—arranged through tables of kinship and affinity; at last, overcome by their prayers, I have yielded. Since I have taken it upon myself to weave together this universal History of all plants, both those described by the ancients and the more recent authors as well as those discovered and presented through our own observations, I have decided to give the first place among all plants to Herbs in this second part of our general history, preferring them over the remaining plants that consist of woody matter. This is because herbs are more difficult to know and distinguish than trees, and they far exceed them in number. Furthermore, that Herbs excel by priority of nature is easily proven from the sacred pages of Genesis, chapter 1, verses 11 and 12, where these words are read: <note>Morison cites the Vulgate Latin Bible to provide a divine mandate for studying herbs before trees.</note> *Let the earth bring forth green herb, and such as may seed, and the fruit tree yielding fruit after its kind, which hath seed in itself upon the earth. And it was so done. And the earth brought forth the green herb, and such as yieldeth seed according to its kind, and the tree that beareth fruit, having seed each one according to its kind; and God saw that it was good, etc.*

For since Herbs (as was said) are more difficult to know and distinguish, and far exceed in number the trees, shrubs, and under-shrubs consisting of woody matter; therefore, we approach the Herbs—which are more numerous, more difficult, and more confusedly handed down by authors—lest the same thing happen to us which happened to Dalechamps <note>Jacques Daléchamps (1513–1588), a French physician and botanist.</note> and Johann Bauhin <note>Johann Bauhin (1541–1613), co-author of the "Historia Plantarum Universalis."</note>, who were snatched away by premature death, their works falling into the hands of those poorly practiced in Botany. Furthermore, observing that the berry-bearing under-shrubs closed the ranks of the first Part; for that reason, it was my intention to begin the History of herbs with the berry-bearing herbs. The First Section contains berry-bearing Herbs, both climbing and non-climbing: climbing bell-flowers, both milky <note>Plants that produce a latex or milky sap.</note> and non-milky: and climbing fruit-bearing plants. The Second section contains Legumes or podded plants, both climbing and non-climbing, and leguminous Trefoils or three-leaved Legumes, etc. The Third, both podded and small-podded plants that are two-capsuled and four-petaled. The Fourth, three-capsuled and six-petaled. The Fifth, three-capsuled plants that are one-petaled and five-petaled: two-capsuled one-petaled: four-capsuled four-petaled: five-petaled five-capsuled and many-capsuled: and five-petaled one-capsuled, etc. First, in the widely extending genera of each Section, we assign a generic and common mark to each superior genus: Second, the marks of the subordinate genera: Third, we propose Tables of kinship and affinity along with the duration of individual plants, with their own characteristic marks indicating their duration (namely ☉ for perennial <note>original: "vivaces"</note>, ♃ for annuals): we add the names at the beginning of each species: the places of birth <note>The native habitat or geographical origin.</note> toward the end: finally, the Temperament and Virtues <note>The medicinal properties and "humoral" qualities of the plant.</note>, and at last we add the Hallucinations <note>Errors or misconceptions found in previous botanical texts.</note> of Caspar Bauhin and other authors: all of which, being almost unusual among authors, facilitate our entirely new method, both for aiding the memory and for retaining the plants and their faculties. Farewell, friend Reader, and enjoy the arrangement of these five Sections, acquired through many travels and midnight labors, until the rest come forth arranged by the same method through Tables of kinship and affinity.

in the college called Uni-
versity, on the Ides of No-
vember, 1680.
