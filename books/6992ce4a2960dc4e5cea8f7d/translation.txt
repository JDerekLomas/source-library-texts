### Wooden Cannon
<note>original: "木砲" (Mu Pao). These were lightweight or emergency cannons made from hollowed hardwood and reinforced with iron hoops. They were cheaper and more portable than bronze or iron guns, though less durable.</note>

### Six Harmonies Cannon
<note>original: "六合砲" (Liuhe Pao). "Six Harmonies" is a cosmological term referring to the four cardinal directions plus zenith (up) and nadir (down), representing the entire universe. This name suggests a weapon of total, all-encompassing power.</note>

### Invincible Bamboo General Cannon
<note>original: "無敵竹將軍砲" (Wudi Zhu Jiangjun Pao). A famous Ming-era weapon made from a thick section of giant bamboo reinforced with iron bands. It was used to fire a mixture of gunpowder, lead pellets, and "poison fire" at close range.</note>

### Paper-Pasted Round Bomb
<note>original: "紙糊圓砲" (Zhihu Yuan Pao). These were incendiary or fragmentation shells with casings made of many layers of tough, lacquered paper. They were designed to burst and scatter fire or blinding chemicals rather than relying on heavy metal casings.</note>

### Flying Mineral-Mist Cannon
<note>original: "飛礞砲" (Feimeng Pao). "Meng" refers to *mengshi* (lapis chlorite or mica), a mineral used in specialized gunpowder recipes. This weapon likely produced a blinding or suffocating smoke cloud.</note>

### Lychee Cannon
<note>original: "荔枝砲" (Lizhi Pao). Named for the bumpy skin of the lychee fruit; this refers to the appearance of the shell, which was packed with numerous small iron balls or "seeds" to create a cluster-shot effect.</note>

### Wind and Dust Cannon
<note>original: "風塵砲" (Fengchen Pao). A poetic name for a weapon used to create a "storm" of projectiles or smoke, obscuring the battlefield like a sudden dust storm.</note>

### Pomegranate Cannon of the Divine Engine for Striking Bandits
<note>original: "擊賊神機柘榴砲" (Jizei Shenji Zheliu Pao). Much like the Western "grenade" (named after the pomegranate), this "Pomegranate Cannon" contained many internal "seeds" (shrapnel) that scattered upon explosion. "Divine Engine" (Shenji) was a prestigious label for sophisticated imperial weaponry.</note>

### Man-Felling Cannon
<note>original: "人墜砲" (Renzhui Pao). Literally "the cannon that makes men fall," this was a dedicated anti-personnel weapon intended to sweep infantry off their feet or breach enemy formations.</note>

### One Mother, Fourteen Children Cannon
<note>original: "一母十四子砲" (Yi Mu Shishi Zi Pao). This refers to a breech-loading swivel gun. The "Mother" is the main barrel, and the "Children" are fourteen interchangeable sub-chambers or cartridges that could be pre-loaded and swapped quickly for rapid fire.</note>

### Thunder-Clap Cannon
<note>original: "轟雷砲" (Honglei Pao). A name emphasizing the weapon's deafening roar and concussive power, likely used for both psychological impact and physical destruction.</note>

### Lead Pellet Hornet’s Nest Cannon
<note>original: "鉛彈一窠蜂砲" (Qiandan Yikefeng Pao). A "Hornet's Nest" refers to a multi-projectile weapon. This cannon was designed to spray a massive cloud of lead pellets—like a swarm of angry hornets—making it devastating against tightly packed infantry.</note>

### Sky-Flying Heaven-Shaking Thunder Cannon to Strike Bandits
<note>original: "飛空擊賊震天雷砲" (Feikong Jizei Zhentianlei Pao). A poetic and descriptive name for a long-range mortar or explosive shell. "Heaven-Shaking Thunder" was a common term for powerful gunpowder explosives that produced a massive blast.</note>

Woven bamboo strips are used to form a round basket. This frame is then covered with forty to fifty thick layers of paper paste and left to dry in the sun. On top of this, apply fifteen additional layers of oiled paper <note>this provides waterproofing and extra structural integrity</note>. When this device is cast onto an <term>enemy ship<note>original: "zeichuan," literally "pirate ship" or "rebel ship"</note></term>, the bamboo casing is guaranteed to shatter, releasing its volatile contents.

# Treatise on Armament Technology, Volume 134

The excellence of a <term>bird-gun<note>original: 鳥銃 (niǎochòng). An East Asian matchlock musket, named for its purported accuracy against small, flying targets.</note></term> lies entirely in its manufacture. The iron must be thoroughly refined until "ripe" <note>This refers to repeatedly folding and hammering the iron to create high-quality wrought iron or steel.</note>. Two iron tubes are wrapped one around the other to form the barrel. The initial bore is kept extremely small, then it is bored out using a steel drill. A worker should drill only about one inch per day, continuing until the bottom is reached. A barrel that takes a full month to drill and polish to a mirror shine is considered of the highest quality.

In recent times, those who truly understand the "ailments" <note>A metaphor for technical defects or manufacturing flaws.</note> of these weapons are few. Furthermore, officials are often unwilling to endure the complaints of workers or to act with genuine sincerity in their supervision. They simply allow the craftsmen to roll iron sheets into tubes. In these inferior guns, the thickness and diameter are inconsistent throughout the length of the barrel. Often, the bore is already wide enough to fit a lead ball of three or four <term>mace<note>original: 錢 (qián). A traditional unit of weight; one mace is approximately 3.7 grams.</note></term> before any drilling has even occurred.

Because the interior of the barrel has not been polished with a steel drill, the lead ball cannot be rammed all the way to the bottom, and it will not fly straight upon exiting. Where the barrel walls are too thick, the ball cannot even enter; where they are too thin, the barrel will burst when the powder ignites. Some guns are even made of a single rolled layer of iron; these explode and shatter the user's hand as soon as they are fired. # The Breech
<note>original: 底 (dǐ). Literally "bottom," referring here to the breech plug that seals the rear of the barrel.</note>

Inside the rear opening of the firearm, a "spiral snail shell" shape is carved. An <term>iron fitting<note>original: 鑲鈇 (xiāng fū). A specialized iron inlay or tool used to create or reinforce the threading.</note></term> is used to form a screw thread that matches the breech plug. Whether the threads are deep or shallow, coarse or fine, turning it to the left will drive it in, and turning it to the right will draw it out. If there is a concern that gunpowder residue has accumulated inside and might cause rust, simply remove the breech plug. Wrap a cloth around a <term>cleaning rod<note>original: 搠杖 (shuò zhàng). Also known as a ramrod; here used for maintenance.</note></term>, dip it in boiling water, and scrub the interior clean.
<note>The use of screw-threaded breech plugs was a sophisticated feature in 17th-century firearms, allowing for easier cleaning and better pressure containment compared to plugs that were simply pinned or welded in place.</note>

# The Muzzle
<note>original: 前口 (qián kǒu). Literally "front mouth."</note>

The muzzle should only be large enough to accommodate a lead bullet weighing three <term>mace<note>original: 錢 (qián). A traditional Chinese unit of weight; three mace is approximately 11.25 grams.</note></term>, or two mace at the absolute smallest. The muzzle and the <term>bore<note>original: 腹 (fù). Literally "belly," referring to the internal chamber or bore of the barrel.</note></term> must be perfectly uniform and straight. If the bore is large but the muzzle is small, the propellant gases will leak out, and the bullet will not travel far. Conversely, if the muzzle is large but the bore is small, the bullet will wobble and sway as it exits, making it difficult to achieve an accurate hit.
<note>This passage highlights an early understanding of internal ballistics: the "windage" (the gap between the bullet and the barrel wall) must be minimized to ensure the expansion of gases pushes the projectile efficiently and straight.</note>

Use **Mao bamboo** <note>original: "máozhú" (茅竹). A large, thick-walled bamboo species known for its strength</note> to cut a tube three feet long. First, soak it thoroughly in **"cold-fire" solution** <note>original: "lěnghuǒ zhī yào" (冷火之藥). Seal the muzzle with **yellow clay**, and fit an **iron hoop** over the opening to reinforce it. Use **hard wood** to create a handle two feet long, and assemble it firmly for use. 

Each soldier on horseback can carry **four bundles** of these, which is very light and convenient. For marching out of the border, pursuing the enemy, or engaging in direct combat, there is no better way to achieve victory than this.
