<note>original: "三才圖會" (Sancai Tuhui). The term "Three Realms" or "Three Powers" refers to the traditional Chinese cosmological triad of Heaven, Earth, and Humanity, representing the entirety of the universe.</note>

This work is a foundational <term>encyclopedia</term> <note>known in Chinese as "leishu," a reference work that categorizes knowledge by topic</note> compiled during the **Ming Dynasty**. It was the lifework of the scholar **Wang Qi** and his son **Wang Siyi**, completed around 1609. <term>Imperial Academy (太學)</term>: The highest central institution of learning in imperial China, where the Emperor and officials performed major sacrifices to Confucius.
<term>Former Teacher (先師)</term>: The honorary title for Confucius used in ritual contexts.
<term>Sacrifice (祭祀)</term>: The formal act of offering food, wine, and prayers to ancestors or deities.
<term>Arrangement (陳設)</term>: The specific, choreographed layout of ritual objects required by state sumptuary laws.
<term>Savory and Basket Vessels (籩豆)</term>: A collective term for ritual food containers; "Bian" (bamboo) and "Dou" (wood/bronze) are fundamental to Confucian liturgy.
<term>Seasoned and Grand Broths (和羹, 大羹)</term>: Two types of meat soup; the "Grand" broth is unseasoned to symbolize the purity of high antiquity.
<term>Prayer Board (祝板)</term>: A wooden tablet used to display the written text of the prayer offered during the sacrifice.
<term>Parched cakes (糗餅)</term>: Cakes made from roasted or parched grains.
<term>Molded salt (形鹽)</term>: Salt carved or molded into symbolic shapes, often a tiger, representing the element of Earth or specific cosmological directions.
<term>Sacrificial Animals (豕, 羊, 牛)</term>: The "Three Animals" (Pig, Sheep, Ox) which constitute the "Great Sacrifice" (Tai-lao), the highest level of offering.
<term>Ritual Silk (帛)</term>: A roll of fine silk offered to the spirit, later burned as a messenger to the divine realm.

# Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms, Volume 8: Ritual Systems

For the positions of the Ten Philosophers, there are five positions on the East. At each position, sacrificial offerings are arranged in front. Additionally, a small table is centrally placed to hold the ritual silk, incense, and candles. There are five <term>Jue</term> <note>original: "爵" (Jue), a tripod wine cup with two small pillars on the rim, used for ritual libations.</note>, with one assigned to each position. When the ceremony begins, the attendants pour wine into the cups. The Secondary Offering Official performs the rites and offers one additional cup in the center.

There is one <term>Xing</term> <note>original: "鉶" (Xing), a ritual cauldron with a lid and two handles, typically used to serve seasoned soup.</note> containing seasoned broth. 

There are four <term>Bian</term> <note>original: "籩" (Bian), a bamboo basket used to hold dry offerings like fruits and nuts.</note> containing salt, dates, chestnuts, and dried venison. 

There are four <term>Dou</term> <note>original: "豆" (Dou), a high-stemmed bowl used for serving pickled vegetables and minced meats.</note> containing pickled greens, pickled celery, minced venison, and minced rabbit. 

There is one <term>Fu</term> <note>original: "簠" (Fu), a rectangular vessel used for holding cooked broomcorn millet.</note> containing broomcorn millet. 

There is one <term>Gui</term> <note>original: "簋" (Gui), a round vessel used for holding cooked foxtail millet.</note> containing foxtail millet. 

There is one <term>Fei</term> <note>original: "篚" (Fei), a rectangular bamboo basket used to hold the ceremonial silk.</note> containing one length of white silk, measuring one <term>Zhang</term> and eight <term>Chi</term> <note>Approximately 6 meters or 19.5 feet.</note> in length.

Regarding the animal sacrifice, the five positions share one pig, which is divided into five portions. These are placed on square wooden trays and set upon the tables. The five positions on the West follow the same arrangement.

# Instructions for the Offerings at the Altars of the Four Associates

For each of the **Four Associate** positions, sacrificial items are to be arranged upon the altar. Additionally, a small side-table is to be placed in front of each altar to hold incense, candles, and the ritual silk for the offering.

Three <term>Jue</term> <note>original: "爵" (Jue). Ancient three-legged wine vessels used to present libations during the three stages of the ceremony.</note> containing wine are to be presented during the first, second, and third offerings.

One <term>Deng</term> <note>original: "登" (Deng). A high-stemmed ceramic vessel.</note> contains the **Great Broth** <note>original: "大羹" (Dageng). A primitive, unseasoned meat broth representing ancient simplicity.</note>.

Two <term>Xing</term> <note>original: "鉶" (Xing). A ritual soup tureen with a lid.</note> contain the **Seasoned Broth** <note>original: "和羹" (Hegeng). A broth flavored with seasonings to contrast with the Great Broth.</note>.

To the **left**, six <term>Bian</term> <note>original: "籩" (Bian). Bamboo baskets used for dry offerings like fruits and dried meats.</note> contain:
*   Salt
*   Fried fish with grain
*   Dates
*   Chestnuts
*   Water caltrops
*   Dried venison

To the **right**, six <term>Dou</term> <note>original: "豆" (Dou). Stemmed bowls used for wet offerings like pickles and sauces.</note> contain:
*   Pickled greens
*   Pickled celery
*   Pickled bamboo shoots
*   Minced venison sauce
*   Minced rabbit sauce
*   Minced fish sauce

One <term>Fu</term> <note>original: "簠" (Fu). A rectangular vessel with a cover used for holding broomcorn millet.</note> contains **broomcorn millet**.

One <term>Gui</term> <note>original: "簋" (Gui). A round vessel with a cover used for holding foxtail millet.</note> contains **foxtail millet**.

One <term>Fei</term> <note>original: "篚" (Fei). A bamboo basket for holding silk.</note> contains the **ritual silk**:
*   One length of white silk, measuring one <term>zhang</term> and eight <term>chi</term> <note>Approximately 18 feet in traditional Chinese measurements.</note>.

The sacrificial animals—**one sheep and one pig**—are each divided into four portions and placed on square trays before the four positions.

According to the system of the Zhou Dynasty, whenever a school was first established, a <term>Shidian ritual</term> <note>original: "釋奠" (Shidian). This term literally means "setting out offerings." It was a solemn ritual performed to honor the spirits of past teachers, primarily using vegetable offerings rather than the grand animal sacrifices used for deities.</note> must be performed for the "Former Sage" and the "Former Teacher." Han dynasty scholars identified the Former Sage as the Duke of Zhou and the Former Teacher as Confucius. Thus, the Shidian ritual has been practiced since the Han; subsequent dynasties have either reduced or expanded its components.

In the second year of the Hongwu Emperor's reign <note>1369 CE, at the start of the Ming Dynasty</note>, officials were dispatched to offer sacrifices at the Temple of Confucius in Qufu. Each year, during the spring and autumn, on the two "Ding" days <note>The "Ding" days (the fourth day of the ten-day cycle) were traditionally chosen for these rites</note>, imperial incense is sent to be offered at the Imperial Academy. The officials of the Central Secretariat perform the "First Offering," the Hanlin Scholars perform the "Second Offering," and the Chancellor of the Imperial Academy performs the "Final Offering." At the beginning of the Jiajing Emperor's reign <note>roughly 1522 CE</note>, the formal titles for the "Former Sages and Former Worthies" were standardized, and those titles are followed today.

### Commentary on the Ritual Standing Positions

According to the original protocols of the <term>Comprehensive Rites</term><note>original: "通禮" (Tongli). A reference to standardized ritual manuals used during the Ming dynasty to ensure Confucian propriety.</note>, the Master of the Household stands facing North at the foot of the <term>Eastern Steps</term><note>original: "阼階" (zuojie). Traditionally, the eastern staircase of a hall was reserved for the host or master of the house.</note>, and the Mistress of the Household stands facing North at the foot of the Western Steps.

If the Master has a living mother, she is given a special position in front of the Mistress of the Household. If the Master has paternal uncles or elder brothers, they are given special positions to the Master’s right, slightly forward, arranged in multiple rows with the westernmost position being the most senior. If there are paternal aunts, wives of paternal uncles, elder sisters-in-law, or elder sisters, they are given special positions to the left of the Mistress of the Household, slightly forward, arranged in multiple rows with the easternmost position being the most senior.

The younger brothers stand to the right of the Master, slightly recessed. Sons, grandsons, and external attendants stand behind the Master. The wives of younger brothers and the Master's younger sisters stand to the left of the Mistress, slightly recessed. The wives of sons and grandsons, the daughters, and the internal attendants stand behind the Mistress.

The older diagrams integrated these positions directly into the drawing of the <term>Ancestral Hall</term><note>original: "祠堂" (citang).</note>. They are presented separately here because we wish for people to understand the layout more easily. The original ritual annotations mention a special position for the mother, yet the older diagrams omitted it. Furthermore, sons and grandsons ought to be arranged in rows according to their <term>order of lineage</term><note>original: "世次" (shici). This refers to the strict hierarchy of generations within a clan.</note>, but the older diagrams confused them into a single group, thereby losing the proper generational sequence. These errors have now been corrected and supplemented. Although the older diagrams separated paternal uncles and elder brothers into first and second ranks—which differs from the literal text of the original annotations—that particular arrangement did capture the intended spirit of the ritual.

# The Family Rituals of the Zheng Clan of Pujiang <note>The Zheng family of Pujiang, known as the "Righteous Gate," was famous for maintaining a communal household for over 300 years and was often cited as a model for Neo-Confucian social order.</note> begin with the "Diagram of Seating Orders in the Ancestral Shrine." It establishes five positions, designating the fourth-generation ancestor as the head of the **Great Lineage** <term>Dazong; the primary line of descent from a common ancestor</term>, situated in the center.

To the right of this central position, the first seat belongs to the High Great-Grandfather <note>original: 高祖考 Gaozu Kao</note>. The second seat is the Great-Grandfather, the third is the Grandfather, and the fourth is the Father. To the right of these four positions are the wives <note>original: 妣 Bi; deceased mother or grandmother</note> of these same four generations. These together constitute the **Lesser Lineage** <term>Xiaozong; the branch of a family descending from a more recent ancestor</term>.

By placing all four generations of deceased fathers to the right of the Founding Ancestor and the mothers to their left, the family clearly considers the West to be the position of honor <note>In traditional Chinese orientation, the "right" corresponds to the West when facing South.</note>. However, when looking at the side-by-side arrangement of the four generations of fathers, the Great-Grandfather is placed to the right of the High Great-Grandfather, and the Father is placed to the right of the Grandfather. This makes it seem as though the East is actually being treated as the position of honor. It is difficult to understand the logic or precedent they intended to follow here.

Furthermore, consider the ancient system for ancestral temples <note>original: 廟制 Miaozhi</note>: the Emperor was permitted seven shrines, Feudal Lords had five, Great Officials had three, High Scholars had two, and lower officials had one. Commoners <note>original: 庶人 Shuren</note> were required to perform their sacrifices in the inner living quarters of their homes <note>original: 寢 Qin</note>. 

Earlier Confucian scholars, who adhered strictly to these ancient rules, already argued that for a family to sacrifice to four generations of ancestors was an act of **usurpation** <note>original: 僭 Jian; to overstep one's social rank by performing rituals reserved for higher nobility</note>. If that is the case, is it acceptable to add a "Founding Ancestor" <note>original: 始祖 Shizu</note> on top of those four? In modern times, many families have moved toward using five niches for their tablets; this appears to be a concession to contemporary social customs.

# Practical Modifications for the Ancestral Hall

According to the old diagrams, a separate set of food offerings was prepared for each deceased father and each deceased mother; for the standard four generations of ancestors, this would require a total of eight tables. Nowadays, the reception halls <term>Ting shi: the main central room of a traditional residence used for ritual and formal occasions</term> in people’s homes are often narrow and cramped, and I fear they cannot accommodate such a large number of tables. 

I now propose that the deceased father and mother <note>original: 考妣 (Kao Bi), formal ritual titles for a late father and mother</note> share a single table for their food offerings, following what is commonly called a "table layout" <term>Zhuo mian: a simplified, unified arrangement of dishes on one surface</term>. This approach should be quite feasible for most households. ## The Protocol for Formal Meetings

According to the ancient **rites of meeting**, the **Host** <note>original: *Zhujun*, literally "Host Lord"</note> employs **Ushers** <note>original: *Bin* or *Pin*</note>, and the **Guest** employs **Attendants** <note>original: *Jie*</note>. The number of Attendants matches the Guest's "number of commands" <term>Number of Commands: *Mingshu*, a ritual ranking system in ancient China where higher-ranking officials were granted a higher number of "commands" or degrees of authority by the Emperor</term>. The Host, however, uses only half that number of Ushers to **show humility**.

If the Host stands outside the **Treasury Gate** <note>original: *Kumen*, one of the five gates of the palace in the Zhou Dynasty system</note> facing the Southwest, the Ushers stand to the Host’s left side, facing Southeast and oriented toward the superior position. The **Primary Usher** <note>original: *Shangbin*</note> stands closest to the Host. When the Host has a command to be sent out, it must first reach the Primary Usher; the Primary Usher passes it to the **Second Usher**; the Second Usher passes it to the **Junior Usher** <note>original: *Mobin*</note>; the Junior Usher passes it to the **Junior Attendant**; the Junior Attendant passes it to the **Second Attendant**; and the Second Attendant passes it to the **Primary Attendant**, who finally conveys it to the **Guest**.

The Guest stands in the Northwest, facing the Host. The Attendants stand to the West of the Guest, positioned to meet and face the Ushers. The Primary Attendant stands closest to the Guest’s side. When the Guest has a message to be brought in, it must first reach the Primary Attendant; the Primary Attendant passes it to the Second Attendant; the Second Attendant passes it to the Junior Attendant; the Junior Attendant passes it to the Junior Usher; the Junior Usher passes it to the Second Usher; and the Second Usher passes it to the Primary Usher, who finally conveys it to the **Host**.

# The Illustration of the Five Punishments
<note>The Five Punishments (*Wuxing*) were a cornerstone of the Chinese legal system from the Sui and Tang dynasties through the Ming and Qing. They represent a scale of increasing severity intended to reform the offender and maintain social order.</note>

---

### Flogging with the Light Bamboo
- **Ten strokes**
- **Twenty strokes**
- **Thirty strokes**
- **Forty strokes**
- **Fifty strokes**

<gloss>The punishment of flogging with the light bamboo <note>original: *chi* 笞</note> is used when a person has committed a minor offense. A small bramble switch is used to deliver the blows. There are five grades ranging from ten to fifty strokes, increasing or decreasing in increments of ten.</gloss>

---

### Flogging with the Heavy Bamboo
- **Sixty strokes**
- **Seventy strokes**
- **Eighty strokes**
- **Ninety strokes**
- **One hundred strokes**

<gloss>The punishment of flogging with the heavy bamboo <note>original: *zhang* 杖</note> is used when a person has committed a more serious offense. A large bramble rod is used to deliver the blows. There are five grades ranging from sixty to one hundred strokes, also increasing or decreasing in increments of ten.</gloss>

---

### Penal Servitude
- **One year, plus 60 strokes of the heavy bamboo**
- **One and a half years, plus 70 strokes of the heavy bamboo**
- **Two years, plus 80 strokes of the heavy bamboo**
- **Two and a half years, plus 90 strokes of the heavy bamboo**
- **Three years, plus 100 strokes of the heavy bamboo**

<gloss>The punishment of penal servitude <note>original: *tu* 徒</note> is for those whose crimes are deemed heavy. They are detained by the authorities and forced into hard labor under supervision, wearing iron shackles <note>original: *tiezhi* 鐵桎</note> and enduring bitterness and pain. There are five grades ranging from one to three years, with the number of strokes and the duration of labor serving as the scale of difference.</gloss>

---

### Banishment
- **2,000 *li*, plus 100 strokes of the heavy bamboo**
- **2,500 *li*, plus 100 strokes of the heavy bamboo**
- **3,000 *li*, plus 100 strokes of the heavy bamboo**

<gloss>The punishment of banishment <note>original: *liu* 流</note> is for those who have committed extremely serious crimes, yet the state cannot bear to execute them. They are sent away to distant regions and are never permitted to return to their hometowns for the rest of their lives. There are three grades ranging from 2,000 to 3,000 *li* <note>a *li* is approximately 500 meters or 0.3 miles</note>, increasing or decreasing in increments of 500 *li*.</gloss>

---

### Death
- **Strangulation** <gloss>Which keeps the limbs and body whole</gloss>
- **Beheading** <gloss>Where the head and body are separated</gloss>

<gloss>The ultimate punishment <note>original: *si* 死</note>.</gloss>
<note>In traditional Chinese thought, dying with an intact body (strangulation) was considered a "lesser" death than beheading, as it was less of an insult to one's ancestors and the physical self.</note>

---

**Removal refers to being removed and separated from one’s native land.**
**Servitude involves moving to a location beyond 1,000 *li*.**
