## General Discourse on Tripods and Ritual Vessels
<note>original: "Ding Yi Zong Shuo" (鼎彝總說). Ritual vessels, particularly the tripod cauldron (Ding), were the most significant symbols of authority and ancestral communication in ancient China.</note>

The form of the tripod <term>Ding</term> is full of symbolism. When it is round, it symbolizes **Yang** <note>The celestial, active principle</note>; when it is square, it symbolizes **Yin** <note>The earthly, receptive principle</note>. Its three legs symbolize the "Three High Ministers" <note>original: "San Gong" (三公), the highest ranking advisors to the Emperor</note>, while four legs symbolize the "Four Auxiliary Officials." 

Its "yellow ears" <note>the handles or loops on the rim</note> symbolize the balanced middle-path of human talent, and the golden carrying-bar symbolizes the decisiveness of talent. The tripod is decorated with the **Taotie** <note>A common bronze motif featuring a gluttonous, mask-like creature</note> to serve as a warning against greed. It features the shape of the **cicada** to house the idea of wisdom. It is decorated with **cloud and thunder patterns** to symbolize the merit of nourishing all things <note>referring to rain</note>, and displays the **Kuilong** <note>A mythical one-legged dragon</note> to symbolize unpredictable and divine transformations. As for specialized vessels like the "Ox Tripod" or "Sheep Tripod," each is decorated according to the symbolic nature of the animal it represents.

However, there are many specific names for these vessels:
*   A tripod of great size is called a **Nai**.
*   One that is round and narrows at the top is called a **Zi**.
*   Those with handles attached to the exterior are called **Xuan**.

When they are named "Chong" or "Guan," it refers to the state of their origin. When named "Chan" <note>Slander/Reproof</note> or "Xing" <note>Punishment/Law</note>, the name records a specific event or legal matter. When named "Lao" or "Pei," it indicates a variation in how they are arranged during a ceremony. When called "Divine" or "Treasure," it represents the highest level of importance and weight.

Social status is strictly distinguished by the material used: 
*   The **Scholar** uses iron.
*   The **High Official** uses bronze.
*   The **Feudal Lord** uses silver <note>original: "Bai Jin" (白金), literally 'white metal'</note>.
*   The **Son of Heaven** <note>the Emperor</note> uses gold.

This distinction is also seen in the number of vessels permitted: the **Son of Heaven** is entitled to nine tripods, **Feudal Lords** to seven, **High Officials** to five, and **Scholars** to three. These are the counts by which rank is recognized.

# The Lei Ritual Vessel <note>original: 罍 (lei). The lei is a large ritual wine container used in ancient Chinese ancestral rites. It is typically larger and more bulbous than the trumpet-shaped zun. According to classical ritual texts, the lei was used to hold clarified sacrificial wine or water.</note>

<note>Terminology featured on this page: Ritual Bronze Vessel (Liqi); Wine Vessel; Antiquarianism—the study of ancient artifacts through illustrations and inscriptions.</note>

<note>The accompanying woodcut illustrates a classic bronze *lei*. # General Discourse on the Zun and Yi Ritual Vessels

In the flourishing times of the **Three Dynasties** <note>The Xia, Shang, and Zhou dynasties (c. 2070–256 BCE), considered the golden age of ritual propriety in Chinese history.</note>, the acts of **pouring offerings** and **performing libations** were commonly practiced during interactions between humans and the spirits. Therefore, the pouring of offerings was used to honor living humans and was likewise used to honor the spirits. The performing of libations was used to show ritual respect to the spirits and was likewise used to show respect to humans. Because of this, the **Zun** and **Yi** vessels <note>Zun (尊) and Yi (彝) are general terms for ritual wine containers. In this context, they represent a hierarchy of sacrificial vessels.</note>, along with the **vessel trays**, were used in a specific sequence.

However, in the *Rites of Zhou* <note>original: 周官 (Zhouguan). An ancient text detailing the bureaucracy and ceremonies of the Zhou Dynasty.</note>, the **Official of the Coverings** <note>original: 冪人 (Miren). An official responsible for covering the ritual vessels to keep them pure.</note> prioritizes the *Yi* vessel over the *Zun*. This is because while the *Zun* vessel is more inherently honorable in status, the *Yi* vessel is primary in the order of use. Furthermore, when the **Minister of Rites** <note>original: 宗伯 (Zongbo). One of the highest-ranking officials in charge of religious and state ceremonies.</note> speaks of their practical application, he also mentions the *Yi* first.

The *Yi* vessel is used for the **libation** <note>The ritual pouring of wine or fragrant liquids onto the earth to summon or appease spirits.</note>. Once the libation is finished, the *Zun* vessel is then used for the act of drinking. Whenever drinking occurs, there must be a continuation or a refilling, and this continuation relies entirely upon the supply within the *Yi* vessels. This is the meaning behind the line in the *Classic of Poetry* <note>original: 詩 (Shi). Specifically referring to the 'Shijing'.</note>: "When the **Gong** cup is empty, it is the shame of the **Lei** jar" <note>This famous metaphor implies that if the smaller drinking cup (gong) is empty, it reflects poorly on the large storage vessel (lei/yi) that failed to provide enough wine.</note>.

Regarding the duties of the **Superintendent of Zun and Yi Vessels**, there are **six types of Zun**; the text specifies their total number and then lists their specific names. In the duties of the **Director of Wine**, there are **eight types of Zun**. The fact that the number is stated but the names are not suggests that these eight vessels serve to expand upon the count of the original six. As for the *Yi* vessel, it is essentially of one category, yet there are **six specific Yi** that act as counterparts to the six Great Zun.

# General Discourse on the *You* Vessel

The *You* is a vessel of the "middle-tier" category of wine containers. During the eras of the Xia and Shang dynasties, such ritual vessels were generally referred to as *Yi* <term>Yí: A collective term for ritual bronze vessels used in ancestral temples.</term>. However, by the time of the Zhou dynasty, the specific vessel used to hold "fragrant tulip wine" <term>Yù chàng: A highly aromatic ceremonial wine made from black millet and infused with turmeric or "tulip" herbs, used to invoke spirits.</term> was uniquely called the *You*.

According to the *Offices of Zhou* <note>original: 周官 (Zhōu Guān), an ancient text describing the bureaucracy and rites of the Zhou dynasty.</note>, there were specific officials in charge of the various ritual vessels (*Zun* and *Yi*) to distinguish their specific uses and the substances they contained. 

The so-called "Six *Yi*" vessels consist of the:
1. Chicken vessel
2. Bird vessel
3. *Jia* vessel <note>Decorated with grain patterns.</note>
4. Yellow vessel
5. *Wei* vessel <note>Decorated with a long-tailed monkey motif.</note>
6. *Xu* vessel <note>Decorated with a tiger motif.</note>

The "Six *Zun*" vessels consist of the:
1. Sacrificial vessel
2. Elephant vessel
3. *Zhu* vessel <note>A vessel with ornate, "conspicuous" patterns.</note>
4. *Hu* vessel <note>A jar-shaped vessel.</note>
5. *Tai* vessel <note>An "exalted" or large vessel.</note>
6. *Shan* vessel <note>A "mountain" vessel.</note>

During the *Di* sacrifice <term>Dì: One of the most important ancestral rites performed by the Son of Heaven to honor the ultimate progenitor of the royal line.</term>, the ritual is intended to bring together all the various spirits for worship. For this reason, the rite employs five types of clarified grain wines and three types of unclarified wines, along with two vessels of the fragrant tulip wine. This brings the total number of vessels used in this ceremony to eighteen.

However, when the *Di* sacrifice is performed specifically for the primordial ancestor from whom the lineage originates, the ritual uses four types of clarified wines and three unclarified wines, omitting two vessels, which brings the total number of vessels to sixteen. 

The reason the *You* is specifically identified is because of its association with these two vessels used for the fragrant tulip wine. How do we know this? 

When King Cheng <note>Reigned c. 1042–1021 BCE.</note> wished to reward the great achievements of the Duke of Zhou, he bestowed upon him two *You* vessels filled with black millet wine. Similarly, when King Ping <note>Reigned 770–720 BCE.</note> recognized the virtue of Marquis Wen <note>The ruler of the State of Jin.</note>, he bestowed upon him one *You* vessel of black millet wine. In both instances, these vessels were filled with the precious black millet wine.

# General Discussion of Vases and Jars

Among the established ritual vessels <term>Ritual vessels (Liqi): Specialized containers used in ancient China for offerings to ancestors and during state ceremonies</term>, the *hu* jar holds a primary position. During the Xia and Shang dynasties, these were generally referred to by the collective term *zunyi* <term>Zunyi: A broad category of sacrificial bronze vessels; "Zun" refers to wine vessels and "Yi" to grain vessels</term>. By the time of the Zhou dynasty, which examined and refined the traditions of the two preceding eras, the regulations for these vessels reached a state of complete preparation. 

Thus, for the *Zheng* and *Chang* <note>original: *Zheng* (winter) and *Chang* (autumn) are two of the four seasonal ancestral sacrifices</note> sacrifices and food offerings, two *hu* jars are typically used, ranked second in importance to the *zunyi* vessels and positioned within the gates of the ritual space. 

However, although the general function of the jar is consistent, there are distinctions between the square and round forms. In the Banquet Rites and the Great Archery ceremonies, the High Officials and Great Officers <term>Qing-Dafu: High-ranking administrative and military officials in the Zhou feudal hierarchy</term> use round jars. This is because the high status of a Great Officer signifies the "upright and square" <note>original: *zhifang*; a reference to the *Book of Changes*, where "squareness" represents the unwavering integrity and moral rectitude required of a minister</note> duty of a subject; hence, the square shape is used to represent this principle. Conversely, for the lower-ranking officers and their retinues who eat together, their humble status suggests that "following orders" is their proper virtue; hence, they use round jars to signify this adaptability. 

Turning to the Ritual of Diplomatic Visits <note>original: *Pinli*, the protocol for state visits between feudal lords</note>, when the bridge is in the north, eight jars are displayed to the south; when the bridge is in the west, six jars are displayed to the east. This is because the East represents the limit of movement and the initiation of interaction, while the South represents the great manifestation of light and civilization. This reflects the time for movement and responding to external things during a diplomatic meeting. Given this, is it not entirely appropriate to arrange the jars in such a manner?

# General Discussion of the Jue

Of all <term>ritual vessels (yiqi)</term> <note>These are the "constant vessels" used in ancestral sacrifices and state ceremonies.</note>—those used for sacrificial offerings—some are modeled after small things yet possess great significance in the performance of rites. Their physical form may be minute, but their application is incredibly vast. The vessel known as the **jue** is exactly such an object.

Because the **jue** is exceptionally small among drinking vessels, yet the formal act of drinking in a ceremony must always begin with it, it is said that "in ritual, it is truly great." Among all ritual apparatus, the **jue** is the most minute; however, whether one is performing rituals for Heaven and Earth, communing with the spirits, or harmonizing with guests—extending even to capping ceremonies <note>A rite of passage for young men.</note>, weddings, funerals, sacrifices, court audiences, diplomatic visits, and local archery contests—there is nowhere it is not used. Thus, its application is indeed vast.

In investigating the standards of previous eras, we find a specific hierarchy of volume:
* A vessel of one *sheng* is called a **jue**. <note>A *sheng* was a small unit of dry or liquid measure, roughly equivalent to a "pint" in ancient terms.</note>
* A vessel of two *sheng* is called a **gu**.
* A vessel of three *sheng* is called a **zhi**.
* A vessel of four *sheng* is called a **jiao** (horn cup).
* A vessel of five *sheng* is called a **san**.

From this, we see that the **jue** holds the smallest amount, confirming that its physical form is the most minute. However, the Zhou dynasty examined the precedents of the ancient past and brought <gloss>ritual culture</gloss> <note>original: 禮文 (*liwen*), referring to the refined patterns and written rules of ritual.</note> to its full completion. They specifically chose the name **jue** to represent the vessels of their era. How could this be without reason? In the Xia dynasty, this vessel was called a *zhan*; in the Shang dynasty, it was called a *jia*; and in the Zhou...

# General Discussion on the Jia, Gu, Dou, Zhi, Zhi, and Jiao Vessels

Since the time of the Three Kings <note>original: 三王; *San Wang*; the legendary founders of the Xia, Shang, and Zhou dynasties.</note>, each generation named the ritual vessels of its era. By the Zhou dynasty, these categories were further expanded. However, all these names were intended to express a sense of earnest caution and warning. ## General Discourse on the Dui <note>original: 敦; *duì*. A spherical ritual bronze vessel used for holding cooked grains.</note>

Regarding the *Dui* vessel: if we examine it through its manufacture, we find that the methods of making it have changed over time. In high antiquity, the *zhi* <note>original: 卮; an ancient drinking cup or goblet.</note> was used. In middle antiquity, metal was used, though some were also made of jade, stone, or wood.

If we examine it through its physical form, the shapes and structures differ. Those equipped with lids are considered "joined" <note>original: 會; *hui*.</note>; those lacking ears or feet are considered "obsolete" <note>original: 廢; *fei*.</note>. Some are similar in category to the <term>pearl basin</term> <note>original: 珠槃; *zhupan*. A basin used to hold the blood of a sacrificial animal during the signing of treaties or covenants.</note>, while others are identical to the <term>Fu and Gui</term> <note>original: 簠簋; *fǔguǐ*. The *Fu* (square) and *Gui* (round) were the standard ritual containers for grain.</note>.

If we examine it through its name, the nomenclature varies. Some consider it an "earthen *gui*" <note>original: 土簋; *tugui*.</note>, while others call it a "jade jar" <note>original: 玉壺; *yuhu*.</note>.

If we examine it through its use, its functions are diverse. Some are used to hold sacrificial blood, held by those presiding over a ritual covenant. Others are used to hold <term>millet and broomcorn</term> <note>original: 黍稷; *shǔjì*. These were the primary ritual grains offered to ancestors.</note>, as assisted by the Inner Steward.

If we examine it through its number, the quantities used in ceremony vary. The <term>Positions in the Hall of Distinction</term> <note>original: 明堂位; *Mingtang Wei*, a chapter of the *Book of Rites*.</note> states that the Sovereign of the Yu clan <note>The legendary Emperor Shun.</note> used two *Dui* vessels. Because of this, the design of the *Dui* cannot be restricted to a single category. By observing these vessels throughout history—their inscriptions, illustrations, and engravings—we see they were crafted according to the needs of their times. ## General Discussion of the Fu, Gui, Dou, and Pu Vessels

Ritual begins by following the inclinations of human emotion, yet it is established through the principle of righteousness <note>original: 義; *yì*. In this context, it refers to the moral fitness or appropriateness of an action.</note> and regulated so that all things return to a state of balance and the Mean. Ritual is used to engage with the living in the world of light and with the spirits in the realm of darkness; there is no occasion where it is not applied. However, ritual must be embodied within physical vessels <term>qi</term> <note>Ritual implements or "vessels." In Confucian thought, the abstract Way (Dao) is expressed through these tangible objects.</note> before it can be enacted. It is for this reason that vessels such as the **Fu** and **Gui** are set out in order.

According to the *Illustrations of the Rituals* <note>original: 禮圖; *Lǐ Tú*. This refers to the *Sanli Tu*, an influential Song dynasty guide that reconstructed ritual objects based on text rather than archaeological finds.</note>, the **Fu** vessel is described as being square on the outside and round on the inside, while the **Gui** is round on the outside and square on the inside. They feature a hollow in the center to be filled with rice, sorghum, and various millets <note>original: 稻粱黍稷; *dào, liáng, shǔ, jì*. The four main cereal crops of ancient China used in offerings.</note>. These manuals claim they were carved from wood, topped with lids shaped like tortoises, and decorated with intricate carvings of insects—designs that moved further and further away from the simplicity of ancient times.

Furthermore, many do not realize that the **Fu** was intended to hold supplementary dishes while the **Gui** held the standard grain offerings. Both were vessels for serving cooked food and were used in ritual sets. If one were to follow the descriptions in the *Illustrations of the Rituals*, these objects would be almost useless as actual food containers. However, when we examine the authentic bronze vessels from the **Three Dynasties** <note>The Xia, Shang, and Zhou periods (c. 2070–256 BCE), the era of the legendary "Sage Kings."</note>, their square and circular forms are perfectly functional for holding food. Moreover, they display the exquisite mastery of ancient bronze casting, and their inscriptions <note>original: 銘載; *míngzǎi*. The historical records cast directly into the metal.</note> remain brilliantly clear. Thus, the craftsmanship of the former kings is still worthy of our study and discussion. How could they have been merely crude shapes carved from wood?

# General Explanation of the **He**

The **He** <term>he</term> <note>original: 盉; a spouted ritual vessel</note> is a vessel for containing the "five flavors" <note>the basic seasonings: sour, bitter, sweet, spicy, and salty</note>. Its specific dimensions and its exact place in ceremonial procedures are not found in the Classics <note>original: 經; referring to ancient ritual texts like the *Book of Rites*</note>. However, the *Shuowen Jiezi* <note>original: 説文; the first Chinese dictionary, compiled c. 100 CE</note> states that the character is composed of the "vessel" radical and the "grain" radical, identifying it as a tool for blending seasonings.

The Song dynasty scholar **Wang Anshi** argued that since the word for "harmony" sounds like the word for "grain" <note>both are pronounced *he*</note>, the use of the grain radical was intended to capture the very idea of harmony. 

Furthermore, while the **Ding** tripod <term>ding</term> <note>original: 鼎</note> is used for the primary boiling of meat, it relies on this vessel to provide the flavors that make the offering acceptable. The **Xin** steamer <term>xin</term> <note>original: 鬵</note> is used for regular cooking, yet it relies on this vessel to make the food palatable.

Although we call it a "vessel," in the logic of our written language, any object that holds things via an opening is categorized as a "vessel" <term>min</term> <note>original: 皿</note>. Yet, specific names are given based on their state:
*   A vessel that is hollow but not full is a **Zhong** <note>original: 盅; a small cup</note>.
*   One that is designed to hold a great deal is **Ying** <note>original: 盈; brimming</note>.
*   One that is lidded for drinking is a **He** <note>original: 盒; a box or container</note>.
*   One used with water for washing is a **Guan** <note>original: 盥; a wash-basin</note>.

# General Discourse on the Yi Pitcher, Yi Basin, Wash-basin, Bowl, Xuan Jar, and Han Pole

The "Ritual of a Duke Feasting an Officer" <note>original: 公食大夫禮 (Gongshi Dafu Li); a chapter in the *Yili* (Book of Etiquette and Ceremonial) describing diplomatic banquets.</note> states that the **Junior Officials** <note>original: 小臣 (Xiaochen); low-ranking court attendants.</note> are to prepare the **Basin** and the **Pitcher** <term>Yi (匜) and Pan (盤): A set of bronze water vessels used for ritual hand-washing; the pitcher (Yi) pours water over the hands into the basin (Pan).</term>. The Han scholar **Zheng Xuan** commented on this, stating that because the Ruler is of such high status, he does not walk over to a fixed wash-basin. **Jia Gongyan** <note>A Tang Dynasty scholar famous for his commentaries on ritual texts.</note> further cited the "Single Victim at the Altar" <note>original: 郊特牲 (Jiaotesheng); a chapter in the *Liji* (Book of Rites).</note> text, arguing that because the Ruler does not go to the wash-stand, the Basin and Pitcher are brought to him instead. **Nie Chongyi** <note>A Song Dynasty scholar known for his "Illustrated Book of the Three Rituals."</note> followed and agreed with this interpretation. Furthermore, it is noted in the "Rituals of the Kaiyuan Era" <note>original: 開元禮 (Kaiyuan Li); the official ritual code of the Tang Dynasty's peak period.</note> that while the Emperor, the Empress, and the Empress Dowager have the Basin and Pitcher set out for them during ceremonies, those from the rank of **Assistant Officiant** <note>original: 亞獻 (Yaxian); the second person to offer a sacrifice in a ritual.</note> downwards, as well as those acting as temporary attendants, do not have them. This was intended to clearly distinguish the Ruler's dignity by the fact that he does not have to approach a common wash-basin.

However, these are all **stubborn and inflexible interpretations** <note>The author is critiquing the traditional view that these vessels were strictly for royalty.</note>. These scholars fail to realize that the "Internal Regulations" <note>original: 內則 (Neize); a chapter of the *Book of Rites* focusing on domestic life and filial piety.</note> discusses the etiquette for serving one's parents and parents-in-law. It says that when children approach their elders, they should show reverence, and they should not dare to touch the elder's **cane or shoes**, nor should they dare to use the elder's **bowls, cups, or pitchers** <note>original: 敦牟卮匜 (Dun, Mou, Zhi, Yi); various types of ritual and domestic vessels.</note> unless they are serving food. 

If the discussions regarding the service of parents and parents-in-law mention the **Yi Pitcher**, then it is clear that these vessels were used by **many people in general** <note>In other words, they were part of standard elite household etiquette, not just for kings.</note>. How could they be enjoyed by the Ruler alone? If it were not so, how could a vessel like the "Yi Pitcher of Ji Jiazhu Boan" <note>A reference to a specific bronze inscription from a non-royal owner.</note> have ever been made? Although they were not exclusively for the Ruler, they were nonetheless significant items.

<note>original: 鑑總說 (Jian Zongshuo)</note>

In ancient times, the Yellow Emperor <term>Huangdi (黃帝): The legendary ancestral sovereign of Chinese civilization, often credited with inventing the fundamental tools of culture and governance.</term> liquefied metal to create divine objects, and thus he fashioned fifteen mirrors. According to older illustrations, during the Xianping era <note>The reign of Emperor Zhenzong of Song, 998–1003 AD.</note>, a commoner from Tongzhou named Tang Shande discovered this vessel by a riverbank and presented it to the authorities. # The Lamp (*Deng*)

Height: six and a half inches; Diameter: five inches; Depth: four-tenths of an inch.

There is no inscription.

-> **Commentary** <-

According to the *Rites of Ministerial Dining* <note>original: "公食大夫禮" (Gongshi Dafu Li), a chapter in the *Etiquette and Rites (Yili)*</note>, the broth of the "great sacrificial soup" is contained within a *deng*. The character for this vessel uses the "metal" radical <note>original: "金" (jin)</note>, indicating it is essentially a "bronze *dou*" <term>dou <gloss>a high-stemmed ritual food vessel with a circular bowl</gloss></term>. 

The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, dating to the late first millennium BCE</note> states that a *dou* made of ceramic is called a *deng* <note>written as "登"</note>; therefore, it is acceptable for the bronze version to share the same name <note>written as "鐙"</note>. Many lamp stands from the Han Dynasty follow this specific shape and design. Within these vessels, there is a central pillar designed to hold a wick <note>original: "炷" (zhu)</note>, and oil or fat is added to provide fuel. 

The *Shuowen Jiezi* <note>An influential dictionary from the Han Dynasty</note> notes that the characters for "master" <note>original: "主" (zhu)</note> and "ancestral tablet" <note>original: "宔" (zhu)</note> were originally written as a pictograph <note>original: "𡵂"</note> that resembles the shape of a lamp. In ancient times, torches and candles were made from bundles of firewood and hemp stalks <note>original: "薪蒸" (xinzheng)</note>; liquid oils and fats were not yet utilized for light. It was only later that people discovered oils could be used for illumination. This function was then assigned to the *deng* vessel, which is how it acquired its name and purpose.
