The border regions of Shaanxi, Henan, and Chenzhou in Gui <gloss>Guiyang County within this prefecture</gloss> span the areas where Fujian, Guangdong <gloss>referred to as Min and Yue</gloss>, and the Chen-Yuan districts meet.

Chenzhou Prefecture <term>Chenzhou Prefecture|Now Huaihua, Hunan; a vital gateway to the southwest</term> serves as a defensive shield for Yunnan and Guizhou. The Great River <note>The Yangtze River</note> flows through its center, while the Five Creeks <gloss>located in the areas of Changde and Chenzhou</gloss> lie in the outlying sub-prefectures.

Intermingled with the strategic passes and bottlenecks of Jingchu <note>The ancient cultural region of Hubei and Hunan</note>, its defenses are fully prepared. Because Heaven and Earth <note>original: "the two ceremonies/dual principles"</note> manifested their spiritual power here and the mountains and rivers offered up their beauty, the "Aura of Kingship" <note>Auspicious energy believed to precede the birth of an emperor</note> was uniquely concentrated in the Hidden Palace <note>The Submerged or Hidden Palace refers to the former residence of the Jiajing Emperor before he ascended the throne; he was born in Huguang province.</note>. However, being hemmed in by rivers and girded by lakes, the people everywhere suffer from the disaster of floods, and bandits also take advantage of these crises.

Beyond this, in the regions between Yongzhou and Baoqing, one must simply be vigilant in preparing against the Miao and other border peoples <note>Referencing the indigenous groups of the "Miao Frontier" who resisted Ming central authority.</note>. Furthermore, because the estates of the Imperial Princes <term>Imperial Estates|Lands granted to members of the Ming royal family, which were often tax-exempt and a burden on the local economy</term> are spread throughout the land, the annual tax burden is truly heavy. The customs of Chu <note>Huguang</note> are marked by a tendency toward extravagance and a lack of interest in saving or accumulation.

Consequently, displaced people from all directions who have lost their livelihoods often flock to this region. As a result, the populace is generally shiftless <gloss>meaning lazy</gloss> and indolent <gloss>pronounced 'yu'</gloss>, making them difficult to govern. The various non-Han peoples of the mountain streams and caves <gloss>referring to Shizhou, Yongshun, and Baojing</gloss> were originally managed through Han Chinese laws using the "Loose Rein" <gloss>meaning to bridle or tether</gloss> system <note>The 'Jimi' or 'Loose Rein' system was a policy of granting local autonomy to tribal leaders who pledged nominal loyalty to the Emperor.</note>, which makes them relatively easy to tame and control. Only the districts of Yongshun and Baojing have enjoyed generations of wealth and power, resulting in annual conflicts and struggles for dominance.

The locations of the Imperial Tombs <note>original: 寢陵 (qinling). This specifically refers to the Xianling Tomb in Zhongxiang, Hubei, the resting place of the Jiajing Emperor’s parents, which required massive upkeep</note> are plagued by excessive expenses and burdensome administrative tasks. In the regions between Jingzhou, Yuezhou, Qianjiang, and Mianyang, floods occur frequently. In the districts of Yunyang and Xiangyang, these waters destroy both the fields and the homes of the people. 

Although the land in Changsha is not particularly fertile, its tax revenue from agricultural land ranks highest among all other districts. Huangzhou is known for its unruly population <note>original: 澆頑 (jiaowan), meaning the people were considered difficult to govern or culturally "thin" in morals</note>, while Changde is exhausted and impoverished. Chenzhou and Yuanzhou are half-composed of bandit dens. The various mountain settlements <gloss>literally "caves" (dong)</gloss> of Mayang are linked together for raids and conflicts; the devastation seen in the Zhiluo region over the years actually originates from here.

Hengzhou and Yongzhou are remote but wealthy, while Baoqing is simple and peaceful. The various indigenous tribes of the river valleys <term>Xi-dong|A collective term for the "Creek and Cave" ethnic groups, primarily the Miao and Yao, living in the mountainous river valleys of southern China</term> were originally managed through the "Loose Rein" policy <term>Jimi|A system of frontier governance where the central government granted titles to local chieftains, allowing them to rule their own people according to local custom provided they remained loyal to the Emperor</term> using Han laws, which made them relatively easy to pacify and manage.

However, the native chieftains of Yongshun and Baojing <note>Two powerful Tusi (tribal headman) domains in modern-day western Hunan</note> have enjoyed wealth and power for generations. Whenever they commit crimes during their internal power struggles, they immediately use their past military service as a pretext to buy their way out of punishment. The Imperial Court often orders troop levies from these very chieftains, which leaves the common people in those distant lands trapped under their overbearing hands. 

These chieftains habitually offend and then "redeem" themselves through service; if the state treats these threats with such indulgence for long, it will surely cause great trouble in the future. It is recommended that a fixed decree be established: any offense must be punished immediately. Do not allow them to be released so lightly—that would be the superior course of action. Those who truly care for the destitute should think of how to rescue them and bring about lasting peace and security.
