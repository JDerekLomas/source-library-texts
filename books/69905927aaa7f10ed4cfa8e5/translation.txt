### PROCLUS ON THE TIMAEUS BOOK III [Preface] 3

<margin>140</margin> For the entire <term>Demiurgy</term><note>The act of divine creation or "crafting" by the Demiurge, the creator figure in Plato's philosophy.</note> is concerned with the wholes and the universal parts, whereas the indivisible and truly particular things he hands over to the "younger gods." He does this so that, by imitating the Father’s <term>providence</term><note>Greek: *pronoia*. The divine foresight and care for the universe.</note> concerning the wholes, they themselves might display a similar craftsmanship regarding individual things and establish a proportion toward him, just as he himself holds toward the <term>Intelligible Paradigm</term><note>The eternal "blueprint" or model of the universe that exists in the realm of pure thought.</note>. For being intellectual in relation to that model, and holding the rank of "Intellect" in relation to the "Intelligible Intellect," he himself becomes an object of intellect for the gods within the world. 

Since there are three levels of creation, as we were saying—one according to wholeness, <margin>10</margin> one according to the division into universal parts, and one according to the division into specific parts—Plato is now about to hand down to us the middle level. He makes this transition following the natural order of the things themselves, and he finds a timely progression into this subject from what has been said previously. For since he defined the world as "one visible living being" [Timaeus 30D] containing within itself all things naturally akin to it, he has already demonstrated that the <margin>15</margin> world is unique based on the "uniqueness" of its model [31A]. Now, the division of the whole universe into its universal parts will make it clear to us that it is visible and that it encompasses all akin things. 

<margin>20</margin> For if we discover for what reason the world is visible, and how all the elements within it are coordinated and through what proportions, we shall easily perceive that it has embraced all akin things and that there is nothing perceptible which has not been included within the single circumference of the world. Having seen this, we would sufficiently possess what was sought; for the question was how the world is visible and how it is all-encompassing of all things naturally akin to it. <margin>25</margin> From what was said before, we have understood that it is unique; from what follows, we learn that it is also complete.

# PROCLUS ON THE TIMAEUS BOOK III [Tim. 31 B]

For even if we set aside the other <insert>means</insert> <note>original: μεσότητας</note> added by the more recent authorities—I mean the followers of **Nicomachus** and **Moderatus** <note>Pythagorean mathematicians who expanded the system of means to ten or more</note> and others of that kind—and speak now only of the three means from which Plato constitutes the Soul—the **arithmetic**, the **geometric**, and the **harmonic**—it is possible to observe how all of these are generated from equality through the following rules.

The **arithmetic mean** is generated in this way: first, make the first term equal to the first; second, equal to the first and second; third, equal to the first, second, and third. <margin>145</margin> Thus, if three monads <note>1, 1, 1</note> are set out, the terms produced according to these rules will be the monad, the dyad, and the triad <note>1, 2, 3</note>, preserving the arithmetic mean. Furthermore, since there is a celestial fire—<margin>10</margin> evidenced by light, which is a form of fire—and a celestial earth—or else how could the moon, when illuminated, create a shadow, and why does the sunlight not pass through its entire body?—it is necessary that the intermediate elements also exist within those celestial realms, though different ones abound in different places. In some places, due to its solidity, the fiery element is far-shining, as in the case of the stellar bodies; in other <margin>15 E</margin> places, due to its thinness, it escapes our notice, as is the case with the spheres that carry the stars. For the characteristic property of all fire is visibility, but not necessarily heat or the tendency to rise to the surface; and the property of all earth is the solid and the tangible, but not necessarily the heavy and that which sinks below all else and moves downward. If we accept these specific properties, we <margin>20</margin> will find both fire and earth in the heaven according to analogy, with fire defining the essence, while the others coexist with it. For this too must be said: that the causes and the productive principles of things have everywhere pre-contained the powers of the things ordered and produced by them, <margin>25</margin> especially when they produce according to nature. For nature itself possesses the form of the tooth, the eye, and the hand, by which it shapes matter; yet not every "eye" is extended in space, but there are places where it possesses its form in an indivisible manner. But for those of like parts, shapes that are "like" according to their value have been fitted to them. And among solids, the sphere is the only "like" shape, since all others are unlike; for some have two surfaces, like the cone, others three, like the cylinder, others four, others five, and others more, such as the pyramids rising from the successive regular polygonal bases. If, therefore, the ether <note>the celestial element</note> is of like parts, and the shape of what is of like parts is also "like," and the "like" among solids is the spherical, then the ether is therefore spherical. In this way, then, we shall approach the matter through natural philosophy.

<margin>2</margin>

<margin>3</margin>

# PROCLUS ON THE TIMAEUS BOOK 3 [Tim. 33 B]

He used the term **spherical** to distinguish it from a **circle**; for the term "in every direction" <note>original: *pante*</note> does not apply to a circle, as it possesses only two dimensions. He also uses the phrase **he turned it as on a lathe** <note>original: *etornōsato*</note>, since among us, bodies are made accurately circular by means of a lathe, which cuts away the irregularities of the <margin>B</margin> bodies. It is clear that both **likeness** and **perfection** belong especially to the spherical; by analogy, likeness relates to the **One**, and perfection to the **Good**. Thus, through both of these, he traced the spherical back to the First Principle, calling it "most like" and "most perfect," which is equivalent to saying "most unified" and "most like the Good." For neither a mixture nor a straight line is "like" or "perfect"—the straight line always admits of addition and is characterized by angles—only the spherical is most like and most perfect. 

Furthermore, it is evident that the **like** is superior to the **unlike**. For likeness is **unifying** <term>unifying: *henopoios*, a power that gathers things into a single unity</term>, while unlikeness is **divisive**; the former belongs to the "Column of the Same," the latter to the "Column of the Other" <note>Referring to the Pythagorean table of opposites where 'Same' is associated with stability and 'Other' with change</note>; and while the former is the cause of simplicity in things, the latter is the cause of variety. Just as the Creator of the universe found that among naturally visible things the **intellectual** was more beautiful than the **unintelligent**, so too he deemed the like superior to the unlike. Indeed, even among the Intelligibles <note>The highest realm of reality in Neoplatonism</note>, likeness is superior to unlikeness—in powers, in thoughts, and in generations. 

Hence, <margin>C</margin> one might wonder at those Platonists who place **Otherness** above **Sameness**, when Plato says that the like is a thousand times more beautiful than the unlike. In general, while unlikeness somehow creeps into beings from **matter** <note>original: *hyle*</note>, likeness reaches down from the **Forms** alone and from the Intelligible causes. Therefore, likeness is a thousand times more beautiful than unlikeness, just as Sameness is more beautiful than Otherness. This is the **Judgment of Zeus** <note>A proverbial expression for a final, divine, and correct decision</note>; because of this, throughout the universe, the like is superior to the unlike—in essences, in motions, in shapes, and in all other things. This is reasonable; for the very **procession** <term>procession: *proodos*, the way reality flows out from the divine source</term> of things is accomplished through the likeness of the products to their causes.

Secondly, he traced the use of taste back to nourishment. <margin>15</margin> For nature established this sense as a judge of flavors, through which nourishment and growth occur; for the phrase "drained of moisture" <note>original: ἐξικμασμένην; referring to the process of digestion or the lack of external fluid in the Cosmos</note> is indicative of this. Furthermore, even the celestial bodies themselves <margin>25</margin> receive growth and decay proportionally according to their motions—through their risings and settings, their appearances and disappearances, their elevations and depressions, and their illuminations and changes of light. And you see again that while the things above the Moon possess growth and decay only by analogy, the things beneath the Moon are receptive to both in their very substance. # PROCLUS ON THE TIMAEUS BOOK III [Tim. 33 CD]

Therefore, those points must also be addressed in the context of the quoted text: if nothing is added to the whole, then no body exists outside of it; if nothing departs from it, there is no void outside the world. For a void would exist in vain if it were incapable of receiving anything, just as <margin>5</margin> that which received the world <note>referring to the "receptacle" or space</note>. Aristotle especially embraced these views, both by doing away with the existence of an actual void and by preserving the world as "only-begotten" <note>unique or one-of-a-kind</note>. <margin>D</margin> That nothing departs from or is added to the whole, he established: the former from the fact that there was nothing else to be added, and the latter—that nothing departs—from the fact that the whole makes its own decay its own nourishment. Just as, therefore, though it possesses no organs for food, it contains nourishment within itself and is itself both the nourisher and the nourished, so too, though it possesses no senses reaching out toward the external, it contains sensation within itself and is itself the object of its own perception. And that Plato clearly intends the same reasoning to apply to these matters is, I believe, made evident when he says: **"and all things happening in it and by it, both acting and being acted upon."** <note>original: "καὶ πάντα ἐν αὐτῷ καὶ ὑφ' ἑαυτοῦ πάσχον καὶ δρῶν"</note> If all things that come to be are the result of the whole acting upon itself and being acted upon by itself, then neither decays nor destructions should be called "evils" in an absolute sense; for even a virtuous man does no evil to himself, let alone the Whole. And <margin>20</margin> this must be taken as a divine conclusion derived from the argument. Again, he has traced back to the true cause of all things within the cosmos: the demiurgic <term>Demiurgic: relating to the Creator or "Craftsman" of the universe</term> art and the perfecting principle of all things. For from that source, art descends even into the demiurgic order; which is why the Oracles <note>The Chaldean Oracles, a sacred text for Neoplatonists</note> have called the Creator of the whole the "Master-Craftsman" <note>original: "ἐργοτεχνίτην"</note>. <margin>E</margin> Plato distinguished this, sometimes calling the things in the world "works" of the Creator: **"of which works I am the craftsman and father"** <note>quoting Timaeus 41A</note>; just as the Oracles say: **"For the paternal Mind, self-begotten, having conceived the works..."** <margin>30</margin> and sometimes addressing His activity as "Art," which defined the self-sufficiency of the whole as its goal; for He is good...
