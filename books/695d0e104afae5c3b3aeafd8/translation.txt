# TO THE MOST ILLUSTRIOUS AND REVEREND LORD BERNARDO SALVIATO
## MOST WORTHY ROMAN PRIOR,

->Angelo Canini of Anghiari sends many greetings.<-

In recent days, at the urging of certain friends of mine, I translated into the English <note>original: "latinam"; translated here as English for the reader's accessibility per instructions</note> language the second book of Alexander of Aphrodisias *On the Soul*, together with his short treatise *On Mixture*. This work has emerged in public as the first fruit of my studies. Truly, I followed the custom observed everywhere by all in publishing books—that they should come into the light under the auspices of some illustrious man. Furthermore, to provide a patron for this labor, however small, I considered in my mind whom I might find suited to these purposes. You, most distinguished Sir, came to mind (for although I am perhaps unknown to you, your illustrious deeds are before my eyes, clearer than light to me as they are to all other men). You alone, I say, occurred to me as being most worthy of this gift and most fit to provide such patronage. For I am well aware how much authority and esteem this book will hold among all people through you. Indeed, your authority and your most celebrated name will blunt all the bites of the envious and malevolent. For who is there who is ignorant of the nobility of your family, the virtues of your ancestors, and finally your own most brilliant deeds? Who has better explored the arts of warfare or all the <term>stratagems</term> <note>original Greek: στρατηγήματα; military maneuvers or ruses of war</note>? I pass over your skill in naval battles, in which you excel with the highest praise, as that is most well known to everyone. Since all these qualities are exceptional in you, you wished to temper the sharpness of military affairs with the gentleness of philosophy. This was done by you, like all other things, most wisely. Thus you philosophize most happily; you possess a mastery of fine literature, no less in Greek than in Latin—which is very rare—so that you may attain the <term>enjoyment</term> <note>original Greek: ἀπολαύσεως; the pleasure or fruition derived from something</note> of all good things. But if I wished to pursue your praises in speech, there would be need of both greater eloquence and longer time. Although they require the commendation of neither myself nor another, since they are clear to all and shine forth with their own light. Receive therefore, most illustrious Sir, with a cheerful countenance, this slight gift which the powers of my natural talent were able to produce, and place me in the number of your clients.
->Farewell.<-

# THE NATURAL ANNOTATIONS, DIFFICULTIES, AND SOLUTIONS OF ALEXANDER OF APHRODISIAS, BOOK ONE.
Alex. Aphro. Quest. A ii

# BY WHAT MEANS ONE MAY ESTABLISH
### the first cause according to Aristotle.

## CHAPTER ONE.

If all substances are corruptible <note>perishable or subject to decay</note>, then all things will be corruptible: for other things cannot be separated from substance. But not all things are corruptible, nor are all substances corruptible. Not all things are corruptible because it is impossible for motion to be corruptible, for it is eternal. For if it were generated <note>brought into being</note>, since everything that is generated is generated by something and from something, those things from which motion arose would also have to exist. But if it were the case that neither the agent nor the patient <note>the thing acting and the thing being acted upon</note> were such that the latter could be acted upon and the former could act, it would require some change; thus motion would already exist, rather than coming into being. If, however, there were something hindering them, some motion would have to occur so that this might act and that might be acted upon, and motion would arise from them. In this way, it would be necessary for there to be a motion before motion began, which was not generated. Yet motion is in that which is moved; therefore, there is also an eternal motion in that which is moved eternally, for it is impossible for anything to be moved by an eternal motion unless it is eternal. For if someone should say that motion is eternal because it passes from one body into another: first, he will not make the motion continuous and one; for motion is continuous when that which is moved by it is one. Furthermore, it will be possible for motion to fail unless some other thing, which is eternal, is the cause of the ordered and limited succession of those things which are moved—an eternal body, which is moved through eternal time. But only circular motion is eternal and continuous; therefore, that which is moved by it is eternal. This is also the best among bodies: for the eternal is better than the non-eternal. That also which is moved by the first of all motions is animate <note>possessed of a soul</note>: for the best of bodies are animate; for an animate body is better than an inanimate one. But the body that turns in a circle is the best, therefore it is also animate: for that which is the best of all bodies is animate, and the body that turns in a circle is of this kind. But indeed, everything that is moved is moved by something: therefore, even that which is moved by a soul is moved by something. If, however, that which is moved by a soul is moved by an impulse: and impulse comes from the desire of something: therefore, even if the body be eternal by force, it will be moved by the impulse and desire of something. If there is that which is moved by impulse and desire, it is necessary that there be something which, desiring this, is moved by an eternal motion, which is itself eternal and in act <note>actualized/active</note>: for everything that has the power of moving something moves it by being in act: and that which moves always and continuously will always be the same in act, and will be entirely devoid of potentiality <note>the capacity to be otherwise</note>. For if there were potentiality, it would be possible for the motions to be corrupted, since that which is moved by the motion itself would not be in act. Moreover, that thing will be immobile: for if this too were to move while being moved, it would in turn require some other mover. But the immobile is incorporeal: for every body, insofar as it is a body, is mobile. Therefore, there will be some eternal substance, simple and immobile, existing as the act of the body that turns in a circle, of eternal and continuous motion. The divine body will be moved by it, because it understands it, appetites it, and desires to become like it: for everything that is moved by some separate immobile thing is moved in this way. This demonstration is by resolution <note>analysis from effects back to causes</note>: for it is impossible for there to be a demonstration of the first principle: but it is necessary, when we have begun from later and manifest things, using resolution through their agreement with these, to have established its nature. That the motive form of circular motion is primarily intelligible <note>knowable by the mind</note>, and most greatly and primarily desirable, could be demonstrated from this. Form is properly intelligible: for matter, since it is none of the things that exist in act, is intelligible only by proportion, and as Plato says: by a "bastard reasoning" <note>original: "ratiocinatione"; referring to Timaeus 52b</note>. But form is intelligible, since it is something in act. Also, of forms, that which is in substance is more intelligible than that which is in something else, because it exists more; and of those in substance, that which is most simple and which is always in act: for this is most intelligible, because it most truly is, since it is always in act, and because it is simple by its own nature. The simple is most intelligible: for things in composites are only intelligible when the intellect has separated them from those things in which they exist, and has made them as if they were simple. The motive substance of the universe is of this kind: therefore, it is most intelligible. But it is also most desirable; for that which is by its nature most good is most desirable. Such is this substance, for good is found more in form than in matter: in that which is, more than in that which is acted upon. That which is acted upon is that which is something in potentiality, while the agent is that which is in act: and it is in the limited more than in the unlimited. Therefore, good is in form more than in matter: and in the form which is in substance more than in any other category: for through this, others also exist. And of those in substance, that which exists most, and is simple, and devoid of potentiality, is the most good. The nature previously described has been shown to be of this kind: therefore, it is properly and primarily desirable and intelligible.

### Some things concerning color. Chapter Two.

Aristotle defined color as the limit of the transparent in a determinate body, insofar as it is transparent: for of a body, insofar as it is a body, the limit is the surface; but insofar as it is transparent, the limit is color. Therefore, color exists simultaneously with the surface. For since the limit of every body, as a body, is the surface, and the transparent itself is a body, the limit of this too, as a body, is the surface. But it also has a limit as something transparent, which is color: and both limits of it exist together, since the limits are not the same as each other. For every body is in a surface and with a surface: yet not every surface is with color: because not every body is a determinate transparent thing. For of transparent bodies, some are determinate <note>having fixed boundaries, like a stone</note> and some are indeterminate <note>like air or water</note>. Second and thirdly, things are transparent by their distance from this. Whichever bodies are black, since they are deprived of color, they are also deprived of surface. For since color is among the number of existing things, and of those things born to exist in others, there must be something, a body, in which color exists: and this is the transparent body. In every body having color, or that which is receptive of color, the nature of the transparent is also mixed.

In those bodies, therefore, where the transparent exists in all things having something transparent in themselves, the extremity is manifest and bounded: this is in solids, and in these color is manifest and proper; and it is in these alone that what is properly called color exists, and more so in those which are more transparent. But the unbounded transparent has no proper or bounded color, because due to its thinness <note>original: "raritatem," referring to low density like air</note> it can retain and restrain nothing. While it may be a recipient and servant of foreign colors, it receives none of them passively; rather, it is moved by them through their presence and a certain relation to those bodies which have their own color, until it happens to be seen through the motion produced by the colors existing in them, supplying the power of seeing to animals that possess it, so that they may comprehend colors through it. The transparent can do this in **light**, being first moved by the presence of those things which have the nature of illuminating, suffering and receiving it as its own color; for this is not its own proper color, but as long as that which has the power of illuminating is present to it, it has light. When that which is in it departs, the light ceases. As long, therefore, as it is illuminated, it receives the qualities and differences of other colors in the same way, just as light is a servant to the senses having the power of seeing; which themselves consist of a body transparent in the same way, through the passion produced in the sights by the transparent external to them, which is moved in the aforesaid manner by the presence of colors.

Just as in the unbounded transparent, which due to its thinness receives no color passively, the presence of that which is born to illuminate is the cause of light, and its absence the cause of darkness, so too in bounded bodies a very great mixture and presence of the most transparent body exists—and such a body is that whose proper color is white: for this is the highest color, and the most visible, and the cause of bodies being more colored and more able to be seen. In every way, the absence of this is the cause of black itself, which is more like a privation of color than a color itself. Colors between these arise from a certain mixture of transparent things and those which are not of this kind, and they have their generation and indifference. It is likewise—

### Chapter Three: Of what things there are definitions.

<margin>Note.</margin>
**D**efinitions of individuals do not exist, because these things exist with certain accidents, and they are not always the same, but are those which fall away and require the declaration of the senses rather than reason itself. Nor are there definitions of any "common thing" separated from individuals, such as an incorporeal and eternal nature: for in what way could a "two-footed thing" be something incorporeal? Or in what way could a "mortal thing" be eternal? We say, when defining **man**, sometimes "a two-footed walking animal," and sometimes "a rational mortal animal." But definitions are of common things which are in the individuals themselves, or of individuals insofar as the common things are in them. For in individuals, some things are proper and singular, while others are common and shared. These common things, in all things in which they may be, are causes, being common and indifferent according to their nature, because all things possessing them are of a similar and same nature. For "rational mortal animal," if taken with material circumstances and differences (by which their subsistence differs from one another), they create **Socrates**, and **Callias**, and individual men; but if taken without these, it becomes a common thing. Not because it is not in each individual man—for with these are the properties of the individuals themselves—but because it is the same in all. Therefore, a thing of this kind is common in this way: because it is the same for many individuals. For this reason, definitions of such things are not of an incorporeal nature separated from individuals. For the definition of man, "a two-footed walking animal," is common in all individual men, whole in each one; common because it is the same in many, but not because the individual participates in it as something external, since each man is a two-footed walking animal. Therefore, definitions are not of common things *as* common things, but of those things to which it happens to be a singular nature: for even if only one man existed, the definition of man would be the same. For its definition is not "that which is in many," but because by such a nature a man is a man, whether there be many sharing this nature or not. They are called definitions of notions and common things because it is the task of the intellect to separate the whole from those things with which it subsists. Does it not seem to you that things which happen according to a series of causes happen in such a way that there are prior causes for all of them, and that each one follows from its prior cause by necessity? For in what other way is it possible to say that things which happen in this manner occur? Furthermore, regarding that which follows necessarily from a specific and prior cause, does it not seem to you to be prevented by that cause from happening in any other way than in accordance with its consequence to it? It seems so. But truly, that which does not happen because its happening was prevented will certainly not be called "possible." For if the "possible" is that which can happen when it lacks an impediment, then according to the opinion of those who say that what is not prevented is possible, that which is prevented from happening would thereafter be "impossible." And this [the alternative] was prevented from happening. You seem to me to say this correctly.

But if these things seem to you to be so, you will undoubtedly grant what follows: among those things which happen according to a series of causes and from prior causes, none can happen otherwise. This being granted, it follows—according to the opinion of those who say all things happen by fate—that only those things which actually happen are "possible," since everything that does not happen fails to happen because it was prevented from happening by the prior causes of the things that do happen. Why should I not grant what I have already conceded? And yet, it was also posited by us that all things which happen in this way happen by necessity. It was indeed posited. From which it follows again that only those things are possible which happen by necessity. ## Why growth is according to form alone, and not also according to matter. Chapter Five.

If the subject grows along with that which is said to grow, and the matter remains, not only the form, there will not be a total change: for which reason growth is according to form alone, and not also according to matter: for not all the matter which is in those things that grow is changed, but while some of it remains, other matter is added: for neither would that which was from the beginning be preserved: nor would it be as great as the pre-existing matter: for the essence of matter is not in being a certain quantity of matter, just as the essence of flesh is not in being a certain quantity. And, since there are two things in that which grows—namely the "what kind" <note>quality</note> and the "how much" <note>quantity</note>—for this is what grows, but this is a substance composed of both, and since the quantity itself, like matter, is the underlying subject, while the form holds the place of the quality: the quantity indeed does not remain the same, but the quality, which is like the form of the thing that grows, remains. Therefore, the quality, which is also the form, remains, but the quantity does not remain: and the cause of its not remaining the same is the flux <note>constant flowing/changing</note> of the matter itself: because of this, things that grow do not grow according to matter, but according to form: for this is what remains the same. For the growth of that which subsists by itself happens according to that which remains in it, and the composite itself subsists by itself, which remains according to form: for matter is that which flows. For even if some matter remains in that which has grown, it is not possible to predicate of this that it has been "grown": but that it itself, even if it remained for a time, afterwards flows away and is changed, for it is not in that which has grown: to take any matter which, remaining the same in number throughout the whole, has received some addition in quantity, for whatever you take, this also flows and is changed, and nothing of it remains the same in number, but the form remains the same as long as the thing is preserved.

### Why sickness is opposed to a "bad condition" rather than a "good condition." Chapter Nine.

The question was asked: why is sickness not opposed to a "good condition" <term>bona habitudo; Greek: euexia</term>—which is an intense state of health—but is instead opposed to a "bad condition"? For contrary conditions are those most distant from each other within the same genus. If a "bad condition" were present in a healthy person, it would be a kind of weakened health; thus, weakened health would be the contrary of intense health. But it is absurd to say that sickness is the contrary of health, but not also the contrary of intense health, or that there is an "intensity" within sickness; for this would be the state furthest removed from health. 

Now, if health and a "good condition," as well as sickness and a "bad condition," occurred regarding the same thing, what was said would be correct, since contraries must occur regarding the same thing. But if health is in one thing and a "good condition" in another—and where there is health, there is also sickness, but where there is a "bad condition," there is a "good condition"—then a "good condition" would not be "intense health," nor would sickness be its contrary, since they do not concern the same starting point. 

For health and sickness concern the powers of the primary bodies from which animals are composed—the elements themselves—whose powers are heat, cold, moisture, and dryness. Health is the mutual proportion <note>original: "mensura"</note> of these powers, while sickness is their lack of proportion. However, "good" and "bad" conditions concern those things related to strength and weakness. A "good condition" is similar to strength, and a "bad condition" to weakness. For one who has more strength than simple health requires is accurately called "well-conditioned," and such a person is strong. One who is weaker than those who are simply healthy is "ill-conditioned" in that respect. 

Yet strength and weakness exist in the proportion or lack of proportion of the "similar parts" <note>tissues like bone or muscle, as opposed to organs</note> in animals. Strength exists in their proportion, and weakness in their lack of proportion. Therefore, "good" and "bad" conditions rightly occur in these parts. Thus, just as weakness is the contrary of strength, so a "bad condition" is the contrary of a "good condition." Therefore, if a "good condition" were concerned with the same things as health, it would make sense to call it "intense health"; but because they are not concerned with the same things, it cannot properly be called that—unless perhaps because a "good condition" comes upon a pre-existing state of total health as an addition, but not according to the definition of health or its specific proportions.

### How, if there are four causes, there will not also be matter as a subject in the divine bodies. Chapter Ten.

If the principles and elements of natural things are those of which Aristotle spoke in the *Physics*—namely matter, form, the efficient cause, and the final cause <note>original: "ipsum cuius gratia"</note>—then the "circularly moving body" <note>the celestial spheres/heavens</note> will either not be a natural body, or there will be matter and a subject in it, or he did not list all the principles of natural things. For what is the subject for that body, since it is also a natural body? Every body is either natural or mathematical. That body is not mathematical, since everything in motion proceeding from itself is a natural body; and that body is in motion, being always mobile and having the cause of motion within itself. 

Now, if nature is the principle of motion and rest in which it exists primarily and not by accident, and that body does not have the principle of rest within itself, then it would not have a "nature." And if it does not have a nature, it would not be a natural body. But if it is not natural, what kind is it? Surely not mathematical, because it is animate <note>possessing a soul or life-force</note>; and every animate body is natural. But if it is not a natural body, it does not have matter as a subject, since matter is that which has the power to receive contraries successively. 

However, if we define matter only as the ultimate subject capable of receiving contraries in succession, then the subject of the "fifth body" <term>the ether or quintessence</term> would not be included in the definition of matter. But if one defines matter also as "the ultimate subject, formless in itself," then the subject of the divine body will be included in what is called matter. For this is a more general definition of a principle acting as a subject, embracing both types of subjects. Nevertheless, if the matter that is the subject of the divine body and the matter in things that are generated and perish are different, they are already composites. For if there were one subject for them, they would need differences to be specified, and having the same subject, they would differ from each other. But if they are entirely different, it is not necessary for them to differ by specific differences; for form and matter are not "composites" simply because they are different from each other.

### In what way it was said in the first book of *On the Soul*: "For the universal animal is either nothing or posterior." Chapter Eleven.

The question was asked how it was said by Aristotle in the first book of *On the Soul*: "For the universal animal is either nothing or posterior." He may have used the "animal" as an example for the soul itself and for those things called universals that have a "first" and a "second" <note>a hierarchy of priority</note>, wishing to show how they relate, as I have said in my commentary on that book. 

However, we can show that what was said about the "animal" also applies to things that are properly "genera." Since genera are substances <note>original: "usia" (ousia)</note>, and substances are universals to certain existing things; for it is not the case that if it is "nothing," it is a universal or a genus or predicated univocally. Rather, there must be some thing to which the universal happens <note>i.e., is an accident of</note>. That thing is indeed something, but the universal itself is not properly a "thing," but an accident of another. For example, "animal" is a certain thing signifying a certain nature: it signifies an animate substance having the power of sensation. According to its own nature, this is not a universal; for it would be no less an animal even if only one single animal were supposed to exist. 

But it belongs to it, being of this kind, to exist in many things which also differ from each other in species. Therefore, this "being a universal" happens to it; for that which is not in the essence of a thing is an accident to it. Since, therefore, a genus of this kind is not a "thing" but an accident to a thing, Aristotle said it was "either nothing"—since it is not properly a thing in this way—for when you wish to signify "animal" as a genus, he adds "universal" to "animal," saying "the universal animal." Therefore, this "animal-as-genus" is either nothing, because it does not signify a specific nature but is an accident upon an accident; or, if one calls such a thing a "being," it will be posterior to that in which it exists. For the thing must be prior to the accident that happens to it. 

That this is posterior to the thing is clear: for when there is an "animal," it is not necessary for "animal-as-genus" to exist; for one could suppose there is only one single animal, since the universal is not in its essence. But if "animal-as-genus" exists, it is necessary for "animal" to exist. If the animate substance with sensation is taken away, the "animal-as-genus" will be taken away, for it is impossible for that which does not exist to be in many things.
