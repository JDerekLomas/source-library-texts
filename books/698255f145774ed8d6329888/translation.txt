Grace and peace in Christ. While the <term>Divine Aurelius Augustine</term> <note>original: "D. Aurelius Augustinus". Augustine (354–430 AD) was the Bishop of Hippo and a foundational theologian for Western Christianity.</note> is great in his other works, he shows how great he truly is—or rather, Christ dwelling within him shows—especially in these two: the one *On Nature and Grace* and the other *On the Spirit and the Letter*. ## OF THE CHAPTERS OF THE DIVINE AURELIUS
### Augustine’s book on nature and grace
### against the Pelagians. <note>The Pelagians followed Pelagius, a monk who taught that human nature was not fundamentally corrupted by original sin and that humans could achieve salvation through their own free will and effort, rather than solely by God's grace.</note>

On the error of those who wish to excuse their sins by blaming nature, as if they were not committed voluntarily, and what the justice of God truly is. Chapter 1.

That no one is sufficient in himself to achieve perfect <term>righteousness</term> <note>original: "iustitiā". In this context, it refers to a state of being right or "justified" before God.</note>, nor can anyone be justified except through faith and the sacrament of the blood of Christ. Chapter 2.

That God indeed established nature well, but that nature has been darkened by vice contracted from <term>original sin</term> <note>original: "originali peccato". This is the foundational Christian doctrine—strongly championed by Augustine—that all humans inherit a stained nature due to the fall of Adam.</note>. Chapter 3.

That the grace of Christ is not granted based on merits, but is given freely, though not to everyone. Chapter 4.

That those who are liberated by grace from the punishment of eternal damnation are called <term>vessels of mercy</term> <note>original: "uasa misericordiæ". A biblical metaphor from the Book of Romans, referring to those chosen by God to receive His kindness.</note>. Chapter 5.

That it is the claim of worldly wisdom that infants do not need a physician and that adults are sufficient for their own righteousness. Chapter 6.

That the author of the submitted book <note>The "submitted book" refers to a work by Pelagius or one of his followers that was sent to Augustine for his review and response.</note> wrote with zeal, yet taught heretical doctrine—that a man can live without sin—by using a deceptive distinction between "the ability to be" and "actually being." Chapter 7.

An infant overtaken by death, who neither lived as a Christian nor had the opportunity to become one, is not absolved from damnation. <note>This reflects Augustine’s rigorous view on the necessity of baptism for the remission of original sin, even in infants.</note> Chapter 8.

That not only infants, but neither young men nor the elderly to whom the Gospel of Christ has not come, can be justified without the grace of Christ. Chapter 9.

...of infants and all his posterity, since they are weak. Chapter 21. <note>original: "uulorũ" likely an OCR error for "parvulorum". Augustine argued that since the Fall of Adam, even infants are born with a weakened nature.</note>

How manifold is the punishment of sin, and the grave retribution of sins through sins. Chapter 22. <note>Augustine often argued that one consequence of sinning is the loss of moral clarity, which leads inevitably to further sins—a "punishment" that is itself a sin.</note>

How God deserts those worthy of being deserted, and how **free will** <term>liberum arbitrium</term> <note>The power of the mind to choose between different courses of action. Augustine argues here that while we can choose to do evil on our own, we cannot choose to do good without divine help.</note> is sufficient for sinning, but to return to righteousness it has need of a **justifying physician**. Chapter 23. <note>original: "iustificatore medico". Augustine frequently uses medical metaphors, portraying God as a doctor who must heal the "disease" of the human soul before it can act rightly.</note>

That free will requires the price of Christ's passion, and how the wonderful mercy of God makes even evils work for good. Chapter 24.

That we ought to acknowledge our misery, and thus seek and implore mercy. Chapter 25.

That God heals not only to erase what we have sinned, but also to provide that we do not sin; proven by examples taken from a wounded person and the eye. Chapter 26. <note>Just as a doctor heals a wound so it stops hurting and also strengthens the body to prevent further injury, or heals an eye to allow sight.</note>

That it is truly said a human cannot be without sin, so that the occasion for **pride** <term>superbia</term> and vainglory—which tends to mingle itself even with good deeds—may be removed. Chapter 27.

That often some things seem to sound "sharp" or clever, which when examined are found to be "blunt" or weak. Chapter 28. <note>original: "acutule... obtusa". A rhetorical critique of the "sharp" arguments used by his opponents, the Pelagians.</note>

That although sins are committed through pride, not everything done wrongly is done proudly; and how the beginning of all sin is pride. Chapter 29.

That the **guilt** <term>reatus</term> <note>The legal or moral liability for an action.</note> of sin belongs to the one sinning, descending from health not used rightly. Chapter 30.

How deep is the counsel of God, who does not quickly heal pride—for which pious souls tearfully pray that it be crushed—as it entangles itself even in good works. Chapter 31.
