## Preface to the Compendium of Sericulture Essentials

The philosopher **Guanzi** <note>original: "Guanzi" (管子); a 7th-century BCE statesman and philosopher whose writings emphasize that the stability of a state depends on the basic economic welfare of its people.</note> once said: "If even one man does not plow, someone may suffer from hunger; if even one woman does not weave, someone may suffer from cold."

Under the governance of the Sage Kings, a hundred *mu* <note>original: "mu" (畝); a traditional Chinese unit of land area. One hundred mu was the standard allocation for a household in ancient agricultural ideals.</note> of land were granted to each family, and five *mu* were set aside specifically for the homestead. From this, we know that among the people of ancient times, there were none who did not cultivate mulberry trees. Can it be that the natural advantages of the land today are somehow different from the past? It is merely that human effort and the study of these matters have not been fully exhausted.

<gloss>Qinglian</gloss> <note>The style name of the author or editor of this compendium.</note> from my youth followed my father to his official posts in **Western Zhejiang** <note>original: "Zhexi" (浙西); a region famous for centuries as the heart of China's silk industry.</note>. There, I became accustomed to seeing the people, household by household, busy with their silkworms. They would trade their silk to obtain the value needed to provide for the <term>daily sustenance</term> <note>original: "zhanzhou" (饘粥); literally thick and thin rice congee, symbolizing the basic food requirements of the common people.</note> of the masters and the laborers in the fields. Thus, the auxiliary benefits provided by silkworms are truly profound.

Within the **Wu region**, districts such as Jiangning, Zhenze, Yixing, and Jingxi are all adjacent to the border of Zhejiang. Being <term>well-versed</term> <note>original: "shuan" (熟諳); meaning to have deep, practical experience and knowledge.</note> in <term>sericulture</term> <note>original: "cansang" (蠶桑); the combined practice of raising silkworms and cultivating mulberry trees.</note>, they reap great profits. However, in the prefecture of **Runzhou** <note>The historical name for modern-day Zhenjiang in Jiangsu province.</note>, only the single county of **Liyang** is known to practice the silk arts, while in the other districts, one hears nothing of it. Now, given that these places are all within the same prefecture, the climate and local customs are essentially the same...

Readers often find these works leave something to be desired.

I have cultivated a garden in a corner of the city with seven or eight hundred mulberry trees. ## Equipment

Cultivating mulberry trees and raising silkworms each requires specific tools; if even one is missing, it is impossible to achieve success. Some of the tools used in Huzhou differ from those in other regions, as they have been adapted to local conditions and follow long-standing customs. Having been used for generations, these designs are now strictly followed as the established rules of the ancestors. Confucius said, "The artisan who wishes to do his work well must first sharpen his tools." <note>A quote from the *Analects* emphasizing preparation.</note> Since silkworms are the very source of our clothing, how can we treat the preparation of their equipment as a matter of secondary importance? 

The *Greater Odes* <note>original: *Daya* (大雅), a section of the *Classic of Poetry*.</note> sing of preparing the hoes and mattocks, and the *Monthly Ordinances* <note>original: *Yueling* (月令), a chapter in the *Book of Rites*.</note> warn that the frames and stands for the silkworm trays must be prepared in advance. One should organize everything early so that being prepared prevents disaster, ensuring one is not hindered by a lack of tools when the busy season begins.

## Weeding Hoe

The weeding hoe is a tool for removing weeds. In ancient times, it was called a *chu* <note>original: 鉏</note> or a *zhuo* <note>original: 鐯</note>. Today, it is commonly known as a "weeder head" (*yuntou*). According to the *Collected Rimes* <note>original: *Ji Yun* (集韻), a dictionary compiled in 1037 AD.</note>, the term *yun* refers to the act of removing weeds. Thus, the "hoe" is the physical object, while "weeding" is its function; naming the hoe a "weeder" is an example of naming an object after its use. It is an essential tool for clearing wasteland and removing filth. Its shape is not uniform: some resemble ritual tablets <note>The *hu* (笏), a long, narrow slate held by officials at court.</note>, while others resemble axes or halberds. Generally, a wide and large blade is considered the most practical. The handle is made of bamboo or wood and is five feet long. The blade's edge must be hard and sharp to facilitate the mowing and clearing of vegetation.

### Mulberry Saw

The mulberry saw is a specialized saw used for pruning mulberry trees. Whenever the trunk or branches of a mulberry tree are particularly thick and sturdy—such that standard pruning shears cannot cut through them—one must use this saw to trim them.

Those manufactured from hardened steel are considered of the highest quality. The blade is over one foot <note>approx. 32 cm</note> long, half an inch wide, and very thin, with fine teeth arranged evenly along the edge. An iron bar is bent into a bow shape and secured to both ends of the blade with nails; a short piece of wood is inlaid at the base to serve as a handle. It is vital that the teeth are sharp and keen <note>original: 銛利 (xianli)</note>. The teeth should be "set" by being angled slightly to the left and right; this ensures the cut is wider than the blade itself, allowing the saw to move back and forth without becoming stuck.

## Mulberry Grafting Knife

The mulberry grafting knife is five inches long and one and a half inches wide. Its most important quality is its sharpness. It must be forged from refined iron, for the entire success of grafting <term>grafting</term> <gloss>The technique of joining a shoot (scion) from one plant onto the trunk or root (stock) of another so they grow together.</gloss> mulberry trees depends on the precision of the splitting and paring of the wood. Therefore, the quality of this knife must never be neglected.

## Mulberry Scraper

The mulberry scraper <note>original: 桑杷 (sangba)</note> is a tool used for scraping pests and insects off the bark of mulberry trees. It is made of iron with a scraping edge over an inch wide. The body of the tool is curved and measures three inches in length. The end is fashioned with a socket <term>socket</term> <gloss>original: 闞孔 (kankong); a hollow opening designed to receive a wooden pole.</gloss> to receive a handle. The handle varies in length from two to three feet, chosen based on the height of the mulberry branches being treated.

# Spray Cylinder

The spray cylinder <note>original: 噴筒 (pentong)</note> is a device used for spraying water. It is constructed from a ring of brass, with a circular diameter of about six or seven *fen* <note>approx. 2 cm</note> and a length of one *chi* <note>approx. 31 cm</note>. A copper plate is fitted into the bottom as a base, with a fine slit drilled into it to serve as the passage for water to enter and exit. 

Additionally, a slender, round wooden rod is used, which is about half a length longer than the cylinder itself. At one end of the rod, a short handle is fixed; at the other end, two or three layers of cowhide are nailed, sized to fit the cylinder perfectly. This rod is inserted into the cylinder and used to suck up and spray out liquid, functioning much like the mechanism of a **bellows** <term>bellows</term> <gloss>An apparatus consisting of a flexible bag and valves, used here as a metaphor for the piston-like suction and pressure of the pump (original: 橐之鼓籥).</gloss>. This allows the liquid to be sent to high branches or over a great distance. Whenever there are **borer insects** <note>original: 蠹蟲 (duchong)</note> on the mulberry trees, one must use this device to draw up medicinal water and spray it upon them.

# Mulberry Shears

Mulberry branches must never be pulled or snapped by hand; they must be removed using shears. These shears are forged from steel and iron. The "head" (the blades) is one and a half inches long, while the "body" (the handles) is five inches long. The "shoulders" <note>the pivot area</note> should not be too wide, so that the tool is easy to grip and handle. The cutting edge must be extremely sharp. One must avoid shears that have "trapped ash" <note>a forging defect where slag is caught in the metal</note>, those that "chew iron" <note>where the blades nick or roll when cutting</note>, or those with "soft edges" <note>referring to improperly tempered steel that dulls quickly</note>.

The **mulberry stool** <note>original: 桑凳 (sangdeng)</note> is used as a platform for climbing to pick mulberry leaves. It stands five *chi* <note>approximately 1.5 meters</note> high, and the dimensions of its seat vary, being either square or rectangular. The legs of the stool are fitted with three or four levels of cross-struts on both the left and right sides to serve as ladder rungs. For mulberry trees that are not particularly tall, one climbs this stool to harvest the leaves.

### Mulberry Ladder

For branches that extend high and far, one relies on a ladder to ascend. There is no fixed rule for the height of the ladder, though nine *chi* <note>approximately 2.7 meters</note> is generally considered ideal. This design uses two ladders joined together; two iron rings are nailed to the sides of the top of each, and a thin piece of wood is passed through them to connect the two. When moved, the bottom halves spread apart; when folded, they join together. In the mulberry groves, using this to reach high and distant branches is far superior to relying on a single ladder leaned against a tree.

# Silkworm Baskets

In ancient times, the vessels used to carry silk fabrics and currency were called *fei* <term>篚; a shallow, often rectangular or circular basket</term>. Containers for storing silkworms were also called by this name. The people of Huzhou <note>a region in Zhejiang Province famous for its silk production</note> calculate the scale of their silkworm rearing by the basket. <gloss>Generally, for silkworms in the "black stage" <note>newly hatched larvae, which are dark and hairy</note>, one *qian* <note>approx. 3.75 grams</note> of larvae constitutes one basket. At the "leaving the fire" stage <note>after the first molt when supplemental heating is reduced</note>, twenty-four or twenty *liang* <note>taels</note> constitute one basket. By the "Great Sleep" <note>the fourth larval molt</note>, six or five baskets <note>original: 𠀘, a specialized unit for a basket-load</note> are required. One basket's worth of silkworms from the "leaving the fire" stage is the standard, though the containers themselves are not always named "baskets." They are also called sieves, trays, or mats. <gloss>The *Prefectural Records* cite the *Records of Huzhou*: When silkworms are first born, they are placed in a sieve (*shai*). Once the "black stage" larvae fill the space, they are moved to a tray (*peng*). After the second sleep, they use...</gloss>

## Silkworm Stands

The *Book of Rites* <note>an ancient collection of texts describing social forms, administration, and ceremonial rites</note> states that in the final month of spring, one should prepare the "baskets and stands." The *zhi* <term>植; stand</term> is the frame used to support the silkworm baskets. While the ancient designs can no longer be verified, modern stands utilize a triangular style. They are constructed using three wooden beams as pillars, each standing eight feet high. Within this frame, nine tiers of cross-bars <term>檔; *dang*</term> are set, spaced eight inches apart—a distance chosen to allow easy insertion and removal of the baskets.

The front cross-bars are six feet long, while the rear bars are three feet long. In the middle of the front bars, a short tenon <term>準; *zhun*, a projecting piece of wood</term> four inches long is inlaid. The ends of the rear bars are carved into matching slots <term>準口; *zhun kou*, a mortise or joint opening</term> to fit onto these tenons. A hole is drilled through the center and secured with a bamboo peg, which allows the entire structure to be rotated and folded for storage. For young silkworms, "small stands" are used; these are approximately four feet high and four feet wide, with five or six tiers. These smaller frames are designed to be placed inside bed curtains <note>silkworm rearers often kept young larvae within their own bed canopies to maintain a stable, warm temperature and protect them from drafts</note>.

## Brushing the Silkworm "Hairs"

The tool for "carrying the silkworm hairs" is made from goose feathers. They are chosen for their lightness and softness, which prevents any injury to the silkworms. When the "new blacks" <note>original: *xin wu*; refers to newly hatched silkworm larvae, which are dark and covered in tiny hairs</note> first emerge, they are as delicate as a single hair. Since it is impossible to handle them by hand without causing harm, one must use these feathers to gently collect and move them.

# Silkworm Chopsticks

<term>Silkworm Chopsticks</term> are made of bamboo. They are five inches <note>approximately 16 centimeters</note> long and are as thick as ordinary eating chopsticks. One end is carved into a point and polished until it is perfectly smooth and glossy. This is an essential tool for lifting and moving small, newly hatched silkworms.

# Silkworm Feeding Stool

The <term>Silkworm Feeding Stool</term> should be thick and heavy for stability. The top surface is a little over one square foot, and the legs stand two feet high. A circular hole is carved into the center of the stool’s surface. Between the left and right legs, a lower cross-bar is installed, featuring a shallow socket that aligns perfectly with the hole in the stool's surface above.

Separately, a round wooden pillar three feet high is prepared. Two cross-bars are fixed horizontally and vertically across the top of this pillar to form a "cross" shape. The bottom of the pillar is inserted through the hole in the stool's surface and set into the socket of the lower cross-bar. This design allows the top frame to rotate freely to the left or right, making it extremely convenient to place a basket on top and turn it while feeding mulberry leaves to the worms.

# Reed Screens (Continued)

<term>Reed screens</term> <note>original: "簾", *lian*</note> are of the utmost importance. When placed upon the mountain scaffolds <note>original: "山棚", *shanpeng*, tiered wooden racks used to hold silkworm trays and maximize space</note>, they are indispensable for gathering the worms for their final stage of cocooning. There is no fixed rule for their length or width; rather, they must be constructed to match the specific dimensions of the rearing room. Generally, a single room should be filled by spreading out four to six screens. It is essential to weave these screens yourself to ensure a precise fit, as those purchased ready-made are quite difficult to piece together effectively.

Among various types of reeds, there is a variety that is slender and perfectly straight called <term>skinny reed</term> <note>original: "篛蘆", *ruolu*</note>, which is the best material for weaving screens. The cord used should also be thin; if the cord is fine, the weave of the screen will be tight, preventing the danger of young silkworms falling through the gaps.

To weave them, first construct a wooden frame seven or eight feet long and over one foot wide <note>a Chinese "foot" or *chi* is approximately 31-33 centimeters</note>. Along the crossbars of the frame, at intervals of every four inches <note>approximately 12 centimeters</note>, fix bamboo nails in a cross pattern. When you are ready to weave, first clean the reeds by removing the husks and leaves <note>original: "籜葉", *tuoye*</note> and trimming off the thin tips. Set the wooden frame level upon a pair of stools or a similar support. At each point where the bamboo nails cross, clamp a long cord. Lay a single reed horizontally across the cords, then take the cords and weave them alternately over and under each successive reed.

The handle is four inches long, a size specifically chosen for ease of grip and maneuverability within the trays.

# Straw Mats

<term>Straw mats</term> <note>original: "草薦", *caojian*</note> are woven from rice stalks. Their construction is exactly the same as that of reed screens; however, because straw is a weaker material, one must insert a thin bamboo pole at intervals of every foot or so of weaving to provide structure. These are used to cover doorways and windows or to wrap around and protect the silkworm racks. They provide significantly more warmth than reed screens.

# Silkworm Transfer Nets

<gloss>The term *Xian* <note>original: "縣", meaning to suspend or move</note> is defined in the *Guangyun* <note>a comprehensive Chinese dictionary of rhymes from the 11th century</note> as <unclear>unclear pronunciation marks</unclear> "moving the silkworms to a more spacious area."</gloss>

These nets are made from spun hemp, following the same design as a fishing net. Their length, width, and breadth are determined by the size of the rearing baskets. The density of the mesh varies: for young silkworms, a fine mesh is appropriate, while for larger worms, a coarser mesh should be used. Using these nets to transfer silkworms is the most efficient and convenient method. <note>The net is placed over the old bed with fresh leaves on top; the worms crawl up through the mesh to the new leaves, allowing the farmer to lift the entire net and move the worms to a clean tray or more space.</note>

# Cocoon Baskets

The <term>cocoon basket</term> <note>original: "繭籃", *jianlan*</note> is a vessel used for holding the harvested cocoons. They are woven from fine bamboo slivers and come in various sizes. It is essential that the interior be smooth and the construction sturdy and fine. <note>Smoothness prevents the silk fibers from snagging on the basket.</note>

# Ground-Level Feeding Stools

These stools stand one foot high <note>approximately 32 centimeters</note> and are seven or eight feet long, with a surface width of eight inches. They must be built for sturdiness above all else. When silkworms are reared on the ground <note>a method where worms are spread on mats on the floor rather than on raised racks</note>, there is no place for the caretaker to step. One must stand upon these stools to distribute the mulberry leaves.

# Straw Brooms

<term>Straw brooms</term> <note>original: "草帚", *caozhou*</note> are placed atop the "mountain scaffolds" <note>terraced racks designed to provide climbing space for silkworms</note> to provide a place for the silkworms to gather and spin their cocoons. It is best to use rice straw from Hangzhou; the stalks of common <term>Indica</term> <note>original: "秈", *xian*</note> or <term>Japonica</term> <note>original: "稉", *geng*</note> rice are too weak and cannot support the weight of the silkworms. 

There are two methods for tying these brooms:

1.  **The Mound-head Broom:** Take a handful of straw approximately two feet long. Tie it tightly around the middle and spread the ends out in a spiral shape. This is known as a <term>mound-head broom</term> <note>original: "墩頭帚", *duntou zhou*</note>.
2.  **The Folded-head Broom:** Take a small bundle of straw three feet long. Twist it tightly at the center and fold it over to create two lengths. Within these two lengths, grasp several stalks and wrap them around the main twist several times, causing the ends to fan out like an umbrella. This is known as a <term>folded-head broom</term> <note>original: "折頭帚", *zhetou zhou*</note>.

Although the methods of tying them differ, their practical use is identical.

### The Silk Balance

The **silk balance** <note>original: "絲稱", *sicheng*. ## Planting Mulberries

The mulberry tree is the very foundation of sericulture. To raise silkworms, one must first cultivate the mulberry. Small weeds must be entirely removed. As the ancients said, **"plow deeply and weed frequently"** <note>original: "深耕易耨", shengeng yinou. Near the base of the mulberry trunk, it is especially important to **completely turn the soil** <term>fanshen</term> <note>original: "翻身". Literally "to flip the body"; in farming, it refers to rotating the deep soil to the surface.</note>. 

Old farmers have a saying: *"It is better to graze the bark than to leave a clod of mud."* This is because if clods of earth are left undisturbed, they will become hardened and compacted over time, making it impossible for the soil to absorb fertilizer or for the tree to flourish. In the spring, when turning the ground, there is much rainfall. The surface must be plowed flat so that water does not stagnate. Any footprints left behind by workers must also be completely leveled. 

During the "silkworm months" <note>The peak spring season when silkworms are being reared and require constant feeding.</note>, the ground in the mulberry groves inevitably becomes packed down from the heavy foot traffic of harvesting leaves. Once the silkworm season is over, you should take advantage of clear weather to **turn and sun-dry** the soil. 

If the **Plum Rains** <note>original: "黃梅", Huangmei. The period of heavy seasonal rains in early summer, named for the time when plums ripen.</note> persist and prevent you from digging, and weeds grow thick, they should be pulled by hand or removed with a spade. During the summer months, it is a common but poor practice to merely surface-weed without turning the soil. This leaves the ground loose on top but hard underneath; as soon as a heavy rain falls, the loose topsoil is washed away. How much soil can be added in a single year to withstand such constant erosion? 

Naturally, **deeply turning the earth** is the correct method. Even if you only intend to weed, you must dig two or three inches deep rather than just scraping the surface of the ground. In all matters of reclamation and turning the soil, one must wait for a period of established clear weather. If you finish digging and it immediately rains, the mud will become a mired, hardened mess—it would be better not to have dug at all. For weeding specifically, extremely clear weather is essential so that the weeds die in the sun the moment they are turned over. It is even better to weed before the seeds have sprouted.

## Layering

For small mulberry trees, growing them from seedlings is not as fast as the "layering" method <term>yātiáo</term> <note>original: "壓條". A propagation technique where a living branch is bent to the ground and covered with soil to produce roots while still attached to the parent plant.</note>. This method is suitable for newly filled land or embankments near water. 

In the winter, pile up mud around the base once. During the leaf-picking season, set aside the branches you wish to layer and pull them down so they touch the ground. Secure them with bamboo or wooden hooks. Wait until the leaf tips point upward and the branch has grown long, then bury that section in the soil. Water them during the **Yellow Plum** rainy season <note>original: "黃梅". The "Meiyu" or "Plum Rains," a period of heavy rainfall in early summer (June/July) in the Yangtze River region, coincide with the ripening of plums.</note>.

### Pruning

As the proverb says: *"Be filial when planting bamboo, but be rebellious when pruning mulberry."* This means that when it comes to pruning mulberry trees, one must not harbor the slightest bit of pity or hesitation <note>The "filial" person treats the plant gently, while the "rebellious" person is ruthless; mulberry trees require aggressive cutting to produce the best silk-producing leaves</note>. In the *Essentials of Agriculture and Sericulture* <note>original: "農桑輯要" (*Nong-sang Jiyao*), a famous Yuan Dynasty agricultural manual</note>, the method for categorizing branches consists of four types:

1.  **Water-shedding branches** <term>lìshuǐ tiáo</term>: Those that hang or droop downward.
2.  **Body-piercing branches** <term>cìshēn tiáo</term>: Those that grow inward toward the center of the tree.
3.  **Parallel-finger branches** <term>piánzhǐ tiáo</term>: Those that grow side-by-side in pairs; one should be selected for removal.
4.  **Superfluous branches** <term>rǒngshèng tiáo</term>: Those that grow in the correct direction but are too crowded or dense.

Pruning should be performed approximately four times a year. During the second leaf harvest, do not strip away too many leaves. Over-harvesting at this stage often causes the tender young branches to droop or snap. To prevent this damage and suppression of growth, you must inevitably leave more branches alone during this period. In the middle of the sixth month, you must inspect the tree closely. Any of those extra young branches or newly sprouted clusters of leaves that were previously spared must now be pruned away ruthlessly. By the seventh month, begin the process of binding the mulberry branches.

Do not be deterred by low prices or driven by a greed for higher prices, which might lead you to leave the leaves growing on the tree. While tender mulberry leaves might endure this, if old leaves are left on the tree for a full year, the very foundation and roots of the tree will wither and fail. Even if you apply piles of fertilizer later, the tree will ultimately succumb to decay. 

If it happens that there are no buyers at all, you must nonetheless prune the trees bare once the silkworm season has ended. Apply **liquid manure** <term>清糞 (qīng fèn): a mixture of water and organic waste used as a fast-acting fertilizer</term> twice in succession, and naturally, new tender branches will grow lushly. By the following spring, if you apply a thick layer of fertilizer, the yield of leaves will not be diminished. It is absolutely vital not to leave old branches growing, as this leads to the rotting of the mulberry tree—this has been proven through repeated experience and should not be doubted. 

Second, the leaves must not be over-harvested. If you take too many, the new shoots will be soft and prone to breaking. In years when leaf prices are high, to prevent theft or damage from people pulling at the branches, if you are forced to thin the leaves even to the very top of the tree, you must use shears to trim them. Leave the leaf-stalks on the branches; only then will no harm be done to the tree. After **Frost Descent** <note>original: "霜降" (Shuangjiang) — the 18th solar term in the traditional Chinese calendar, typically beginning around October 23, marking the time when morning frosts begin</note>, gather the old leaves and dry them. Wrap them in rice straw to store as fodder for sheep.

## The Red Pestle Breed

The Red Pestle <note>original: 丹杵 (*dān chǔ*); named for its resemblance to a cinnabar-colored pestle used in traditional medicine</note> is a "four-sleep" variety. ### The Second Silkworm Breed

The Second Silkworms <note>original: 二蠶 (*èr cán*)</note> are the summer silkworms. This breed also originates from the spring stock but emerges several days earlier than the standard first crop; they are called "First-Second" silkworms.

## The Third, Fourth, and Fifth Silkworm Breeds

The first batch <gloss>the young silkworms</gloss> are considered the summer silkworms; the third, fourth, and fifth batches are the autumn varieties. These stocks originate from the spring crop. The first batch to emerge does so at the same time as the primary "ant-silkworms" <gloss>newly hatched larvae, so-called for their tiny, dark appearance</gloss>. In terms of the diligence and speed of their growth and development, no other breed matches these varieties. However, the weight of the silk they produce is somewhat lighter than that of the summer’s second silkworms.

## Driving Away Flies

There are many types of flies, and their offspring are maggots. One specific kind is known as the "Flesh Fly" <note>original: 麻蠅 *maying*, likely referring to the *Sarcophagidae* family, which are known for depositing larvae rather than eggs</note>. They are large with a loud buzz. When they fly into and gather in the silkworm room, they attach their larvae to the silkworm's body. Furthermore, their nature is as deceptive and harmful as malicious spirits <note>original: 鬼蜮 *guiyu*, a legendary creature that shoots sand at people's shadows to harm them; a metaphor for treacherous people or pests</note>, making them difficult to defend against. While they are relatively scarce during the spring silkworm season, six or seven out of every ten silkworms are harmed by them from mid-summer through autumn. ## Frost and Snow Bathing

In the middle of the twelfth lunar month <note>the final month of the traditional Chinese calendar, falling in mid-winter</note>, use a sieve to hold the seed paper <note>original: 種紙 *zhongzhi*, paper sheets where silkworm moths have laid their eggs</note> and place it atop the eaves of the house. Allow the frost and snow to freeze and congeal upon the sheets. When the Spring Equinox arrives, begin to collect the sheets and bring them indoors; this process is known as the "Heavenly Bath." 

This practice is based on the fact that when silkworm moths lay their eggs, some are "solid" <note>viable and healthy</note> while others are "hollow" <note>unfertilized or weak</note>. Once they have been subjected to the biting cold of winter, the solid eggs will eventually hatch into silkworms, while the hollow ones will not survive. For this reason, the ancients believed that this method produced silkworms that were hardy and easy to raise. Today, the silk farmers of Hangzhou largely follow this method, though it is rarely seen in the local customs of Huzhou.

## Saltwater Bathing

In the twelfth lunar month, fill a basin with lukewarm water and spread the seed paper flat across the water's surface. Separately, take three or four *liang* <note>a traditional unit of weight; 4 *liang* is approximately 150 grams</note> of table salt and dissolve it in a container of water, then pour this into the basin. After waiting for half a *shichen* <note>original: 時辰, a traditional unit of time equal to two hours; half a *shichen* is one hour</note>, remove the papers. 

Place the papers inside a sieve and set them on the eaves of the house, exposing them to the open air and dew for three full days and nights. Afterward, store them in a high, dry location. 

Huang Shengzeng <note>A prominent Ming dynasty agricultural scholar and author of the *Book of Sericulture* (1530)</note> stated: "If the seeds are soaked in brine, it benefits the process of silk reeling <note>original: 繰絲 *qiaosi*, the drawing out of silk filaments from the cocoons</note>, as the salt provides a necessary smoothness and moisture." I have often observed in the coastal areas of Hangzhou, near the sea dikes, that farmers take their seed paper in the twelfth lunar month and hang it on bamboo poles stood upright before their eaves. They allow the sea breezes to blow over the sheets so they may absorb the "briny air" <note>original: 鹵氣 *luqi*, saline or sea-salt vapors</note>. This follows the same principle as the saltwater bath.

### Lime Water Bath

In the twelfth lunar month <note>the final month of the traditional Chinese calendar</note>, use a basin of lukewarm water <gloss>the water must not be hot; it should be as warm as a human body</gloss>. On the twelfth day of the second lunar month, take a basin of clear water. Gather the flowers of various herbs and trees, rub them into the water, and bathe the silkworm eggs in it. ### Timing the Hatching

The hatching rate of silkworms is traditionally timed to the Grain Rain <note>original: *Guyu*, a solar term beginning around April 20th</note>. An old proverb says: "On Grain Rain, do not hide the insects," meaning that by this time, the silkworms will naturally emerge on their own.

However, waiting for them to hatch naturally is often considered too late. Farming families dread the "Three Littles" <note>original: *San Xiao*</note>: *Little Full* <note>the solar term *Xiaoman*, late May</note> for silkworms, *Slight Heat* <note>the solar term *Xiaoshu*, early July</note> for the fields, and *Slight Snow* <note>the solar term *Xiaoxue*, late November</note> for the wheat. If the production cycle for these items is delayed until these specific times, the harvest will be meager. 

When the silkworm eggs are on the paper sheets, if you "protect" <note>original: *hu*, meaning to incubate or shelter</note> them, they will hatch early; if you do not, they will be late. To "protect" essentially means to assist; it refers to using human warmth to assist the vital energy of the living creature. 

In the Huzhou region, silkworm-rearing households draw the shape of a curved bow with lime in front of their doors when the Qingming Festival <note>Tomb Sweeping Day, usually occurring in early April</note> arrives. Tradition holds that this is done to "dispel silkworm evil spirits" <note>original: *qu can sui*</note>. However, it is not that the silkworms are actually haunted; rather, because Qingming is the designated time to begin "Protecting the Seeds," drawing this bow serves as a self-reminder to the household to avoid idling or wandering about and to focus on the work at hand.

There is a fifteen-day gap between the Qingming Festival and Grain Rain. Experienced farmers have a saying: "Molting at the head of Grain Rain" <note>original: *Guyu zhuangtou mian*; "Sleep" or "molting" refers to the dormant phases of the silkworm's growth</note>. If one follows the proper protection methods during these fifteen days to make the silkworms hatch quickly so they reach their first molt by the Grain Rain solar term, the silkworms will be early and the harvest will naturally be bountiful. 

Generally, those who practice "cold rearing" should begin protecting the eggs early, while those who practice "warm rearing" can afford to wait a bit longer. <gloss>The people of Huzhou refer to raising silkworms as "watching silkworms" (original: *kan can*). Using fire for warmth is called "warm rearing" (*rekan*), while not using fire is "cold rearing" (*lengkan*).</gloss> 

If the mulberry buds have not yet opened before Qingming <unclear>矣</unclear> and the weather suddenly turns hot, you must wrap the egg sheets and place them in a cool, shaded area. They must not be exposed to hot winds.

## Scattering the Hatchlings <gloss>The character for "scattering" (Yang) is pronounced here in the departing tone.</gloss>

It generally takes about three days for the hatchlings <note>literally "ant-children" (*yier*), so called for their tiny, dark appearance upon hatching</note> to emerge fully from their eggs. Pine wood should be avoided because its smoke is heavy. Charcoal comes in large and small sizes. Among the large varieties, charcoal made from burned chestnut wood is considered the most superior. Chestnut charcoal is dense in body and powerful in heat; its fire lasts the longest. ### The Fire Basin

Use one medium-sized earthenware basin. First, spread a layer of ash on the bottom. Use hardwoods such as mulberry, elm, sandalwood, or chestnut. Saw the wood into lengths of approximately four inches <note>about 12–13 centimeters</note> and fill the basin halfway. The wood should stand vertically; it must not be laid flat. Fill the gaps between the wood with ash; be careful not to leave empty spaces. If there are empty spaces, flames will break out, and the fire will not last. Use charcoal dust to fill the basin to the rim, then cover it again with ash. For the charcoal, it is best to use "River charcoal" <note>charcoal transported via the Yangtze River</note> or "Szechuan charcoal dust." Although the small charcoal produced locally catches fire easily, it does not actually last through a long burn.

### Testing for Cold and Heat

Using fire to warm silkworms is a method by which humans govern nature <note>original: 天 "Heaven"</note>. The climate is inconsistent; morning and evening differ, and overcast days are distinct from sunny ones. By regulating the environment with fire, one can ensure that even when the weather fluctuates between cold and warmth, the silkworms remain unaware of the shifts between heat and chill. Within the silkworm room, use a single-layered garment <note>a light, unlined robe</note> as your standard. If you feel cold while wearing only a single layer, the silkworms are also cold—add fire immediately. If you feel hot in a single layer, the silkworms are also hot—reduce the fire immediately. As for the subtleties of these fluctuations, test them with your own body to achieve the proper balance.
