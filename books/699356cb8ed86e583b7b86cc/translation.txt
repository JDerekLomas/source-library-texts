I have heard that the <term>Sage Confucius</term> <note>original: "孔聖"</note> said: "Shen! My Way is bound together by a single thread." <term>Zengzi</term> <note>original: "曾子"; one of Confucius’s most influential disciples.</note> replied: "Yes." After the Master had left, the other disciples asked: "What did he mean?" Zengzi said: "The Way of our Master consists of <term>loyalty and empathy</term> <note>original: "忠恕"; "Zhong" (loyalty/integrity) and "Shu" (empathy/reciprocity), considered the essence of Confucian ethics.</note>, and nothing more." 

I have also heard that when <term>Shakyamuni</term> <note>original: "釋迦"; the historical Buddha.</note> was at <term>Vulture Peak</term> <note>original: "靈山"; a sacred mountain in India where the Buddha is said to have delivered many sermons.</note>, he held up a flower and <term>Kasyapa</term> <note>original: "迦葉"; the Buddha's disciple.</note> smiled. The <term>World-Honored One</term> <note>original: "世尊"; a title for the Buddha.</note> said: "I possess the <term>Treasury of the True Dharma Eye</term> <note>original: "正法眼藏"; the essential, wordless teaching of Buddhism.</note> and the <term>Wondrous Mind of Nirvana</term> <note>original: "涅槃妙心"</note>. I entrust them to Mahakasyapa." 

Though the practical applications of these two traditions differ, their underlying meaning is one and the same. Viewing it from this perspective, the principle of the "single thread" and the <term>mind-to-mind transmission</term> <note>original: "以心傳心"; the Zen concept of transmitting enlightenment without relying on words or scriptures.</note> has continued through thousands of years without the slightest break. The deep lineage of the study of the Way truly stems from within Zengzi's single "Yes"...

Behold! The Buddha's Dharma is radiant and clear, enduring for many thousands of <term>kalpas</term> <note>original: "劫"; an eon or an unimaginably long period of cosmic time.</note>. It makes manifest the <term>Ancestral Wind</term> <note>original: "宗風"; the specific spirit, style, or tradition of a Zen lineage.</note>, and its source follows in unbroken succession. 

This <term>separate transmission outside the teachings</term> <note>original: "教外別傳"; a foundational Zen principle asserting that the ultimate truth is transmitted directly from heart to heart, rather than through the study of written scriptures.</note> began within the smile of <term>Mahakasyapa</term> <note>original: "迦葉"; the disciple of the Buddha who achieved enlightenment when the Buddha simply held up a flower; Kasyapa responded with a smile.</note>. How could it ever branch off into two separate ways? 

Since the <term>Jingde era</term> [1004–1007], the *Record of the Transmission of the Lamp* <note>original: "傳燈錄"; specifically the *Jingde Chuandeng Lu*, the first great history of Zen lineages.</note> has circulated in the world. This was followed by the *Extensive Lamp*, the *Linked Lamp*, the *Continued Lamp*, and the *Universal Lamp*. Thus, lamp after lamp has continued the flame. Though the lineages and branches differ, they all return to the same principle. We know that a "lamp" is that which shatters the darkness of ignorance in order to illuminate this Way.

Now, the Head Monk <term>Huiming</term> has gathered these five "Lamp" records into a single collection, titled the **Compendium of the Five Lamps** <note>original: "五燈會元"; *Wudeng Huiyuan*.</note>, to make it convenient for viewing and study. The <term>Layman Shen</term> <note>original: "沈居士"; a "layman" is a non-monastic follower of Buddhism.</note> donated the funds to hire craftsmen and <term>carve the woodblocks</term> <note>original: "鋟梓"; literally "to carve on catalpa wood," the traditional method of printing.</note> at <term>Lingyin Mountain</term> <note>original: "靈隱山"; the location of the famous Lingyin Temple in Hangzhou, a major center of Zen culture.</note>. The masters <term>Dachuan</term> and <term>Laolu</term>, as well as the Monastic Superintendent, all assisted in bringing this to fruition. 

When the work was quickly completed, the monk <term>Fo'an</term> brought a copy to me and repeatedly entreated me to write this preface. I replied: "The entire Great Treasury of Teachings is like using paper to wipe away filth. From this, one knows that the Buddha's Dharma does not reside in letters, nor does it exist within words. If one is a <term>person of great resolve</term> <note>original: "大丈夫漢"; literally a "great husband" or "hero," referring to a serious practitioner.</note>, they must see clearly and awaken thoroughly. It is like a swift falcon seizing its prey—once caught, it is gone. If one turns their head or tilts their brain, hesitating even for a moment, they will find themselves 'passing by <term>Silla</term> in vain' <note>original: "空過新羅"; a Zen idiom referring to the kingdom of Silla in Korea. It suggests that if you miss the moment of enlightenment, you have traveled a great distance but missed the destination entirely.</note>."

Ancient Buddhas have manifested in this world for ages beyond number; they are too numerous to be known or counted in their entirety. In recent times, it has been said that during the <term>Auspicious Aeon</term> <note>original: "賢劫" (Bhadrakalpa); in Buddhist cosmology, an era in which 1,000 Buddhas appear.</note> there are one thousand Tathagatas <note>A title for a Buddha, meaning "One who has thus come."</note>. Long ago, when the Tathagata was traveling and teaching in Mathurā, he saw a green forest with lush branches and leaves. He said to Ānanda: "The ground of this forest is named Urumunda. One hundred years after my Nirvana, there will be a monk named Śāṇavā-

After the Venerable had fulfilled his teaching duties for a long time, he sought to entrust the True Dharma to a successor. In the kingdom of Patali, he found **Upagupta** <note>original: 優波掬多</note>, who became his attendant. The Venerable asked Upagupta: "How old are you?"

# The Fourth Patriarch, the Venerable Upagupta

The Fourth Patriarch, the Venerable Upagupta <note>Upagupta was a major figure in early Buddhism, traditionally said to be the spiritual advisor to Emperor Ashoka</note>, was a native of the Kingdom of Tali. He was also known as Upaguruta or Ubhagupta. His surname was Shudra; his father was named Shanyi ("Good Intent"). He left the household life to become a monk at seventeen and realized the fruits of the path <note>attained enlightenment</note> at twenty. As he traveled from place to place to teach and transform others, he arrived in the Kingdom of Mathura, where a great many people were liberated through his guidance. Because of this, the palace of the Maras <note>celestial beings who represent temptation and attachment</note> was shaken. Papiyan <note>original: 波旬 (Papiyan), another name for Mara, the "Lord of Desire" who sought to distract the Buddha</note> became distressed and fearful, and he exhausted all his demonic powers in an attempt to harm the True Dharma.

The Venerable One immediately entered a state of Samadhi <note>deep meditative absorption</note> to observe the cause of this disturbance. Papiyan waited for an opportunity and secretly placed a jeweled necklace around the Venerable One’s neck. When the Venerable One emerged from his meditation, he took the corpses of a human, a dog, and a snake, and transformed them into a beautiful flower garland. With a calm voice, he comforted Papiyan, saying:
> "You have given me a jeweled necklace that is truly rare and wonderful; I have this flower garland to offer you in return."

Papiyan was overjoyed and stretched out his neck to receive it. Immediately, the garland transformed back into the three kinds of foul-smelling corpses, which were rotting and infested with maggots. Papiyan was filled with loathing and great distress. Even using all his supernatural powers, he could not move or remove the corpses. He then ascended to the Six Heavens of Desire to inform the heavenly lords, and even went to the Great Brahma Heaven to beg for release. Each of them told him:
> "This is a supernatural transformation performed by a disciple of the One of Ten Powers <note>An epithet for the Buddha, referring to his ten unique strengths of knowledge</note>. How could we, who are ordinary and lowly, remove it?"

Papiyan asked:
> "Then what can be done?"

The Brahma King replied:
> "You must take refuge in the Venerable One; only then can it be severed and removed."

The Brahma King then spoke a verse to guide his return to the path:
> "If one falls because of the ground,
> One must rise by relying on that same ground.
> To seek to rise while leaving the ground
> Is a principle that can never be realized." <note>This famous Zen teaching suggests that the source of one's problem is also the source of its solution; one must face the reality of their situation to transcend it.</note>

Having received this teaching, Papiyan descended from the heavenly palace and prostrated at the feet of the Venerable One, mournfully repenting. The Venerable One asked:
> "From this day forward, will you cease to harass and harm the True Dharma of the Tathagata <note>The Buddha</note>?"

Papiyan replied:
> "I vow to turn toward the Buddha’s path and forever sever my unvirtuous ways."

The Venerable One said:
> "If that is so, you may chant your refuge in the Triple Gem."

The Demon King joined his palms and chanted the refuge three times, and the foul garlands vanished. Overjoyed and leaping with spirit, he bowed to the Venerable One and spoke a verse:
> "I bow to the Lord of Samadhi,
> The holy disciple of the One of Ten Powers.
> I now wish to return to the path;
> May I never again be weak or inferior."

During his time in the world, the Venerable One guided and transformed many, and those who attained the fruits of the path were numerous. Every time he liberated someone, he would place a wooden tally <note>original: 籌 (chou) - a small stick used for counting</note> in a stone chamber. That chamber, which was eighteen cubits long and twelve cubits wide, was eventually filled to capacity with these tallies. Finally, there was the son of an elder named Xiangzhong ("Fragrant Multitude"), who came to pay respects to the Venerable One, wishing to leave the household life. The Venerable One asked him:
> "Do you wish to leave the household in body, or in mind?"

The youth replied:
> "I come to leave the household, not for the sake of body or mind."

The Venerable One asked:
> "If not for body or mind, who then leaves the household?"

He replied:
> "One who leaves the household does so because there is no 'I' or 'mine.' Because there is no 'I' or 'mine,' the mind is neither born nor extinguished. That which is neither born nor extinguished is the Eternal Way. All Buddhas are likewise eternal; the mind has no physical form, and its essence is the same."

The Venerable One said:
> "You have achieved a great awakening; your mind has attained through-penetration. You should rely on the Buddha, the Dharma, and the Sangha to continue the lineage of the sages."

He then performed the head-shaving ceremony and granted him full ordination. He told the youth:
> "Your father once dreamed of a golden sun when you were born; therefore, you shall be named Dhritaka." <note>original: 提多迦 (Dhritaka)</note>

He further said to him:

Now, I entrust it to you. Listen to my verse:"

> "The mind is originally the mind,
> But this original mind contains no fixed laws.
> Where there are laws, there is the original mind,
> But that which is called mind is not..." <note>The verse is interrupted at the end of the page.</note>

## The Fourteenth Patriarch, the Venerable Nagarjuna

The Patriarch was a native of Western India. He was also known as "Dragon Victory." After receiving the Dharma from the Venerable Kapimala, he traveled to Southern India. The Patriarch answered, "It is neither large nor small, neither broad nor narrow. It has no merit and no retribution; it does not die and is not born." 

Hearing this superior reasoning, they all turned away from their initial beliefs. The Patriarch then manifested his "Sovereign Body" upon his seat, appearing like the disc of a full moon. The entire assembly heard only the sound of the Dharma but could not see the Patriarch’s physical form.

## The Seventeenth Patriarch, Sanghanandi
<term>Sanghanandi<note>original: 僧伽難提 (Sengjia Nanti) — An Indian prince turned monk, recognized as a pivotal figure in the Zen lineage for his deep meditative practices.</note></term> was the son of King Precious Ornament of the city of <term>Shravasti<note>original: 室羅伐城 (Shiluofa Cheng) — An ancient Indian city, famous as the place where the Buddha spent many rainy seasons.</note></term>. He was able to speak from birth and frequently praised the works of the Buddha. At the age of seven, he grew weary of worldly pleasures and addressed his parents with this verse:

> I bow my head to my great, compassionate father,
> And pay respects to my mother of bone and blood.
> I now wish to renounce my home—
> Grant me this through your grace and pity.

His parents firmly forbade it, so he refused to eat for an entire day. Eventually, they permitted him to live as a "monk remaining at home." He was given the name Sanghanandi. He was further commanded to take the <term>Shramana<note>original: 沙門 (shamen) — A wandering monk or ascetic.</note></term> Chanlituo as his teacher. For nineteen years, he never wavered or grew tired. He often thought to himself, "Since I live in a royal palace, how can I truly be one who has 'left home'?"

One night, a celestial light descended, revealing a smooth and level path. Without realizing it, he walked slowly for about ten <term>li<note>A traditional unit of distance, roughly 500 meters.</note></term> until he reached a great cliff. There, he found a stone cave and entered into silent meditation within it. When his father, the King, discovered this, he banished the teacher Chanlituo and searched the country for his son, but could not find him. 

Ten years passed. After the Patriarch <gloss>Sanghanandi</gloss> had received the Dharma and the prediction of his enlightenment, he traveled to the kingdom of Madhi to teach. Suddenly, a cool breeze swept through the assembly. Everyone felt a sense of joy and comfort that was quite extraordinary, though they did not know why. The Patriarch said, "This is the wind of virtue and Dao. A sage is about to appear in this world to continue the <term>Patriarchal Lamp<note>original: 祖燈 (zudeng) — A metaphor for the lineage of enlightened wisdom passed from teacher to student.</note></term>." 

After saying this, he used his spiritual power to lead the assembly through mountains and valleys. In the time it takes to eat a meal, they arrived at the foot of a peak. He told the assembly, "At the summit of this peak is a purple cloud like a canopy. A sage lives here." 

They lingered there for a long time until they saw a young boy coming from a mountain hut, holding a circular mirror. He walked straight up to the Patriarch. The Patriarch asked, "How old are you?"
The boy replied, "I am one hundred years old."
The Patriarch said, "You are still so young; why do you say you are a hundred?"
The boy replied, "I do not understand the ultimate principle <gloss>the boy suggests that without understanding, age is meaningless</gloss>, so I am exactly one hundred years old."
The Patriarch asked, "Do you possess the potential for the Buddha's mechanism?"
The boy replied, "The Buddha said: 'If a man lives a hundred years but does not understand the mechanism of the Buddhas, it is not as good as living for a single day and attaining the final realization of it.'"

The Patriarch then asked, "What does that object in your hand represent?"
The boy replied:
> The Great Round Mirror of all Buddhas
> Is without flaw or shadow, inside or out.
> When two people see it together,
> Their hearts and eyes are both the same.

When the boy's parents heard his words, they allowed him to leave home. The Patriarch took him back to his dwelling and administered the full precepts. He was named <term>Gayashata<note>original: 伽耶舎多 (Jiaye Sheduo) — The Eighteenth Patriarch.</note></term>. 

Later, they heard the sound of the wind blowing the bells of the temple hall. The Patriarch asked, "Is it the bells ringing, or is it the wind ringing?"
Gayashata replied, "It is neither the wind nor the bells ringing; it is my heart that rings."
The Patriarch asked, "And who is this 'heart'?"
Gayashata replied, "Because both <gloss>wind and bell</gloss> are utterly silent and still."
The Patriarch said, "Excellent, excellent! Who else but you could succeed to my Way?" He then transmitted the <term>Dharma Eye<note>The true insight into the nature of reality.</note></term> to him and spoke this verse:

> The ground of the mind is fundamentally birthless;
> From the ground of causes, conditions arise.
> Seeds and conditions do not obstruct one another;
> So it is with the flowers and the fruit.

Having transmitted the Dharma, the Patriarch grasped a tree with his right hand and passed away. The assembly discussed this, saying, "The Honorable One has returned to stillness beneath a tree; surely he intends to provide shade for his descendants." They intended to bury his body and build a stupa on a high plateau, but even with their combined strength, they could not move him. Therefore, they built the stupa right there beneath the tree. This occurred in the thirteenth year of the reign of Emperor Zhao of the Han Dynasty <note>approximately 82 BCE</note>.

### The Nineteenth Patriarch, the Venerable Kumārata

The Nineteenth Patriarch, the Venerable Kumārata, was the son of a Brahmin in the Great Yuezhi Kingdom. In a former life, he was a celestial being in the <term>Sixth Heaven<note>original: 自在天 (Zizai Tian) — Also known as Paranirmita-vaśavartin, the highest heaven of the Desire Realm.</note></term> <gloss>The sixth heaven of the Desire Realm</gloss>. Because of his sharp faculties, he was skilled at explaining the essentials of the Dharma, and the gods honored him as a guide. When the time came for him to succeed as a Patriarch, he descended to the Yuezhi Kingdom.

Later, he traveled to Central India, where he met a Great Being named <term>Jayata<note>original: 闍夜多 (Sheyeduo) — Who would become the 20th Patriarch.</note></term>. Jayata asked him: "My parents have always believed in the <term>Three Jewels<note>The Buddha, the Dharma (teachings), and the Sangha (community).</note></term>, yet they are constantly plagued by illness. Everything they attempt ends in failure. Why is he so fortunate while I am so cursed?"

The Patriarch replied, "Why should you doubt? The retribution for good and evil occurs across three periods of time. Most people only see the kind dying young and the violent living long, or the treacherous being lucky and the righteous meeting misfortune <gloss>'short' refers to death</gloss>. They conclude that cause and effect are false, and that sin and merit are indistinguishable. They do not realize that consequences follow actions like a shadow follows a form; not even a hair’s breadth is missed. Even after hundreds of millions of ages have passed, karma does not fade away."

Upon hearing these words, Jayata’s doubts were instantly cleared. The Patriarch continued, "Although you now believe in the <term>Three Karmas<note>Actions of body, speech, and mind.</note></term>, you do not yet understand that karma arises from delusion, delusion exists because of consciousness, consciousness relies on non-awakening, and non-awakening relies on the Mind. The Mind is fundamentally pure—without birth, without destruction, without striving, without retribution, without superiority or inferiority. It is utterly silent and yet profoundly aware. If you enter this Dharma-gate, you will be equal to all Buddhas. All good and evil, all things conditioned or unconditioned, are like dreams or illusions."

Jayata received these instructions and understood the core meaning. His innate wisdom from past lives was awakened, and he begged to leave the household life. After Jayata received full ordination, the Patriarch told him, "The time for my entry into nirvana has arrived. You must now continue the work of transformation." He then transmitted the Eye of the Dharma to him with this verse:

> Within the nature [of mind], there is fundamentally no birth;
> It is only spoken of for the sake of those who seek.
> Since nothing is actually gained in the Dharma,
> Why harbor thoughts of 'certainty' or 'uncertainty'?

He further added, "These are the words of the Tathāgata of Wondrous Sound regarding the pure seeing of one's nature. You should spread them to future students." Having finished speaking, he sat in his seat and scratched his face with his fingernails. It opened like a red lotus, emitting a great light that illuminated the four assemblies of disciples, and then he entered into silence. Jayata built a pagoda for him. This occurred in the fourteenth year of the <term>New Dynasty<note>original: 新室 (Xin Shi) — The brief reign of Wang Mang (9–23 CE).</note></term>, a year of the Water Horse.

## The Twentieth Patriarch: The Venerable Jayata

The Patriarch was a native of Northern India. His wisdom was profound and vast, and he guided countless beings toward enlightenment. Later, he traveled to the city of <term>Rajagriha<note>original: 羅閱城 (Luoyue Cheng) — An ancient city in India, modern-day Rajgir, which was a major center for early Buddhism.</note></term> to spread the "Sudden Teaching" of direct awakening. 

In that city, there was a group of scholars who valued nothing but intellectual debate. Their leader was named **Vasubandhu** <gloss>translated as "World-Kin"</gloss>. Vasubandhu practiced extreme asceticism: he ate only one meal a day, never lay down to sleep, and performed rituals to the Buddha six times a day. He lived a life of pure desirelessness and was deeply revered by his followers.

The Patriarch, wishing to guide him, first asked the group: "This <term>ascetic practitioner<note>original: 徧行頭陀 (bianxing toutuo) — A monk practicing the 'dhutāṅga' or austere disciplines intended to purify the mind.</note></term> is able to practice pure conduct; can he attain the Way of the Buddha?"

The crowd replied, "Our master is extremely diligent. Why should he not?"

The Patriarch said, "Your master is far from the Way. Even if he practices for countless <term>eons<note>original: 塵劫 (chenjie) — A metaphor for an immeasurably long period of time, as numerous as grains of dust.</note></term>, it is all rooted in illusion."

The crowd asked, "What virtues do you possess, O Venerable One, that you would mock our master?"

The Patriarch replied: "I do not seek the Way, yet I am not deluded. I do not bow to the Buddha, yet I am not disrespectful. I do not sit for long periods, yet I am not lazy. I do not eat once a day, yet I do not eat a variety of foods. I do not know contentment, yet I am not greedy. When the heart has nothing it hopes for, that is called the Way."

When the practitioner Vasubandhu heard this, he awakened to pure, untainted wisdom and praised the Patriarch with great joy. The Patriarch then told the crowd: "Do you understand my words? I spoke this way because he was too desperate in his pursuit of the Way. If a string is tuned too tight, it will snap. Therefore, I did not praise him, so that he might dwell in a state of peace and enter the wisdom of all Buddhas."

He then said to Vasubandhu, "I just humbled you in front of your followers. Are you not harboring any resentment?"

Vasubandhu replied, "I remember that seven eons ago, I lived in the Land of Eternal Peace. My teacher, 'Wise Moon-Purity,' predicted I would attain the fruit of a <term>Once-Returner<note>original: 斯陀含 (situohan) — A 'Sakadāgāmin,' one who has reached the second stage of enlightenment.</note></term>. At that time, a great Bodhisattva named Great Radiance appeared in the world. I went to visit him, supporting my elderly aunt and leaning on a staff. My teacher scolded me, saying: 'To value the child while slighting the father—how lowly!' At the time, I thought I had done nothing wrong and asked for an explanation. My teacher said: 'While you were bowing to the Bodhisattva Great Radiance, you leaned your staff against a painting of the Buddha's face.' Because of this act of negligence and pride, I lost the two higher stages of enlightenment. Since then, I have reflected on my fault and repented. Now, when I hear harsh words, they are to me like the wind or an echo. How much more so now, when I have tasted the supreme nectar of your teaching? Why would I feel resentment? I only pray that you will show great compassion and instruct me in the wondrous Way."

The Patriarch said, "You have long cultivated many virtues, and you shall succeed my lineage." He then recited this verse:

> When words are spoken, they harmonize with the Unborn,
> Becoming one with the nature of the Dharma-realm.
> If you can understand in this way,
> You will master the ultimate truth of all things.

Having transmitted the Dharma, the Patriarch did not rise from his seat but peacefully entered <term>Nirvana<note>original: 歸寂 (guiji) — Literally 'returning to stillness,' a term for the passing of an enlightened master.</note></term>. His body was cremated, and his relics were enshrined in a pagoda. This occurred in the 17th year of the reign of Emperor Ming of the Eastern Han Dynasty <note>approx. 74 CE</note>.

# The Third Patriarch, Great Master Sengcan

The Third Patriarch, Great Master Sengcan, is of unknown origin. He first visited the Second Patriarch as a layman. After receiving ordination and the transmission of the Dharma, he went into hiding at Mount Wanggong in Shuzhou. ## <insert>The Fourth Patriarch, Grand Master Daoxin</insert>

The Master’s secular surname was Sima. His family lived in Henei for generations before moving to Guangji County in Qizhou. From birth, he was exceptionally gifted. Even as a youth, he admired the "School of Emptiness" <note>A reference to the Mahayana Buddhist focus on the emptiness (Sunyata) of all phenomena.</note> and the various gates of liberation as if they were habits learned in a past life. After inheriting the ancestral tradition <note>The lineage of Zen transmission.</note>, he practiced "gathering the mind" without sleeping; his back did not touch a mat for nearly sixty years.

In the thirteenth year of the Daye era of the Sui Dynasty [617 CE], he led his followers to Jizhou. It happened that a band of rebels had surrounded the city for seventy days, and the siege could not be broken. The people were terrified. The Patriarch took pity on them and taught them to recite the *Mahaprajna* <term>Mahaprajna: The "Great Wisdom" or the Perfection of Wisdom sutras</term>. When the rebels looked toward the city battlements, they saw what appeared to be divine soldiers. They said to one another, "There must be an extraordinary person within the city; we cannot attack it." They gradually withdrew.

In the *Wude* era of the Tang Dynasty, the year of *Jia-shen* [624 CE], the Master returned to Qichun and resided on Mount Potou <note>Literally "Broken Head Mountain," later renamed Shuangfeng (Twin Peaks) Mountain.</note>. The Patriarch asked, "What is your surname?"

The child replied, "I have a surname, but it is not an ordinary surname."
The Patriarch asked, "What surname is it?"
The child replied, "It is Buddha-nature." <note>This is a famous Zen pun. In Chinese, "surname" (姓) and "nature" (性) are pronounced the same (*xing*). The child is asserting that his true identity is his inherent enlightened nature.</note>
The Patriarch asked, "Do you have no surname then?"
The child replied, "Because nature is empty, I have none."

The Patriarch silently recognized the boy as a "vessel of the Dharma" <term>Dharma-vessel: A person with the capacity to receive and hold the profound teachings</term>. He sent an attendant to the child’s mother to ask permission for him to join the monkhood. Because of their past-life connections, the mother showed no hesitation and gave him up to be a disciple. *The flower seeds have the nature of life,*
*From the ground, the flowers grow.*
*When the Great Cause matches this nature,*
*That which is born, grows from the unborn.*

He then entrusted his followers to the student. One day, he told the assembly: "During the *Wude* era, I traveled to Mount Lu. I climbed to the very peak and looked toward Mount Potou. Hongren <note>The child from the previous story, now a senior disciple.</note> asked, "Could it be that the Master will later produce another branch of the Buddha-Dharma?" Later, in the nineteenth year of the *Zhenguan* era [643 CE], Emperor Taizong admired the Master’s reputation and wished to see his character. He issued an imperial decree summoning him to the capital. The Patriarch submitted a petition politely declining. This happened three times; he finally refused on account of illness. The fourth time, the Emperor told the envoy, "If he still does not rise, bring back his head."

When the envoy reached the mountain and delivered the decree, the Patriarch simply stretched out his neck toward the blade, his expression calm and composed. The envoy was amazed. He returned and reported the situation to the Emperor, who admired the Master even more. The Emperor sent gifts of precious silks to allow the Master to fulfill his original vow to stay on the mountain.

In the *Yonghui* era of Emperor Gaozong, the year of *Xin-hai* [651 CE], on the fourth day of the intercalary ninth month, the Master suddenly gave a final warning to his disciples: "All phenomena are completely liberated. Each of you must protect this teaching and let it flow into the future." After speaking, he sat peacefully and passed away. He was seventy-two years old. A stupa was built for him on the mountain. On the eighth day of the fourth month the following year, the door of the stupa opened by itself for no reason. His appearance looked as if he were still alive. Thereafter, his disciples did not dare close it again. One day, while carrying wood to the market, he heard a customer reciting the *Diamond Sutra*. When he heard the line, “One should develop a mind that does not abide in anything,” <note>original: "應無所住而生其心" (Ying wu suo zhu er sheng qi xin). This is the pivotal moment of Huineng's enlightenment, emphasizing a mind free from attachment.</note> he experienced a sudden awakening. He asked the customer, “What scripture is this, and from whom did you receive it?” 

The customer replied, “This is the *Diamond Sutra*, and I received it from the Great Master [Hong]ren at Huangmei.” 

Huineng immediately informed his mother of his intention to seek the Dharma and find the Master. He traveled directly to Shaozhou, where he met a man of high virtue named Liu Zhilüe, and they became close friends. Zhilüe’s aunt was a nun named Wujinzang. She frequently recited the *Nirvana Sutra*. Huineng listened to her for a short time and then explained the meaning of the text to her. The nun then held up the scroll to ask him about certain characters. 

Huineng said, “I do not recognize the characters. However, please ask me about the meaning.” 

The nun said, “If you do not even recognize the characters, how can you understand the meaning?” 

Huineng replied, “The profound truths of all Buddhas have nothing to do with written words.” 

The nun was greatly astonished. She told the village elders, “Huineng is a man who possesses the Way; we should invite him to stay and offer him support.” Consequently, the local people vied with one another to come and pay their respects. Nearby were the ruins of the ancient Baolin Temple; the community decided to rebuild it so that Huineng could live there. Followers gathered like clouds, and it soon became a great monastery. 

One day, Huineng suddenly thought to himself, “I seek the Great Dharma; how can I stop halfway?” The next day, he traveled to the West Mountain in Changle County. In a stone grotto, he met Zen Master Zhiyuan and asked for further instruction. 

Zhiyuan said, “Looking at your appearance, you are extraordinary and spirited, unlike an ordinary man. I have heard that the Western monk Bodhidharma transmitted the Heart Seal to Huangmei. You should go there to resolve your doubts.” 

Huineng took his leave and traveled directly to the East Mountain of Huangmei. This was in the second year of the Xianheng era [671 AD].

“Sunlight is a metaphor for wisdom, while darkness represents affliction <term>Klesha; the mental states like anger or greed that cloud the mind</term>. If a practitioner of the Way does not use wisdom to illuminate and break through affliction, by what means can they escape the beginningless cycle of birth and death?”

The Patriarch replied: “Affliction is itself Bodhi <note>Awakening</note>; they are not two different things, and there is no distinction between them. If one uses wisdom to ‘illuminate’ affliction, this is the narrow view of the Two Vehicles <note>The ‘Small Vehicle’ paths of the Shravaka and Pratyekabuddha, which Zen often critiques for being too dualistic</note>, fit only for those with the potential of a ‘sheep’ or a ‘deer.’ Those of great wisdom and superior capacity are not like this.”

Jian asked: “What then is the perspective of the Great Vehicle?”

The Patriarch said: “Light and lack of light are, in their nature, not two. This non-dual nature is the Real Nature. This Real Nature does not decrease when among the common and foolish, nor does it increase when among the wise and holy. It is not chaotic when dwelling in affliction, nor is it merely silent when dwelling in meditative concentration. It is not permanent, yet not interrupted; it does not come and it does not go. It is not in the middle, nor is it inside or outside. It is never born and never destroyed. Its nature and its appearance are ‘just as they are’ <note>original: 如如, *ru ru*; refers to Thusness or Tathata</note>, eternally abiding without changing. This is what we call the Way.”

Jian said: “Master, you speak of ‘no birth and no destruction.’ How is this different from the teachings of the non-Buddhists?”

The Patriarch said: “When non-Buddhists speak of no birth and no destruction, they use ‘destruction’ to stop ‘birth,’ and use ‘birth’ to reveal ‘destruction.’ For them, destruction is not truly destroyed, and they speak of birth while calling it ‘unborn.’ When I speak of no birth and no destruction, it is because things are fundamentally without birth, and thus now they have no destruction. This is why it is not the same as the non-Buddhist paths. If you wish to know the essentials of the mind, simply do not think of any good or any evil. You will then naturally enter the pure substance of the mind, which is deep, still, and eternally silent, with wonderful functions as numerous as the sands <gloss>One version says: ‘like the sand’</gloss> of the Ganges.”
