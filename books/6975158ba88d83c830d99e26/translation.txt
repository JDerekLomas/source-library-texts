# Preface.

If various reviewers, following the <term>laudable traditional fashion</term> <note>The author uses "laudable" (löblich) sarcastically here to mock the predictable habits of contemporary literary critics.</note>, would like to review my book—and, what is nowadays almost one and the same, decry it—without taking the trouble to read it themselves: then, after making a start with a few <term>commonplaces</term> <note>Clichés or predictable arguments.</note> (for example, that much useless material has already been written about <term>Masonry</term> <note>original: Maurerey; refers to Freemasonry.</note>), they need only humbly add: The author has cobbled his material together from wretched books, and even where he has happened upon a good book, he understands everything incorrectly. He falsely claims to be a <term>Freemason</term>, but is probably a secret <term>Jesuit</term> <note>A common 18th-century conspiracy theory suggested that Jesuits had secretly infiltrated Freemasonry to further the interests of the Catholic Church.</note>, or at least a <term>dry staff</term> <note>An idiomatic expression for a lifeless tool or a pawn being used by others.</note> in the hands of the Jesuits, from which one can easily draw further conclusions.

<summary>In this satirical opening, the author anticipates and mocks the knee-jerk reactions of critics who might dismiss his work on Freemasonry as unoriginal or as secret Jesuit propaganda without reading it.</summary>
<keywords>Freemasonry, Jesuits, literary criticism, 18th-century satire, conspiracy theories, book reviewers</keywords>

Those gentlemen who undertake the laborious task of leafing through my book will perhaps encounter passages that do not conform to the principles which they, as contributors to certain scholarly periodicals, are obliged to follow in their <term>reviews</term> <note>original: Recensionen; an archaic spelling of Rezensionen. In the 18th century, "learned journals" often had specific ideological or theological agendas.</note>. To extract themselves from any such embarrassment, the safest course of action is for them not to announce my book at all, so that at least a large portion of the <term>public</term> <note>original: Publikum.</note> remains unaware of its existence. Silence in this case is better than ripping passages out of context or twisting them. For even that might entice some people to examine the book for themselves.

To those, however, who wish to read it in its entirety and subject it to a rigorous and honest <term>examination</term> <note>original: Prüfung.</note>, I have nothing further to say except that they place me under an obligation; and however their <term>judgment</term> <note>original: Urtheil; an archaic spelling of Urteil.</note> may turn out, they will certainly be of service to the <term>truth</term> <note>original: Wahrheit.</note>.

<summary>The author challenges biased reviewers to either ignore his work entirely or engage with it honestly, asserting that a thorough and fair critique—regardless of its verdict—serves the ultimate goal of truth.</summary>
<keywords>literary criticism, scholarly journals, intellectual honesty, book reviews, 18th-century publishing, pursuit of truth</keywords>
