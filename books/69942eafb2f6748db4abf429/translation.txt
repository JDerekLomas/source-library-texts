**S**ince many things have been usefully achieved, most learned Didymus, by scholars in the study of ancient writings, that work is especially profitable which, by weighing the sincerity and reliability of whatever records have survived, has rescued what good remains from the corruption and injustice of time. For these scholars thought it mattered little how many commentaries by ancient writers they counted in their libraries, but rather how many were genuine and sound. Thus, among the books which a previous age venerated, some were found to be inscribed to the wrong author, others were substituted in place of lost works, and many were foully corrupted. For such great greed, to the ultimate injury of literature, has always possessed the <note>In the early modern period, scholars often blamed "librarii" (copyists or booksellers) for introducing errors into texts to save time or increase profit.</note> copyists. 

But only the passage of time will uncover all frauds (perhaps it is not even permitted to hope for such a thing), either because learned men have not yet dwelled long or deeply enough upon certain writers, or because some things have been so disguised with such a pretense of truth that you would scarcely suspect a trick lies beneath. You yourself have provided one such example, detected in your book *On the Pronunciation of the Divine Name*, which was argued with subtle skill (as is your way). In that work, you proved to your opponents the deception of the vowel points, through which the ancient and authentic pronunciation of the <term>tetragrammaton</term> <note>original: "τετραγράμματου"; the four-letter Hebrew name of God, usually rendered as YHWH.</note> was anticipated and revealed.
