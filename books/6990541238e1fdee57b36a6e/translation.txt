Just a moment ago, we were entirely surrendered to the noisy, ephemeral bustle of the present; then, something very distant and foreign appears to us, directing our gaze toward things of a different order: a looking up from the confusing variety of the present toward a higher historical continuity. We will suddenly remember that at this very spot, where we now busily run to and fro, a similar life and activity prevailed 2,000 years ago in somewhat different forms; similar passions moved people, and those people were equally convinced of the uniqueness of their existence. This impression, which a first acquaintance with the monuments of antiquity easily leaves behind, I must compare to the one made by Freud’s reference to the legend of Oedipus.

Just moments before, we were occupied with the confusing impressions of the infinitely variable individual soul, when suddenly the view opened upon that simple grandeur of the Oedipus tragedy—that never-extinguished light of the Greek theater. This expansion of vision has something of a revelation about it. For us, antiquity had psychologically long since sunk into the shadows of the past; at our school desks, one could hardly suppress a skeptical smile when indiscreetly calculating the matronly age of Penelope <note>The faithful wife of Odysseus in Homer's Odyssey</note> and the stout age of Jocasta <note>The mother and wife of Oedipus</note>, bringing the result of the calculation into comic comparison with the tragic-erotic storms of legend and drama. We did not know then (and who knows it even today?) that a son can harbor an all-surpassing, consuming passion for the mother, which perhaps undermines and tragically destroys his entire life, so that the magnitude of the Oedipus fate appears not one iota exaggerated. Rare cases perceived as pathological, such as Ninon de Lenclos and her son <margin>1) He is said to have killed himself when he heard that Ninon, whom he ardently adored, was his mother.</margin>, are usually too remote from us to give us a vivid impression.

It does not happen without an initial outrage of the moral feeling to admit such possibilities within oneself; resistances that all too easily blind the intellect and thereby make self-knowledge impossible.

The motive behind these works is the unlocking of historical problems through the application of psychoanalytic insights—that is, insights drawn from the activity of the modern unconscious soul—to given historical material. I must refer the reader entirely to the cited works so that they may inform themselves there regarding the scope and nature of the insights already attained. In specific details, the interpretations are still uncertain in many places, but this in no way impairs the overall result; it would be significant enough if it merely demonstrated the far-reaching analogy between the psychological construction of historical relics and the structure of recent products of individual psychology <note>The term "individual psychology" here refers to the study of the single person's psyche, rather than the specific school later founded by Alfred Adler</note>. This proof should be considered established for every discerning person by the works produced thus far. The analogy prevails especially in symbolism, as *Riklin*, *Rank*, *Maeder*, and *Abraham* have shown with illuminating examples, and furthermore in the individual mechanisms of unconscious work, such as repression, condensation, etc., as *Abraham* specifically demonstrated.

The psychoanalytic researcher has hitherto turned his interest mainly to the analysis of individual psychological problems. Given the current state of affairs, however, it seems to me to be becoming a more or less unavoidable requirement for the psychoanalyst to expand the analysis of individual problems by drawing in historical material, as *Freud* has already done in an exemplary manner in his writing on *Leonardo da Vinci*¹). For just as psychoanalytic insights promote the understanding of historical-psychological formations, conversely, historical materials can shed new light on individual psychological problems. Such and similar considerations have prompted me to turn my attention somewhat more toward the historical, in the hope of gaining from there new insights into the foundations of individual psychology.

# II.

## On the Two Kinds of Thinking.

As is well known, it is one of the fundamental principles of analytical psychology <note>The school of psychology founded by Carl Jung, focusing on the individual psyche and the quest for wholeness.</note> that dream images are to be understood symbolically; namely, that they are not to be taken literally as they present themselves in sleep, but that one must suspect a hidden meaning behind them. It is this ancient idea of dream symbolism that has provoked not only criticism but indeed the most violent opposition. Now, to common sense, it does not seem so outrageous that a dream should be something meaningful and therefore something interpretable; by saying this, one is merely expressing a truth familiar to humanity for millennia, and therefore something of a commonplace. One still remembers from one's school days hearing of the Egyptian and Chaldean dream interpreters. One heard of Joseph, who interpreted the dreams of Pharaoh; one also heard of the dream book of Artemidorus <note>A professional diviner from the 2nd century AD who wrote the "Oneirocritica," the only surviving Greco-Roman work on dream interpretation.</note>. From countless written monuments of all times and peoples, we know of significant and prophetic dreams, of dreams foreboding disaster, and also of the healing dreams which the God sent to the sick sleeping in the temple. We know the dream of the mother of Augustus, who dreamed she was impregnated by the deity transformed into a serpent. We do not wish to pile up references and examples to testify to the existence of a belief in the symbolism of the dream. When a truth is so old and so generally believed, it must be true in some way—specifically, as is usually the case, not "really" true <note>meaning physically or factually true in the external world</note>, but psychologically true. [This distinction is to blame for the fact that scientific philistinism <note>original: "Banausentum" - a term for a narrow-minded, uncultured approach that lacks appreciation for higher values.</note> has from time to time discarded an old heirloom of truth because it was not "really" but psychologically true, for which these kinds of people have never at any time had an understanding.]

It is hardly conceivable to our experience that a God existing outside of us causes the dream, or that the dream by itself <note>original: "eo ipso"</note> prophetically foresees the future. But if we translate this into...

Psychic—this is how the ancient view sounds, much more conciliatory: namely, that the dream arises from a part of the soul unknown to us but important, and concerns itself with wishes for the coming day. This psychological formulation, derived from the ancient superstitious view of the dream, coincides exactly, so to speak, with Freudian psychology, which assumes a wish rising from the unconscious as the source of the dream.

As the old belief says, the deity or the demon speaks in symbolic language to the sleeper, and the dream interpreter has to resolve the language of riddles. In modern language, it is said that the dream is a series of images that are apparently contradictory and nonsensical, but descend from psychological material that yields a clear meaning.

Should I have to assume an extensive ignorance of dream analysis among my readers, I would probably have to support this sentence with manifold examples. Nowadays, however, these things are already too well known, so that out of consideration for a psychoanalytically educated public, one must proceed sparingly with everyday dream case studies <note>original: "Traumkasuistik"</note> so as not to become boring. A particular drawback is that one cannot tell a dream without having to add half a life story afterward, representing the individual foundations of the dream. There are a few typical dreams which can be told without too much baggage. One of them is the dream of sexual violence, which is very common, especially among women. A girl who falls asleep after a night of merry dancing dreams: A robber breaks open her door with a crash and pierces her body with a lance.

This theme, which is immediately understandable, has countless variants that are sometimes simple, sometimes complicated. Instead of the lance, it is a sword, a dagger, a revolver, a rifle, a cannon, a hydrant, a watering can; or the act of violence is a break-in, a pursuit, a theft; or someone is hidden in the closet or under the bed. Or the danger is illustrated by wild animals, e.g., a horse that throws the dreamer to the ground and kicks her in the body with its hind leg, lions, tigers, elephants with threatening trunks, and finally snakes in endless variation.

# The Symbolic Nature of Dreams

These few references to the symbolic nature of the dream may suffice. To anyone for whom this seems insufficient as proof (and for every beginner it truly is insufficient), I refer them to the foundational work of **Freud** and to the more detailed works of **Stekel** (this Yearbook 1909) and **Rank** (this Yearbook 1910). We must reckon here with **dream symbolism** as an established fact in order to muster the necessary seriousness to admire this fact. Otherwise, one fails to be astonished that a mental structure projects into our conscious psychic activity which apparently obeys such completely different laws and different purposes than the conscious psychic product.

Why are **dreams symbolic?** Every "why" in psychology dissolves into two separate questions: First, **to what end are dreams symbolic?** We wish to answer this question only to leave it immediately. Dreams are symbolic so that they are not understood, so that the wish lying behind them as the source of the dream remains misunderstood. Why this is so and not otherwise is a question that leads out into the extensive experiences and lines of thought of Freudian psychology. We are interested here in the second question, namely: **From where does it come that dreams are symbolic?** That is, where does this capacity for symbolic representation come from, of which we are apparently unable to discover any traces in our conscious daily thinking? Let us look closer: Can we really discover nothing symbolic in our thinking? Let us follow our thought processes; let us take an example: We think about the War of 1870–1871 <note>The Franco-Prussian War, a pivotal event in the formation of the German Empire</note>. We think of the series of bloody battles, the siege of Strasbourg, Belfort, Paris, the peace treaty, the founding of the German Empire, etc. How did we think? We take a starting idea or "governing idea," as it is also called, and without thinking of it every time, but merely guided by a sense of direction, we thought about the individual reminiscences of the war. In this, we can find nothing symbolic, and yet our entire conscious thinking proceeds according to this type¹).

If we observe our thinking from very close up and follow an intensive train of thought—for example, the solution to some difficult problem—we suddenly notice that we **think in words**, that during very intensive thinking we begin to speak to ourselves, and that we also occasionally write down or draw the problem in order to be completely clear. Anyone who has lived for a long time in the territory of a foreign language will certainly have noticed that after some time they began to think in the language of that country. A very intensive train of thought, therefore, plays out in a more or less **linguistic form**; that is, as if one wanted to say it, teach it, or convince someone of it. It is evidently directed **entirely outward**. In this respect, directed or logical thinking is for us a **reality-thinking**¹), that is, a thinking that **adapts itself to reality**²), where we—expressed in other words—imitate the succession of objective-real things, so that the images in our head follow one another in the same strictly causal series as the historical events outside of our head³). We also call this thinking "thinking with directed attention." It furthermore has the peculiarity that it makes one tired, and that it is therefore only put into function periodically. Biologically, this ideal is to be seen as an exercise in psychological projection (the paranoid mechanism, as Freud would say)¹). Projection consists of the repression of conflict into the unconscious and the displacement of the repressed content into apparent objectivity (which is also the formula for paranoia). Repression serves, as is well known, to provide deliverance from a painful complex <note>A "complex" in Jungian and Freudian terms refers to a core pattern of emotions, memories, and perceptions organized around a common theme.</note> that one must get rid of at all costs, fearing its compelling and oppressive power. Repression can lead to an apparently complete suppression, which corresponds to a strong sense of self-control. Unfortunately, however, self-control has limits that are drawn all too narrowly. Finer observation of human nature shows that while composure is maintained at the appropriate moment, certain after-effects occur, which fall into two categories:

I. The suppressed affect <note>In psychology, "affect" refers to the underlying experience of feeling or emotion.</note> comes to the surface immediately afterward, and rarely directly, but usually in the form of a displacement onto another object (e.g., one is polite, submissive, patient, etc., in an official relationship, and vents all the anger on one's wife or subordinates).

II. The suppressed affect creates compensations elsewhere. For example: people who strive for excessive ethics, who always think, feel, and act altruistically and idealistically, take revenge for the intolerability of their ideal through subtly devised acts of malice—which, of course, do not enter their consciousness as such—leading to misunderstandings and strained relationships. Apparently, these are then all just "particularly unfortunate circumstances" or the fault and malice of other people or tragic complications. One has consciously gotten rid of the conflict, but it stands invisibly before one's feet and one trips over it at every step. The technique of apparent suppression and forgetting is inadequate because, in the final analysis, it is unfeasible—it is actually a mere makeshift. A far more effective aid is provided by religious projection: one keeps the conflict (worry, pain, fear, etc.) in view and hands it over to an external personality, the Deity. We have, therefore, three types of symbolic designations for the libido:

1. The *analogous* comparison: just like sun and fire.
2. The *causative* comparisons: *a)* Object-comparison. The libido is designated by its object, e.g., by the beneficent sun; *b)* Subject-comparison. The libido is designated by its place of origin or its analogue, e.g., by the phallus or (analogously) the serpent.

In these three basic forms of comparison, a fourth also plays a part, namely the *activity-comparison*, where the <term>basis of comparison<note>original: "Tertium comparationis" — a Latin rhetorical term for the shared quality that links two different things in a metaphor.</note></term> is the activity (e.g., the libido fertilizes like the bull; it is dangerous—through the power of its passion—like the lion or the ravening boar; it is lustful like the ever-lustful donkey, etc.).

The activity-comparison can belong just as well to the category of the analogous as to that of the causative comparison. These possibilities for comparison represent just as many possibilities for symbols, and for this reason, all the infinitely various symbols—insofar as they are images of the libido—are actually reducible to a very simple root: namely, to the libido and its fixed, primitive qualities.
