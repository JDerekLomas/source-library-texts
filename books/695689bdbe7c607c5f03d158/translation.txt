# INTRODUCTION

The treatise offered here in facsimile is the major work of David Hartley, M.A. (1705–1757), a physician, philosopher, and Christian apologist. His associationist principles <note>The theory that mental processes are formed by the association of one idea with another.</note> were generally respected for a span of perhaps seventy-five years after their publication in 1749. *Observations on Man* will be a useful sourcebook for scholars who need to consult first-hand and in its entirety <note>original: "in toto"</note> a work which figured significantly in eighteenth-century English philosophy, religion, and aesthetics. As a pioneer in the field of physio-psychology, it provided a foundation for Utilitarianism <note>The ethical theory that the best action is the one that maximizes overall happiness.</note> and helped shape the literary doctrines of Samuel Taylor Coleridge and William Wordsworth.

The first edition <note>original: "editio princeps"</note> of the *Observations on Man* is unquestionably the two-volume octavo <note>A book size resulting from folding a sheet of paper into eight leaves.</note> printed in "London, Printed by S. Richardson for James Leake and William Frederick, Booksellers in Bath," 1749. Hartley’s son, David, in his "Sketch of the Life and Character of Dr. Hartley" (1791), confirms what a comparison <note>original: "collation"</note> of the later editions indicates: that the 1749 text is complete and final. He lived about nine years after the publication of his work. The labor of organizing the whole system and the process of composition were exceedingly great and constant upon his mind for many years, as may be supposed from the very great scope of learning which it embraces. However, after its completion and publication, his mind was left in perfect repose. He had left no additional paper on the subject whatsoever” (1791 Quarto, pp. v-vi).

In March 1791, with “liberal encouragement given to the present edition by the Author’s Family,” the London bookseller J. Johnson brought out the second edition, “Reprinted from the Author’s Edition in 1749,” in one large quarto volume. This included “Notes and Additions to the Second Part; Translated from the German of the Rev. Herman Andrew Pistorius, Rector of Poseritz in the Island of Rugen” [first published in Rostock and Leipzig, 1772]. The entire work was preceded by “A Sketch of the Life and Character of Dr. Hartley” contributed by his son, and a “Head of the Author” <note>A portrait of David Hartley.</note> engraved by “Mr. Blake” <note>Likely the poet and artist William Blake, who worked as a commercial engraver.</note> from a painting by Shackelton. 

In the same year, 1791, J. Johnson published what must be considered the *third edition*, identical in contents with the quarto but printed in three octavo volumes <note>A smaller, more portable book size.</note> with the engraving of Hartley left out, though it was advertised as available “in quarto, price two shillings and six-pence.” The *fourth edition* was reprinted by J. Johnson in 1801 in three octavo volumes. Volume III, containing the Notes of Pistorius and the Sketch of the Life, was reprinted from the second edition and is designated “The Third Edition” on its title page. A *fifth edition* was printed in 1810 in Bath, Hartley’s hometown, by Richard Cruttwell. This edition reverts to the original two-volume format of the 1749 *Observations on Man*, “To Which are Now First Added, Prayers, and Religious Thoughts.”
