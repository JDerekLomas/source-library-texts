## The Publisher to the Ingenious Reader.

*In this curious and inquisitive age, when men—altogether dissatisfied and wearied out with the wranglings and idle speculations of the* **Schools** <note>original: "Schools"; referring to traditional Scholastic philosophy taught in universities, often criticized by early modern scientists for being too theoretical.</note>—*are with equal zeal and industry so earnest in their quest and pursuit of a more solid, rational, and useful* **Philosophy**, *it may prove a work very obliging and meritorious to help and guide them in their studies and researches. It is helpful to hang out a* **Light** *to them (as the* **Egyptians** *used to do from their highly celebrated* **Pharos** <note>The Lighthouse of Alexandria, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World.</note> *for direction to the* **Mariners who sailed in those dangerous seas near Alexandria**), *whereby they may, with better success, steer their course through the vast* **Ocean of Learning** *and make more full and perfect* **Discoveries** *of hitherto unknown* **Philosophical** *truths.*

# The Publisher to the Reader.

With all this *labor* and *toil* of the *brain*, they are at last known to prove impertinent and *useless* for explaining with *satisfaction*, or even tolerably, the ordinary *phenomena* <note>original: "Phænomena"; natural occurrences or observable facts</note> which *Nature* presents to the world every day. Instead, these principles are built upon the firm and immovable foundation of *reason*, *sense*, and *experience*. They are plain and obvious to the *eye* as well as the *understanding*, and are no less accurate and certain in their *application*. 

And though the most noble *author* <note>Referring to Robert Boyle</note> has herein, for the most part, embraced the *Atomic Philosophy* <note>The theory that all matter is composed of small, indivisible particles</note>—corrected and purged of the wild fancies and extravagances of its first inventors regarding the *origin* of the *universe*, which are still embraced with such kindness and tenderness by some *pretenders*, and against which *he* has so *learnedly* disputed in the first part of his book *Of the Usefulness of Experimental Philosophy*, page 74, etc.—in explaining these *appearances*; yet considering the several *alterations* and *additions* (the happy product of his penetrating judgment) made therein, I do not hesitate to call it a *New Hypothesis*. This theory is peculiar to the *author*, established by daily *observations*, familiar *proofs*, and *experiments*, and by exact and easily practicable *chemical* processes. Through these means, one of the most hidden parts of *Natural Philosophy* <note>The study of nature and the physical universe; the precursor to modern science</note>, the *Origin of Forms*...

# The Publisher to the Reader.

*Forms and Qualities* <note>In Aristotelian philosophy, "Forms" were the essential nature of things and "Qualities" were their characteristics; these were central debates in early science.</note>, which so much vexed and puzzled the *Ancients*, and which—if I may speak with the permission of the *Cartesians* <note>Followers of René Descartes.</note>—their ingenious *Master* <note>René Descartes.</note> hardly dared venture upon, or at least was unwilling to handle at length, is now fully cleared and made manifest. So much so, that from this very *Essay* we may well take hope and joyfully expect to see the noble project of the famous **VERULAM** <note>Francis Bacon, Lord Verulam, who advocated for the inductive scientific method.</note> (hitherto reckoned among the *Desiderata* <note>original: "Desiderata"; things desired but missing.</note>) receive its full and perfect *accomplishment*. I mean, a real, useful, and experimental *Physiology* <note>In this context, "Physiology" refers to the general study of natural philosophy or physics.</note> established and grounded upon *easy, true*, and generally received *principles*.

But I shall not forestall your judgment regarding either the *excellence* of the *Author* or his *subject*, as he has so freely shared with the world those treasures of learning with which his mind is enriched. Instead, I shall soon refer you to the work itself, after I have given you these few *advertisements* <note>original: "Advertisements"; here meaning notices or introductory explanations.</note>.

The following *discourse* was written several years ago, whole and entire as it is now (as is easily perceivable by various *passages* within it). # The Publisher to the Reader.

These following *Disquisitions* <note>original: "Disquisitions"; formal inquiries or investigations</note> aimed not at the raising or supporting a *Faction* in *Philosophy*, but at the *Discovery* of the *Truth*. Therefore, the author is not so concerned with what every sort of *Reader* will think of his *Attempts*—which it is easy to foresee are not likely to be very welcome to the *Devotees* of *School Philosophy* <note>The traditional Aristotelian philosophy taught in universities at the time.</note>—as he is to refuse a *Compliance* with the desires of his *Friends*. These friends have for a long time been very earnest with him not to spend that time in *Replies* to particular *Persons*, which might be more usefully employed in pursuing further *Discoveries* of *Nature* by *Experiments*. 

If he meets with any cogent and material *Objections* against any of his chief *Opinions*, *He* is enough a *Lover of Truth* to be disposed to think himself obliged to those who shall show him his *Mistakes*, and to take the opportunity to reform them. But if nothing new or weighty is urged, *He* considers that he lives in an *Age* wherein he has observed (even in his *Own* case) that *Truths*, if recommended by real *Experiments*, will in time make their own way. Furthermore, he lives among a great number of *Ingenious Men* who, for the most part, approve of the *Opinions* and probably will not dislike the *Arguments* he has proposed. ## The Publisher to the Reader.

*Scholarly arguments may be refuted by answers of a similar nature. Therefore, the publisher does not doubt that some learned supporters of the Corpuscularian Philosophy <note>The "Corpuscularian Philosophy" was a 17th-century theory—championed by Robert Boyle—proposing that physical phenomena are produced by the motion and organization of tiny particles or "corpuscles."</note> (of which the author has endeavored to explain those parts wherein they almost all agree) will be both able and willing to defend those discoveries by rational debates, even if they do not have the opportunity to expand upon them through new experiments.*

*In the meantime, I have no reason to doubt in the least that this curious and excellent piece will be welcomed and received by all who have any regard for the great concerns of learning with that zest, delight, respect, and esteem which it so highly merits.*
