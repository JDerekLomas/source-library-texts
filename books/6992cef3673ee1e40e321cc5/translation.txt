### Surveying, Section 16
### Garrisons and Fortifications, Part 1
<note>original: "Zhencheng" (鎮成). This refers to the "Nine Garrisons" (Jiu Zhen) system, a series of strategic military districts along the Great Wall established to defend against northern incursions.</note>

Master Mao says: The greatest threat to the realm lies in the Northwest. Therefore, the <term>Imperial Ancestor<note>Referring to the Hongwu Emperor, the founder of the Ming Dynasty (reigned 1368–1398).</note></term> gave instructions, noting that the northern and western tribes are in close proximity to our northwestern borders. Because there have been wars for generations, one must select capable generals and train the soldiers at all times, maintaining constant and cautious preparedness. How grand are these Imperial words! No one who rules the Central Kingdom should ever forget them. 

Therefore, I have arranged this section by listing the maps of the various <term>garrisons<note>Zhen (鎮): Massive administrative military districts responsible for defending specific sectors of the frontier.</note></term>, followed by the commanders, then the castles and forts, then the troops and horses, and finally the grain and provisions, all organized according to their official duties.

# Ji Garrison
<note>The Ji Garrison (Jizhen) was the military district responsible for the Great Wall sectors directly north and northeast of Beijing. It was considered the most vital of the frontier commands because of its proximity to the Emperor.</note>

The Ji Garrison serves as the left flank of the Capital. At the beginning of the dynasty, the <term>Xuanfu and Liaodong<note>original: "Xuan-Liao" (宣遼). The "Three Passes" of the Ji region were originally considered internal territory. A historical term used by the Ming to refer to Mongol groups.</note></term>.

The Ji Garrison is divided into twelve routes, governed by three wings.
To the east, it borders the <term>Liaodong<note>original: "Liao-zuo" (遼左). Literally "Left of the Liao River," referring to the Liaodong Peninsula region.</note></term> region, where defense must be maintained by both land and sea.
In the center, numerous mountain passes are established; the duties of pacifying the tribes and distributing imperial rewards are very frequent here.
To the west, it internally shields the regions of Changping and Yongping, while externally it controls the Great Wall.
This sector includes the Chao River and the <term>Gubei Pass<note>original: "Gubeikou" (古北口). One of the most famous and strategic passes of the Great Wall, leading directly toward Beijing.</note></term>.

Since the <term>Gengxu Incident<note>original: "Gengxu" (庚戌). This refers to the year 1550, when the Mongol leader Altan Khan bypassed the border defenses and laid siege to the suburbs of Beijing, prompting a massive overhaul of the city's defenses.</note></term>, a Supreme Commander has been specifically appointed to be stationed at Miyun to coordinate and control the region alongside the local Governor and General-in-Chief. In recent years, the repair of fortifications and the training of guards have been gradually completed. Troops from both the north and the south are trained together; thus, the defenses can truly be described as secure.

## Jizhou
<note>Jizhou was perhaps the most critical defense sector of the "Nine Frontiers," located directly northeast of Beijing. It guarded the passes through which Mongol or Jurchen forces could most easily strike the capital.</note>

The <term>original quota (Yuan'e): the administrative baseline for supplies established in the early Ming period</term> for <term>garrison grain (Tunliang): grain produced by the military's own agricultural colonies</term> was 116,600 plus <term>shi: a unit of volume roughly equal to 100 liters</term>. The <term>civic transport grain (Minyunliang): grain taxed from the local populace and transported to the frontier</term> was 110,000 *shi*. The quota for cloth was 100,000 <term>pi: bolts of cloth</term>, and cotton was 100,000 <term>jin: "catties," approximately 600 grams each</term>. The <term>tribute grain (Caoliang): grain transported via the Grand Canal from the productive south</term> was 240,000 *shi*. <term>Salt license silver (Yanyin yin): revenue generated from the government-controlled salt trade</term> amounted to 13,581 taels and 3 mace. <term>Capital transport silver (Jingyun yin): emergency or top-up funds sent directly from the central treasury in Beijing</term> was 50,000 taels. The current quota for the <term>main force (Zhubing): the primary combat troops</term> regarding civic transport silver is 9,731 taels, 4 mace, and 9 fen.

<gloss>In the early years of the dynasty, there were quotas for cloth and cotton from Shandong; "wide cloth" from the prefectures of Guangping and Baoding; and tax grain and horse fodder from Shuntian and other prefectures. From the Jingtai era [1450–1457] onward, these amounts were increased year by year.</gloss>

During the Jiajing era [1522–1566], additional levies—both in goods and <term>silver commutation (Zhezheng): the practice of paying taxes in silver instead of actual grain or cloth</term>—were imposed on Shandong, Henan, and Shuntian prefectures. By the 44th year [of Jiajing, 1565], the Yongping Garrison finally received half of its allocated share. During the Longqing era [1567–1572], silver payments were further added from four locations, including Daming, Baoding, and Jizhou. During the Wanli era [1573–1620], it was proposed that the silver from Shandong and Henan be sent to the Ministry [of War] for central redistribution; those districts sending funds directly to this garrison were then limited to Shuntian Prefecture and the Jizhou area.

Ever since the regional capital fell to foreign tribes <note>original: "yi" (夷)</note> in the *Renzi* year of the Hongwu era <note>1372, during the reign of the Ming dynasty's founder</note>, the territory of Liaodong has been split into two distinct sectors. ## Tieling and Shenyang are both Critical Strategic Hubs

The old site of Liaoyang lies outside the border wall. Traveling west for 200 *li* <note>approximately 115 kilometers</note> brings one to Guangning, while traveling northeast for 300 *li* leads to Kaiyuan. The territory between Guangning and Kaiyuan is more than 500 *li* across. However, because the Jia River <note>literally "the Sandwiching River"</note> is currently used as the defensive barrier, the route is forced to wind for more than 800 *li*. This land is naturally fertile, and the northern tribes <note>original: "lu" (虜), a derogatory term for nomadic raiders</note> bring their livestock here to graze. From this central position, they strike northeast at Kaiyuan, southeast at Guangning, and at the center against Liaoyang and Haizhou. This is truly a "heart and belly" threat to the security of Liaodong <term>Liaozuo; literally "Liaodong Left," a common historical name for the region east of the Liao River</term>.

---

Historically, Liaodong was linked to Shandong by sea, with routes running from Jinzhou and Lushun Port <note>the southern tip of the Liaodong Peninsula, later known as Port Arthur</note> to the New River Pass in Dengzhou. The sea voyage is estimated at over 500 *li*. Throughout the journey, the various islands in the sea remain within sight of one another—the furthest being perhaps 100 *li* apart and the closest only a few dozen *li*. This makes it easy for ships to find safe anchorage. In former years, Shandong would send annual shipments <term>Suiyun; the state-managed transport of tax grain</term> to provide for Liaodong, ensuring stability regardless of whether the local harvests were plentiful or poor.

### Xuanfu
<note>Xuanfu (modern-day Xuanhua, Hebei) was a critical military hub guarding the northwestern approach to Beijing. It was one of the most heavily fortified sectors of the Great Wall.</note>

...5 taels, 4 mace, 1 candareen, and 8 li. <gloss>Increased issuance</gloss> From the 6th year of the Zhengtong era [1441] through the Jingtai era [1450–1457], 10,000 taels of silver were allocated, later replaced or supplemented by salt silver. <note>Salt silver refers to revenue generated from the state salt monopoly, which was increasingly "commuted" into cash to pay for frontier defense instead of shipping actual salt or grain.</note> During the Longqing era [1567–1572], this was increased to 163,000 [taels] for grain rewards, clothing, and monthly rations. In the 8th year of the Wanli era [1580], an additional 16,740 taels of silver were added for newly recruited military pay at Tieling. In the 13th year [1585], "leap month" silver was added. <note>The lunar calendar periodically requires a 13th month to stay in sync with the solar year; this "leap month" required an extra month of pay for the standing army.</note> The annual allocation for "guest troops" <term>guest troops (kebing): specialized reinforcements or mercenary units brought in from other provinces to bolster the local garrison</term> transported from the capital was 26,000 taels, to be requested and issued according to this amount whenever a leap year occurs.

Since the 20th year of the Wanli era [1592], deliberations were held to provide for recruited soldiers and the labor for repairing border defenses. ### Strategic Geography of Xuanfu

The *Treatise on Geography* states: Xuanfu was the Shanggu Prefecture during the Han dynasty. In the early years of our dynasty <note>The Ming dynasty (1368–1644).</note>, this area served as the "Upper Capital" of the fallen Yuan dynasty. We established the Kaiping Guard there to control and restrain the northern tribes. 

To the east of Kaiping lay Daning, and to the west was Dushi. These positions were intended to support Kaiping like the two horns of an ox <note>original: "犄角" (jījiǎo) — a military term for a pincer formation or mutually supporting positions.</note>. However, once Daning was relocated and Xinghe was abandoned, Kaiping became isolated and impossible to defend. During the Xuande era [1426–1435], the court abandoned over 300 *li* of territory <note>One *li* is approximately 500 meters or one-third of a mile.</note>, pulling the defensive line back to Dushi. 

Dushi projects outward behind the mountains and is the inevitable path for any enemy invasion; it is the northern gateway to Xuanfu, but the northern tribes now swarm around it. 

The defensive line begins in the east at Yongning, reaching the borders of the Chang Garrison and the Huanghua Garrison. To the west, it stretches to the Xiyang River Fort, meeting the boundaries of Datong and the Tiancheng Garrison. The total length of this border is more than 1,200 *li*. 

Beyond the border, tribal chieftains such as Huang Taiji <note>original: "黃台吉" — a title often held by high-ranking Mongol nobles; here it likely refers to a descendant of Altan Khan rather than the later Qing Emperor.</note> and Qing Badu lead their various tribes in nomadic grazing.
