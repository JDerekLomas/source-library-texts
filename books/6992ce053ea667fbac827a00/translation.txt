## Herbs, Part 1

### Ginseng Runners <note>original: "參條" (Shen tiao). These are the lateral roots or side branches that grow from the main ginseng body.</note>

The *Materia Medica Revised* <note>original: "從新" (Congxin), referring to the *Bencao Congxin* published in 1757.</note> states: The runners of Liao Ginseng <note>Ginseng from Liaodong, highly valued for its potency.</note> that grow horizontally from the rhizome head <term>rhizome head<note>The "Lu tou" or "reed head" is the scarred top of the ginseng root where the stem emerges each year.</note></term> possess very weak medicinal strength. They should only be used for the regulation of common illnesses, to promote the production of bodily fluids, and to quench thirst. Because their physical growth habit is horizontal, their medicinal "nature" is said to move horizontally through the arms; they are remarkably effective for those suffering from weakness in the fingers and arms.

○ The *Essential Formulas Worth a Thousand Gold* <note>original: "千金方" (Qianjin Fang), the influential 7th-century work by Sun Simiao.</note> states: Whenever boiling a ginseng decoction, one must use flowing water <note>such as water from a river or spring</note> to achieve the best results. If stagnant water is used, the medicine will not prove effective.

### Ginseng Beard <note>original: "參鬚" (Shen xu). These are the fine, hair-like rootlets of the ginseng plant.</note>

The *Mirror of a Hundred Herbs* <note>original: "百草鏡" (Baicao Jing)</note> states: Among the varieties of ginseng beard, those coming from Ningguta <note>A historical region in Manchuria, modern-day Heilongjiang.</note> are yellow, thick, and robust. The merchandise from Chuanchang <note>Literally "The Shipyard," a historical name for Jilin City, then a major trade hub.</note> is of secondary quality.

# Ginseng Leaves

These are the leaves of the Liaodong Ginseng <note>original: "遼參" (Liaoshen); ginseng harvested from the Liaodong region of Northeast China.</note>. Generally, they were brought south by ginseng traders. Because their aroma is fresh and fragrant and their taste is slightly sweet, they are excellent at **generating fluids** <term>generating fluids<note>Known as "Sheng jin," this refers to the herb's ability to stimulate saliva and treat thirst or internal dryness.</note></term> without exhausting one's *qi* <note>vital energy</note>. Consequently, ginseng merchants used to dry them and bring them along as gifts <note>original: "餉遺" (Xiangyi); souvenirs or presents for associates.</note> to be used as a tea substitute in decoctions; they were not originally intended for formal medicinal use, and few people employed them.

However, in recent times, as Liaodong ginseng has grown increasingly expensive every day, physicians have begun using the leaves as a substitute for the root. Whenever a patient’s condition requires ginseng but they lack the financial means to afford the root, they purchase the leaves instead. For this reason, the leaves are now in great demand. In the ginseng markets of Suzhou <note>A major center of commerce and medicine during the Qing Dynasty.</note>, these leaves are traded at prices that vary based on quality (sometimes fluctuating by a factor of three to five). Genuine leaves are those that are not yellowed or withered, but are emerald green as if still living, and which release a fresh, sweet fragrance when pressed between the fingers.

# Ginseng Seeds

Ginseng seeds are shaped like small kidneys. They are green when raw and turn red when ripe. Recently, the ginseng merchants coming from **Liaodong** <note>The region in Northeast China famous for producing the highest quality wild ginseng.</note> have brought seeds that are all greenish in color and about the size of a small soybean. They are found in great numbers atop the ginseng leaves. In the region of **Ningguta** <note>original: "窩古塔" (Woguta); a historical frontier region in Manchuria known for its harsh, cold climate.</note>, the frost is so heavy in the seventh and eighth months that it is difficult to enter the mountains. Consequently, the harvesters cannot wait for the seeds to ripen; they gather them while still raw and return to sell them to customers, which is why they are mostly green. 

These seeds are used to **promote the eruption of pox** <note>Refers to the treatment of smallpox or similar eruptive diseases, where the goal was to help the "poison" exit the body through the skin.</note> and the movement of fluids. Whenever the pox cannot properly erupt or fail to form "heads" and fill with fluid, adding ginseng seeds to the medicinal formula will ensure that there is no risk of the sores itching or collapsing <note>A "collapse" of pox was considered a life-threatening sign that the body's energy was failing to push the illness outward.</note> in the following days.

# Pearl Ginseng

*Research into Medicinal Properties* states: Its nature is acrid and warm, and its flavor is sweet. It is capable of supporting the interior <note>Strengthening the body's internal defenses or "upright Qi."</note>; it can also be used for external ailments <note>Likely referring to skin ulcerations or sores.</note>.

Note: **Pearl Ginseng** is fundamentally not of the ginseng species. In earlier times, one never heard of it; it has only begun to circulate in recent years. However, in the South, it is used very rarely. Some say it comes from **Guangxi** <note>original: "粵西" (Yuexi).</note> and is the seed of **Notoginseng** <note>original: "三七子" (Sanqizi); *Panax notoginseng*.</note>. Others say it is simply a grass root. Generally speaking, because it is named a "ginseng," its nature must be tonifying, yet physicians often complain of its bitterness and coldness. 

My friend **Zhu Qiuting**, while staying in **Shandong** <note>original: "山左" (Shanzuo).</note>, heard that those who trade in Pearl Ginseng possess a preparation method. If taken after being processed this way, it can serve as a substitute for **Liaoning Ginseng** <term>Liaoning Ginseng<note>original: "遼參" (Liaoshen); high-quality wild ginseng from Northeast China, considered the gold standard of tonics.</note></term>. For every five *qian* <note>Approximately 18–19 grams.</note> of the processed root, they demanded a price of fifty pieces of gold. Qiuting exhausted a thousand pieces of gold to purchase the formula, which he kept secret and did not easily share. I pleaded with his younger brother, **Tuigu**, and finally obtained the technique. I have recorded it here to aid the poor.

### The Secret Preparation Method

Slice five *qian* of Pearl Ginseng. Take three *fen* <note>A very small unit, approx. 1 gram.</note> of **Aconite** <term>Aconite<note>original: "附子" (Fuzi); the lateral root of *Aconitum carmichaelii*. It is a powerful "hot" herb used to restore yang energy and counteract cold.</note></term> and grind it into a fine powder. Mix the two evenly. Take one chicken egg and remove both the yolk and the white. Into the empty shell, pack the five *qian* of ginseng slices and seal the opening. 

Place the egg under a brooding hen. Wait until the chicks hatch, then take the egg out. Use a brush to draw one circle around the egg as a mark. Repeat this entire process seven times, until there are seven circles marked on the shell. <note>This indicates a processing period of seven full incubation cycles—roughly 147 days—using the natural, constant warmth of a hen to "mature" the medicine.</note>

However, it is said that carrying this herb onto a riverboat will cause the vessel to sink, preventing one from crossing. Those who are wise use a long string to tie the herb and leave it on the riverbank; they board the boat with the end of the line in hand, and only after they have successfully crossed the water do they pull the string to bring the herb over to the other side. 

This plant is frequently found in the border regions of Yunnan Province <note>Diānshěng; an ancient name for the region</note>. Those who have used it report: "To the man, the woman—though she be as ugly as **Momu** <note>A legendary figure of antiquity known as the personification of ugliness</note>—will appear more beautiful than **Xishi** <note>The most famous of the Four Beauties of ancient China</note> or **Wang Qiang** <note>Also known as Wang Zhaojun, a famous beauty sent to marry a nomadic Chanyu</note>. To the woman, the man—no matter how hideous—will appear as handsome as **Pan An** <note>An official of the Jin Dynasty renowned for his legendary physical beauty</note> and as vigorous as a young warrior."

To treat mutual hatred and resentment between husband and wife: Decoct the herb in wine and consume.

## Salt Seepweed and Alkali Seepweed <term>Yánpéng and Jiǎnpéng; species of Suaeda</term>

**Reflections on Medicinal Properties** <note>original: "Yaoxing Kao"; a mid-18th century pharmaceutical text by Wang Ang</note>: Both species are produced in the saline lands of North Zhili <note>A Qing Dynasty administrative region encompassing modern-day Hebei, Beijing, and Tianjin</note>. The locals harvest the plants, burn them into ash, and soak the ash to create a lye. This liquid is then boiled down to obtain salt. The leaves resemble those of mugwort, being rounded and long. By autumn, both the stems and leaves turn a deep red. The salt obtained from burning and boiling this plant is considered superior to salt produced from seawater.

**Properties:** The flavor is salty and the nature is cooling. It clears internal heat and eliminates stagnations <note>refers to "accumulations" of food or fluids in the body</note>.

## Wind-Knowing Grass <term>Zhīfēngcǎo</term>

**Reflections on Medicinal Properties:** This plant grows in the Leiqiong region <note>The area encompassing the Leizhou Peninsula and Hainan Island</note>. It is a creeping vine and is non-toxic. In the spring, the locals observe its sprouts: for every "node" or joint found on the stem, there will be one major windstorm that year. When used for medicine, the plants without nodes are selected and soaked in wine. This preparation treats all forms of "wind-numbness" <term>fēngbì; a traditional category for wandering joint pain or rheumatism</term> that has settled deep into the bones. It possesses the power to "pull" the affliction out from the body.

### Phoenix-Head Lotus <term>Fèngtóu Lián</term>

This plant is produced in the inner mountains of Taiwan <note>referring to the rugged Central Mountain Range, which in the Qing Dynasty was often associated with rare or indigenous botanical species</note>. Its shape resembles Goldthread <term>Huánglián; Coptis chinensis</term>, but its color is purple. It possesses many fine, fibrous roots that appear fuzzy and downy. It branches out in a manner resembling the head of a phoenix, which is why it is so named.

**Properties:** Its nature is balanced. It is used to treat all varieties of throat ailments.

## Li-song Fruit <term>Lísōngguǒ</term>

This fruit resembles a soapberry <term>féizào; likely referring to the fruit of the Sapindus mukorossi tree used for cleaning</term> and is produced in Taiwan. 

**Uses:** It is used to treat boils and skin ulcers <term>dīngchuāng</term>. It should be ground down and applied topically to the affected area.

## Pouch Grass <term>Púbāocǎo; likely a species of cattail, Typha</term>

The text *The Book for Saving Lives* <note>original: "Huórén Shū"; a famous medical treatise by Zhu Gong (1108 AD)</note> also refers to this plant as the "Ghost Candle." The *New Sayings* <note>original: "Xīnyǔ"</note> calls it the "Water Candle." It is a herbaceous plant that grows in wild ponds. In the final days of autumn <term>qiūmiǎo</term>, it bears fruit that looks exactly like a candle. A poet once wrote:

->The wind shakes it, yet it casts no playful shadow;<-
->It possesses the wick and soot, yet emits no smoke.<-

Because its flowering and fruiting stages so closely resemble a physical candle, it was given these names.

### Pouch Grass <term>Púbāocǎo</term> (Continued)

They are quite common in reed marshes. Local people harvest the fruit to treat wounds caused by metal blades <term>jīnrènshāng</term> and to stop bleeding.

**To treat scrofula** <term>luǒlì; a swelling of the lymph nodes in the neck, often tuberculous</term>: Harvest the Pouch Grass along with its roots. Wash away the mud, cut the plant into small sections, and decoct into a soup in a sand pot. Drink this as a substitute for tea. Whether the patient is male or female, they will be cured. However, if a woman takes this medicine, she will be unable to conceive after her recovery. To counteract this, she must take four to five ounces of "True Motherwort Pills" <term>zhēn yìmǔ wán; a classic herbal formula used in traditional Chinese medicine to regulate menstruation and fertility</term> from Beijing.

People in the south call it Nut-grass <term>suōcǎo</term>, while those in the north call it the "Plank-branch Flower." When it bears fruit, it is known as the "Ghost Candle." Its powder is Cattail Pollen <term>púhuáng</term> <note>a common ingredient in traditional medicine used to stop bleeding and disperse blood stasis</note>.

## Catfish Whiskers <term>Niányúxū</term>

According to the *Manual of Medicinal Plant Harvesting* <note>original: "Cǎiyào Lù"</note>: The stems and leaves of the Catfish Whiskers plant are green. The surface of the leaves features prominent straight veins, and each individual leaf has two tendrils resembling whiskers. Its root system is shaped like a bamboo rhizome.

It is used to treat furuncles <term>dīngchuāng; deep-seated, painful sores or boils</term> and all varieties of skin ulcers.

Wang Lianshi <note>a Qing Dynasty physician and author of several botanical manuals</note> states: Catfish Whiskers grows along a vine and possesses leaves similar to those of a bean plant. Each leaf produces two whiskers from its inner side. The roots are white and thick. It is a specialized treatment in external medicine for all types of furuncles, swellings, and toxins. If the plant is applied as a poultice <note>original: "yǎn zhī"; to cover or apply a medicinal compress</note>, the affliction will vanish immediately.

# Sparrow Oats <term>Quèmài</term>

**Wang’s Manual of Medicinal Plant Harvesting** <note>original: "Wāng Shì Cǎiyào Shū"</note> states: This is the Sparrow’s Foot Flower <note>original: "Quèjiǎohuā"</note>. This flower causes people to feel anger if touched <note>this likely refers to a physical irritation or a traditional belief regarding the plant’s effect on the spirit</note>. The flower itself resembles the shape of a sparrow’s foot.

Hunters harvest this plant to boil down into a toxin for medicinal arrows, and thus they call it "Gate-Breaking Grass" <note>original: "Pòguāncǎo"</note>. Because it has the power to corrode hemorrhoids and fistulas <term>zhìlòu; chronic anal sores or abnormal openings in the skin</term>, people also refer to it as "Tube-Breaking Grass" <note>original: "Pòguǎncǎo"; the "tube" refers to the fistula</note>.

The nature of this plant is hot and its energy is fierce; it damages human skin and can immediately cause swellings to ulcerate. It must be stir-fried with rice vinegar before use. Because it is a substance that "corrodes the intestines," it must never be included in oral decoctions. It is strictly for external use, dabbed onto hemorrhoids and fistulas. — From **Wang’s Prescriptions**.
