# Cosmos.

Viewed in the light which, according to the measure of my strengths, I had imagined as attainable before the ultimate end of the sciences, it has now been my endeavor to represent nature under a manifold variety of forms. I have felt the inner joy that in the world, objects of external opinion—as well as in the life of an image received through the senses—act upon the interior of man with firm certainty and his feelings— <note>Humboldt is describing the "subjective" side of science: how the physical world triggers emotional and intellectual responses in the observer.</note>

This begins with the stars, which receive light reflections in the most distant parts of space, and follows immediately, through various precautions, down to local observation and to the sight of how the case appears along the unlimited path.

### Introduction.

Striving toward the goal which I envisioned as attainable, according to the measure of my strengths and the current state of the sciences, I have, in the two previously published volumes of the <term>Cosmos</term> <note>Humboldt’s monumental work "Kosmos" (1845–1862) attempted to unify all known physical sciences</note>, considered nature from a twofold perspective. I have attempted to represent it first in the pure objectivity of external appearance, and then in the reflection of an image received through the senses upon the interior of man—upon his circle of ideas and his feelings.

The terrestrial body was described in its formation, its average density, the gradations of its heat content increasing with depth, its electro-magnetic currents, and polar light processes <note>referring to the Aurora Borealis/Australis</note>. The reaction of the planet's interior upon its outer crust determines the sum total of volcanic activity, the more or less closed circles of seismic waves and their effects—which are not always merely dynamic—and the eruptions of gas, hot water springs, and mud. The origin, transformation, and upheaval of the earth's layers determine, changing by epochs, all the peculiarities of the natural shaping of the earth's surface; they determine the spatial distribution of solid and liquid, and the extent and structure of the continental masses in both horizontal and vertical directions.

# The Role of the Ordering Mind

Parts of the separating spirit. The world-ordering Reason <note>original: "νοῦς" (nous)</note> governs the continuously progressing formation of the world, the primal source of all movement and thus also of all physical phenomena. By assuming a centrifugal rotation <insert>12</insert>, the slackening of which—as we have already mentioned above—causes the fall of meteoric stones, Anaxagoras explains the apparent (east-to-west) celestial circulation. This hypothesis marks the starting point of vortex theories, which gained great cosmic importance more than two thousand years later through the work of Descartes, Huygens, and Hooke. Whether the world-ordering spirit of the Clazomenian <note>Anaxagoras was born in Clazomenae</note> signifies the Deity itself or, in a pantheistic sense, merely a spiritual principle of all natural life <insert>13</insert>, remains outside the scope of this work.

In a stark contrast with the two divisions of the Ionian school stands the mathematical symbolism of the Pythagoreans, which likewise encompasses the universe. The gaze remains fixed one-sidedly within the world of sensually perceptible natural phenomena upon the regularity in formation (the five fundamental forms), and upon the concepts of numbers, measure, harmony, and opposites. Things are reflected in numbers, which are, as it were, an "imitative representation" <note>original: "μίμησις" (mimesis)</note> of them. The boundless repeatability and increase of numbers is the character of the eternal, of the infinity of nature. The essence of things can be recognized as numerical ratios, and their changes and transformations as numerical combinations. Among the most debated phenomena in both natural and telescopic vision is the nightly sparkling (the blinking, or <term>scintillation</term> <note>from the Latin 'scintillare', meaning to sparkle or flash</note>) of the stars. According to Arago's investigations <margin>Arago's Works, Vol. 40</margin>, two essential factors must be distinguished in scintillation: 1) a change in light intensity, characterized by a sudden decrease until extinction followed by a flare-up; and 2) a change in color. Both changes are even stronger in reality than they appear to the naked eye; for once individual points of the retina are stimulated, they preserve the received light impression: so that the disappearance of the star, its darkening, and its change of color are not perceived by us in their full, entire measure.
