Is it not because the Scholar exerts their mind to the utmost, and through the application of careful measures <note>original: "jì liàng" (劑量); here used metaphorically to compare the "dosage" of herbal medicine to the "measures" taken in social governance</note>, is able to bring peace and harmony to the entire world?

He grew concerned, however, that the writings of the various schools did not converge into a unified system. # Preface to the Engraved Great Compendium of Acupuncture and Moxibustion

Medicine elucidates the **Mandate of Heaven** <note>original: "tiān mìng"; the natural order and divine will regarding human life</note>; its Way is truly lofty. The famous physicians of **great antiquity** <note>original: "shuò gǔ"</note> primarily used **acupuncture and stone needles** <note>original: "zhēn biān"; the earliest medical tools used to pierce the skin or apply pressure before the development of metal needles</note>. The dialogues between the **Yellow Emperor and Qi Bo** <note>original: "Huáng Qí"; referring to the *Huangdi Neijing*, the foundational text of Chinese medicine</note> are exceptionally detailed regarding this specific branch of medicine. Those who master its techniques can immediately cure **chronic, deep-seated ailments** <note>original: "chén kē"; stubborn diseases that have resisted other treatments</note>, achieving results more swiftly than herbal medicines. In recent times, however, the methods of acupuncture have ceased to be transmitted, which is truly a great pity. While I was serving in **San-Jin** <note>original: "Sān Jìn"; a classical literary name for Shanxi Province</note>, I encountered a period of many troubles. Local rebels held to their **mountain strongholds** <note>original: "fù yú"; literally 'relying on the corner of the hills', a metaphor for those resisting government authority</note> and the common people were in a state of **dire suffering** <note>original: "dǎo xuán"; literally 'hanging upside down', a common idiom for extreme distress</note>. Witnessing the hardships of the people and being unable to offer them relief, I was filled with indignation and frustration.

# The Origin and Development of the Art of Acupuncture

The *Suwen* <note>original: "素問"; the "Basic Questions," the first half of the most fundamental text in Chinese medicine</note> consists of twelve volumes. It is known to the world as a book of dialogues between the Yellow Emperor and his physician, Qi Bo. However, when one examines its underlying meaning, it is likely not the words of a single era, nor was it written by a single hand. Liu Xiang <note>a prominent Han dynasty scholar and bibliographer</note> identified it as a work written by the various princes of the Han state. Master Cheng <note>likely referring to the influential Song dynasty philosopher Cheng Yi</note> stated that it emerged at the end of the Warring States period. Its general nature is very much like the *Book of Rites*, which was a compilation by Han dynasty scholars and circulated alongside the teachings of Confucius and his grandson, Zisi.

Chapters such as the "Secret Canon of the Spiritual Orchid," the "Five Constant Powers," and the "Great Record of the Six Origins" <note>titles of specific chapters within the *Suwen*</note> exist solely to clarify the principles of <term>Yin and Yang</term> <note>the concept of dualistic harmony in nature</note> and the <term>Five Elements</term> <note>original: "Wuxing"; the five phases of wood, fire, earth, metal, and water</note>. These chapters explain how these forces generate and control one another, matching them with natural virtues and applying them directly to the human body. The various descriptions of healthy complexions, pulses, names of diseases, and the essentials of acupuncture treatment all derive from and expand upon these principles.

Thus, Huangfu Mi’s *Jiayi Jing* <note>The Systematic Classic of Acupuncture and Moxibustion, written c. 260-282 CE</note> and Yang Shangshan’s *Taisu* <note>The Grand Basis, a 7th-century reorganization of the Inner Canon</note> are also rooted in this text, though they contain slight variations. The fundamental laws for physicians do not exceed what is found in this book. However, according to the "Treatise on Literature" in the *Book of the Western Han*, there is the *Inner Canon* in eighteen volumes and the work of Bian <note>The text likely refers to Bian Que, a legendary physician of the 5th century BCE, but cuts off here</note>...

### [Preface to the Illustrated Manual of the Channels]
In the *Gui-mao* year of the Dade era <note>1303 CE</note>, Shao Wenlong, a scholar from Yanling in the Pingjiang Prefecture, wrote this preface. The work begins with two illustrations of the **Internal Organs** <term>zangfu: the collective name for the solid and hollow organs in Chinese medicine</term>, showing both front and back views. The middle section describes the movement and connections of the three *yin* and three *yang* channels of the hands and feet, followed by the flow of the **Fourteen Meridians** <term>jingluo: the network of pathways through which life-energy, or Qi, flows</term>. Each section includes detailed annotations and is followed by illustrative diagrams. These teachings were passed down in this region; however, it was only after Mr. Dong of Mount Heng carved the printing blocks that the work began to circulate widely among the practitioners of Wumen <note>modern-day Suzhou</note>.
