Long ago, the Yellow Emperor <term>Huangdi; the legendary ancestor of the Chinese people</term> first organized the world and established it as ten thousand states. In the ages that followed, the records of these systems were lost, until the time of Emperor Ku <term>Di Ku; a legendary monarch and descendant of the Yellow Emperor</term>, who first established the Nine Provinces <term>Jiuzhou; the traditional geographic divisions of ancient China</term> to bring order and governance to the ten thousand states.

According to the commentary by Kong Anguo <term>a Han dynasty scholar and descendant of Confucius</term>:

Jizhou <gloss>The imperial capital; its specific boundaries are not described, as they can be understood by where the other provinces meet it.</gloss>
Yanzhou <gloss>Bordered by the Ji River to the southeast and the Yellow River to the northwest.</gloss>
Qingzhou <gloss>Bordered by the sea to the northeast and Mount Dai <note>original: "Dai"; referring to Mount Tai, the sacred peak of the east</note> to the southwest.</gloss>
Xuzhou <gloss>Reaching the sea to the east, Mount Dai to the north, and the Huai River to the south.</gloss>
Yangzhou <gloss>Bordered by the Huai River to the north and the sea to the south.</gloss>
Jingzhou <gloss>Bordered by the southern slope of Mount Jing to the north and reaching Mount Heng.</gloss>

In the past, when **Emperor Yao** <note>original: "堯"; a legendary sage-king of ancient China traditionally dated to the 3rd millennium BCE, revered as a model of wisdom and virtue.</note> encountered the great flood, the world was divided and cut off. He commanded **Yu** <note>original: "禹"; known as Yu the Great, the founder of the Xia Dynasty, famous for his success in flood control through irrigation rather than damming.</note> to pacify the waters and the soil, restoring the land to the **Nine Provinces** <note>original: "九州"; the traditional regional divisions of ancient China.</note> according to the old system established by **Emperor Ku** <note>original: "帝嚳"; one of the Five Emperors of high antiquity.</note>.

When it came to the time of **Emperor Shun** <note>original: "舜"; the successor to Yao, who further refined the administrative borders of the realm.</note>, he initially divided the land into twelve provinces. This was because the ancient **Ji Province** <note>original: "冀州"; historically covering parts of modern-day Hebei, Shanxi, and Henan provinces.</note> was so vast from north to south that he partitioned the area around the **Wei River** to create **Bing Province** <gloss>The Wei River is located in Zhending Prefecture</gloss>. He also partitioned the region north of **Yan** to create **You Province**, and from **Qing Province**, he crossed the sea and separated the lands of **Liaodong** to establish **Ying Province**.

The **Tribute of Yu** <note>A canonical chapter of the *Book of Documents* describing the legendary Great Yu’s taming of the floods</note> states: Regarding **Ji Province**, work began at **Hukou** <note>literally "Kettle-Mouth," a famous waterfall on the Yellow River</note>, then management moved to Mount Liang and Mount Qi. After the **Great Plain** <note>Taiyuan</note> was cultivated as far as the southern slopes of **Mount Yue**, the efforts reached **Tanhuai** and were completed at the **Heng** and **Zhang** rivers. The soil there is white and mellow. The **Heng** and **Wei** rivers were made to follow their proper courses, and the **Great Continent** <note>Dalu, a vast ancient marshland</note> was drained and made into arable land. The "island barbarians" <note>peoples from the northeastern islands or coasts</note> brought tributes of skin garments; keeping **Jieshi Rock** to their right, they sailed into the Yellow River.

Emperor Shun, because Ji Province was so vast from north to south, divided the area of the **Wei River** to create **Bing Province**, and the region from **Yan** northward to create **You Province**. He established regional governors <term>mu (牧)|literally "pastors," these were high-ranking officials tasked with "shepherding" the people</term> for both. 

The *Rites of Zhou* <note>A foundational text on the administrative structure of the Zhou Dynasty</note>, in the section on the **Office of Geography**, states: The area within the bend of the Yellow River is called **Ji Province**. Its natural resources are pine and cypress. Its people follow a ratio of five males to three females. Its land is suitable for raising cattle and sheep, and its soil is best for growing broomcorn and foxtail millet. 

The terrain provides both strategic obstacles and easy passage, making it the site where emperors and kings establish their capitals. When there is chaos, one hopes for peace; when there is weakness, one hopes for strength; when there is famine, one hopes for abundance. Therefore, it is called the **Province of Hope** <note>original: "Ji" (冀), which sounds like and is used interchangeably with the word for "hope" or "longing"</note>. 

In the celestial offices <note>Chinese uranography, where earthly regions are mapped to stars</note>, the constellations of the **Hairy Head** <note>Mao, part of the Pleiades</note> and the **Net** <note>Bi, part of Hyades</note> correspond to the **Zhao** region. The **Tail** and the **Winnowing Basket** constellations correspond to the **Yan** region. The province also encompasses the borders where the states of **Qin**, **Wei**, and **Wei** meet.

The <term>Tribute of Yu</term> <note>Original: *Yu Gong* (禹貢), a chapter of the *Book of Documents* describing the legendary flood control and geography of Yu the Great.</note> states: Between the Ji and Yellow Rivers lies **Yan Province** <note>Original: *Yanzhou* (兗州), one of the Nine Provinces of ancient China, roughly corresponding to modern western Shandong and parts of Henan and Hebei.</note>. Once the nine branches of the Yellow River were channeled and <term>Lake Leixia</term> <note>Original: *Leixia* (雷夏), a historical marshland.</note> was drained into a marsh, the Yong and Ju rivers were joined to flow together. The soil there is black and loamy; the grass grows lushly, and the trees grow tall. One may travel by boat upon the Ji and Ta rivers to reach the Yellow River.

The Great <term>Emperor Shun</term> <note>One of the legendary Three Sovereigns and Five Emperors.</note> established twelve governors, and Yan was one of these jurisdictions. 

The "Office of Geography" section of the <term>Rites of Zhou</term> <note>Original: *Zhou Li* (周禮), a ritual text describing the ideal bureaucracy of the Zhou dynasty.</note> says: The region east of the River is called Yan Province. Its natural resources are reeds and fish; its population ratio is two men for every three women. The livestock suitable for the area are the <term>six domestic animals</term> <note>Commonly the horse, ox, goat, pig, dog, and chicken.</note>, and the crops suitable are the four varieties. 

The province is named after the **Yan River**. Furthermore, the term *Yan* signifies "uprightness" and "trustworthiness." To speak of "uprightness" is to say that the essence of <term>Yang</term> <note>The active, masculine, and bright principle in traditional Chinese philosophy.</note> is perfectly straight and correct; therefore, its climate is sharp and cleansing.

In the study of <term>astral mapping</term> <note>The practice of correlating terrestrial regions with celestial constellations.</note>, Yan Province corresponds to the constellations of the **Encampment** <note>Original: *Yingshi* (營室).</note> and the **Eastern Wall** <note>Original: *Dongbi* (東壁).</note>. Terrestrially, it encompasses the overlapping borders of the ancient states of Wei, Wei, Song, Qi, and Zhao.

The <term>Tribute of Yu <note>A chapter of the *Book of Documents* (Shangshu) describing the legendary King Yu's survey of the Nine Provinces of ancient China.</note></term> states: The region between the sea and Mount Dai is **Qing Prefecture**. The territory of the <term>Yuyi <note>A group of people or a tribe located in the eastern frontier.</note></term> was brought under order, and the **Wei** and **Zi** rivers were cleared to flow within their proper channels. The soil there is white and mellow, though the coastal areas consist of vast salt-marshes. The people of <term>Lai <note>Another ancient tribal group in the Shandong peninsula.</note></term> became herdsmen. For transport, people float upon the **Wen River** to reach the **Ji River**.

Emperor **Shun** divided Qing Prefecture to create **Ying Prefecture**, and he established regional governors in both. During the **Zhou Dynasty**, Xu Prefecture was merged into Qing Prefecture, making its territory even larger. 

The "Offices of the Regions" in the <term>Rites of Zhou <note>A classic text detailing the organizational structure of the Zhou government.</note></term> states: The region of the true East is called **Qing Prefecture**. Its natural resources are reeds and fish. Its population maintains a balance of two men to two women. The livestock best suited for the land are chickens and dogs, and the grains best suited for cultivation are rice and wheat. 

This is because the land occupies the position of <term>Lesser Yang <note>The phase in Five Elements theory associated with the East, Spring, and the color blue-green (Qing).</note></term>; its symbolic color is blue-green, which is why it is called **Qing Prefecture**. In the system of "Celestial Offices," it corresponds to the constellations of the <term>Emptiness and the Rooftop <note>Original: "Xu" (虛) and "Wei" (危), two of the Twenty-Eight Mansions of the Chinese zodiac.</note></term>; this is the astrological domain of the **State of Qi**.

The <term>Tribute of Yu<note>original: "禹貢" (Yu Gong); an ancient text describing the geography of China during the time of the legendary Great Yu.</note></term> states: Between the sea, Mount Tai, and the Huai River lies **Xu Province**. 

The Huai and Yi rivers were brought under control; Mount Meng and Mount Yu were cultivated for agriculture. The soil of this region is red and clayey. The vegetation grows thick and lush. From the valleys of Mount Yu come the "Xia" pheasants <note>These birds were highly valued for their colorful plumage, used in ritual banners and costumes.</note>. This region was also one of the twelve administrative sectors governed by the legendary Emperor **Shun**. During the Zhou dynasty, Xu Province was merged into **Qing Province**. Currently, it is divided into the territory of **Yan Province**. 

The name "Xu" is likely derived from the meaning of "relaxed" or "gentle" <note>original: "舒緩" (shuhuan); suggesting the character of the land or its people.</note>. Alternatively, some say it was named after Sui Hill. In terms of celestial astrology, it corresponds to the constellations of the **Astride** <note>original: "奎" (Kui); the 15th Chinese star mansion.</note> and the **Bond** <note>original: "婁" (Lou); the 16th Chinese star mansion.</note>. A square of land measuring one hundred *li* on each side—comprising one hundred *cheng* and totaling ten thousand wells—is called a <term>tong<note>Literally a "unified" unit of land.</note></term>. Between these units of *tong*, there are <term>large canals<note>Original: *hui* (澮).</note></term>, and upon these canals are <term>main roads<note>Original: *dao* (道).</note></term>. I will not provide a separate diagram for the *tong* here, as its structure can be inferred from the *cheng*. A later diagram titled "One *Tong* of a Fiefdom" will suffice for cross-reference.

In the middle of the fields, the horizontal water channels are all called <term>furrows<note>Original: *sui* (遂).</note></term>, while the vertical channels are all called <term>trenches<note>Original: *gou* (溝).</note></term>. The water from these smaller channels flows into the ditches, and the water from the ditches flows into the large canals. The large canals run horizontally, while the ditches run vertically.
