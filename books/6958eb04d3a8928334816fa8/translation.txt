<image-desc>A decorative historiated initial 'I' set within a square frame featuring ornamental scrollwork and foliage.</image-desc>IT is now nearly twenty years since the first volume of this history was published, and at the same time, the greatest part of the plates belonging to this volume were engraved. The first part met with a reception in these kingdoms and foreign countries much beyond my expectation, considering that the book was published in English. This created a very earnest solicitation from many people, for whom I have a very great regard, to publish this second volume. It has been delayed chiefly by a multiplicity of business in the practice of medicine <note>original: "Physick"</note>, which I esteem one of my first cares; it must be attended to with due attention to the various symptoms and changes of diseases if the lives of persons are to be protected. 

This business has occasioned many interruptions and faults of many kinds in this work. The putting into some kind of order my curiosities <note>The author refers to his "Cabinet of Curiosities," a private collection of natural and historical objects that eventually formed the foundation of the British Museum.</note>, numbering them, and entering their names and the accounts received with them into books—which was necessary for their preservation and use—has taken up some of the time I have had to spare from the exercise of my profession. Because some people have represented me as careless and negligent in not providing this second volume sooner, I think it proper in my own justification to acquaint the reader that I have entered into books and numbered the following natural and artificial things.

<margin>The library of *van der Linden*.</margin>
Library, descended to him from his learned ancestors, and which he intended to publish in two folio volumes, but was prevented by death. When I had sent him my thoughts about this work, along with some part of my corrections and additions intended for his notice, he returned me thanks, with an offer of whatever my library lacked which he possessed. The war and his death interrupted that design. I am not without some hopes of starting this undertaking of publishing *van der Linden* <note>Johannes Antonides van der Linden (1609–1664) was a Dutch physician and librarian known for his medical bibliographies.</note> in Latin, purged of many errors, with an account of other books of the same nature in the European languages added to it. I also intend to include books of voyages and travels which are of the same nature and are extremely useful for many purposes—in particular to natural historians, physicians, and others. However, as finding the books, bringing them from all parts of the world, and putting them into some sort of order has cost me much time and great expense, I am not without hopes that they may, some time or other, serve for the propagating of useful knowledge.

<margin>The death of Mr. *Petiver*.</margin>
Another cause of the delay in the publication of this volume was the death of Mr. *Petiver* <note>James Petiver (c. 1665–1718), an influential London apothecary and botanist.</note>, a person well known throughout the learned world for his understanding of natural history. When I first knew him, he was an apprentice to Mr. *Feltham*, who was the apothecary to St. *Bartholomew's* Hospital. He was appointed by his master to dispense the medicines there according to the directions of Dr. *Brown*, Dr. *Bernard*, and other physicians of that place. Afterward, he set up his own business in *Aldersgate Street* and was chosen as the apothecary to the *Charterhouse*. 

He had taken great pains to gather together the productions of nature in *England*, and through his correspondents and acquaintances all over the world, he procured, I believe, a greater quantity than any man before him. He did not take equal care to preserve them, but put them into heaps, sometimes with small paper labels, where many of them were injured by dust, insects, rain, and other elements. Accordingly, they did. I found myself obliged to take immediate care of all of them, and in the first place of the animal substances, which are most subject to decay. This was the more necessary to be done with attention because, as he has taken great pains to give the synonymous names used by other authors for those specimens that were already described, there are also many among them that were not noticed by any natural historian <note>original: "Natural Historian"; a scholar of biology, geology, and the natural world</note> before him. Therefore, his samples were necessary to confirm what he meant by such short names and descriptive titles as are found in his writings—whether those published anonymously or those publicly acknowledged as his own. Some few of them, perhaps, may be only varieties of the productions mentioned by other writers, and not specifically different from what was described by them; however, this could not be determined with certainty without the very originals from which he took his descriptions and illustrations. 

I have taken as much care as I can to bring his collections and papers out of the confusion I found them in. I will take further care that what he has gathered together, through very great and indefatigable industry, shall not be lost, but preserved and published for the benefit of the public—doing justice to his memory and my own reputation.

Upon these considerations, and many more too tedious to recite, I hope the delay in the publication of this—admittedly very ordinary—book will be pardoned. And yet, I think it more curious <note>original: "curious"; in this context, meaning "full of interesting detail" or "scientifically significant"</note> than the first volume. This is because it contains an account of the *animals, etc.*, as well as illustrations of the fruit trees of *Peru* and many parts of the world hitherto unknown. These were drawn from life in their natural size, and also include views of them from a distance, wherein their manner of growth and general appearance <note>original: "tota facies"</note> is shown. These illustrations continue the numbering of the plates <note>original: "Tables"</note> after the introduction to the first volume and immediately follow this section: Plates V, VI, VII, VIII, IX, X, and XI.

# The Introduction to the Second Volume.

Plate 10, Figure 1. Shows a branch of the Logwood tree, with its flowers and seeds, sent to me from *Jamaica* by Mr. *Barham*. From these specimens, I wrote the following description of what is called "Logwood" on page 182 of this volume; it is given the same name by *Tradescant* <note>John Tradescant the Younger (1608–1662), a famous English botanist and collector</note> on page 36, or *Campeigiana* and *Campechia* on page 35 <note>original: "Ej. p. 35." referring to the same author</note>.

The leaves of this tree are "winged" <note>pinnate; having leaflets arranged on either side of a stem</note>, with two or three pairs attached to a middle rib without a single leaflet at the tip. Each leaflet is shaped like, and in other respects resembles, the leaves of the *Lignum Vitae* tree or Boxwood. From the "armpits" of the leaves <note>original: "ex alis foliorum"; the axils where the leaf meets the stem</note> emerges a string two or three inches long, upon which many flowers are set. These flowers resemble the growth of Sycamore flowers; they have six petals <note>term: Hexapetalous</note> with a stalk <note>term: Stylus; the part of the pistil that bears the stigma</note> and have pollen-bearing filaments <note>term: Stamina</note> of a yellowish-brown color. 

These are followed by small pods <note>term: Siliculae</note> or follicles that grow in the manner of "Ashen keys" <note>the winged seeds of an Ash tree</note>. They are light green and shaped like the head of a spear; they are flat and open in the middle, from which small flat seeds emerge. The branches are covered with a smooth, ash-colored thin bark and have sharp thorns, one or two inches long, where the leaves sprout. Because these thorns grow so thickly, the tree is very suitable for hedges in *Jamaica*, where it is now planted from seeds sent to Mr. *Barham*. 

This inquisitive gentleman directed an Indian—whom he had sent to the Bay of *Campeche* <note>A region in the Gulf of Mexico famous for its dyewood</note> to cut that wood—to provide him with seeds so the tree could be propagated. He also informs me that when the men cut that wood, they must stand up to their knees in the water where it grows. They are in constant danger of being attacked by the *Spaniards*, who claim that no European nation has the right to cut it but themselves. Another danger to them is being bitten terribly by mosquitoes, from which many of the Logwood cutters have died. Through Mr. Barham's care in bringing over the seed, these dangers may be prevented in the future. Since the first sowing of this seed in *Jamaica* in the year 1715, many trees have now produced ripe seeds.

Among the rest is a small black *Trochus* <note>a genus of sea snails with top-shaped shells</note> which, when strung by the natives on fish guts or nerves and worn as bracelets and necklaces, acquires an extraordinarily fine color, even surpassing that of the finest Oriental pearl. I intended to have given a further account of this voyage to the Straits of *Magellan* and the *South Sea* <note>the Pacific Ocean</note>, and of natural objects from those places, but I must leave them for another opportunity.

It is appropriate in this place to address some objections that may have been made against my former volume, as well as those things I find fault with myself, both in that volume and this one. In the first place, I must admit that many of the illustrations were drawn from dried plants, and that the person who fastened them into the books, the artist who sketched them afterward, and the engravers have all committed several mistakes. I had observed that books of natural history and travel accounts were so filled with illustrations of natural specimens created from memory or word-of-mouth reports, that I was perhaps too strict in not correcting what was wrong. My reasoning was that if there were any errors of that kind in the prints, they could easily be corrected by reading the descriptions. For example, if any leaves had fallen off or been neglected by the various workmen—resulting in them being placed alternately on the stalk instead of opposite one another—such an error is corrected in the text. This is so true that I have not found anyone in *Jamaica* who pays attention to these matters ever failing to find the plants I intended by using my descriptions and illustrations, as may appear by what follows.
