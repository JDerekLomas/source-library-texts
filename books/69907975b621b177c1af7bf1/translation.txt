# Foreword

The Vedas are the sole guides on the path to the highest good in Indian life. According to the saying, "Whatever is found here may be found elsewhere, but what is not here is found nowhere else," <note>original: "Yadihāsti tadanyatra yannehāsti na tatkvacit." This famous verse, often associated with the Mahabharata, emphasizes the comprehensiveness of sacred knowledge.</note> all things are contained within the Vedas; this has been the belief of all Indians. Divided into "Rig, Yajur, Sama, and Atharva," there are four Vedas. There are six limbs of the Veda <term>Vedāngas; "limbs of the Veda" referring to auxiliary sciences</term>, namely: Phonetics, Ritual, Grammar, Astrology, Etymology, and Prosody. Additionally, there are four sub-limbs <term>Upāngas</term>: the Puranas, Logic, Hermeneutics, and Law. The teacher Sage Yajnavalkya has directed his focus to these fourteen sciences. Furthermore, when including Medicine, Archery, Music, and Economics, there are a total of eighteen sciences.

Among these fourteen or eighteen sciences, Astrology <term>Jyotiḥ Śāstra</term> is the stainless eye of the Veda! As it is said: "Astrology is the pure, stainless eye of the Veda." <note>original: "Vedasya nirmalaṃ cakṣuḥ jyotiḥ śāstramakalmaṣam"</note> Without this, all activities—whether they are rites based on the Vedas <term>Śrauta</term> or those based on traditional law <term>Smārta</term>—remain obstructed. Truly, "one who is deprived of the organ of sight can do nothing." <note>original: "cakṣuṣāṅgena hīno na kiñcitkaraḥ"</note> Even in today's scientific age, the importance of this science has grown, and eminent members of society wish to prove its scientific validity to offer something special to the world.

The "Wishing-Tree of Astrology" <note>original: "Jyotiṣa Kalpadruma"</note> has three main branches <term>Skandhas</term>: Samhita (Mundane/Omens), Hora (Horoscopy), and Siddhanta (Mathematical Astronomy). Each of these maintains its own distinct significance today. Within the twelve divisions of the Samhita branch, the collections of texts on Query Astrology <term>Prashna; the branch of astrology that answers specific questions based on the moment they are asked</term> hold great importance. These texts are extremely useful for satisfying those curious about astrology. From time to time, ancient teachers have woven their experiences together, offering them to the service of the public after thousands of trials. Today, many texts on queries, as well as sentences scattered here and there, appear as evidence in the tradition; among these texts is the present *Prashnachandeshwara*.

In the book *Indian Astrology* <note>original: "Bhāratīya Jyotiṣa"</note> by Shivnath Jharkhandi (p. 638), the names *Prashna Naradi* and *Prashna Jñana* or *Prashna Samapti* by Bhattotpala are mentioned, but no indication is given regarding *Prashnachandeshwara* or the author Ramakrishna Daivajña. Similarly, in Dr. Nemichandra Shastri's *Indian Astrology* (p. 508), no mention is found. Furthermore, in the index of texts used in the writing of the Doctor's history, works such as *Kerala-prashna-ratna*, *Kerala-prashna-sangraha*, *Kevala-jñana-prashna-chudamani*, *Prashna-kutuhala*, *Prashnopanishad*, *Prashna-kaumudi*, *Prashna-chintamani*, *Prashna-naradiya*, *Prashna-vaishnava*, *Prashna-siddhanta*, and *Prashna-sindhu* are listed...

Their names are indeed mentioned, but within the massive historical records of these great figures, it did not seem necessary to provide anything regarding the work *Prashnachandeshwara* or the astrologer Ramakrishna.

Whatever the highly respected teacher Sudhakar Dwivedi has mentioned in his <term>Ganakatarangini; "The Stream of Mathematicians," a biographical history of Indian astronomers</term> reveals that Ramakrishna was born into the family of <term>Ganesha Daivajna</term>, the creator of the <term>Jatakalankara; "Ornament of Birth-Horoscopes"</term>. From the verse provided at the end of the *Jatakalankara*, it is known that Ramakrishna was the brother of Ganesha Daivajna and that they were Gujarati Brahmins. The curiosity regarding his lineage is clearly satisfied by consulting the *Ganakatarangini* or the *Jatakalankara*.

The astrologer Ramakrishna has woven this entire garland of 108 verses across seven chapters, containing 5, 11, 9, 15, 5, 23, and 40 verses respectively. In this book, he has included almost all practical "query-based" questions. Some of these questions appear to be translated versions of verses found in other works on horary astrology <note>original: "Prashna granthas," texts focused on answering specific questions based on the moment they are asked</note>, which has been demonstrated by providing notes in the appropriate places. In addition to the seven chapters, a <term>Prakirnakadhyaya; a miscellaneous or supplemental chapter</term> has also been added, which contains collections from other texts as well as elements from traditional memories. Readers will also realize the importance of this miscellaneous chapter through their study. The fulfillment of the benefits hoped for from its structure now rests in the hands of the readers. I have merely made an attempt to make this previously unavailable book available. If the world of <term>Jyotish; the science of light, encompassing astrology and astronomy</term> gains any benefit through this, I will consider my labor successful. A human can be highly learned but not omniscient; therefore, readers will forgive any errors, seeing them as a human trait, duty, or nature. At the same time, I hope readers will inform me by letter regarding such errors so that they can be removed in the next edition.

# Introduction of the Commentator

I bow again and again to the world-renowned and highly wise masters, experts in the science of astrology <note>original: "Jyotirvidya," the traditional Indian science of light, encompassing astronomy and astrology</note>, beginning with the teacher Ramakrishna. ॥ 1 ॥

In the northern regions, the Ballia district shines as a home for the learned, the powerful, and those virtuous souls who embody the goals of human life <term>Purushartha; the four traditional goals: virtue, wealth, pleasure, and liberation</term>. ॥ 2 ॥

The lineage of the <term>Gautama Gotra; a clan tracing its ancestry back to the sage Gautama</term>, which is the ornament of the town of Sikar-Arkpur, flourishes here in the virtuous village named "Vigahi." ॥ 3 ॥

In this village lived the famous scholar Sri Gokula Mishra, a master of various sciences and a highly respected leader among the twice-born <term>Dvija; "twice-born," a term for those who have undergone spiritual initiation, typically referring to the scholarly or priestly classes</term>. ॥ 4 ॥

He had a wise son named Shivagovinda, in whom the qualities of both Shiva and Vishnu <note>original: "Govinda"</note> were perfectly manifested. ॥ 5 ॥

Through his intense spiritual practice, he obtained five sons who were honored by the people, much like the five celestial trees of heaven <note>original: "Devadrumas," the five mythical trees that grant wishes in Indra's paradise</note>. ॥ 6 ॥

In order, they were Sri Dinabandhu, Sri Devasarana, and then the wise Sri Girijadatta—who stood like the central gem of a necklace. ॥ 7 ॥

The fourth was Sri Kuberadatta, esteemed by the virtuous, and the fifth was known among the great-souled by the name Rudradatta. ॥ 8 ॥

Among them, from the father Sri Girijadatta Mishra and the mother Srimati Nageshwari, a son named Ramjanma was born. ॥ 9 ॥

At the incomparable university of the venerable Sri <term>Malaviya; Pandit Madan Mohan Malaviya, founder of Banaras Hindu University</term>, he acquired his traditional knowledge <note>original: "Agama," referring to the inherited body of sacred and technical knowledge</note> from teachers whose feet are worshipped by the wise. ॥ 10 ॥

He studied under the teachers Vindhyeshwari Prasad, Ramavyasa, and Kedaradatta Joshi. ॥ 11 ॥

Having attained the position of professor, he remained at the college <note>original: "Vidyalaya"</note>, teaching students with affection and bringing satisfaction to the masters of wisdom. ॥ 12 ॥

By composing this new commentary and placing himself at the forefront of scholars, this Ramjanma Mishra finds true satisfaction within his soul. ॥ 13 ॥

I bow to those wise inspirations; without the nectar of their affection, there would be no movement on the path of writing this book, which would otherwise be like a dry desert. ॥ 14 ॥

If the experts who understand the difference between merits and flaws are well-pleased, only then will I consider my labor a success in my heart. ॥ 15 ॥

**Explanation—** To determine the quality of the current time, add the values of the lunar half-watch <term>Yamardha: a period of about 1.5 hours, representing half of a three-hour "watch" or Prahara.</term>, the zodiac sign currently occupied by the moon (counting from Aries), the lunar day <term>Tithi: a lunar day in the Hindu calendar.</term>, and the day of the week. Divide this sum by three. If the remainder is 1, the quality is **Sattva** (Purity); if the remainder is 2, it is **Rajas** (Passion); and if the remainder is 3 or 0, it is **Tamas** (Ignorance).

The result is as follows: if the time of inquiry is found to be in the Sattva phase, the task will be completed quickly. If it is in the Rajas phase, the success will occur after some time. If the Tamas phase is obtained, one should state that the task will not be successful. || 2-3 ||

**Example:—** Someone makes an inquiry at 10:00 AM on Sunday, the 7th day of the bright half of the month of Pausha, in the Year 2034 <note>This corresponds to approximately late 1977 or early 1978 CE in the Vikram Samvat calendar.</note>. The inquiry occurs 1 hour and 15 minutes after sunrise, which falls within the first half-watch <note>There are eight watches (yamas) in a 24-hour period, four for day and four for night, each roughly 3 hours long. The Moon's sign is Pisces (the 12th sign) = 12; the lunar day is the 7th = 7; the day is Sunday = 1. Their sum (3 + 12 + 7 + 1) <note>Note: The "3" here likely refers to the calculation value for the first half-watch used in this specific system.</note> = 23. Dividing this by 3 (23 ÷ 3), the quotient is 7 and the remainder is 2. Therefore, the Rajas phase is established, and one should say that the work will be completed with some delay.

**Prose Order—** The day, the star constellation <term>Nakshatra</term>, and the lunar day <term>Tithi</term> combinations; also the units of time <note>ghatikas and similar divisions</note>; the months passed since the start of the year <note>the month of Chaitra</note> and the letters of the traveler's name. One should divide the sum of these by seven, and based on the remainder, speak of good or ill fortune. Thus: on a remainder of one or two, it should be said the traveler is gaining profit. In the third and fourth, the traveler has met with hardship. In the fifth, an untimely death. In the sixth, certainly afflicted with a chronic disease. In the seventh, death occurs, according to the words of Sharada.

**Vernacular—** At the time a seeker asks a question, the astrologer should perform a calculation. Sum the following: the number of the day (counting from Sunday), the number of the star constellation, the number of the lunar day, the number of the astrological combination <term>Yoga</term>, the number of time-units <term>Ishta Ghati</term> elapsed, the number of months passed since the month of Chaitra, and the numerical value of the letters in the traveler’s name. Divide this total sum by 7. The prediction should be made according to the remainder:
If the remainder is 1 or 2, the traveler is safe and well.
If the remainder is 3 or 4, it should be said that the traveler is in distress.
If the remainder is 5, it indicates an untimely death <note>apamrityu: an accidental or sudden death before one's natural time</note>.
If the remainder is 6, they are suffering from a chronic illness.
If the remainder is 7 <note>effectively a remainder of 0</note>, it should be said that the traveler has died. (Even if this is true, the diviner <term>Daivajña</term> should not speak such harsh words directly, but should express them in a gentle, roundabout manner). || 33-35 ||

**Prose Order—** In marriage, in all auspicious rites, in journeys, and in the transit of planets, the birth sign <term>Janma Rashi</term> is primary; therefore, one should not consider the name sign.

**Vernacular—** To determine the favorable or unfavorable outcome for a marriage, for all auspicious ceremonies like the sacred thread ritual <term>Upanayana</term>, for journeys, and when considering the influence of planetary transits <term>Gochar</term>, the birth sign (the sign the moon was in at the time of birth) is the most important. In these matters, one should not use the name sign (the sign associated with the name one is commonly called by). || 36 ||

> **In the country, the village, the home, in war, in service, and in business dealings,**
> **the name sign is primary; one should not consider the birth sign. || 37 ||**

**Prose Order—** In matters of the country, village, home, war, service, and business, the importance of the name sign should be understood. One should not consider the birth sign.
