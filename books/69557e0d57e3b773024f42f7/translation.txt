*his memory must too deservedly be charged.*

It was thought a book of this nature would be of some use in this degenerate age; for since our **modern libertines** <note>original: "Libertines"; here referring to people who reject traditional moral and religious restraints</note> refuse to hear **Moses** and the **prophets**, let them behold one risen from the dead to convince them. Let them, with confusion, hear him reason them out of their absurd maxims and shame them into better principles.

Besides, it will appear that a work of this nature is very proper to give us a just notion of **Pythagoras** himself. Many things were attributed to him which bore no relation to him, and several absurdities were accepted by the world as part of his **doctrine**, which could not help but lessen his character among men of **judgment** and **learning**. So, he now appears reformed of those many errors with which they had disguised him, and comes forth clothed in his **primitive simplicity** <note>original: "Primitive Simplicity"; meaning his original, uncorrupted teachings</note>.

Upon these considerations, we thought an **English translation** would be a proper **undertaking**. We hope the reception **Pythagoras** found in **France** will be no **reason** why he should not meet with a **welcome** here, since a brave man ought to be well-received everywhere. He had a natural antipathy to **tyranny**, and therefore he seems to claim respect from a free **people**; surely his doctrine ought to flourish nowhere better than among generous **English spirits**.

## MR. DACIER’S
# PREFACE.

I have not forgotten the debt I owe the world following the publication of my first volume of *Plutarch*, and two volumes of *Plato*. They met with a reception so favorable that I thought myself obliged to proceed until the promise I had made the public was performed. If God grants me life and health, I shall apply both entirely to this end, and hope in a short time to present the world with all of *Plutarch’s Lives*, together with his *Morals*, and the dialogues of *Plato*. It is neither a want of resolution, nor an uneasiness regarding the attempt that has interrupted me in my design, but rather some weighty reasons arising from the sense of my duty, which must always be preferred over our private inclinations.

Among all those princes who have been renowned for their love of letters <note>original: "Letters"; meaning literature and scholarship</note>, no one has honored them with a protection so eminent, so efficacious, and so glorious as *Louis the Great* <note>Louis XIV of France (reigned 1643–1715)</note>. Amidst his most important cares, which fill up every hour of his life, and in the heat of the most expensive wars, His Majesty has still persisted in his encouragement of learning. He has appropriated some moments to the cherishing and improvement of it; for which reason, it never appeared in a more flourishing condition than under his reign. *Athens itself*, in its greatest...

### Mr. Dacier's Preface

The greatest splendor could never show an assembly of men so celebrated in all sciences, or distinguished by such a variety of talents, as those who meet every day at the *Louvre* <note>The royal palace in Paris, which housed various royal academies at this time.</note> under his Majesty's order and protection. In honor of this age, the arts and sciences have recovered their ancient luster. The nicety of taste, strength of judgment, and politeness of wit break out afresh after an eclipse of several ages. This is one of the most distinguishing characteristics of the most glorious reigns. 

As I have shown elsewhere, the arts and sciences sympathize with the fortune of the Prince. The same influence that a good soil and friendly climate have upon seed and fruit, the glory, grandeur, magnificence, and liberality of Princes have over the arts and sciences. It may be said that these disciplines thrive not so much *during* their reigns, but *because* of them. It was this that gave the ancients such a just and sublime idea of a Hercules conducting the Muses—*Hercules of the Muses* <note>original: Hercules Musarum</note> or *Hercules, Leader of the Muses* <note>original: Hercules Musagetes</note>—thereby denoting the eternal and inviolable union that exists between the Muse and the Hero.

His Majesty, having ordained a new regulation for the Academy of Medals <note>The Académie des Inscriptions et Belles-Lettres, originally established to compose Latin inscriptions for monuments and medals honoring Louis XIV.</note>, was unwilling that it should be wholly employed in medals and inscriptions. Instead, he wished that it should, through a general application, extend itself to all branches of learning. To make this establishment even more useful, he seemed desirous that—besides those studies which all the members were to embrace in common—each of them should undertake something in particular. 

Thus the Muses, who according to the fable work together under Apollo's care and discipline, are not content to unite in a general concert <note>original: Consort; meaning a musical ensemble or harmony.</note>, but moreover apply themselves each to some particular employment.
