## POEM
### On the
# THE HAGUE PROMENADE,
### AND
# PRECIOUS FOLLY,
### BY THE GENTLEMAN
## CONSTANTIJN HUYGENS.

**H**ow fortunate are the lime trees <note>The "Voorhout" in The Hague was famous for its rows of lime (linden) trees, where the elite would stroll.</note>
That find such a sweet writer,
To, through such a clear sound,
Proclaim their praise abroad!
For when their withered branches
Shall sink down toward the earth;
Or if the fierce steel <note>A reference to an axe or woodsman’s tool.</note>
Shall destroy them altogether:
Or if an old graybeard
With the leaves and the twigs,
In a corner of the hearth
Shall seek protection from the cold;
Then shall this poem live,
And soar up to the heavens,
As a record, and for the joy
Of the youth yet to come.
Then shall a courtly maiden,
Sweeter than a sweet thrush,
Warble this *Song of the Promenade* <note>original: "Voor-houtsche liet"</note>
As a comfort in her sorrow.
Indeed, she shall the green lime trees,
Though they are nowhere to be found,
Though they have long since perished,
See standing within her mind:
Indeed, she shall the sweet conversations,
The tangles of love, the flirtations,
And whatever goes on today,
Then also know in every detail.

<column-break/>

Then she will often say, (ge <note>The text includes the syllable "(ge" as a catchword, a common printing practice to indicate the start of the next page or column.</note>
Where might the writer lie—
Who brought this graceful Court-poem <note>The "Hof-gedicht" was a specific genre of Dutch poetry focused on the praise of a country estate or park.</note>
To the light of day?
May myrtles and laurels
Constantly adorn his dry grave;
May it be covered with roses
Where he stretches his weary bones.
Above all, she can learn there
Of all the misuse of clothing,
Which in our foolish times
Glides through almost all the world.
Huygens, master of morals,
Disciplinarian of misused limbs,
Writer of the *Costelick Mal* <note>original: "Diere-mal," a variation of the title "Costelick Mal," Huygens’ satire on the "expensive folly" of extravagant fashion.</note>,
*Huygens*, witty in all things;
Oh! That your manly words
Might tear apart all collars,
Might break all the pageantry,
Brought by Lady *Novelty* <note>original: "Nieuw-gierin," personifying the constant desire for new and expensive fashions.</note>;
Then I, and all people,
Would wish for your clever verses
Never to be rejected,
Nevermore to be torn up
Either to wrap up figs,
Or to bind up pepper: <note>In the 17th century, unsuccessful poems were often sold to grocers as scrap paper for wrapping spices and fruit.</note>
For, that which edifies with pleasure
I hold to be the best poetry.

->*I. A. F.*<-

This **Lord of Conceit**, master of all, makes the North his throne,
His footstool of the West; he calls the Sea his cauldron,
The sunlight his hearth-fire, the birds of the air his roast,
The moon’s disc his torch, and the starry path his boulevard.
The roll of thunder is his speech, the winds are his sighs,
The rain is his spittle, the lightning his lantern.
He evicts Princes from their estates, argues Lords off their benches,
Fells Emperors with a mere wink, and brings Kings under his thumb.
But what is he in truth? Ask those who see and hear him:
A penniless fool, a beggar born.

Another takes fright at everything that approaches him;
What is his trouble? Everything that touches him is made of glass! <note>This refers to the "glass delusion," a famous form of melancholy in the 17th century where sufferers believed their bodies were made of glass and liable to shatter.</note>
