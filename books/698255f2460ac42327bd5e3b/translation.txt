# TO DOMENICO MOLINO,
# PATRON OF THE MUSES,
## URANIA WISHES HEALTH,
## BONIFACIUS WISHES HAPPINESS.

*Whoever first invented the art—whether he was called **Simmias of Rhodes** <note>A Greek poet from the 3rd century BCE famous for "pattern poetry" or "technopaegnia," where the lines are arranged to form a shape like an axe or wings.</note> or **Theocritus of Syracuse** <note>The creator of pastoral poetry, also credited with some shaped poems like "The Syrinx."</note>—of misusing the variety of verses, and enclosing them within certain boundaries, or rather constricting them with shackles and chains, twisting and turning them, and like the **bed of Procrustes** <note>In Greek myth, Procrustes was a smith who forced travelers to fit his bed by either stretching them or cutting off their legs; Bonifacius uses this as a metaphor for forcing words into rigid poetic shapes.</note>, now contracting, now extending them; he seems to have received the invention from the ancient mythologists (if we think they should be called so, and not rather astronomer-poets), which he immediately kissed like a little daughter, judging it a miracle, the most marvelous of all marvels. Nor was anything else to be expected from a man whose name was so close to "ape" <note>A pun on the poet’s name, Simmias, and the Latin word "simia" (ape).</note>; it was altogether necessary that his wit should not shrink far from apes. For these animals, fashioned as a mockery of nature, like human monsters—the most beautiful of which, as **Heraclitus** <note>A pre-Socratic Greek philosopher.</note> said, is ugly—as soon as they have given birth to those most foul cubs of all animals, they lick them, embrace them, smile at them, fawn over them, and never set them down from their breast. Just as those ancients who brought myths into the heavens, circumscribing certain stars with certain lines, imagined now a lion, now a bull, now something else; so that clearly they might consecrate those animals among the stars by **apasterosis** <note>original: "apasterosi". A Greek-derived term meaning catasterism, or the transformation of a character into a star or constellation.</note>, or, so to speak, **stellification** <note>original: "stellificatione". The process of being turned into a star.</note>: so Simmias, by the fitting arrangement of short and long verses, exhibited various figures to us; which I finally, born so many centuries later, might consecrate as being inserted in the heavens among the poetic stars, expecting perhaps from there no unhappy influences and impressions. Because, however, together with their parents in—*

...thrice-dead poetry, no one afterwards has cared to bring back to life; and few before us have given even a tiny specimen of it. I would not dare to say that they were deterred by the supreme and insurmountable difficulty of its craftsmanship, or that they could not even conceive in thought this huge and vast mechanism—one almost beyond human genius. Rather, regarding it as trivial and more than childish—as "childishness," if I may say so—they judged that such <note>original: λῆρα, καὶ φλυαρία (lēra kai phlyaria)</note> nonsense and trifles—the absurdities of men with too much idle time—should be ignored by men of action. For what else is this than disturbing prose with verse and verse with prose, spending good hours badly, squandering a precious patrimony of time for nothing, and voluntarily undertaking useless labor? Without any necessity forcing it, why should one seize upon an arduous and troublesome journey through ruts and briars?

Furthermore, when a pernicious desire recently urged us, and an almost furious madness for emulating even worse things took hold, we wished not to appear to have added nothing of our own. Thus, having passed by those common and obsolete figures of **Theocritus** and **Simmias**, we devised something similar. We substituted a **shield** for an egg, an **organ** for a syrinx, **winged sandals** for wings, a **column** for an altar, and a **double-axe** for a hatchet; for that is not the hatchet which Theocritus once designed, but a double-axe, sharp on both sides.

These figures—extemporaneous and sudden—I present to you, Lord, clinging to the **MOLINIAN WHEELS** <note>A play on the name of the dedicatee, Domenico Molino; "Molino" means "mill," and his coat of arms featured mill-wheels.</note> as if to a seal. I have added so many corollaries besides, that the <term>mantissa</term> <note>A Latin term for a "makeweight" or something added to make up the weight; here meaning the additions are more substantial than the main text.</note> has become far richer than the meal, and the <term>epifagma</term> <note>From the Greek 'epiphagma,' referring to a condiment or side dish.</note> larger than the sack. However, I have not presented these trifles to you, Lord, because I value them as anything of substance. I am accustomed to despise even my own "sweated-over" and much-elaborated works, even after I have polished and smoothed them more than enough. I present them only so that you might recognize how easy it is to imitate this second childhood.

Otherwise, if **Livia Augusta** <note>The wife of Emperor Augustus; the author refers to a historical anecdote where her "fecundity" (eutochia) was mocked because she gave birth shortly after marriage.</note> was mocked with a courtly sneer because she had given birth in the third month, the court will insult our <term>eutochia</term> <note>original: εὐτοχίαν (eutochian), meaning "happy delivery" or "fruitfulness."</note> much more sharply and justly, for it miscarries more than seven-fold before the third day. In truth, we have cast out these monsters, dead before they were born, rather than published them. 

Farewell. 

From **Treviso**. The Nones of July <note>July 7th.</note>, 1627.

He lists the specific shapes he has created, such as a shield and a double-axe, as updates to classical Greek models.</summary>

## OFFERING GOOD WISHES. <note>original: ΕΥΔΟΞΑΖΕΙΝ (eudoxazein). A Greek greeting wishing the reader well or seeking a good reputation for the work.</note>

**M**aecenas <note>Gaius Maecenas (70–8 BC), the famous Roman patron of the arts, was also known for his extravagant and sometimes eccentric tastes.</note> seasoned young donkeys in such a way that they were counted as delicacies by gluttons. The Indians serve the haunches of tigers and entire lions at their tables. The Locust-Eaters <note>original: Acridophagi; a people of Ethiopia described by ancient geographers as living on dried locusts.</note> consider grasshoppers to be the most delicious of foods. It is a well-established fact that ostriches can devour iron itself. Even the mice on the island of Gyaros, and in Egypt under King Sethon, were known to gnaw on swords and shields. 

We also once heard a certain Spanish "Pyrgopolynices"—a boastful soldier whom other actors mockingly called "The Scarecrow"—bragging on the stage that he had been well-received by the Emperor Charles Augustus. He claimed that at a banquet, the Emperor had ordered ash-wood spears to be served to him instead of asparagus, lead bullets instead of sausages, iron balls instead of bread, breastplates and helmets instead of meat, and barbarian blood instead of wine. 

In no different a manner, Reader, I now present to you—as if in a blood-sausage <term>apexabone; an ancient Roman term for a sausage made of liver and blood</term> or some other stuffed meat—fragments of wheel rims, the ruined walls of towers, the rubble of columns, the plating of shields, the fibers of hearts, the shells of snails, the teeth of rakes and files, the scraps of a hat, the leaves of a garland, the feathers of wings, and fragments of spindles, axes, ladders, organs, tripods, jars, and cups for you to "eat."

And why shouldn't they seem tough to you, when we ourselves, the cooks and preparers of this feast, have found them to be most difficult? We truly confess that there is scarcely anything good in these dish—

Know, however, that in better ages, iron rings were not only not despised, but were preferred over those of gold and silver. For Jupiter gave an iron ring to Prometheus, <note>In mythology, after Prometheus was freed from his chains, he was ordered to wear an iron ring set with a piece of the rock to which he had been bound as a symbol of his former punishment and Zeus's authority.</note> and the Senators of Rome and the Kings of Sparta themselves wore iron rings on the fingers with which they governed the greatest part of the world. O, how things have changed! In those days, "golden men" wore iron rings; now we little men of lead—or rather, of Sicilian flint—wear gold everywhere. 

One should also consider this a bit more closely: those hands, burdened by iron rings, plundered the gold of Asia and utterly overthrew the wealth of Libya. Nor were they ever bound behind the backs of barbarians until the Roman soldier brought more than three thousand bushels of golden rings into the camp. <note>An allusion to the Battle of Cannae (216 BC), where the Carthaginian general Hannibal collected thousands of gold rings from the fingers of fallen Roman knights to prove the scale of his victory.</note> But let us now set aside matters foreign to our purpose, as we are about to say a few things regarding the method of the poems.

# THE AUTHOR TO THE READER
### TO SEEK GOOD REPUTE. <note>original: "ΕΥΔΟΞΑΖΕΙΝ" (Eudoxazein)</note>

Maecenas <note>Gaius Maecenas (70–8 BC), the famously wealthy Roman patron of Virgil and Horace, known for his refined and sometimes eccentric tastes.</note> seasoned young donkeys so skillfully that they were counted among the finest delicacies by gluttons. The Indians serve tiger haunches and whole lions at their tables. The Locust-eaters <note>The *Acridophagi*, a legendary Ethiopian tribe mentioned by ancient geographers like Diodorus Siculus, said to live entirely on locusts.</note> consider locusts to be among the delights of food. It is a very well-established fact that ostriches devour iron itself. But even the mice on the island of Gyaros, and in Egypt under King Sethon, <note>Sethon was a priest-king of Egypt mentioned by Herodotus; legendary mice were said to have gnawed the leather of the invading Assyrian army's equipment, though here the author specifies shields and swords.</note> gnawed through swords and shields. 

We have also heard a certain Spanish "Pyrgopolynices" <note>A reference to the "Braggart Soldier" character from the Roman comedies of Plautus; here used as a satirical archetype for a boastful soldier.</note>—a vainglorious soldier whom other actors mockingly called "The Scarecrow"—boasting on stage that he was quite well received by the Emperor Charles. <note>Likely referring to the Holy Roman Emperor Charles V (1500–1558).</note> He claimed that at a banquet, he ordered ash-wood spears to be served instead of asparagus, lead bullets instead of sausages, iron cannonballs instead of loaves of bread, breastplates and helmets instead of meat, and the blood of barbarians instead of wine.

These dishes will seem a bit tough to you, and, as Theophrastus <note>Theophrastus (c. 371 – c. 287 BC), a Greek philosopher and successor to Aristotle.</note> says, they are "unsoftenable," <note>original: "ἀτέραμνα" (ateramna), a term used for legumes that remain hard and won't cook soft no matter how long they are boiled.</note> the kind of legumes that Plato said fell to the ground through the horns of oxen.
