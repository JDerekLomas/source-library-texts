# FROM THE WORKS OF SUIDAS <note>original: "EK TON TOU SOUIDA"; refers to the Suda, a massive 10th-century Byzantine encyclopedia</note>

Apollinarius of Laodicea in Syria lived during the days of the Emperors Constantine and Julian the Apostate, and continued until the beginning of the reign of Theodosius the Great. He was a contemporary of the admired Basil and Gregory of Cappadocia <note>referring to the influential Church Fathers Basil the Great and Gregory of Nazianzus</note>. Indeed, he was an associate of both of them, as well as of Libanius the Sophist <note>a famous pagan teacher of rhetoric</note> and several others. This man was not only a grammarian and skilled in the arts of poetry, but he had trained much more extensively in philosophy; he was also a versatile orator <term>rhetor: a professional public speaker and teacher of persuasion</term>. He wrote thirty volumes in prose against the impious Porphyry <note>a Neoplatonist philosopher who wrote a famous critique of Christianity</note>, and he also translated the entire Hebrew Scripture into heroic verse <note>he rewritten the Bible in the style of Homeric epic poetry</note>. He also wrote letters and many other commentaries on the Scriptures.

Philostorgius <note>an Arian church historian whose work survives mostly in fragments</note> also makes mention of Apollinarius in his own history, saying: "For Apollinarius flourished during those times in Laodicea of Syria, as did Basil in Caesarea of Cappadocia, and Gregory in Nazianzus. (This latter place is a station <note>a military or postal relay station</note> within that same Cappadocia.) At that time, these three men were the champions of the 'same substance' <term>homoousios: the orthodox doctrine that Jesus is of the same essence as God the Father</term> against those of the 'different substance' <term>heteroousios: the Arian view that the Son’s essence is different from the Father’s</term>. They far surpassed all the previous and subsequent leaders of that sect up to my own time, so much so that even Athanasius <note>the famous defender of Orthodoxy from Alexandria</note> was judged as a child compared to them. For they had progressed to the furthest extent in what is called 'secular education,' and they possessed great experience in the Holy Scriptures—both in reading them and in ready memorization. This was especially true of Apollinarius, for he was even able to understand the Hebrew language. Furthermore, each was highly capable of writing in his own particular style. For Apollinarius, the style of commentary-writing was by far his best, whereas Basil [excelled] in the panegyric style..." <note>The text ends mid-sentence, describing the different literary strengths of these Church Fathers.</note>
