**Zhao Pu**, whose courtesy name was **Zeping**, was a native of Ji in Youzhou <note>modern-day Beijing area</note>. During the **Mutiny at Chen Bridge** <term>the 960 CE military coup where Zhao Kuangyin was declared the first emperor of the Song Dynasty</term>, Zhao Pu earned great merit for his role in assisting the founding of the new state. For this service, he was appointed as the Right Grand Master of Remonstrance and served as an Academician in the Bureau of Military Affairs. In the second year of the **Qiande** era <note>964 CE</note>, he was appointed as **Chancellor** <term>the highest-ranking civil official in the imperial government</term>.

The Emperor <note>Emperor Taizu, the founder of the Song</note> regarded Zhao Pu as his own left and right hands. No matter the scale of the issue—whether large or small—the Emperor consulted him on everything before making a final decision. After successive promotions, he was granted the title of **Duke of Xu**. Following his death, he was posthumously honored as the **Prince of Zhending**.

Commentators and critics have noted that when **Emperor Taizu** was gravely ill, Zhao Pu was unable to properly assist regarding the succession of **Emperor Taizong** <note>Taizu's younger brother</note> in accordance with the **Golden Casket Covenant** <term>a controversial secret agreement allegedly made between the first emperor and his mother to ensure the throne passed to his brother rather than his son to maintain stability</term>. Because of this failure in his duties during the transition of power, it is said that even though his spirit tablet is honored alongside Taizu's in the imperial temple, his ghost would wear a look of shame.

**Cao Bin**, whose <term>courtesy name</term> <note>original: "zi," a name given at adulthood to be used by peers out of respect</note> was **Guohua**, was a native of Lingshou in Zhending <note>modern-day Hebei Province</note>. When Emperor Taizu <note>The founding emperor of the Song Dynasty, r. 960–976</note> accepted the abdication of the previous throne and founded the dynasty, Cao was promoted to the position of Commissioner of the Guest Council. 

In the early years of the Qiande era <note>roughly 963 CE</note>, he was reassigned as the General of the Left Divine Martial Army and concurrently served as a Recipient of Edicts in the Bureau of Military Affairs. For his distinguished service in the pacification of the Shu region <note>the conquest of the Later Shu kingdom in modern Sichuan</note>, he was granted the titles of Commissioner of the South Palace of the Bureau of Ceremonies and Military Governor of the Yicheng Army. 

Following his military success in the pacification of Le, he was promoted to the rank of Acting Grand Preceptor. After successfully pacifying the Jiangnan region <note>the Southern Tang state</note> and securing the surrender of **Li Yu** <note>the last ruler of the Southern Tang, renowned as a master of classical poetry</note>, he was rewarded with 200,000 strings of cash and appointed as the Commissioner of Military Affairs. 

He served through the successive reigns of Emperor Taizong and Emperor Zhenzong. Upon his death, he was posthumously honored with the title of **Prince of Jiyang Commandery** and granted the supreme honor of being worshipped alongside Emperor Taizu in the imperial ancestral temple.

Dou Yi and his four brothers—Yan, Kan, Cheng, and Xi—all successfully earned the degree of <term>Jinshi</term> <note>The "Presented Scholar" degree, the highest achievement in the imperial examination system</note>. Emperor Taizu <note>Zhao Kuangyin, the founding emperor of the Song Dynasty</note> admired Dou Yi’s personal conduct and wished to employ him in a high capacity, but he was obstructed by the official Zhao Pu.

The Emperor’s favor toward Dou Yi solidified when the scholar demonstrated his superior historical knowledge. Dou Yi was able to point out that the Emperor's chosen era name, <term>Qiande</term> <note>original: "乾德"; used by the Song from 963–968 CE</note>, had previously been used by the "Pseudo-Shu" <note>Referring to the state of Later Shu, which the Song had recently conquered</note>. This revelation led the Emperor to conclude that a Grand Councilor must be a man of extensive reading and learning. Dou Yi’s younger brothers all held esteemed official positions, including the roles of Court Diarist, Imperial Remonstrator, and Rectifier of Omissions.

Lü Mengzheng, whose **courtesy name** <note>An adult name given in addition to one's given name, used as a mark of respect among peers</note> was Shenggong, was a native of Henan. In the second year of the **Taiping Xingguo** <term>Taiping Xingguo (976–984 AD), an era name of the early Song Dynasty under Emperor Taizong</term> era, he was promoted to first place in the **Jinshi** <term>Jinshi (literally "presented scholar"), the highest degree in the imperial examination system</term> examinations. 

He served as the Vice-Prefect of Shengzhou and was successively promoted to the positions of Left Palace Censor and Drafter of Edicts. Upon the death of his father, he entered a period of **ritual mourning** <note>original: "丁父憂"; the traditional Chinese practice where an official retired from public life for up to three years to honor a deceased parent</note>. He was later recalled to service and promoted to Director in the Ministry of Justice, eventually entering the **Hanlin Academy** <term>Hanlin Academy, an elite institution of scholars who performed secretarial and literary tasks for the Emperor</term> as a Scholar.

Lü was further promoted to the rank of Left Remonstrator. When the official Li Fang was dismissed from the Chancellorship, Lü was appointed as Vice-Director of the Chancellery. While in office, he was known for a character that was honest, kind, lenient, and straightforward. He dared to speak his mind whenever issues arose, and the Emperor praised him for having no hidden agendas. Since the founding of our dynasty, the only individuals to have served as Chancellor on three separate occasions have been Zhao Pu and Lü Mengzheng.

**Fu Bi**, whose courtesy name was **Yanguo**, was a native of Henan. While serving as the <term>Military Commissioner of the Jingdong Circuit<gloss>Anfushi; a high-ranking regional official responsible for maintaining order and civil administration</gloss></term>, a massive flood struck the <term>Heshuo region<gloss>The lands north of the Yellow River</gloss></term>. Fu Bi distributed relief and saved the lives of more than five hundred thousand people. This achievement was celebrated throughout the empire and became the standard model for disaster relief.

In the second year of the **Zhihe** era [1055 AD], he was summoned to court and appointed as <term>Joint Manager of Affairs with the Secretariat-Chancellery<gloss>Tong Zhongshu Menxia Pingzhang Shi; one of the highest executive offices, effectively the role of Chancellor</gloss></term> and Grand Academician of the Hall of the Collection of Sages. He was appointed at the same time as **Wen Yanbo**. 

On the day the imperial decree was proclaimed, the scholar-officials congratulated one another in the imperial court. The Emperor remarked to the Academician **Ouyang Xiu**, “Since the public sentiment is so favorable, is this appointment not far better than choosing a minister based on dreams or divination?” <note>The Emperor is referencing historical legends where rulers claimed to find their ministers through divine visions or oracles; he suggests that Fu Bi's obvious merit and popularity are a more "sage-like" way to govern.</note>

Wen Yanbo, whose courtesy name was Kuanfu, was a native of Jiexiu in Fenzhou <note>Located in modern-day Shanxi Province.</note>. In the second year of the Zhihe era [1055], he was reappointed as the Minister of Personnel and served as a Joint Manager of Affairs with the Secretariat-Chancellery and Grand Academician of the Zhaowen Pavilion <note>These titles signify the highest level of administrative power, essentially serving as a Grand Councilor or Prime Minister.</note>. He was appointed to these positions alongside Fu Bi <note>Fu Bi (1004–1083) was another highly respected statesman and diplomat of the Northern Song Dynasty.</note>, and the entire imperial court celebrated, believing that the right men had been chosen for the job.

In the third year of the Yuanfeng era [1080], he was appointed as <term>Grand Marshal (Taiwei)</term> and was again assigned to govern the Henan region. Before his departure, the Emperor held a banquet for him in the Qionglin Garden <note>The Qionglin Garden was an imperial park in the capital, Kaifeng, used for hosting grand celebrations and honoring high officials.</note>. Twice the Emperor sent high-ranking court messengers to present him with poems to see him off on his journey. People of the time considered this a supreme honor.

After a long period of service, he requested to retire due to old age. He was granted retirement with the title of <term>Grand Preceptor (Taishi)</term> and resided in Luoyang. At the beginning of the Yuanyou era [1086], the Empress Dowager Xuanren, acting on the recommendation of Sima Guang <note>Sima Guang (1019–1086) was the preeminent historian and conservative leader who compiled the *Zizhi Tongjian*.</note>, commanded Wen to return to oversee the management of important military and state affairs. He was required to attend court once every six days and participate in the <term>Classics Seminar (Jingyan)</term> <note>These were formal lectures where scholars explained Confucian classics and history to the Emperor.</note> twice a month. After holding this position for five years, he retired once more.

Li Hang (courtesy name <term>Taichu</term> <note>A "courtesy name" was a secondary name given at adulthood, used by peers to show respect.</note>) was a native of Feixiang in Luozhou. In the second year of the Chunhua era <note>991 CE</note>, while presiding over the Personnel Bureau of the Ministry of Civil Appointments, he attended a private imperial banquet.

Emperor Taizong <note>The second emperor of the Song Dynasty, who reigned from 976 to 997 CE.</note> followed him with his gaze as he departed and remarked, "His bearing is so composed and dignified; he is truly a man of noble character."

During the **Chanyuan Campaign** <note>A pivotal military conflict between the Song Dynasty and the Khitan Liao Empire in 1004–1005 CE.</note>, Li Hang was left in charge of the capital city. Under his watch, the populace remained solemn and disciplined, maintaining perfect order. 

He was promoted through several high offices, eventually serving as the <term>Vice Director of the Chancellery</term> and the <term>Right Vice Director of the Department of State Affairs</term>. He passed away in the first year of the Jingde era <note>1004 CE</note> at the age of fifty-eight. 

Upon his death, the Emperor <note>Emperor Zhenzong</note> personally visited to mourn him twice, weeping for his loss, and suspended all imperial court sessions for five days in his honor. He was posthumously awarded the titles of <term>Grand Commandant</term> and <term>Secretariat Director</term>, and granted the posthumous name <term>Wenjing</term> <note>original: "文靖"; a high honor meaning "Cultured and Serene."</note>.

**Zhang Yong**, whose courtesy name was Fuzhi and who styled himself **Guaiya** <note>original: 乖崖 (Guaiya), literally "The Eccentric Precipice"</note>, was a native of Juancheng. At the beginning of the **Dazhong Xiangfu era** <note>1008–1016 CE, a period of the Northern Song Dynasty</note>, he presented bold and opposing arguments against the treacherous ministers **Ding Wei** and **Wang Qinruo**. As a result, he was sent out from the capital to serve as the Governor of Chen Prefecture.

Not long after, he passed away at the age of seventy. He was posthumously conferred the title of **Vice Director of the Left** <term>Zuo Pushe: a high-ranking executive position within the Department of State Affairs</term> and given the posthumous name **Zhongding** <note>original: 忠定 (Zhongding), meaning "Loyal and Settled"</note>. 

**Emperor Zhenzong** <note>the third emperor of the Song Dynasty</note> once praised his talents, remarking that Zhang possessed the caliber of a great military commander; however, due to Zhang's persistent illness, the Emperor was unable to make full use of his abilities in service to the state. Zhang once remarked of himself:

->"*Guai* means to run contrary to the crowd, and *Ya* means a steep cliff that offers no foothold for worldly things."<- 

For this reason, he styled himself with the name **Guaiya**.

Zhen Xishan—whose given name was **Dexiu** and courtesy name was **Xiyuan**—was a native of Pucheng in Min <note>Min is a historical name for modern-day Fujian Province</note>. His original surname was Shen <note>original: 慎</note>, but he changed it to avoid the taboo personal name of Emperor Xiaozong <note>In imperial China, it was a sign of respect—and often a legal requirement—to avoid using characters that appeared in the Emperor's name</note>. 

He presented his work, *The Extended Meaning of the Great Learning* <term>Daxue Yanyi: A seminal text in Neo-Confucian political philosophy intended to guide the Emperor's moral and political education</term>, to the throne, and His Majesty highly praised it. When his residence at Yueshan was completed, he named his personal study "Learning the Changes" <note>Referring to the *I Ching* or *Book of Changes*, one of the oldest and most profound Chinese classics</note>. 

A poem written for the Spring Festival stated:
->Sitting to gaze upon the colors of the Wu and Yue mountains;<-
->Silently communing with the eternal hearts of Fu Xi and King Wen.<-
<note>Fu Xi and King Wen are the legendary figures credited with creating the trigrams and hexagrams of the *Book of Changes*.</note>

Having attained the rank of *Jinshi* <term>The highest degree in the imperial examination system</term>, he served as an **Academician of the Palace of Assisting Governance**, **Superintendent of the Longevity Observatory**, and concurrently held the position of **Imperial Reader** <note>A prestigious role involving the selection and explanation of classical texts for the Emperor's education</note>.

**Liu Yunzhuang**, whose given name was **Ningzhi**, was a native of Yingshang. He resigned from his official post to return home and later moved his residence to **Mount Lu** <note>One of China's most sacred mountains, historically a haven for scholars, poets, and monks seeking seclusion.</note>. 

Lord **Ouyang** <note>Referring to Ouyang Xiu (1007–1072), a preeminent statesman, historian, and poet of the Song Dynasty.</note> was a <term>year-mate <note>Those who passed the highest level of the imperial civil service examinations in the same year; this created a significant social and political bond in traditional China.</note></term> of his and held Liu’s moral conduct and integrity in high regard. Ouyang composed the poem *The Heights of Mount Lu* to praise him, which contains the line: "Few men possess a heroic integrity like yours." 

Later, Lord **Zhu** <note>Referring to the famous Neo-Confucian philosopher Zhu Xi (1130–1200).</note>, while serving as the Governor of Nankang, authored the *Record of the Pavilion of Heroic Integrity* to honor Liu's memory.

**Jia Huangzhong** was a man of great learning. His mother, Lady Xue, was a woman of great virtue. ### **Li Fang**
**Li Fang**, whose courtesy name was Mingyuan, served during the Taiping Xingguo era <note>976–984</note>. When the Emperor sought his advice on appointments, if a candidate's talent was insufficient, Fang would refuse them with a stern countenance, even if they were powerful. However, once a person was promoted based on merit, he would treat them with a warm face and gentle words, showing them great kindness. He never showed personal favoritism in this way.

### **Qian Ruoshui**
**Qian Ruoshui**, whose courtesy name was Chengyou, was brilliant from childhood and skilled in composition. Early in the Chunhua era, he was promoted to the History Institute. When Emperor Zhenzong took the throne, Ruoshui accompanied the imperial procession to Daming and submitted strategies for resisting enemies and stabilizing the borders. His plans were so sophisticated that even veteran generals could not surpass them. The Emperor said to those around him: "Among the Confucian officials, Ruoshui is the one who truly understands military affairs."

### **Chen Shu**
**Chen Shu**, whose courtesy name was Zhongyan, possessed a meticulous mind for planning. He worked to eliminate long-standing corrupt practices and served for a long time as the Commissioner of the Three Commissions <term>Three Commissions: The central agency in charge of the national budget, salt and iron monopolies, and the census</term>. Emperor Zhenzong ordered him to prepare a detailed report on the internal and external state of finances and grain. Shu took a long time to submit it, as he refused to rush the accuracy of the data. The Emperor praised his diligence. He was well-versed in history and classical precedents.

## **Han Shizhong**
**Han Shizhong** initially served as a Commander <note>original: "統制" (Tongzhi)</note>. His courage surpassed that of the three armies, and he achieved dozens of great merits. He was appointed Junior Guardian <note>original: "少保" (Shaobao), a high-ranking honorary title</note>. The Emperor personally inscribed the words "Loyal and Brave" <note>original: "忠勇" (Zhongyong)</note> on his banners. He defeated the Jin troops at Zhenjiang, nearly capturing the prince Wuzhu. He defeated the Jin again at Yizhen, capturing their entire vanguard and elite cavalry. The "Fierce Formation Axe" and the "Enemy-Overcoming Bow" <note>original: "悍陣斧" and "尅敵弓"</note> were techniques left behind by him. Once the peace negotiations were concluded, he retired and lived behind closed doors, finding solace in the teachings of Buddhism <note>original: "釋教" (Shijiao)</note>. He was enfeoffed as the Prince of Qi, with the posthumous title "Loyal."

## **Liu Qi**
**Liu Qi** served as the Regent of the Eastern Capital <note>Kaifeng</note>. When the invading army of 600,000 attacked Shunchang, they deployed elite troops in three wings, including the "Linked Horses" <note>original: "拐子馬" (Guaizima), a specialized heavy cavalry formation where horses were chained together</note> known as the Ever-Victorious Army. **Liu Qi** used short-range weapons to strike back with great vigor. The deeds of **Yue Fei** <note>China's most famous loyalist general</note> are detailed elsewhere. **Wu Jie** also defeated them soundly. When **Liu Qi** reached Chenzhou again, the invaders saw his banners from afar and retreated. He served as the Pacification Commissioner of Western Shu <note>modern Sichuan</note>; he was resolute and possessed great moral character. Under his watch, the borders were without worry. He was promoted to Opener of the Office <note>original: "開府" (Kaifu)</note> and given the posthumous title "Wu'an" (Martial Peace). His son Ziqi and his younger brother Lin followed his disciplined methods of command, and the soldiers were happy to serve under them. He governed Shu for twenty years, his reputation for both kindness and majesty being well-known. He was appointed Grand Mentor and enfeoffed as the Martial and Obedient Prince of Xin'an Prefecture. His son Yuting inherited his title.

## **Liu Ao**
**Liu Ao** was a native of Jingzhao. In his official positions, he was honest and clear-headed, and his governance was compassionate and beneficial. Whether in matters of justice or benevolence, he was so effective that wherever he went, the people would say, "Master Liu has saved my life." He reached out to aid the poor and rescue those in hardship, saving many lives. He was also meritorious in suppressing the Yi bandits. Upon his death, he was given the posthumous title "Zhongjian" (Loyal and Brief). His grandson Liu Chun suppressed the Shao bandits and was granted a temple and the title "Zhonglie" (Loyal and Heroic). His grand-nephew Liu Tao died at Zhending and was titled "Zhongxian" (Loyal and Illustrious). His son Liu Ziyu died and was titled "Zhongding" (Loyal and Settled). Ziyu’s son Liu Gong was titled "Zhongsu" (Loyal and Solemn). The world refers to them as the **Five Loyalists of the Liu Clan**.

## **Hong Hao**
**Hong Hao**, style name Guangbi. In his youth, he possessed extraordinary principles and a generous spirit, harboring ambitions to govern the four corners of the world. He was a man of great character. During the Zhenghe era of Emperor Huizong, he attained the rank of *Jinshi* <note>the "Presented Scholar" degree, the highest level of the imperial examinations</note>. When Xiuzhou suffered a great flood, Hao served as the regional governor; he opened the granaries and lowered prices to sell grain to the people, saving many lives. The Jin general Nianmohe <note>the Jurchen commander Wanyan Zonghan</note> pressured him to serve the puppet ruler Liu Yu, but Hao refused even at the risk of death. He was exiled to the desolate Cold Mountains <note>Lengshan, in modern Heilongjiang</note>. Upon his eventual return to the Emperor, the Sovereign remarked: "Your loyalty pierces the sun and moon; even Su Wu <note>a famous Han dynasty envoy who remained loyal during 19 years of captivity</note> could not surpass you." The treacherous official Qin Hui was deeply disrespectful toward him.

## **Zhang Jiucheng**
**Zhang Jiucheng**, style name Zishao. During the Shaoxing era, his responses to the palace examination were so excellent he was selected for high office. He was later stationed in Yingzhou. While serving as the Vice Minister of Justice, the judicial organs presented cases of capital punishment for review. Jiucheng discovered many who had been coerced into false confessions. When the court wished to reward him for overturning these wrongful convictions, Jiucheng declined the honors. When the Jin proposed peace negotiations, Jiucheng warned: "The enemy's nature is full of deceit; we must not fail to scrutinize them." Qin Hui grew to hate him intensely.

## **Zhao Fang**
**Zhao Fang**, style name Zhenyan. He once served as the Magistrate of Qingyang County. He famously said: "To collect taxes without causing disturbance is the true way to nurture the people within tax collection; to apply punishments without error is the true way to educate the people within the penal system." People regarded these as famous words. When the Jin invaded, he submitted a memorial advocating for war. The Jin failed many times to achieve their goals against him. Suddenly falling ill, he summoned his generals such as Hu Zaixing and urged them to work with one heart to serve the country. He sent a letter to the Chancellor discussing grand border strategies and died shortly after. His sons, Fan (style name Wuzhong) and Kui (style name Nanzhong), both inherited his noble ambitions.

## **Meng Gong**
**Meng Gong**, style name Puyu. He was the son of Meng Zongzheng. When the Jin general Wu Tianxi attempted to invade Guanghua, Gong's military achievements were heaven-sent; he took Tianxi's fortifications with a single beat of the war drums. When the Mongols <note>referred to here as the "Yuan people"</note> invaded Jiangling, Gong led his army to destroy twenty-four of their camps, repeatedly defeating the Mongol forces and recovering territory.

## **Yu Jie**
**Yu Jie**, style name Yifu. His family was poor and lived in the lower reaches of the Wan River. He harbored a great desire for military achievement and fame. He was eventually appointed as the Pacification and Dispatch Commissioner <note>a high-ranking military and civil governor</note> of Sichuan. **Jie** majorly overhauled the corrupt policies of the region and carefully selected local governors and magistrates. He recruited Ran Jin and Ran Pu to plan the fortifications of **Diaoyu Mountain** <note>a strategic fortress that famously resisted the Mongols for decades</note> and established military farming colonies in Chengdu. Through these efforts, the region of Shu <note>Sichuan</note> became wealthy and strong. **Jie** was unyielding in his discipline of the military; when he sought to execute the commander Yao Shi’an for insubordination, Shi’an resisted him. This conflict persisted until **Jie** passed away.

## **Jiang Wanli**
**Jiang Wanli**, style name Ziyuan. He was known for his great character, though his advice was not always taken by Yu Jie. When the Mongol <note>Yuan</note> troops arrived in the later years, **Wanli** dug a pond in the rear garden of Zhishan and built a pavilion over it, naming it "Still Water" <note>original: "Zhishui"</note>. At the time, no one understood the meaning of this name. When the city of Raozhou fell, he finally fulfilled his intent by drowning himself in the "Still Water" pond.

## **Wang Lixin**
**Wang Lixin** attained the rank of *Jinshi* <note>the "Presented Scholar" degree</note> in the sixth year of the Chunyou era. When Emperor Lizong saw him, he remarked to his attendants, "This man has the appearance of a great general." **Lixin** served as the Pacification and Dispatch Commissioner of Jinghu. He sent a letter to the Chancellor **Jia Sidao** <note>often criticized by historians for his corruption and for hiding the Mongol threat from the Emperor</note>, warning him that the court was too focused on "leisurely banquets and ease." **Jia Sidao** was furious at the critique. When the Mongol forces launched their massive invasion, **Lixin** realized the situation was hopeless. He sighed and said, "I was born a Song official; I shall die a Song [ghost]."

## **Chen Jing**
