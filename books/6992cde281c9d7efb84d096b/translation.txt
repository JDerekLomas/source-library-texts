### 16. Verification of the Transmission of Orders

Whenever a verbal briefing is concluded, wait until officers of all ranks—from the highest to the lowest—have finished their delivery. The **Commanding General** <note>original: 主將 *Zhujiang*</note> shall then select a single soldier from a squad to step forward and ask: "What was announced today?"

If the soldier is able to explain the general gist of the orders, then the matter is settled. However, if the soldier is entirely ignorant of the orders, the **Squad Leader** <note>original: 隊總 *Duizong*; the leader of a small unit of 10–12 men</note> shall be summoned and questioned. 

If the **Squad Leader** can explain the orders, then the **soldier** shall be punished for the crime of failing to listen and receive instructions. 

If the **Squad Leader** cannot explain the orders, then his superior, the **Platoon Leader** <note>original: 旗總 *Qizong*; responsible for several squads</note>, shall be summoned and questioned. If the **Platoon Leader** can explain the orders, then the **Squad Leader** shall be punished for his failure, and the soldier shall be exempt from prosecution, as it was the Squad Leader who failed to transmit the orders clearly <gloss>.</gloss>

If the **Platoon Leader** cannot explain the orders, then the **Centurion** <note>original: 百總 *Baizong*; an officer commanding roughly 100 men</note> shall be summoned and questioned. If the **Centurion** can explain them, then the **Platoon Leader** shall be punished. If the **Centurion** cannot explain them, then the **Company Commander** <note>original: 把總 *Bazong*; an officer in charge of 400–500 men</note> in charge of that unit shall be summoned and questioned.

This protocol of accountability continues in this manner all the way up to the **Watch Commander** <note>original: 哨將 *Shaojiang*; a high-ranking officer over a division</note>; the same principle of verification applies to all. Each time a briefing is finished, the **Watch Commander** must select one soldier from under each **Battalion Commander’s** <note>original: 千總 *Qianzong*; commanding roughly 1,000 men</note> jurisdiction to test, and each **Battalion Commander** must likewise select a soldier from under each **Company Commander** <gloss>.</gloss>

If the **battalions** <term>original: 哨 *Shao*; a tactical unit often comprising several squads</term> perform a combined drill, they are divided into four sections. When five **regiments** <term>original: 營 *Ying*; a larger military body</term> drill together, the Left and Front units march side-by-side, with their respective **Command Staff** <term>original: 中軍 *Zhongjun*; the central command element</term> positioned in the center of their columns. The Center unit marches alone, dividing itself into three sub-units. The Right and Rear units march side-by-side, also with their Command Staff in the center. 

For every additional regiment or unit added to the drill, one **cannon** <term>original: 砲 *Pao*</term> is fired, one **signal rocket** <term>original: 起火 *Qihuo*; a firework-like signal</term> is launched, and one large **unit-leading flag** <term>original: 領隊大旗 *Lingdui Daqi*</term> is displayed. 

The troops must listen for the metal instruments: the sounding of the drum signals them to stop and stand fast; the sounding of the hand-gong signals them to sit and rest. After a short interval, a cannon is fired and the **horn** <term>original: 哱囉 *Bola*; a curved brass horn or conch used for signaling</term> is blown to command them to stand up. The horn is blown again to signal them to mount their horses, and the rhythmic beating of the drums signals them to march forward.

Upon reaching the center of the **drill ground** <term>original: 教場 *Jiaochang*</term>, the **scouts** <term>original: 塘馬 *Tangma*; courier or reconnaissance riders who maintained communication lines</term> holding small yellow flags circle the formation on horseback. The Command Staff then fires one cannon and blows the **formation-ordering trumpets** <term>original: 擺隊伍喇叭 *Baiduiwu Laba*</term>.

## Drill Practice for Chariots and Vehicles, Part 6

When the **battalions** <term>original: 哨 *Shao*</term> and the **Command Staff** <term>original: 中軍 *Zhongjun*</term> combine, they form the outer camp. When four battalions set up camp, the Left and Right divisions of each unit form the outer layer, while their respective Command Staffs form the **inner layer** <term>original: 子層 *Ziceng*; literally "child-layer," referring to a nested defensive circle</term>. 

If five battalions set up camp, the outer four units follow the same layout as the four-battalion model to form the exterior. The inner circle consists of two layers: the command staff forms the second layer, and the **Central Battalion** <term>original: 中哨 *Zhongshao*</term> forms the third, innermost layer. This formation constitutes a camp of fifteen thousand soldiers and is known as a **"Small City"** <term>original: 小城 *Xiaocheng*</term>.

Upon the striking of the gong, all soldiers dismount. The troops of the outer camp must tether their horses together according to the established drill. Simultaneously, **Crouching Tiger cannons** <term>original: 虎蹲礮 *Hudunpao*; a light, mobile mortar stabilized by support legs, shaped like a crouching tiger</term> and other artillery pieces are positioned in the open spaces in front of the unit flags. 

The troops must listen for the rhythmic beating of the drums. Those carrying **firearms** <term>original: 火器 *Huoqi*</term> shall march forward on foot, stopping approximately ten paces in front of the horses. When the hand-gong sounds, the drumming ceases. 

The gunners must be organized in advance: within every **five-man squad** <term>original: 伍 *Wu*</term>, three men are designated as a **firing section** <term>original: 班 *Ban*</term>. The second squad becomes the second section, and the third squad becomes the third section. 

When it is estimated that the enemy has reached a distance of fifty paces <note>approximately 75–80 meters</note>, the Command Staff fires a single signal cannon. The **trumpets** <term>original: 喇叭 *Laba*</term> then sound a "sky-piercing" blast. As soon as the trumpet blast concludes, the first squad fires its cannons in unison. From this point forward, the Command Staff does not fire any more signal cannons, and the trumpets sound the high blast again <note>this signal likely prompts the next squad in the rotation to fire</note>.

Once the resonance of the **Heavenly Startle** signal <term>original: 天驚 *Tianjing*; a specific, sharp trumpet blast used for signaling</term> concludes, the second **five-man squad** <term>original: 伍 *Wu*</term> shall fire their cannons in unison. When the trumpet blows the **Heavenly Startle** signal again and the sound ends, the third squad shall fire together. Each time the trumpet is heard blowing a single **Heavenly Startle** note, the cannons are to be fired in rotation, one after another. Those who have fired must reload with haste; those who have finished reloading shall fire again. 

However, if **firearms** <term>original: 銃 *Chong*; referring here to hand-cannons or matchlocks</term> are few, or if the reloading cannot keep pace with the rotation, or if the enemy's main force has already drawn near, a single **signal rocket** <term>original: 起火 *Qihuo*</term> must be launched urgently. 

Upon seeing the signal rocket, all **fire-arrows** <term>original: 火箭 *Huojian*; rockets propelled by gunpowder</term> and **Crouching Tiger cannons** <term>original: 虎蹲砲 *Hudunpao*</term> are to be fired in rotation as a single body. The gunners must continue to listen for the **Heavenly Startle** signal, maintaining their rotational fire without pause. 

If the enemy is not repelled and continues to advance until they reach the edge of the camp, the drums shall be struck rhythmically. At this signal, the **archers** <note>These soldiers acted as dual-purpose units, carrying both projectile and melee weapons</note> shall place their **spears, staffs, and rakes** on the ground, pinning them firmly with their feet. In this position, they shall loose arrows like falling rain. 

When the drumming stops and then begins again, the outer layer of infantry shall return their bows and arrows to their bags. They shall then take up their melee weapons and, with heads lowered, charge forward. To the sound of rolling drums and the **Heavenly Startle** signal, they shall raise a great shout and engage the enemy in close combat. After advancing no more than several dozen paces, the enemy's momentum will typically break into a retreat. At this point, the **gong is sounded** <term>original: 鳴金 *Mingjin*; a signal to cease engagement or retreat</term> to stop the pursuit. Then, the drums are rolled again for the **battalions**...

When the **standard flag** <term>original: 標旗 *Biaoqi*; the commander's personal flag used to direct movements</term> is deployed with haste, the **cavalry** <term>original: 馬兵 *Mabing*</term> from the central division shall move out to engage. They are to shoot their bows while advancing, continuing until they have passed in front of the **infantry** <term>original: 步兵 *Bubing*</term>.

Soldiers wielding the **double-bladed staff** <term>original: 夾刀棍 *Jiadaogun*; a staff weapon with blades on both ends</term> must focus their strikes: they shall only stab at the enemy’s face or the belly of the horse. Those carrying **great clubs** shall strike only at the horse's head. The objective is not to capture a live horse; if you strike the horse down, there is no need to worry about the enemy rider not being killed. <note>In this period, horses were highly valued as spoils of war. This instruction explicitly commands soldiers to prioritize killing the animal to ensure the rider is neutralized, rather than risking the formation to capture the mount.</note> Every soldier must focus solely on striking and hacking forward; they are strictly forbidden from stopping to **behead the enemy** <note>Ming dynasty military rewards were often based on the number of enemy heads presented. This command ensures soldiers do not break formation or stop the momentum of the charge to collect trophies for personal merit.</note>. The goal is simply to force the enemy into a total retreat.

When the drums stop, listen for the sound of the **signal horn** <term>original: 率𨦀 *Shuaihua*; a specific brass horn used for assembly</term>. At this sound, every man must recognize his own **patrol and squad** <term>original: 哨隊 *Shaodui*</term> to regroup as one. Do not rely blindly on the signal horn if you are separated from your unit.

During the **drill** <term>original: 操 *Cao*; military exercises</term>, it is not necessary to have the units take turns charging through a single gate. Instead, they should perform a **simultaneous charge** <term>original: 齊衝 *Qichong*</term> through all four gates. This specific routine is an indispensable part of training.

Once the drill is completed according to the standing orders, listen for a single cannon shot. This will be followed by the beating of drums. The **Central Command** <term>original: 中軍 *Zhongjun*; the commander's headquarters</term> shall then raise the Yellow and Black flags and open the camp gates.

Simultaneously, dispatch four **signal officers** <term>original: 旗牌官 *Qipaiguan*; officers who carry the commander's authority via signal plaques</term>—one to each gate—to count and release the troops and horses as they are sent out. Once this dispatch is finished, immediately send out the **logistics soldiers** <term>original: 火兵 *Huobing*; literally 'fire soldiers' tasked with cooking and labor</term> to gather fuel and water, and the central division to water their horses. When the release of these men is completed, each officer must return to Central Command to report the exact number of men and horses that were let out, which must be recorded in writing. <note>This rigorous accounting was essential to prevent desertion and ensure all personnel were accounted for during the chaotic transition from drill to camp life.</note>

When the watering of the horses is nearly finished, sound the signal horn once. The Yellow and Black flags should be waved together (a signal known as **磨旗** <term>original: *Moqi*; waving flags in a circular motion</term>). Upon this signal, all cavalry and the fuel and water gathering parties shall return to camp. Put away the Yellow flag, beat the drums again, and wave the Black flag.

The Left Division of the cavalry shall then depart the camp to water their horses; the counting of their exit and reentry must be handled exactly as before. Wave the Black flag and sound the horn to recall them. Follow the same set of signals to release the Right Division. Once all divisions have finished watering their horses, lower the flags. A **grand fanfare** <term>original: 大吹打 *Dachuidia*; ceremonial military music</term> shall be played, and the camp gates opened. Every unit must then proceed to properly set their cauldrons and light their fires to cook. <note>The phrase "truly settle the cauldrons" implies a shift from the simulated urgency of the drill to the necessary routine of camp sustenance.</note>

## Midday Routine and Post-Drill Procedures

It is precisely at the **Hour of the Horse** <term>original: 午時 *Wushi*; 11:00 AM – 1:00 PM</term> that the actual cooking of meals should take place. During this time, the **signal flute** <term>original: 號笛 *Haodi*</term> shall be played while everyone awaits the distribution of orders. 

At this time, those who have previously met the standards in battle formations or who have strictly followed their commands are to be universally rewarded and encouraged, and their assignments finalized. If there is a need to alter any existing orders or signals, these changes must be clearly announced and explained to the entire assembly.

The troops then disperse and return to their stations. A report is made to sound the gong to **remove armor** <term>original: 解甲 *Jiejia*</term>. Once the gong sounds, everyone removes their armor. 

After the meal has been distributed and finished, a **Central Command officer** <term>original: 中軍官 *Zhongjunguan*</term> is dispatched to the general’s platform to report that the camp is being struck to return to the garrison. Once the **official command** <term>original: 鈞旨 *Junzhi*; an honorific for a superior's order</term> is received, three cannons are fired. 

First, the signal is sounded. Each soldier takes up his equipment and the **horns** <term>original: 哱囉 *Bolo*; a conical brass trumpet</term> are blown for the men to stand. The horns are blown again for the men to mount their horses. Three large cannons are fired, followed by three **battle cries** <term>original: 呐喊 *Nahan*</term>. 

Three large flags—Yellow for the Center, White for the Right, and Blue for the Left—are erected. The army moves forward to muster the men and horses, and the scouts are organized into three routes. 

The gong sounds and the flags are lowered, followed by one more cannon shot. The Central Command then raises the summoning flag, and the troops listen for the **grand fanfare** <term>original: 大吹打 *Dachuidia*; a ceremonial musical performance</term> to signal the return to camp. When the gong sounds again, the music stops; the gong is struck once for the men to dismount, and struck a second time for them to sit and rest. <note>This sequence illustrates the high level of discipline required, where even the acts of dismounting and sitting were governed by rhythmic signals to prevent chaos.</note>

Whether stationed for rest in large groups such as ten or five **battalions** <term>original: 營 *Ying*</term>, or in smaller units like a **company** <term>original: 局 *Ju*</term>, a **platoon** <term>original: 宗 *Zong*</term>, or down to a **five-man squad** <term>original: 伍 *Wu*</term>, these three individuals <note>likely referring to the triad of instructors mentioned in the gloss</note> are to be found at the **drill ground** <term>original: 教塲 *Jiaochang*</term> during times of peace. In times of emergency, they are positioned at the battle lines. Both mock combat and actual warfare must follow these regulations for attacking, advancing, and halting; not a single word of these instructions is to be altered.

**Second**, be vigilant against startled horses. When cannons and other firearms are discharged, if the horses belonging to officers or soldiers are not properly tied or held, and this leads to chaos in the camp or horses bolting, the soldier responsible and the horse-tenders shall be punished for their crimes.

**Third**, regarding the training of **wagon soldiers** <term>original: 操車兵 *Caochebing*</term>: during ordinary times, the new recruits, the **wagon master** <term>original: 車正 *Chezheng*</term>, and the **steersman** <term>original: 舵工 *Duogong*</term> must first practice pushing and transporting the wagons up and down mountain slopes until they can maneuver them with speed and proficiency. 

Simultaneously, a **Folanggi cannon** <term>original: 伏郎機 *Fulangji*; a breech-loading swivel gun</term> instructor shall be appointed. One cannon is to be assigned to each wagon, and the **Folanggi gunners** <term>original: 佛郎機手 *Folangjishou*</term> must practice firing until they are expert. At the same time, a **matchlock** <term>original: 鳥銃 *Niaochong*; literally "bird-gun"</term> instructor shall be appointed to ensure the men study and master the matchlock musket until they are proficient. Additionally, small **fire-arrows** <term>original: 火箭 *Huojian*</term> without metal heads should be manufactured, and an instructor appointed to train the men in their use.

### Setting the Markers

To set the markers, first determine how many companies <term>original: 司 *si* — a military unit roughly equivalent to a company</term> are in the army and how many squads <term>original: 隊 *dui*</term> will make up the outer ramparts. From the **Central Army** command, the formation splits into two directions, with each side assigned a specific number of paces. The inner walls <term>original: 子壁 *zibi* — literally "child walls," secondary defensive perimeters within the main camp</term> follow the same measurement logic. 

The Great Banners of the four directions are positioned as markers for the gates, while the High Signal Flags <term>original: 高招 *gaozhao* — tall banners used for long-distance signaling</term> of the four directions are positioned as the internal markers. Those tasked with holding the flags and setting the markers must listen for the sounding of the gong.

Those carrying the **Five-Direction Flags** <note>These represent the cardinal directions and the center, as established on the previous page.</note> must first gather at the very center of the **Central Army**. They then march straight out in several rows; once they have reached the required number of paces, they stand still to serve as the markers for the **Four Cardinal Directions** <term>original: 四正 *sizheng* — the North, South, East, and West positions</term>.

Each of the flags marking the four corners, along with the Five-Direction Flags, proceeds together from beneath the Main Gate flag. They move in parallel to the left and right; once the required number of paces is reached, they stand still to serve as the markers for the **Four Corners**.

### On Ambush Troops

The nature of the **Japanese pirates** <note>original: 倭夷 *Woyi*. A historical term for the *Wokou*, the "dwarf pirates" who raided the Chinese coast during the Ming Dynasty.</note> is such that each man fights for himself. They are skilled at **flanking our rear**, and even when they suffer a great defeat, they will set ambushes as they flee. Even just one or two men will hide themselves in a foot of wood or a bowl-sized hollow <note>A metaphorical description suggesting the enemy's ability to utilize even the smallest bit of cover.</note> to wait for us. 

Our troops have often fallen into their traps. During the **Campaign of 1561** <note>original: 辛酉之役 *Xinyou zhi yi*. The Xinyou year of the Ming Jiajing era. This was a pivotal campaign where Qi Jiguang's "Qi Family Army" achieved famous victories in Zhejiang province.</note>, we achieved ten victories in a single month, yet our soldiers' losses did not even reach six or seven men. 

Commentators at the time claimed this was not because our troops were skillful, but because the rebels were clumsy. They argued that these particular pirates were not as adept at using ambushes as others. They little realized that the methods I mentioned previously had already been taught and practiced thoroughly during peacetime. 

Therefore, in victories such as the **Battle of Huajie** <note>A significant battle in 1561 where Qi Jiguang's forces decisively defeated a pirate landing force.</note>, where we pursued the enemy for ten *li* <note>Approximately 5 kilometers or 3 miles.</note> and secured a total victory, it was not that the rebels had failed to set ambushes. It was because I have **methods for searching and holding**, which rendered their ambushes useless. 

The method is as follows: when the rebels lose a battle and begin to retreat, and our soldiers move up to pursue them, whenever they encounter woods, houses, a stream crossing, or a corner <gloss>location</gloss>, we must act. Depending on the size of the woods, the garden walls, or the bends in the path, we must **immediately leave behind one squad or one platoon** <term>squad/platoon (隊/哨): A *dui* (squad) was the basic 12-man unit of Qi’s army; a *shao* (platoon) was a larger tactical unit composed of multiple squads.</term> to secure the area.
