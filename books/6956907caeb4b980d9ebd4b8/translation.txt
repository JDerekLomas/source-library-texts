# The PREFACE.

<margin>The importance of proving that virtue is the agent's greatest happiness.</margin>
It is of more importance in morals to prove what we call the *reality* of *virtue*—or that it is the *surest happiness* of the *person acting*—than one would at first imagine.

In reflecting upon our external *senses*, we plainly see that our *perceptions* of *pleasure* or *pain* do not depend *directly* on our *will*. *Objects* do not please us simply because we want them to. The presence of some *objects* necessarily pleases us, and the presence of others just as necessarily displeases us. Nor can we, by our *will*, procure *pleasure* or avoid *pain* in any other way than by seeking the former kind of *objects* and avoiding the latter. By the very frame of our nature, the one is made the occasion of *delight*, and the other of *dissatisfaction*.

The same *observation* holds true for all our other *pleasures* and *pains*; for there are many other sorts of *objects* which please or displease us as necessarily as physical *objects* do when they act upon our *organs of sense* <note>original: "Organs of Sense"; referring to the eyes, ears, nose, etc.</note>. There is scarcely any *object* that our *minds* are occupied with which is not constituted as the necessary cause of some *pleasure* or *pain*. Thus, we find ourselves pleased with a regular *form*, a piece of *architecture* or *painting*, a *musical composition* <note>original: "Composition of Notes"</note>, a mathematical *theorem*, an *action*, an *affection* <note>original: "Affection"; here meaning a person's disposition or emotional state</note>, or a *character*.

<summary>The author argues that pleasure and pain are not chosen by the will but are necessary responses dictated by human nature, applying this logic to both physical sensations and the appreciation of art, logic, and morality.</summary>

The proper occasions for perception through our external senses occur to us as soon as we come into the world; for this reason, perhaps, we easily consider these senses to be natural. However, the objects of the superior senses—those of beauty and virtue—generally do not appear so early. It is probably some time before children reflect, or at least let us know that they reflect, upon proportion and similarity, or upon affections, characters, and temperaments. It also takes time for them to recognize the external actions which are evidence of these internal qualities. Because of this delay, we imagine that their sense of beauty and their moral sentiments regarding actions must be entirely due to instruction and education. In reality, it is just as easy to conceive how a character or a temperament—as soon as they are observed—may be established by NATURE as a necessary cause of pleasure or an object of approval, just like a taste or a sound; even though it takes some time before these objects are presented to our observation.
