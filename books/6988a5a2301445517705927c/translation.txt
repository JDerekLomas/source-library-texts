After it came to pass at one time in Rome that a great discord arose between the Council <note>The Roman Senate</note> and the Common People, to such an extent that the Commons marched out of the city against the Council, it was found that they could not be calmed in any other way unless the Council agreed that they might elect several Tribunes <note>German: *Zunfftmeister*, literally "guild-masters," an early modern term the author uses to explain the Roman *Tribuni plebis* to his readers</note> from among the Commons. Likewise, they were to keep a diligent watch over the freedom of the Commons. Accordingly, they were neither permitted to wear purple clothing nor to have any attendants with them; however, they were not forbidden from keeping a lackey or a messenger.

On the other hand, they possessed this liberty: even at a time when a Dictator was elected and all other offices ceased and had to stand still with their governance—so that they were not permitted to use their jurisdiction, as was mentioned above in the chapter on the Dictator—the Tribunes remained in place, one way or another, and exercised their office. However, they did not have the power to summon anyone or to hear and decide the cases of the parties involved; rather, they alone held the authority to take people into custody, regardless of whether they were Mayors, Judges, or Censors.
