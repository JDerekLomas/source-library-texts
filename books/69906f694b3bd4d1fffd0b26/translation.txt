<margin>Place the regulars <term>regular|Fixed numbers assigned to months to assist in calendar calculations.</term> above; add the concurrent of whatever year you wish to them. If you divide the sum by 7, the remainder will be the letter indicating which day of the week the Kalends <term>Kalends|The first day of the month in the Roman calendar.</term> of April will be. This is the place of the concurrent, because for whichever day of the week it results in... <unclear>ies</unclear>... <unclear>ex</unclear>plonit... weeks... will be in that same year.</margin>

In truth, all months have eight Ides <term>Ides|A division of the Roman month, usually falling on the 13th or 15th.</term>. January, March, May, July, August, and October have 6 Kalends [before the Nones]. December has 4 Kalends. They have 18 Kalends. They have 19 Kalends after the Ides, and after the Ides, and days 25 ones. 25 one. <note>The scribe is listing the number of days between major calendar markers; the phrasing is shorthand and somewhat repetitive.</note>

Thus begins the method of calculation. April. The year is February for the epact. And November. The Kalends have 15. December. 4 Kalends. 18 Kalends after the Ides. And 28 days. And thirty days. If it is a leap year <note>original: "bissextus"</note>, 29.

Join the regulars of the individual months with the epacts of whatever year. And if it is 25, that is the age of the moon. In the 6th Kalends of April, to find the epacts from the moon: however many days the age of the moon is there, it will have that many epacts in that year.

# On the Heavenly Bodies

**S**ince the Sun is of a fiery nature, it grows hotter through its perpetual motion, by which it circles the globe in the space of one day and night. God established different courses, locations, and times for the Sun, lest its daily heat consume everything if it always lingered in one place. If the Sun remained fixed in the sky, all days and nights would be equal. The Sun, however, holds its path through the midday; when it has come to its setting in the west, it travels through unknown paths beneath the earth and returns again to the east. 

Poets claim that the Sun is an eye, and that it is larger than the earth. Among the ancients, the Sun is figuratively depicted as a boy, because it rises daily and is born with new light. He is shown having a **quadriga** <term>quadriga: a chariot drawn by four horses, often symbolizing the four seasons or the four parts of the day</term> of horses and the outpouring of a **biga** <term>biga: a two-horse chariot</term> when he is driven with a rapid course through the ether. It is a fable that the Sun was once called Apollo or Titan <note>original: "titana"</note>. Many have written about his son, Phaethon, who set the lands on fire while recklessly driving his father's chariot.

**L**una, the Moon, is so named because she receives her light from the Sun and reflects what she has received. She is depicted as a maiden and has a **biga** drawn by oxen; for this reason, she moves in an oblique course, so that the Sun does not fall into the center of the earth and frequently suffer an **eclipse** <term>eclipse: the obscuring of one celestial body by another; here, the author explains the moon's tilted orbit</term>. It has been concluded by the wise that the Moon is the six-hundredth part of her own entire orbit, and that the orbit itself is a hundred times larger than the earth <note>original: "tellure"</note>.

### The Nature of the Rainbow

When the heat of the sun raises moisture, it evaporates like a damp smoke: the water in the cloud, as if in a glass vessel, reflects the splendor of the sun. ->**The Donestre**<-

There is an island in the Red Sea <note>original: "rubro mari"</note> where there is a race of people who among us are called the **Donestre**. From the head down to the navel, they are changed from the human form <note>The accompanying miniature often depicts them with the manes or heads of lions.</note>, but for the rest of the body, they exist in a human likeness. They speak the languages of various nations. 

When they see a person of a foreign race, they call out to them in their own language and inquire after the names of their parents and relatives with flattering speech, so that they might deceive and capture them. When they have seized them, they kill and eat them; and afterwards, they take up the head of the man they have eaten and weep over it <note>This "mourning" after a meal is a recurring motif in medieval monster lore, suggesting a deceptive or demonic nature.</note>.

->**The Panotii**<-

Likewise, toward the east, men are born who are fifteen feet tall and ten feet wide. They have large heads and ears like winnowing fans <term>vannum</term> <note>A large, flat basket used for tossing grain into the air to separate it from the chaff.</note>. At night, they lie upon one of these ears and cover themselves with the other; they shelter themselves with these ears. Moreover, they have light and white bodies, almost like milk.
