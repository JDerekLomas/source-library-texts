# Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms: Palace and Housing, Volume 2
<note>original: *Sancai Tuhui: Gongshi Er Juan*. The *Sancai Tuhui* is a famous Ming Dynasty encyclopedia. This section focuses on "Palace and Housing" (*Gongshi*), specifically ritual architecture.</note>

**King Tai** <note>original: *Da Wang*. The posthumous title of Gugong Danfu, the grandfather of King Wen and a key ancestral figure of the Zhou Dynasty.</note> moved to the lands of **Qi** <note>Mount Qi, the ancestral heartland of the Zhou people.</note>, where he surveyed the terrain to build residences and ancestral temples. He established the **Gao Gate** and the **Ying Gate**, and raised the **Great Altar of the Earth**. <note>original: *Zhongtu*. A massive mound of earth representing the god of the soil and the foundation of the state’s territory.</note>

**Gugong Danfu** was later posthumously honored with the title of **King Tai**. The outer gate of the King's city is called the **Gao Gate**; the King’s main gate of audience is called the **Ying Gate**. During the time of King Tai, a formal system of ritual regulations had not yet been established, yet he constructed these two gates in this specific manner. Once the **Zhou** dynasty came to rule all under heaven, these structures were revered as the exclusive gates of the **Son of Heaven** <note>original: *Tianzi*. The supreme ruler, believed to be the intermediary between Heaven and Earth.</note>, and the **Feudal Lords** <note>original: *Zhuhou*. The regional rulers and vassals of the King.</note> were not permitted to build them.

<summary>This page explains the historical origins of the Gao and Ying Gates, tracing them back to King Tai's settlement at Mount Qi. It notes that while these gates were originally practical structures, they later became exclusive symbols of imperial authority under the Zhou ritual system.</summary>

# Commentary on the Ritual System of Temples

The consensus among various Confucian scholars states that once King Wu <note>The first king of the Zhou Dynasty (r. 1046–1043 BCE), who overthrew the Shang.</note> had gained control of the world, there were initially only **five temples**; however, by including the two "Great Halls" <term>original: *Shishi*. Eternal shrines dedicated to ancestors of immense merit, which were intended to remain standing forever rather than being dismantled as generations passed.</term> of King Wen and King Wu, the total was brought to **seven temples**. 

The theory of Liu Xin <note>A prominent Han Dynasty scholar and imperial librarian (c. 50 BCE – 23 CE) known for his work on ancient rituals.</note>, however, suggests that as soon as King Wu possessed the world, he immediately added two new temples to establish a formal set of seven, and that the Great Halls of King Wen and King Wu were distinct from this count. 

Master Zhu <note>Zhu Xi (1130–1200), the preeminent Neo-Confucian philosopher of the Song Dynasty whose interpretations became the standard for the imperial examinations.</note> considered this line of reasoning to be the most logical. 

In my humble opinion, the system of seven generations of temples was already established as early as the **Shang Dynasty** <note>The dynasty preceding the Zhou, roughly 1600–1046 BCE.</note>; thus, Liu Xin’s theory is truly the superior one.

# The Nine Temples of Zhou

This Zhou system of **Nine Temples** was established because King Wen and King Wu, as close kin <note>Specifically, the father and son who founded the dynasty</note> of the lineage, possessed such exceptional merit and virtue that they were designated as "Ancestors of Merit" <term>Zong (宗): A title given to ancestors with significant achievements, ensuring their shrines are never removed from the active ritual cycle.</term> who could never be removed from the ritual succession. 

For this reason, separate **Eternal Chambers** <term>Shishi (世室): Literally "World Room" or "Generational Chamber," these are shrines designated to stand forever, exempt from the standard rule where older ancestral shrines are dismantled to make room for newer generations.</term> were established for them. These shrines remain unmoved for a hundred generations, sharing the same permanent status as the Great Ancestor. "Eternal Chamber" is the name given to a shrine that is **not destroyed** <note>original: "buhui" (不毀), meaning a temple that is never dismantled or its lineage extinguished</note>. 

From this point forward, those of the **Mu rank** <note>The ritual position for odd-numbered generations, located on the right</note> whose shrines were eventually retired were housed within the **Eternal Chamber of King Wen**, while those of the **Zhao rank** <note>The ritual position for even-numbered generations, located on the left</note> whose shrines were retired were housed within the **Eternal Chamber of King Wu**.

# Commentary on the Five Schools of the Son of Heaven

Mr. Lu Dian <note>Lu Dian (1042–1102) was a prominent scholar and ritualist of the Northern Song Dynasty, known for his commentaries on the Classics.</note> states that according to the *Book of Rites*, the Son of Heaven established four schools. In truth, the Son of Heaven established four schools plus the central school, totaling five, all constructed together in a single location. 

The people of the Zhou Dynasty placed the **Biyong Academy** <term>Biyong (辟雍): The central imperial academy, defined by its circular moat. It served as the primary site for the Emperor's own rituals and learning.</term> at the very center of this complex. 

*   To its south was the **Chengjun** <note>The "Hall of Harmony."</note>.
*   To its north was the **Shangxiang** <note>The "Higher School."</note>.
*   To its east was the **Dongxu** <note>The "Eastern Study."</note>.
*   To its west was the **Guzong** <note>The "Ancestral Hall of Music."</note>.

The distribution of learning was specialized according to these locations: 
Those who studied the **Rites** attended the *Guzong*. 
Those who studied the **Odes** <note>The *Classic of Poetry* or *Shijing*.</note> attended the *Shangxiang*. 
Those who studied the **dances of shields and axes** <note>Martial dances (gan-ge).</note> or **feathers and flutes** <note>Civilian or ritual dances (yu-yue).</note> attended the *Dongxu*. 
Those who studied the **virtues, language, and dances of music** attended the *Chengjun*. 

The central **Biyong** was reserved exclusively for the Son of Heaven. It was here that he would honor his teachers, inquire about the Way, and provide for the **Three Elders and Five Seniors** <term>Three Elders and Five Seniors (三老五更): These were retired, virtuous officials whom the Emperor treated as honorary elders to demonstrate his humility and respect for age and wisdom.</term>. It was also the site for ceremonial military rites, such as the departure of the army or the reporting of a successful campaign. 

When the Son of Heaven entered the Great Academy, the students and teachers from the surrounding four schools would gather along the banks of the circular moat to observe him. This arrangement—the ruler at the center of a ring of water—is precisely what is meant by the term **Biyong** <note>The name suggests a jade ring (pi) and the encircling water (yong).</note>. Broadly speaking, the four surrounding schools are also considered part of the **Great Academy** <note>original: 大學 (Daxue).</note>.

# The Biyong

# The Altar to the First Sericulturalist

The **Altar to the First Sericulturalist** <note>original: 先蠶 (Xiān Cán)</note>. The term "First Sericulturalist" is used in the same way as "First Vintner" or "First Cook"—it signifies a sacrifice to the original founder of a craft. An "Altar" <note>original: 壇 (tán)</note> refers to a mound of earth constructed specifically as a site for ritual sacrifice.

The primary consort of the Yellow Emperor, the **Lady of the Xiling Clan**, was the first to practice sericulture; thus, she is the First Sericulturalist. <gloss>Note: The primary consort of the Yellow Emperor, the Lady of the Xiling Clan, was named **Leizu**. She was the first to encourage the labor of silkworm cultivation. When the moon reached the Great Fire <note>The star Antares; its position in the sky signaled the traditional time to begin silkworm rearing.</note>, she washed the silkworm seeds. The Empress, dressed in her ceremonial regalia, personally picked mulberry leaves and offered cocoons, performing the labors of reeling and weaving. Through her efforts, weaving spread widely to provide the necessary garments for sacrifices in the suburban altars and ancestral temples. The *Essential Overview of the Imperial Map* states: "Fuxi transformed the silkworms, and the Lady of the Xiling Clan raised them." The *Silkworm Classic* of the Prince of Huainan says: "The Lady of the Xiling Clan encouraged silkworm cultivation; the observation of silkworms began with her."</gloss>

The "Monthly Ordinances" <term>Yue Ling (月令): A classic text within the Book of Rites that describes the ritual and agricultural duties for each month.</term> states: In this month of late spring, the Empress and her consorts undergo a ritual fast and purification. They offer sacrifices to the First Sericulturalist and personally pick mulberry leaves to encourage the people in the labor of silkworm production.

In the "Offices of Heaven" section of the *Rites of Zhou*, it is recorded that the **Internal Minister** <term>Neizai (内宰): A palace official who managed the internal ritual affairs of the imperial household.</term>, in the middle of spring, announces the edict for the Empress to lead the noblewomen of both the inner and outer courts to perform the first sacrifice at the **Northern Suburb**. <gloss>Note: Silkworms are worshipped at the Northern Suburb because they represent "Pure Yin" <note>In the system of Yin and Yang, the North and the Empress are both associated with Yin; sericulture, being a female-led industry, followed this alignment.</note>.</gloss>

The *Treatise on Rituals* in the *Book of Han* states that the Empress’s sacrifice to the First Sericulturalist employs the **Middle Animal Offering** <term>Zhonglao (中牢): A mid-level sacrificial offering consisting of a sheep and a pig, rather than the "Great Offering" of an ox, sheep, and pig.</term>.

During the Huangchu period of the Wei dynasty [220–226 CE], an altar was established in the Northern Suburb, following the ancient precedents of the Zhou dynasty.

The system of the Jin dynasty stipulated that the Altar to the First Sericulturalist be one *zhang* <note>A traditional unit of length, approximately 3.3 meters.</note> high and two *zhang* and four feet square, with stairs descending in four directions. The stairs were five feet wide. The Empress would travel to the Western Suburb <note>The Jin dynasty shifted the location from the North to the West.</note> to personally perform the sacrifice and pick mulberry leaves.

In the Northern Qi dynasty, the Altar to the First Sericulturalist was five feet high and two *zhang* and four feet square. The stairs were each five feet wide. The outer boundary was forty paces on each side...

# The Guard House

The **Guard House** <gloss>pronounced *Shu Ping*</gloss> is a hut for the grain fields. It is constructed by gathering timber and thatch <note>original: 苔草 (táicǎo); refers to mosses, sedges, or coarse grasses used for rudimentary roofing.</note> into a simple structure that can accommodate two people. When the **standing crops** <term>héjià (禾稼): a general term for ripening cereal crops like rice or millet.</term> are nearly ripe, the farmer sleeps and dwells within it to remain on guard against damage from both trespassers and wandering livestock.

Sometimes these huts are built directly upon the **field ridges** <term>chéngkǎn (塍坎): the raised embankments or paths that divide paddy fields and manage irrigation.</term> by binding bundles of grass together. However, in mountainous regions or wilderness areas, the structure should be built upon high wooden stilts to avoid the danger of tigers and wolves.

**Zhen Xishan** <note>Zhen Dexiu (1178–1235), a celebrated Southern Song dynasty scholar-official known for his Neo-Confucian commentaries and writings on the welfare of the common people.</note> writes in his *Preface to Agricultural Matters*:

"When the grain begins to droop its heavy heads and the kernels become firm, there is a constant fear of damage or ruin caused by humans and animals. # The Granary

<note>original: "廩倉" (Lǐn Cāng)</note>

The **Granary** is also known by the name "Abundant Year." The *Classic of Poetry* <note>The *Shijing*, China's oldest existing collection of poetry</note> states in the poem *Abundant Year*: "An abundant year with much <term>millet|Broomcorn millet (黍, shǔ)</term> and much <term>rice|Glutinous rice (稌, tú)</term>; there are also high granaries, holding ten thousand, a hundred million, and even <term>trillions|An ancient term for an incredibly large number (秭, zǐ)</term> of grains."

A commentary notes that the **Lǐn** <term>Lǐn: A specific type of roofed granary</term> is the place where the ears of grain intended for <term>ritual offerings|Grain placed in sacrificial vessels (粢盛, zīshèng)</term> are stored. The *Shuowen Jiezi* <note>An early 2nd-century Chinese dictionary</note> explains that the **Cāng** <term>Cāng: A general term for a granary</term> is a structure that is wide and faces outward so that grain may be taken from it; thus, it is called *cāng* <note>This is a linguistic play on the word "take" or "pass through"</note>.

The *Tangyun* <note>A rhyme dictionary from the Tang Dynasty</note> says that a granary with a roof is called a *lǐn*. It seems that *cāng* is the general name for any place where grain is stored, while the terms *lǐn* and *yǔ* <note>An open-air granary or stack</note> are distinguished by whether or not the structure possesses a roof.

Regarding the square form of the **Jing** granary, the *Guangya* <note>original: "廣雅" (Guangya); an influential 3rd-century Chinese dictionary used to interpret ancient texts</note> states that the character for building eaves (*yu*) is derived from the radical for "shelter" and originally referred to a granary on a plain. The **Qun** and the **Jing** each have their regional suitability for the North and the South respectively. It is hoped that those with knowledge of such matters will distinguish between them and choose the appropriate design to use.
