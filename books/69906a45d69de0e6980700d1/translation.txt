It must be recognized—whatever impact such an admission may have upon whether the belief is actually true or false—that the belief in its current form is a natural development <note>original: "organic outgrowth"</note> from previous forms of mysticism. Historically, and perhaps logically as well, it is the inevitable result of well-documented earlier events.

In 1871, the Committee of the **London Dialectical Society** <term>London Dialectical Society: A group of secularist intellectuals founded in 1867 to discuss social and philosophical issues, famous for its inquiry into the supernatural.</term>, in presenting the results of a long investigation into the phenomena of **Spiritualism** <term>Spiritualism: A religious movement based on the belief that the spirits of the dead can communicate with the living, often through a person called a medium.</term>, reported that while they were overwhelmed with testimony from believers in these claimed wonders, their request for information received almost no response from the much larger group of people who held the belief to be based on fraud and delusion.<sup>1</sup> The Committee's experience is typical of the entire history of Spiritualism up to the present day. Within the last fifty years, there have been scores of journals throughout the civilized world devoted to this cause; hundreds, perhaps thousands, of books have been published representing the views either of those who have accepted the Spiritualist creed entirely, or of the minority who, after more or less lengthy investigation, have found themselves unable to reach a final judgment on the ultimate meaning of these events, yet remain convinced that some of the claimed occurrences <note>original: "manifestations"</note> were genuine.

The other side is represented at most by a few dozen <note>original: "few score"</note> books, pamphlets, and magazine articles. Not one of these can be said to have considered the movement as a whole, or to have discussed the strongest evidence available. Among the more obvious reasons for this neglect—which may well have seemed like a wise <note>original: "judicious"</note> choice at the time—one in particular must be mentioned here. There can be no doubt that in each succeeding decade, those who had convinced themselves (with or without a proper inquiry) that the claimed physical wonders were at least without foundation, were equally convinced that the belief in them would soon die out on its own. They felt that treating the matter seriously might even serve to delay that desired end.

# INTRODUCTION

The theory of suggestion is now common knowledge. It is probably not as well known that, despite James Braid's endorsement of the genuineness of most of these phenomena—including those of Phreno-Mesmerism <note>A Victorian practice combining phrenology (the study of skull shapes) with mesmerism, claiming that touching certain parts of the head during a trance would trigger specific emotions or behaviors.</note>—his writings and his very existence were almost completely ignored by his contemporaries who also believed in the facts. No contribution from Braid, nor any review of his numerous writings, appears in *The Zoist* <note>A prominent mid-19th-century journal dedicated to mesmerism and phrenology.</note>; indeed, the entire thirteen volumes of that periodical contain only two or three contemptuous references to his views. Nature, it may be said, abhors a "mugwump" <note>original: "Mugwump"; here used to describe a person who remains independent or takes a middle ground, often earning the disdain of partisans on both sides.</note>.

This recent episode in the history of science relates directly to one of the problems presented to us by Modern Spiritualism. Alongside the now-accepted manifestations of automatic activity—which, as mentioned, make up the bulk of psychological phenomena—there have been found since ancient times (and perhaps more abundantly in the records of Animal Magnetism and Mesmerism) facts that seem to indicate some mode of perception or form of communication between minds that is not yet recognized. 

It would be difficult to claim that these indications are as clear and unmistakable as the evidence for creating a state of complete insensibility to pain <term>insensibility to pain: surgical anesthesia produced through hypnosis or suggestion</term> through suggestion. However, we should remember that fifty years ago, the medical profession and scientific leaders were almost entirely in agreement in rejecting the evidence for that class of facts, despite the proof accumulated by James Esdaile and others. This realization is encouraging for those of us who now believe that the case for thought-transference has not yet received a fair hearing. 

The early proponents of mesmeric anesthesia <note>original: "anæsthesia"</note> were, with one or two exceptions, influenced by mystical theories. For instance, John Elliotson and James Esdaile believed—based on evidence we now see as quite insufficient—in the operation of an invisible fluid flowing from magnets and other objects. Because of these mystical leanings, their medical contemporaries felt they were excused from investigating the actual claims of anesthesia. # CHAPTER II

## ON POLTERGEISTS

Mysterious knocks and rappings, accompanied by the throwing of stones, the ringing of bells, the breaking of pottery, and other more violent disturbances, have been commonly reported in all civilized countries for the past two or three centuries, and even earlier. We propose here to analyze some of the best-known and most frequently quoted cases of this kind that occurred before 1848—those for which we have first-hand testimony. This analysis is conducted for the reasons mentioned in the previous chapter, rather than because of any exaggerated belief in the value of these stories as evidence.

The first case we will cite strongly supports the statement made in the last chapter: that so-called "poltergeist" <term>poltergeist: from the German for 'noisy ghost'</term> performances in modern times are a direct inheritance from the witchcraft of the Middle Ages. While this case involves the same general types of disturbances that characterize 19th-century reports, it also includes a 17th-century reference to the malicious actions of a supposed wizard. Furthermore, the reports claim that the disturbances stopped and then restarted in a way that matched the conviction and later escape of the suspected agent. This pattern—where the victim's suffering aligns with the legal status of the accused—is a common feature in the stories told by alleged victims of witchcraft. The evidence offered for these disturbances, as we shall see, is roughly on the same level as the evidence for witchcraft phenomena in general.

### A. *First-hand Contemporary Accounts.*

We will take first the contemporary letters and diary, and in the first instance we will consider only the statements made by the actual eye- or rather ear-witnesses of the things described. Mrs. Wesley <note>Susanna Wesley (1669–1742), the mother of John and Charles Wesley.</note> writes on January 12th, 1717, that, beginning from an early date in December, she heard unaccountable knockings, mostly in the garret or the nursery:

> "One night it made such a noise in the room over our heads as if several people were walking; then it ran up and down stairs, and was so disturbing <note>original: "outrageous"</note>, that we thought the children would be frightened; so your father and I rose and went down in the dark to light a candle. Just as we came to the bottom of the broad stairs, holding onto each other, on my side it seemed as if somebody had emptied a bag of money at my feet, and on his as if all the bottles under the stairs (which were many) had been smashed into a thousand pieces. We passed through the hall into the kitchen, got a candle, and went to see the children. The next night your father had Mr. Hoole <note>Joseph Hoole, the vicar of nearby Haxey.</note> stay at our house, and we all sat together until one or two o'clock in the morning and heard the knocking as usual. Sometimes it would make a noise like the winding up of a jack <term>jack: a clockwork device used to turn a roasting spit over a fire</term>; at other times, as on that night Mr. Hoole was with us, it sounded like a carpenter planing deals <term>planing deals: smoothing down wooden planks with a tool</term>; but most commonly it knocked three times and stopped, and then three times again, for many hours together."

That is practically all that Mrs. Wesley relates of her own personal experience.
There are two letters from Miss Susannah Wesley, dated January 24th and March 27th. In the first she records her own experience as follows:

We inquired whether anybody had been in the garden, or in the room above us, but there was nobody. Soon after, my sister Molly and I were up after all the family were in bed, except my sister Nancy, working on some business. We heard three bouncing thumps under our feet, which soon made us throw away our work and tumble into bed. There is one letter from Miss Emily <note>Emilia Wesley (1692–1771), the eldest Wesley daughter.</note>, undated, but obviously written at about this time. She describes various noises, more particularly groans, and the sound as if “a vast coal” were being thrown down in the kitchen. She also mentions the sound of a stone being thrown in among the bottles under the “best” stairs; “something like a quick winding up of a jack <term>jack: a clockwork device used to turn a roasting spit over a fire</term> at the corner of the room by my bed’s head”; and knocks on the floor and elsewhere, mostly three times in a row.
