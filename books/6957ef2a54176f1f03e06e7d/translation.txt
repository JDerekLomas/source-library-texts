# JOHANNES JORDANUS TO THE
## KIND READER
### SENDS GREETINGS. <note>original: "S. D." for Salutem Dicit, a standard formal greeting.</note>

<image-desc>A large ornamental woodcut drop cap 'S' featuring a detailed background of scrolling vines and foliage motifs.</image-desc>

IF there are any writers on the subject of botany <term>botany: original "rei plantariæ," the study of plants</term> who have wished to be of use to as many students as possible, and whom it greatly concerns us to read and know, Theophrastus must be held as the foremost among them. For while others investigated the nature of plants only as they pertain to herbal medicine, or others only to agriculture, he—intending to benefit a greater number—handed down those things which can contribute no small amount to those who wish to cultivate both the field and the schools of the philosophers, as Varro <note>Marcus Terentius Varro, a prolific Roman scholar and writer on agriculture</note> rightly judges. And he did this so learnedly and eloquently that you may recognize him as a disciple of the greatest philosophers, Plato and Aristotle, and as the successor of the latter in the profession of philosophy. For this reason, he seems plainly worthy of having students of both medicine and rural life spend a great deal of effort and labor in learning him thoroughly. Indeed, it will result in all those who wrote after him about the nature of plants—whether in Greek or Latin—being understood with less trouble, inasmuch as they borrowed a good portion from him; a fact which anyone who does not find it a burden to compare their records will easily see.

Theodore Gaza <note>A 15th-century Greek humanist and translator instrumental in the revival of learning</note>, a Greek and a physician most learned in both languages, rendered Theophrastus’s books on botany into Latin with the highest fidelity and diligence. He strove not to translate word-for-word, but to express the mind of Theophrastus so well that he might almost seem to have written in Latin. Furthermore, by imitating his own language, he is believed to have not unsuccessfully enriched the Latin tongue with most aptly fashioned terms, which was otherwise impoverished in this subject matter that had been treated rather negligently by the Latins. But since, as he himself complains, he used a manuscript mutilated and corrupted by the injury of time, he was forced to omit what was missing and to restore what was corrupted by whatever conjecture he could. We have clearly detected this through the repeated reading of these books, as well as by the comparison of better copies. For a Greek copy printed in Germany by learned men...

*...purged by the industry of men and the help of more corrected manuscripts, and it contains other things, and more in several places, than Gaza <note>Theodorus Gaza (c. 1398–1475), a key humanist scholar who translated Greek works into Latin.</note> had translated. Therefore, having consulted the judgment of learned men who have professedly dealt with these matters, we have added from a German copy those things which had been omitted, each in its proper place, which seemed to be approved by the learned and relevant to the subject. Some things that were still corrupted we have restored to their original integrity as far as was possible. Furthermore, in a few places where the translator had read the text differently, we have noted the Greek in the margins without changing anything, so that everyone might be left with free judgment. Those things, however, which had been corrupted through the negligence of the printers during the typesetting, we have faithfully replaced.*

*Moreover, I would wish the reader of these books to be forewarned that the translator sometimes renders certain Greek names of plants into Latin so variously that unless one looks more closely at the matter itself, he may not easily extricate himself. For, to point out a few from many: he translates <term>pityn <note>Greek: πίτυν</note></term> as fir, larch, and most often as pitch-pine; <term>elaten <note>Greek: ἐλάτην</note></term> mostly as fir, sometimes as pine; <term>peuken <note>Greek: πεύκην</note></term> generally as pine, sometimes as pitch-pine; yet each of these Latin names has its own specific meaning. All species of nightshade, although they differ among themselves, he calls "bladder-herbs"; he believes <term>kypeiron <note>Greek: κύπειρον</note></term> to be the sword-lily <note>gladiolus</note>, following Pliny <note>Pliny the Elder, Roman naturalist.</note>, I suppose, who confuses the one with the other. The diligent reader will notice these and many other things not to be despised; but why that most skilled translator expressed those Greek names in Latin in such a way, let that be the judgment of the experts.*

*These are the things which we have contributed here with as much faith and industry as we could, passing over many things to be observed by those with sharper eyes and more learning. Accordingly, we singularly ask those devoted to botanical matters to interpret our labor kindly, which we have undertaken solely out of a desire to be helpful, hoping that these books of Theophrastus might be read in Latin a little more corrected and, in some places, more enlarged than before. Whether we have achieved this, let someone judge only when he has compared this edition with previous ones—this edition which Guillaume Rouillé <note>Guliielmus Rouillius, a prominent printer in Lyon.</note>, a most diligent printer, is preparing, sparing neither expense nor labor so that he might benefit all literate people, especially students of medicine. Farewell. At Lyon,*

->on the Calends of April, in the year 1550.<-
