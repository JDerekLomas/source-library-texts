# Diagrams of the Three [Rites]
<note>Original Chinese: *Sanli Tu*. This refers to the illustrated study of the three ancient classics of ritual: the *Rites of Zhou*, the *Etiquette and Ceremonial*, and the *Book of Rites*.</note>

### The Ritual of the Lesser Shrouding
**The Lesser Shrouding** <term>Xiaolian: a preliminary ritual of dressing and wrapping the deceased, typically performed on the morning of the second day after death</term>. First, lay out three horizontal **binding bands** <term>jiao: fabric straps used to secure the burial clothes and shroud</term> upon the **corpse bed**. Next, place one longitudinal binding band on top of them. Next, lay the **shroud** <note>original: *qin*; a large ceremonial coverlet or quilt</note> over the binding bands. Next, lay the specific garments for the Lesser Shrouding on top of the shroud. Only after this is prepared should you move the body onto these layers.

Spread out silk and fold garments to support the head. Roll the ends of the cloth to cover the eyebrows. **Fill the hollows** <term>saiyu: the practice of using wadded fabric to fill gaps around the face and neck to maintain the body's natural shape</term>. Additionally, roll garments to pad the sides of the lower legs. Cover the body with a "removal garment" <note>an outer robe used to temporarily veil the body</note>. The lapel must be folded to the **left** <note>original: *zuoren*; in Chinese tradition, the living fold their robes to the right; the dead are dressed with the left lapel over the right to signify their departure from the world of the living</note>. Gather the shroud around the body. Use a separate shroud to cover it. Wait until the time for the Greater Shrouding; at that time, remove the outer covering shroud and tie the binding bands. Always tie the longitudinal band first, followed by the horizontal bands.

### The Ritual of the Greater Shrouding
**The Greater Shrouding** <term>Dalian: the final ritual of wrapping the body and placing it into the coffin, usually occurring on the third day after death</term>. First, lay out five horizontal binding bands inside the **coffin**. Next, lay one longitudinal binding band over the horizontal ones. Next, lay a **wadded shroud** <note>a shroud containing silk or cotton batting, intended to provide comfort and warmth for the deceased</note> on top of the bands. Let the edges of the shroud hang over the four sides of the coffin. Then, move the body into the coffin. Carefully feel for any **empty spaces** <note>original: *kongque*; gaps between the body and the coffin walls</note> and fill them with rolled garments, striving to make the interior completely firm and full. Use the shroud to cover the feet first, then the head, then the left side, and finally the right side. Tie the binding bands. First, tie the longitudinal band, and then tie the horizontal bands.

# Diagram and Measurements for the Spirit Tablet

When making a tablet, use chestnut wood <note>original: *li*. Chestnut was preferred for tablets because its name is a homophone for "reverence" or "dread" (*li*), symbolizing the awe one feels toward ancestors.</note>. The construction follows the patterns of the seasons, months, and days: the square base <note>original: *fu*. The pedestal or "feet" of the tablet.</note> is four inches wide, symbolizing the four seasons of the year. The height is one foot and two inches, symbolizing the twelve months. The width of the body is thirty tenths-of-an-inch <note>original: *fen*. A traditional unit of measurement where 10 *fen* equal 1 *cun* (inch).</note>, symbolizing the days of the month. Its thickness is twelve tenths, symbolizing the twelve double-hour periods of the day. <gloss>Both the body and the base are one inch and two tenths thick.</gloss> Carve the top five tenths to form a rounded head. One inch below the top, carve a "collar" <note>original: *ling*. A notch or indentation on the sides of the tablet.</note> on the front and split the piece into two parts. One part stays in front and two parts stay in the back <gloss>The front section is four tenths thick and the back section is eight tenths thick.</gloss> Hollow out the center of the wood to write the deceased's titles, name, and lineage rank <gloss>The inscription reads: "Spirit Tablet of the Late [Dynasty Name] Official, Lord [Surname], Taboo Name [Name], Courtesy Name [Name], [Order of birth]."</gloss> <gloss>The hollow center is six inches long and one inch wide.</gloss> Join the two parts together and plant the tablet into the base <gloss>The body stands one foot and eight tenths above the base; the base itself is one inch and two tenths high.</gloss> Additionally, bore a hole through the side to reach the hollow center; the hole should be one-third the thickness of the body <gloss>Meaning a circular diameter of four tenths.</gloss> It should be positioned above the lower two-thirds of the height <gloss>Specifically at the seven-inch two-tenths mark.</gloss>

### The Design of the Grand Hearse

The design of the Grand Hearse <term>Daju</term><note>original: 大舉; the "Grand Hearse" was a complex lifting frame used for the funeral processions of high-ranking officials or nobles.</note> utilizes two long main poles. On top of these poles, "crouching rabbit" supports <term>futu</term><note>original: 伏兔; these are carved wooden blocks shaped like crouching rabbits that act as structural supports or saddles to distribute the weight of the load onto the poles.</note> are added. At the points where these supports attach to the poles, circular sockets are carved. Separately, small square-shaped blocks are fashioned to support the feet of the coffin <term>jiu</term><note>original: 柩; specifically refers to a coffin containing a body.</note>; these feet stand two inches high.

Two pillars stand vertically at the sides. On the exterior of these pillars, circular pins are fitted so that they may enter the sockets and extend through to the outside. The interface between the pin and the socket must be carved to be extremely smooth and should be coated with grease <note>original: 膏; likely animal fat or oil used as a lubricant to allow the mechanism to pivot easily during transport over uneven ground.</note>.

Near the tops of these two pillars, horizontal cross-frames <term>hengju</term><note>original: 橫局; a stabilizing framework.</note> are added. Where the ends of these frames extend beyond the pillars, smaller auxiliary frames are further attached. Horizontal bars are fixed to the ends of the main poles; on top of these horizontal bars, short bars are added, and sometimes even smaller bars are placed on top of those. This description follows the original annotations.

According to the notes found under the section "Preparing the Coffin," it is stated: "The design of a coffin should be just large enough to contain the body; it should not be made excessively tall or grand." From this instruction, we can infer that the width of the frame is approximately no more than two <term>chi</term><note>original: 尺; a Chinese "foot." In the Ming dynasty, this was roughly 31-32 centimeters.</note> and a few inches.

If one were to follow the "Gong Shou" diagrams <note>original: 恭首圖; likely referring to an older or alternative ritual manual's illustrations</note>, which place short bars between the two main poles, four pallbearers would be forced to walk side-by-side within that narrow space. This would be cramped and restrictive, making it truly difficult to turn or maneuver the carriage. 

Furthermore, the original annotations do not explicitly require such a cramped arrangement. I now propose placing the horizontal bars so they extend *outside* the two main long poles; in this way, the space is spacious enough for the men to move. Additionally, because the weight of the burial goods <term>lianwu</term><note>original: 斂物; items of clothing, jade, or personal effects placed inside the coffin with the deceased.</note> varies, the ability of the pins and sockets to rotate [allows the frame to remain level despite shifting weight].

# The Merit Banner, Funerary Fans, and the Spirit Carriage

<term>The Merit Banner</term> <note>original: 功布 (*Gongbu*). This was a banner made of hemp cloth, approximately seven to eight feet long. Its name, "Merit Cloth," refers to its ritual use in wiping the coffin to purify it. During the procession, it was carried at the front to announce the status and "merit" of the deceased to onlookers.</note>

<term>Funerary Fans</term> <note>original: 翣 (*Sha*). These are large, ceremonial screen-like fans mounted on long poles. In ancient Chinese ritual, they were used to shield the coffin from the sun and from public view. The designs on these fans—often featuring axes, fire, or floral patterns—were indicators of social rank; high-ranking officials were permitted more fans with more intricate patterns than commoners.</note>

<term>The Spirit Carriage</term> <note>original: 靈車 (*Lingche*). Unlike the heavy bier that carried the physical body, the Spirit Carriage was a lighter, more decorative palanquin or carriage. It was intended to transport the "spirit tablet" or a portrait of the deceased. It functioned as a symbolic vessel for the soul, ensuring that the spirit of the departed was treated with the same hospitality and mobility it enjoyed in life.</note>

<note>The woodcut illustration on this page provides a visual guide to the arrangement of these items within the cortege. Attendants are shown in a synchronized line, holding the poles of the <term>Funerary Fans</term> upright to create a mobile corridor of honor. The <term>Spirit Carriage</term> follows, often placed ahead of the actual coffin to lead the soul toward the burial site. These elements combined the practical need for privacy with the metaphysical need to honor the transition from the world of the living to the world of the ancestors.</note>

<note>The "Five Grades of Mourning" (Wufu) represent the core of Confucian social ethics. By looking at a person’s clothing—the coarseness of the fabric and whether the edges were neatly hemmed or left raw—one could immediately identify their exact relationship to the deceased.</note>

<term>Untrimmed Sackcloth (Zhancui)</term> <note>The highest grade of mourning, worn for a father or by a wife for her husband. The word "Zhan" means to cut with a cleaver; the bottom edge of the garment was left raw and unhemmed to symbolize a grief so overwhelming that there is no time or heart for neatness.</note>

<term>Trimmed Sackcloth (Qicui)</term> <note>The second grade. Unlike the first, the edges of this garment were neatly sewn (Qi). The "staff" mentioned refers to a mourning stick made of bamboo or wood, used to support the mourner who is theoretically weakened by grief and fasting.</note>

<term>Merit (Gong)</term> <note>This refers to the "work" or labor required to process the hemp into cloth. "Greater Merit" cloth is coarse because it requires less labor to refine, whereas "Lesser Merit" and "Fine Hemp" use cloth that has been boiled, bleached, and woven more finely, indicating a more distant kinship and a shorter mourning period.</note>

According to the ancient teachings: By loving one’s kin, one honors the ancestors. By honoring the ancestors, one respects the lineage head <term>Zong — the head of the kinship group</term>. By respecting the lineage head, the clan is gathered together. When the clan is gathered, the ancestral temple remains solemn. 

It is also said that sons of concubines <note>shuzi — sons not born to the primary wife</note> do not lead the primary sacrifices. In ancient times, the Lineage Law <term>Zongfa — the system of patriarchal succession and kinship organization</term> was held in high regard and temple sacrifices were strictly observed. When a young man underwent the Capping Ceremony <note>The rite of passage into adulthood</note> or married a wife, it had to be reported to the ancestors; when someone died, the clan members were obligated to attend. 

Therefore, when the Great Lineage is established, the entire clan is gathered. When the Lesser Lineage is established, a specific branch of the lineage is gathered. 

In the present day, the Lineage Law is not established and temple sacrifices are not maintained. Consequently, even brothers from the same grandfather drift apart and are not close to one another. How much worse is it when the five-generation mourning period <note>The "Wu Fu" or Five Grades of Mourning, after which ritual obligations officially end</note> is finished? They become so estranged that they regard each other as mere strangers on the road. 

If one wishes to harmonize the clan today, one must first clarify the Lineage Law and value the Lineage Heir <term>Zongzi — the eldest son of the primary wife who inherits the role of ritual head</term>. Even if the Lineage Heir is slow-witted or impoverished, the clansmen must collectively provide <gloss>clothing</gloss> and support for him. During the seasonal festivals, he shall preside over the sacrificial rites and lead the clansmen. 

When a young man undergoes the Capping Ceremony or marries, he must report it; when there is a death, the clan must attend. This method of uniting the distant and the near, the estranged and the close, into a single body is the true way to harmonize the clan. Furthermore, if genealogical records are established and charity fields <term>Yitian — land donated to a clan to fund ancestral rites and support poor members</term> are set up, the virtuous and the capable members of the lineage should take it upon themselves to fulfill these responsibilities.

It proposes practical solutions for clan unity, including supporting impoverished heirs and maintaining communal "charity fields."</summary>

->**The Rituals for the Grand Duke of the Year, and the Masters of Wind, Clouds, Thunder, and Rain**<-

The **Grand Duke of the Year** <term>Taisui: The deity governing the current year's position in the twelve-year zodiac cycle, associated with the movement of Jupiter.</term> is the deity of the twelve branches. During the Tang and Song dynasties, he was not included in the official sacrificial statutes. It was only during the Yuan dynasty that rituals were established to sacrifice to the Grand Duke, the Monthly Generals, and the Daily Officials at the Bureau of Astronomy <note>Taishi Yuan: The imperial office responsible for calendrical calculations and astronomical observations.</note>.

As for the worship of the **Master of Wind** and the **Master of Rain**, these rituals appear in the *Rites of Zhou* <note>Zhouguan: A classic text detailing the ideal bureaucratic and ritual structure of the Zhou dynasty.</note>; the Qin, Han, Sui, and Tang dynasties all performed these sacrifices. During the Tianbao era <note>742–756 CE, a period of high culture during the Tang dynasty.</note>, the **Master of Thunder** was added to follow the Master of Rain in order of precedence. Consequently, the Masters of Wind, Rain, and Thunder were elevated to the status of "Middle-level Sacrifices." The Song and Yuan dynasties continued this practice.

In our **current dynasty** <note>Guochao: Refers to the Ming Dynasty (1368–1644 CE), during which this encyclopedia was compiled.</note>, the Grand Duke of the Year and the Masters of Wind, Rain, and Thunder are included as secondary sacrifices at the **Circular Mound** <term>Yuanqiu: The Great Altar of Heaven where the Emperor performed the most solemn annual rites.</term>. Additionally, the **Master of Clouds** has been added to follow the Master of Wind. 

Furthermore, three days after the "Awakening of Insects" <note>Jingzhe: The third solar term of the traditional calendar, marking the beginning of spring growth, usually around March 5th.</note> in the spring and the "Autumnal Equinox" in the autumn, dedicated sacrifices are performed for the Grand Duke of the current year, along with the Masters of Wind, Clouds, Thunder, and Rain. These take place at the Altar of Various Sacrifices located in the southern suburbs of the capital. For these occasions, the Emperor presents incense and dispatches designated officials to carry out the proceedings. The sacrifices to the Masters of Wind, Clouds, Thunder, and Rain in the various prefectures and counties are to follow the ritual protocols established by previous generations.

The "Herdsman" <term>Muren: An official title from the Rites of Zhou responsible for the royal stables.</term> section of the *Rites of Zhou* <note>original: "Zhouguan"</note> states that this officer oversees the six categories of horses. According to commentaries on the *Classic of Filial Piety*, the **Horse Progenitor** is the "Heavenly Quadriga" constellation <note>original: "Tiansi" — a group of four stars in the Room (Fang) mansion of the Chinese zodiac, associated with the celestial chariot.</note>; it is said that the "Room" mansion is the Dragon-Horse. The **Horse Strider** is the deity who watches over the disasters and harms that may befall horses. 

The Sui Dynasty adopted these systems from the *Rites of Zhou*, performing the sacrifices in the middle month of each of the four seasons. The Tang and Song dynasties followed this tradition, but the Yuan Dynasty did not maintain these specific sacrifices. 

In the **Present Dynasty** <note>The Ming Dynasty (1368–1644), under which this text was authored.</note>, the sacrifice is held to the south of the national capital. A dedicated shrine was built there, where the deities are worshipped together during the middle months of spring and autumn.

"Only you, O Spirits, existed at the dawn of Heaven and Earth, when horses first came into this world. Through herding and breeding, they have multiplied; through training and riding, they have found their proper place. Throughout the ages, they have assisted in the rise of nations, and we have relied entirely upon these war-horses to suppress disorder and end calamities. Thus, the people have found peace. 

Since the beginning of my righteous uprising <note>This refers to the founding of the Ming Dynasty by the Hongwu Emperor.</note>, I have relied heavily on the strength of horses. In shattering the strong and breaking the enemy, they have performed great service. Examining antiquity and following the established rites, I now offer these high honors. Here, I express my gratitude to the source of these blessings to manifest the divine merit. I reverently offer these ritual silks and sacrificial animals upon the altar built to the north of the capital. Let these offerings be presented with decorum. May you enjoy this offering."

# Ritual of the Great Imperial Ancestral Temple

The Master of Ceremonies calls for the musicians and dancers to take their positions. The various attendant officials attend to their respective duties.

The guiding officials lead **the Emperor** to his Imperial Seat. The Interior Usher announces that the Emperor has taken his place. The Master of Ceremonies announces the "Welcoming of the Spirits" and music is played.

The music stops. The Interior Usher calls for four bows <gloss>the civil and military officials do the same</gloss>. The Master of Ceremonies calls for the Presentation of Silk and the performance of the First Offering. Music is played. The attendant officials each carry the rolls of silk and the gold ritual wine cups, receiving the wine to offer before **the Divine** Presence. The Prayer-Reader <unclear>official</unclear> carries the prayer scroll and kneels to the right of **the Divine** Presence. The Interior Usher announces the command to kneel. The Master of Ceremonies calls for the reading of the prayer. After the reading is finished, the scroll is advanced before **the Divine** Presence. The Interior Usher announces to prostrate, then rise, then stand at ease <gloss>the civil and military officials do the same</gloss>.

The music stops. The Master of Ceremonies calls for the performance of the Secondary Offering. The attendant officials each use ceramic ritual wine cups to receive wine and offer it before **the Divine** Presence. The music stops. The Master of Ceremonies calls for the performance of the Final Offering <gloss>the ritual is the same as the Secondary Offering</gloss>.

The music stops. The Minister of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices <note>The Taichang Si was the high office responsible for religious rites and the calendar.</note> advances and stands to the east of the hall, facing west. He calls out to "Bestow the Blessings and Sacrificial Meats." An official from the Court of Imperial Entertainments <note>The Guanglu Si was responsible for the preparation of sacrificial foods and imperial banquets.</note> carries the Blessed Wine and Sacrificial Meats. These are brought from before **the Divine** Presence, exiting through the left side of the central gate, and are brought before **the Emperor**. The Interior Usher announces the command to kneel and to insert the ritual jade tablet into the sash.

<term>Ritual of the Great Temple (Taimiao Yi): The specific protocol for worshipping the imperial ancestors in the Great Temple.</term>
<term>Master of Ceremonies (Dianyi): The official who directs the timing and flow of the ritual by calling out commands.</term>
<term>Presentation of Silk (Dianbo): The formal offering of fine fabric to the spirits, a mark of high status and wealth.</term>
<term>The Three Offerings (Sanxian): The core of the ceremony, consisting of the Initial Offering (Chuxian), Secondary Offering (Yaxian), and Final Offering (Zhongxian).</term>
<term>Court of Imperial Sacrifices (Taichang Si): The government bureau in charge of state rituals and music.</term>
<term>Court of Imperial Entertainments (Guanglu Si): The bureau responsible for providing the actual food, wine, and banquet supplies for the ceremony.</term>
<term>Inserting the Tablet (Jin gui): A ritual action where the Emperor tucks his ritual jade tablet (gui) into his sash to free his hands to receive the blessed wine and meat.</term>
<term>Blessed Wine and Meat (Fuzuo): Food and drink that has been offered to the spirits and is then consumed by the Emperor to receive the ancestors' protection and "blessing."</term>
