# PREFACE

This book would never have been written had I not been honored with an appointment as Gifford Lecturer <note>The Gifford Lectures are a prestigious series established in 1887 to promote the study of "Natural Theology."</note> on Natural Religion at the University of Edinburgh. In searching for subjects for the two courses of ten lectures each for which I became responsible, it seemed to me that the first course might well be a descriptive one on "Man’s Religious Appetites," and the second a metaphysical <note>Metaphysics: the branch of philosophy that examines the fundamental nature of reality.</note> one on "Their Satisfaction through Philosophy." 

However, the unexpected growth of the psychological material as I began to write it out has resulted in the second subject being postponed entirely; the description of man’s religious constitution now fills all twenty lectures. In Lecture XX, I have suggested rather than stated my own philosophical conclusions. The reader who desires to know them immediately should turn to pages 511–519 and to the "Postscript" of the book. I hope to be able at some later day to express them in a more explicit form.

The question, "What are the <term>religious propensities</term> <note>original: "propensities"; refers to natural inclinations or tendencies toward religious belief</note>?" and the question, "What is their <term>philosophic significance</term>?" are two entirely different orders of question from a logical point of view. <margin>
Existential and spiritual judgment
</margin>

In recent books on logic, a distinction is made between two types of inquiry concerning any subject. First: what is its nature? How did it come about? What is its constitution, origin, and history? And second: what is its importance, meaning, or significance, now that it exists? The answer to the first question is given in an <term>existential judgment</term> <note>a statement of fact regarding how something exists or originated</note> or proposition. The answer to the other is a <term>proposition of value</term>—what the Germans call a *value judgment* <note>original: "Werthurtheil"</note>, or what we may, if we like, name a <term>spiritual judgment</term>. Neither judgment can be derived immediately from the other. They proceed from different intellectual concerns, and the mind combines them only by making them first separately and then adding them together.

In the matter of religions, it is particularly easy to distinguish these two types of questions. Every religious phenomenon has its history and its derivation from natural causes. What is nowadays called the <term>higher criticism</term> <note>a branch of literary analysis that investigates the origins and dates of biblical texts</note> of the Bible is only a study of the Bible from this existential point of view, which was neglected too much by the earlier church. Under exactly what biographical conditions did the sacred writers produce their various contributions to the holy volume? And what, exactly, did they have in their individual minds when they delivered their utterances? These are clearly questions of historical fact, and one does not see how the answer to them can decide, without further evidence, the additional question: of what use...

## CIRCUMSCRIPTION OF THE TOPIC

<margin>Religion cannot be defined by a single principle or essence, but is instead a collective name.</margin>
**M**ost books on the philosophy of religion try to begin with a precise definition of what its essence consists of. We are dealing with a field of experience where there is not a single concept that can be sharply defined. # LECTURE III: THE REALITY OF THE UNSEEN

<margin>Religion implies the belief in an unseen order and that our supreme good lies in adjusting ourselves to it. This belief and this adjustment constitute the religious attitude of the soul.</margin>

If one were asked to characterize the religious life in the broadest and most general terms possible, one might say that it consists of the belief that there is an unseen order, and that our supreme good lies in harmoniously adjusting ourselves to it. This belief and this adjustment are the religious attitude within the soul. I wish during this hour to call your attention to some of the psychological peculiarities of such an attitude—specifically, the belief in an object which we cannot see. 

All our attitudes—whether moral, practical, emotional, or religious—are caused by the "objects" of our consciousness: the things which we believe to exist, whether in reality or as an ideal, alongside ourselves. Such objects may be present to our physical senses, or they may be present only to our thoughts. In either case, they provoke a *reaction* from us; and the reaction caused by things of thought is notoriously, in many cases, as strong as that caused by physical presences. <margin>The attitude which a sense of religious objects awakens—Solemn and joyous.</margin>

So much for our sense of the reality of religious objects. Let me now say a brief word more about the attitudes they characteristically awaken.

We have already agreed that these attitudes are solemn; and we have seen reason to think that the most distinctive of them is the sort of joy which may result, in extreme cases, from absolute self-surrender. The sense of the kind of object to which the surrender is made has much to do with determining the precise character of that joy; the entire phenomenon is more complex than any simple formula allows. In the literature of this subject, sadness and gladness have each been emphasized in turn. The ancient saying that "fear was the first creator of the Gods" <note>original: "Primos in orbe deos fecit timor" — a sentiment attributed to the Roman poet Statius.</note> receives voluminous support from every age of religious history; but nonetheless, religious history shows the part which joy has always tended to play. Sometimes the joy has been primary; sometimes secondary, being the gladness of deliverance from fear. 

This latter state of things, being more complex, is also more complete. As we proceed, I think we shall have abundant reason for refusing to leave out either the sadness or the gladness, if we look at religion with the breadth of view it demands. Stated in the most complete terms possible, a person's religion involves both moods of contraction <note>feelings of withdrawal, fear, or shrinking</note> and moods of expansion <note>feelings of growth, outreach, or liberation</note> of their being. However, the quantitative mixture and order of these moods vary so much from one age of the world, from one system of thought, and from one individual to another, that you may insist either on dread and submission, or on peace and freedom, as the essence of the matter, and still remain substantially within the limits of the truth. The constitutionally gloomy <note>original: "sombre"</note> and the constitutionally optimistic <note>original: "sanguine"</note> onlooker are bound to emphasize opposite aspects of what lies before their eyes.

<header>THE VARIETIES OF RELIGIOUS EXPERIENCE</header>

<margin>The sombre mind.</margin>
The person with a constitutionally sombre religious temperament makes even their religious peace a very serious and sober thing. Danger still seems to hover in the air around it. The tension and contraction of the soul are not entirely gone. To such a person, it would seem trivial and childish to burst into lighthearted laughter or playful antics after being delivered from trouble, utterly forgetting the hawk lurking on the branch. Instead, the impulse is to lie low—lie low indeed, for you are in the hands of a living God. In the Book of Job, for example, the helplessness of man and the all-powerful nature of God are the exclusive focus of the author’s mind. "It is as high as heaven; what can you do? Deeper than hell; what can you know?" There is a sharp, bitter satisfaction in the truth of this conviction which some people can feel, and which for them is the closest possible approach to the feeling of religious joy.

"In Job," says that coldly truthful writer, the author of *Mark Rutherford* <note>The pen name of William Hale White (1831–1913), a British novelist and theologian</note>, "God reminds us that man is not the measure of His creation. The world is immense, constructed on no plan or theory which the human intellect can grasp. It is transcendent everywhere. This is the core of every verse, and is the secret, if there be one, of the poem. Whether this explanation is sufficient or insufficient, there is nothing more. . . . God is great, and we do not know His ways. He takes from us all we have, but yet if we keep our souls in patience, we *may* pass through the valley of the shadow and come out into the sunlight again. We may or we may not! <margin>Will we?</margin> . . . What more do we have to say now than what God said from the whirlwind over two thousand five hundred years ago?"¹

<margin>Seeley</margin>
Among some writers, an attitude might be called religious even if it contained no trace of sacrifice or submission, no tendency to kneel, and no bowing of the head. # LECTURES IV AND V

## THE RELIGION OF HEALTHY-MINDEDNESS

<margin>Religion and Happiness</margin>
If we were to ask the question, "What is human life’s chief concern?" one of the answers we would receive is, "It is happiness." How to gain, how to keep, and how to recover happiness is, in fact, the secret motive for most people at all times in everything they do and everything they are willing to endure. The school of hedonism <note>original: "hedonistic school"; a philosophy arguing that pleasure or happiness is the sole or chief good in life</note> in ethics derives the moral life entirely from the experiences of happiness and unhappiness that different kinds of conduct produce. Even more so in the religious life than in the moral life, happiness and unhappiness seem to be the poles around which all interest revolves. 

We do not need to go so far as to say, like the author I recently quoted, that any persistent enthusiasm is, by definition, religion; nor do we need to call mere laughter a religious exercise. However, we must admit that any persistent enjoyment may produce the sort of religion that consists of a grateful admiration for the gift of such a happy existence. We must also acknowledge that the more complex ways of experiencing religion are new methods of producing happiness—wonderful inner paths to a supernatural kind of happiness—especially when the initial gift of natural existence proves to be unhappy, as it so often does.

<margin>People regard happiness as proof of a religion's truth.</margin>
Given these relations between religion and happiness, it is perhaps not surprising that people come to regard the happiness which a religious belief provides as a proof of its truth. If a creed <note>original: "creed"; a formal statement of Christian beliefs or a set of principles</note> makes a person feel happy, they almost inevitably adopt it. Such a belief, they feel, ought to be true;

# THE RELIGION OF HEALTHY-MINDEDNESS

Therefore it is true—such, rightly or wrongly, is one of the "immediate inferences" of the religious logic used by ordinary men.

<margin>The feeling of happiness connected with the nearness of God is the most indispensable proof of God's reality. — C. Hilty</margin>

"The near presence of God’s spirit," says a German writer,¹ "may be experienced in its reality—indeed *only* experienced. And the mark by which the spirit’s existence and nearness are made irrefutably clear to those who have ever had the experience is the utterly incomparable feeling of happiness which is connected with that nearness. This is therefore not only a possible and altogether proper feeling for us to have here on earth, but is the best and most indispensable proof of God’s reality. No other proof is equally convincing, and therefore happiness is the point from which every effective new theology should start."

In the hour immediately before us, I shall invite you to consider the simpler kinds of religious happiness, leaving the more complex types to be treated at a later date.

In many persons, happiness is congenital <note>original: "congenital and irreclaimable"; meaning a natural, inborn trait that cannot be altered.</note> and cannot be suppressed. "Cosmic emotion" <note>A 19th-century term for a feeling of awe or connection toward the universe as a whole.</note> inevitably takes the form of enthusiasm and freedom in them. I speak not only of those who are physically or "animally" happy. I mean those who, when unhappiness is offered or proposed to them, positively refuse to feel it, as if it were something mean and wrong. We find such persons in every age, passionately flinging themselves upon their sense of the goodness of life, despite the hardships of their own condition and despite the sinister theologies into which they may be born. From the outset, their religion is one of union with the divine. The heretics who lived before the Reformation are frequently accused by Church writers of antinomian <note>original: "antinomian"; the belief that Christians are released by grace from the obligation of observing moral laws.</note> practices, just as the first Christians were accused of indulgence in orgies by the Romans. It is probable that there never has been a century in which the deliberate refusal to think ill of life has not been idealized.

This perspective has been adopted by a sufficient number of persons to form sects, whether open or secret, who claimed that all natural things are permitted. Saint Augustine’s maxim, *Love, and do what you will* <note>original: "Dilige et quod vis fac"</note>—if you but love God, you may do as you incline—is morally one of the profoundest observations. Depending on their individual characters, these people have been either refined or gross; but their belief has at all times been systematic enough to constitute a definite religious attitude. For them, God was a giver of freedom, and the sting of evil was overcome. Saint Francis and his immediate disciples were, on the whole, part of this company of spirits, of which there are, of course, infinite varieties. Rousseau in the earlier years of his writing, Diderot, Bernardin de Saint Pierre, and many of the leaders of the eighteenth-century anti-Christian movement were of this optimistic type. They owed their influence to a certain authority in their feeling that Nature, if you will only trust her sufficiently, is absolutely good.
