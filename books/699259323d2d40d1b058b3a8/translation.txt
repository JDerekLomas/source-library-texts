# PREFACE.

---

<term>SECTION 4</term> of the Burma Courts Act, 1875, states that “where in any lawsuit or proceeding, it is necessary for any Court to decide any question regarding succession, inheritance, marriage, or caste, or any religious usage or institution, Buddhist law shall serve as the rule for the decision in cases where the parties are Buddhists. This applies except in cases where such law has been altered or abolished by legislative enactment, or where it is opposed to any custom that has the force of law in British Burma.”

At the present time, Buddhist Law is in a very unsettled state and is full of difficulties and contradictions. It is found in various <term>Damathats</term> <note>traditional Burmese legal treatises or collections of customary law</note>, of which the <term>Menu Kyay</term> <note>also known as the "Laws of Menoo"; an 18th-century Burmese legal text that became a primary source for the British administration</note>, or Laws of Menu, is the best known and most frequently referenced. This prominence may be explained by the fact that the *Menu Kyay* is the only *Damathat* that has been translated in its entirety. Translations of certain sections regarding marriage, divorce, and inheritance from other *Damathats* have been published by Mr. Jardine, the late Judicial Commissioner of British Burma, in his "Notes on Buddhist Law."

# PREFACE.

...*Menu Kyay Damathat*, along with the local law <note>original: *lex loci*</note> or local custom. This Code, which is reproduced in this manual, is still viewed as an authority on Buddhist Law, though some portions have been ruled invalid <note>original: "bad law"</note>; I have noted these specific cases.

There are many rulings from the Special Court and the Court of the Judicial Commissioner of Burma on various points of Buddhist Law. These are currently scattered throughout the Circulars issued by the Judicial Commissioner, Warde-Jones' Reports, and Christophers' Handbook; additionally, a collection of rulings by Mr. Sandford has been published. All these rulings are undoubtedly important and carry the same weight as legislation. In the following pages, I have brought them together as notes to accompany Major Sparks' Code.

I hope this manual will, to some extent, reduce the effort required for those who need to study Buddhist Law.

### OF INHERITANCE.

**The Damathats <note>Traditional Burmese legal treatises</note>.**—Translations of the law of inheritance and the division of property <note>original: "partition"</note> found in the *Manu Wunnana*, *Wagaru*, *Mohavicchedani*, and *Dhammavilasa* Damathats can be found in Mr. Jardine’s *Notes on Buddhist Law* (Volumes V, VI, and VII).

->*General Principles.*<-

**60.** The fundamental principle of Burmese law is that, whenever it is possible to avoid doing so, an estate shall never move upward to ancestors <note>original: "ascend"</note>.

<margin>Inheritance does not move upward. D. 10-1.</margin>

The source of authority for this section is the *Menu Kyay Damathat*, Book X, Section 1.

**Mutual right of inheritance.**—It is a principle of Buddhist Law that husband and wife inherit from each other (*Menu Kyay Damathat*, Book X, Section 38). This principle was cited in the case of *Moung Shway Ngoan (representing the deceased Ma Thin) v. Mah Min Dway* (Warde-Jones’ Reports, 17). In the judgment for that case, Justice Jardine remarked:

"Two principles must be kept in mind when deciding a case according to the spirit of Buddhist Law: the wife is entitled to a portion of the estate because, while she lived with her husband, she held a joint interest in all household matters. Even if the property was inherited from the husband’s ancestors, it might have been lost or become unproductive if the wife had not done her part in caring for it. 

At the same time, the surviving husband or wife is strictly prevented from taking full ownership of property that came directly to the deceased spouse from their own family. Such property is not to be completely diverted away from blood relatives to half-siblings or to the step-parent's own family, who are not even related by blood.

"These rules were both followed in the legal decision regarding property inherited by a mother from her ancestors during her marriage <note>original: "coverture"</note> in a dispute between a daughter and a step-father. The step-father received half because of his position and duty as the husband and partner of the deceased; the daughter received the other half because the property originated from her mother’s family."

**61.** The three orders of inheritance <note>original: "succession"</note> are:
*1st.* The descendant line—children and their descendants.
*2nd.* The collateral line—brothers and sisters and their descendants.
*3rd.* The ascendant line—parents and their representatives in order.

This is based on the authority of the ***Manugye Dhammathat*** <note>original: "Menu Kyay Damathat"; the most influential traditional Burmese legal code, compiled in 1756</note>, Book 10, Section 19.

**Absolute power of parents to dispose of property.**—Parents have absolute power over the disposal of their own property. A parent may even revoke a gift given to a child at any time during that child’s life. *Ma Aung and 2 others v. Ko Shwe O and 3 others*, Circular No. 19 of 1886; Justices Meres and MacEwen.

**The eldest child's right to claim a share.**—Upon the death of one parent, the eldest son or daughter may claim his or her share of the estate. The remainder of the property then legally belongs to <note>original: "vests in"</note> the surviving parent, held for themselves and the remaining children. *The same case* <note>original: "ibid."</note>.

**The surviving parent's right to divide property.** Although the other children cannot obtain their shares without the consent of the surviving parent, the surviving parent may choose to divide <note>original: "partition"</note> the inheritance. In such a case, the parent retains their own share, and they have the absolute right to dispose of that share however they wish. *The same case* <note>original: "ibid."</note>.

**A widow's power to sell property in case of necessity.**—A Burmese Buddhist widow does not have an absolute right to the entire family property upon the death of her husband. She has an absolute right only to her own share and a life interest <note>the right to use the property for her lifetime, but not necessarily to sell the principal asset</note> in the remainder. She does not have the right to freely sell the remainder of the estate, but possesses only a power of sale in cases of genuine necessity. *The same case* <note>original: "ibid."</note>.
***Legally Decided*** <note>original: "Held"</note>, that upon the death of the father, the children gain a legal interest in the property. A widow has no power to sell land in which the children have a right to share without the children's consent. *Nga Shwe Yo v. Mi Saw Byu and five others*, Warde-Jones' Reports, 13; Justice Jardine.
