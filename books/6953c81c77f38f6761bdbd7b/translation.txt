# PREFACE

Since the dawn of history, various extraordinary phenomena have been recorded as happening among human beings. Witnesses are not lacking in modern times to attest to the reality of such events, even in societies living under the full light of modern science. The vast majority of such evidence is unreliable, as it comes from ignorant, superstitious, or fraudulent persons. In many instances, these so-called miracles are merely imitations. But what do they imitate? It is not the sign of a candid and scientific mind to discard anything without proper investigation. Surface-level scientists, unable to explain various extraordinary mental phenomena, strive to ignore their very existence. They are, therefore, more blameworthy than those who think that their prayers are answered by a being, or beings, above the clouds, or those who believe that their petitions will make such beings change the course of the universe. The latter have the excuse of ignorance, or at least of a false system of education in their childhood which has taught them to depend upon such beings for help; this dependence has now become a part of their degenerate nature. The former—the scientists—have no such excuse.

For thousands of years, such phenomena have been investigated, studied, and categorized. The entire scope of the human religious faculty has been analyzed, and the practical result is the science of <term>Rāja Yoga</term> <note>Literally "Royal Union" or the "Royal Path"; a system of meditation and self-control aimed at realizing the ultimate reality.</note>. <term>Rāja Yoga</term> does not, in the unpardonable manner of some modern scientists, deny the existence of facts which are very difficult to explain. On the other hand, it gently, yet in no uncertain terms, tells the superstitious that miracles, answers to prayers, and the powers of faith—though true as facts—are not

# RAJA YOGA

The phenomena of the mind are rendered comprehensible through the superstitious explanation of attributing them to the agency of a being, or beings, above the clouds. *Raja Yoga* <note>literally "Royal Union" or the "Royal Path" of meditation</note> declares to mankind that each being is only a conduit for the infinite ocean of knowledge and power that lies behind. It teaches that desires and wants are in man, and that the power of supply is also in man. Wherever and whenever a desire, a want, or a prayer has been fulfilled, it was out of this infinite magazine <note>original: "magazine," meaning a storehouse or treasury</note> that the supply came, and not from any supernatural being. 

The idea of supernatural beings may rouse the power of action in man to a certain extent, but it also brings spiritual decay. It brings dependence; it brings fear; it brings superstition. It degenerates into a horrible belief in the natural weakness of man. There is no "supernatural," says the *Yogi* <note>a practitioner of Yoga</note>, but there are in nature gross <note>physical or material</note> manifestations and subtle manifestations. The subtle are the causes, and the gross are the effects. The gross can be easily perceived by the senses, but the subtle cannot. The practice of *Raja Yoga* will lead to the acquisition of these more subtle perceptions.

All the orthodox systems of Indian philosophy have one goal in view: the liberation of the soul through perfection. The method is by *Yoga*. The word *Yoga* covers an immense ground, but both the *Sankhya* <note>one of the oldest schools of Indian philosophy, focusing on dualism between consciousness and matter</note> and the *Vedantist* <note>followers of Vedanta, a school based on the teachings of the Upanishads</note> schools point to *Yoga* in some form or another.

The subject of the first lectures in the present book is that form of *Yoga* known as *Raja Yoga*. The aphorisms <note>concise statements of principle</note> of *Patanjali* <note>the sage who codified the Yoga Sutras around 400 CE</note> are the highest authority and textbook on *Raja Yoga*. Other philosophers, though occasionally differing from *Patanjali* in some philosophical aspects, have, as a rule, granted his method of practice their decided consent. <header>AUTHOR’S PREFACE</header>

These are the <term>Aphorisms</term> <note>original: "Sūtras"</note> of <term>Patanjali</term> <note>An ancient Indian sage who organized the philosophy of Yoga into a series of concise statements.</note>, accompanied by a running commentary. Every effort has been made to avoid technical jargon as much as possible and to maintain a free and easy conversational style. In the first part, some simple and specific directions are provided for the student who wishes to practice. However, all such students are especially and earnestly reminded that, with very few exceptions, <term>Yoga</term> can only be safely learned through direct contact with a teacher. If these conversations succeed in awakening a desire for further information on the subject, the teacher will surely appear.

The system of Patanjali is based upon the <term>Sankhya</term> philosophy <note>One of the oldest schools of Indian philosophy, focusing on the dualism between consciousness and matter.</note>, with very few points of difference between them.

The two most important differences are: first, that Patanjali admits a <term>Personal God</term> in the form of a "first teacher," while the only God the Sankhyas admit is a nearly perfected being who is temporarily in charge of a cosmic cycle. Second, the <term>Yogis</term> <note>Practitioners of Yoga.</note> believe the mind to be equally all-pervading as the soul, or <term>Self</term> <note>original: "Puruṣa"; the pure, eternal consciousness.</note>, whereas the Sankhyas do not.
