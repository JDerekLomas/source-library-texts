### *To the most illustrious and excellent man*

# JOHANNES KEPLER,

## MOST CELEBRATED PROFESSOR
## OF MATHEMATICS.
<note>original: *Mathesis*. In the 17th century, "Mathesis" referred to the broad study of the mathematical sciences, including astronomy, music theory, and the proportions of the universe.</note>

THAT your letter (sent on short notice) reached my hands so suddenly (Most Excellent Man), I was bathed in no small joy and filled with unexpected delight. Nor indeed would I want you to think that I emerged happy and eager merely because of the long-awaited arrival of those letters, seeing as I noticed, both from the letters and the *Apology* <note>Kepler’s *Apologia* (1622) was a direct critique of Fludd’s earlier work, defending Kepler’s own theories of planetary harmony.</note> attached to them, that your indignation against me has publicly flared up; rather, my sadness would be increased and doubled on that account, had I not perceived that you—hardly called or incited to do so—had dipped a finger into my works, armed for the violation of an honest reputation, with a rash appetite. 

But because your *Apology*, appearing in the light of day like a new public envoy, seems in a way to excuse its master to me and to the world—in which matter provident caution advised me and forced necessity urged me to accuse him sharply in my *Analytical Discourse*—Indeed, by Hercules, <note>original: *Verum hercle*. A common Latin exclamation in academic writing used to emphasize a point.</note> you say you wonder what necessity drove me to respond to your *Analytical Discourse*, since you promised nothing, asked nothing, and objected to nothing. 

Is it thus, my Johannes, that you play with us? Do you not know that I can still judge colors? Or do you think I am fashioned from a spirit entirely unfeeling, or one least subject to the touch of the tongue, as one who does not know how to feel the hard blows of words and the burning wounds of writing? But who, carefully judging or diligently deliberating on this matter with himself, would conceive in his mind that a man so exalted by the light of the mind, second to none in mathematical science, most celebrated throughout almost all Europe for various gifts of spirit, and divinely singing the Harmony of colors <note>Fludd is likely referring to Kepler’s work on optics and the "music of the spheres."</note> in his own mathematical way, had fallen into such a moral antipathy that, by way of comparison and with no evident cause preceding it, he would attack a man plainly unknown and least offensive, or provide an occasion...

# PREFACE

*...offering a just cause, should he strike him down with a leafy club of words, as it were, and pursue him with the thick and hostile kicks of malice? And did you think you would bring up these insults against me with impunity? Or did you think such a great injury of vexation should be passed over in silence, and not be washed away by a pen dipped in the sharpness of truth? For what mortal would not strive to rebuild his own fame and dignity, when it is wasting away under the insolence of aspersions, by a mutual effort—as if by a counter-strain—and restore it with the Balm of Truth?* <note>original: *Opobalsamo*. A precious resin or balm used in antiquity for healing wounds; Fludd uses it here as a metaphor for restoring a damaged reputation.</note> *Since even the fly is provided with its own gall, and the little worm is armed with bile, they seem to declare by a certain natural instinct that the invasions of enemies and the insults of opponents must be driven back by every means.*

*But in truth, you dream that you are established in your usual stage-pulpit, and you do not blush to assert that you have spoken to your readers as if to instruct them from that height: as if this were a just and fit excuse offered for the insult to Ptolemy and Robert,* <note>Fludd refers to himself in the third person here as "Robertus," defending himself alongside the ancient astronomer Claudius Ptolemy.</note> *by means of which—secure and thinking no further question would ever be raised in this matter—you might bring forward the established foundations of their science onto the public stage like a kind of sacrificial victim to be offered up to your own will.*

*What? Is it because that once great and royal Ptolemy (now buried in the earth by a small cruelty of fate) cannot plead his own cause with a living voice, nor repel the injuries and crimes of error they bring against his honor and dignity? Did you therefore think in your mind that Robert—surviving in Ptolemy’s life, if not his glory—would not defend his own harmonic cause for himself, even if Johannes* <note>Johannes Kepler.</note> *opposed him bravely? Even if he had progressed far in the learning of resonance,* <note>original: *resymphoniaca eruditione*. Refers to the study of musical harmony and its application to the cosmos.</note> *even if he were surrounded by a whole chorus of listeners and disciples, and accompanied by his entire crowd or troop of followers; did you think I would not manfully be present?*

*See then and behold: beyond your expectation (for you say I have arisen unexpectedly from the crowd, not of my own will, but driven to a contest of this kind by just reason), I appeared at the next-to-last Spring Fairs,* <note>original: *Nundinis vernalibus*. Refers to the Frankfurt Book Fair, the most important marketplace for books in early modern Europe, where Fludd and Kepler’s works were sold and debated.</note> *I say, I have long since come forward into the public view under the title of the Analytical Discourse.* <note>Refers to Fludd’s *Veritatis Proscenium* (1621), in which he first responded to Kepler’s criticisms.</note>

*For hey there, you! You who had begun the comparison—the offspring of this present contention—and who first cast the law of speech against my Harmony; you who, with the subtle gaze of Lynceus himself and the many-fold eyes of Argus,* <note>Lynceus and Argus are mythological figures known for superhuman sight; Fludd is accusing Kepler of being an overly-critical "nitpicker" of his work.</note> *inquired into the deeds of another’s property more curiously than is fair: let it not grieve or shame you if I have destined those lavish, wordy coins of the same condition to be the reward of a slanderer or detractor—by returning like for like, of course. For even this is a Christian admonition: that we should not do to another what we would not want done to ourselves; and we find it commanded in the scriptures that an eye is to be restored for an eye, blood is to be washed away with blood, and wordy reproaches must sometimes be repressed by similar means. For they say that poison is expelled by poison, and a scorpion crushed into its own pulp is said to heal the wounds of its own sting.*

*Nor indeed am I moved by any malice against you or your Harmony, nor stirred to such an end by the stings of envy...*

# PREFACE.

*...I was moved to construct or contrive; but a double occasion invited me, and a twin caution persuaded me, to boldly plead my own case against the errors cast upon me. Namely, that I might first show the learned of the world how easy it is for one skilled in Philosophy—even one endowed with only modest natural talents—to defend the foundations of his Philosophy, however much they are besieged and attacked by words smeared with insult. This is especially true since they are fortified by a triple demonstration, like a faction of invincible soldiers; armed, I say, with the strongest reasons of Physics,* <note>original: *Physices*. In Fludd’s time, "Physics" referred to the study of the natural, material world and its underlying spiritual forces, rather than just modern mechanics.</note> *surrounded by the frequent authority of both philosophers and Theosophers,* <note>original: *Theosophorum*. Those who seek knowledge of God through mystical insight and the study of nature.</note> *and finally (what is more effective than any argument) derived and confirmed by a most certain chorus of visible conclusions from the hidden mystery of the five elements and their spiritual operations. And then afterward, so that I might point out to the reader, lifted up on the wings of justice, that it is just as possible for me, Robert, to reject and belittle the laws of Johannes’s Harmony with closed eyes and a hardened heart, as it is for you, Johannes, to refute his or treat them with injury. For it is not difficult for one who is willing to fashion those lies—supported by the most perverse opinions and arguments with some semblance of probability—and things least consistent with the truth, which might seem either new to the ear or lofty beyond the reach of common thought. Nevertheless, if these are inspected a little more closely, they are felt to be not only evident in discovery but also possible in action—indeed, easy.*

*Therefore, you have tried to overthrow the bases of Robert’s physical harmony with mathematical cunning and subtlety. If indeed I were to attack and tear down those laws of Johannes with equal skill and reasons gushing from the storehouse of nature by a mutual effort, would anyone think that Johannes, roused to unjust indignation, would rage beyond measure (a habit familiar to scholars wrestling with wordy swords in the fortress of Minerva),* <note>original: *in castro Minervae*. Minerva was the Roman goddess of wisdom and war; Fludd suggests the academic world is a battlefield of wits.</note> *when it is considered fair and honorable for like to be returned for like? But at the end of your Defense,* <note>original: *Apologia*. Refers to Kepler’s "Apologia" against Fludd published in 1622.</note> *you say you were irritated by frequent injuries, and had cause for burning with anger everywhere. By Hercules, you are singing my song! I, who struck you only once while you were striking me twice, and having now been hit by a double blow, would have had a double cause for anger compared to your single one—had I not listened to you yourself on page 50, politely asserting that the wit and jokes of disputants were merely "sauces."* <note>original: *embammata*. A Greek term for a zesty sauce or condiment; Kepler used the term to downplay his insults as mere academic seasoning.</note>

*Just as I have now been present as a guest at two Philosophical Banquets with you, I discover there that the arrangements of the feast are not so much prepared with physical harmony as they are seasoned with the bitterness of words. So also I feel you—though invited only once to my feast and table—are burdened by indignation and boredom rather than by the food, even with a smiling palate. Thus I see both of us are inclined toward peace and quiet. Therefore, with the dishes and vessels of malevolence quickly removed, and the harmful cups of this enmity banished into exile beyond the Hyperborean mountains,* <note>original: *ultra montes hyperboreos*. In mythology, a land of perfect sunshine in the far north; here used to mean sending something as far away as possible.</note> *we at last...*

*Thus appeased, let us conclude these complaints—or rather, if you please, these philosophical brawls. Let us approach friendship according to your wish; let us bind it together and cherish it, even though it began from a dispute, albeit a philosophical one. Let us, I say, hold its reins most loosely, and so that I may correspond almost word for word to your speech delivered on page 50: Indeed, through that composure of yours, look, you find me softened and appeased. Nor do I see (provided you act with fairness and goodness) what will hinder us in the future from cultivating a literary friendship* <term>literary friendship (amicitia literaria): a formal intellectual bond and correspondence between scholars, common in the Renaissance "Republic of Letters."</term> *and that whatever talent anyone has received from God* <note>A reference to the biblical Parable of the Talents in Matthew 25, implying intellectual gifts must be used for God's glory.</note>*, he may bestow it to the praise of the Creator and for the education of his neighbor, without disparaging another. Whatever well-written things one finds in the other, he should accept and enjoy with a grateful heart, honoring the spirit of the Giver. For truly, I judge him worthy of suffering the most extreme consequences—if indeed there is anything more extreme than the last—who, without any compulsion or provocation, delights in preferring quarrels and contentions over the peaceful contemplation of God and Nature. Therefore, whichever of us attempts in the future to resist the rules of this friendship previously proposed by you, let him be deservedly struck down. But in the meantime, I want you to know this: just as I have resolved not to think of anything to your ruin hereafter, nor to act against the laws of your Harmony* <note>original: *Harmoniae*. Referring to Johannes Kepler’s book, *Harmonices Mundi* (The Harmony of the World).</note> *so also may God grant me better than that, through even the slightest negligence on my part, any small part of either my Harmony or my Physical Works* <note>original: *Operum meorum Physicorum*. Fludd refers to his own massive volumes on the history of the macrocosm and microcosm.</note> *should suffer even a tiny bit of unworthiness or the sting of insult. On the contrary, if anything in your Defense* <note>original: *Apologia*.</note> *looks unfavorably upon any part of them, I will not cease to wash it away entirely with arguments drawn from the fountain of truth (with your leave, and with the bonds of our friendship remaining intact). In which business, nevertheless, I hope Johannes will admit the effectiveness: that Robert, in this Reply of his* <margin>☛</margin> *desires neither to twist things toward a calm appearance for his own sake (as he says on page 4), nor to hurl them back at anyone except the one who strikes first.*

# REPLY TO THE APOL-
# OGY TO ANAL. I.
<note>original: "REPLICATIO IN APOLOGIAM AD ANAL. I." This is Fludd’s point-by-point response to Johannes Kepler’s "Apologia," in which Kepler had criticized Fludd's mystical approach to cosmic harmony.</note>

**W**hether Johannes <note>Johannes Kepler (1571–1630), the famous astronomer.</note> seems to accuse me of ignorance in his Comparison, even if done secretly, I gladly leave to you, Reader, who weighs all things in the scales of justice. And who in this deceitful world is found so just, and so sincere in the truth of the matter, that when comparing his own work with another's, he does not usually prefer his own, or place his own below another’s, however much more excellent it might be? Johannes made a voluntary comparison between his Harmony and that of Robert <note>Robert Fludd (1574–1637), the author of this text.</note>. What then is more to be expected than that Johannes, being closest to himself, should grant extraordinary honors to his own Harmony, but should contaminate Robert’s with the foulest opinions, and attempt through his mathematical tricks to make those things appear as lies which are most truly put forward by us, and are felt to be easy in the doing. But now, the outcome will prove the deeds. For

Pag. 41. he bursts forth into these words: 
> *I have uncovered more of the divine works in my harmonies than you have in yours, you who indulge in private conceptions of the world, confirmed by no sense.* <note>Kepler is criticizing Fludd for relying on mystical intuition rather than sensory observation and mathematical data.</note>

But because I would by no means wish the Reader to be burdened by a multitude of pages on my account, I here begin to recite and re-examine in a few words those portions of Johannes's Comparison that cast I know not what errors upon me. By these, it may be more clearly shown to the benevolent and just Reader that a just occasion has been offered to me for repelling the insults of my attacker, and for washing and wiping away my dignity—besmirched by the exposures of envy—as if with the waves of truth. These are his words:

> Text. 10. *For this reason, in his work there are mostly pictures; in mine, there are mathematical diagrams provided with letters.*

What? Or why this comparison between pictures and mathematical diagrams, unless it were to intimate to the world from this the vanity of pictures and the utility of mathematical diagrams?

> Text. 11. *Let [the reader] see that he delights most in the dark riddles of things, while I strive to bring forth those very things, wrapped in obscurity, into the Light of the intellect. The former is indeed familiar to Chemists, Hermeticists, and Paracelsians; the latter is the property of Mathematicians.*

<term>Hermeticists: followers of a philosophical and religious tradition based on writings attributed to Hermes Trismegistus, focusing on the relationship between the divine, the cosmos, and humanity.</term>
<term>Paracelsists: followers of the Swiss physician Paracelsus, who emphasized chemistry and mystical correspondences in medicine.</term>

Here it indeed seems to be made known to the world that my Harmony is useless for this reason: because it is wrapped in dark riddles. And thus, he unjustly tries to distract the learned from my Harmony with his most perverse opinions; but indeed, so that his own might be held in respect and honor, he claims that it is open, clear, and entirely devoid of parabolic shadows <note>parabolic shadows: a reference to allegorical or metaphorical teaching that hides a secret meaning.</note>.

Text 12. *Where he treats the same subject matter as I do, this is the difference between us: he takes from the ancients, while I extract from the nature of things and establish it from the very foundations. He uses what he has received in a confused manner because of the varying opinions of those who passed them down, and he uses them uncorrected. I proceed by a natural order, so that all things are amended according to the laws of nature and confusion is avoided.* <note>Kepler is claiming that Fludd merely repeats the errors of old authors (like the Neoplatonists or Alchemists), while Kepler himself works from the "laws of nature."</note>

He seems to preach much about himself in this place, most cherished Reader, but if you look at the matter a little more closely, you will feel that while mountains are promised, scarcely hillocks are produced. <note>Fludd is using a variation of a classical idiom: "The mountains are in labor, and a ridiculous mouse is born."</note> Meanwhile, regarding me (his cause having been minimally heard or even utterly ignored by him), how harsh a judge and how unfair an arbiter he is can be gathered from what was said above.

Text 13. *Where I expressly refute the ancients who differ on the causes of harmonies, he follows them without any risk of doubt; he does not even think of the truer causes.*

Go ahead then, and fix the wordy trophy of your victory; but meanwhile, I warn you to be careful, lest you be deceived by your incomplete knowledge of the causes of harmony. Indeed, I perceive clearly enough from your writings that you have not yet tasted the sweetness of the Heliconian springs, nor have you ever visited Parnassus, the seat of the Muses. <note>Fludd is accusing Kepler of being a dry mathematician who lacks the "divine inspiration" or deeper mystical insight (represented by the Muses' home, Mt. Parnassus) required to understand the universe.</note> But go on, I say, with your Comparison, which (on page 5 of this work) you delight in calling a comparison and *not an accusation.*

Text 14. *Where he introduces Music into the world. Here indeed there is a massive difference between us. First, those Harmonies he intends to teach are mere symbolisms; regarding which I say the same as I do of the symbolisms of Ptolemy: they are poetic or oratorical rather than Philosophical or Mathematical.* <note>Kepler compares Fludd to the ancient astronomer Ptolemy, suggesting both use "harmonies" as pretty metaphors rather than hard physical laws.</note>

I see, by Hercules, that Johannes holds my Harmony to be poetic symbolism rather than philosophy for this reason: because he observed that its basis is that Chaos alleged by Ovid, and the Waters, Light, and Darkness so often sung of by Homer and Hesiod—yes, and even by Virgil. But no wise man will judge that there is therefore no Chaos, no waters, nor Light, nor darkness, simply because they were celebrated by the Poets. For indeed, all Philosophers, both pagan and Christian, confess that these are the original subjects of Physics <note>In this era, "Physics" referred to the general study of the natural, material world.</note>. What, then, prevents the foundations of my Harmonies—even if they are regarded as symbolisms—from being philosophical symbolisms? Are they not more so than those of Johannes, which are founded upon a "circle in the soul" and its divisions? He proves this must necessarily follow (on pages 16 and 17) because the soul is the "image of God," and thus he seems to say that all geometric figures are derived in a plane. Is anything more symbolic and parabolic than this? For the Soul is called a Circle, and even God himself is so called, but in an analogical and plainly metaphorical manner.

Yet I do not wish or desire to take anything away from the laws of Johannes’s Harmony because of this, since number is also said to have been the principal exemplar in the mind of the Creator for things to be created. But in what way? Namely, insofar as through number, He is declared to be infinite—for nothing is more infinite than number. And also through number, the immutability of God is declared, insofar as nothing is more immutable than number and its properties. Number therefore suggests a simple God, insofar as He is immutable; it suggests a wise God, insofar as—

<header>In Response to the Apology, Analysis 1.</header>

...foreknowing, it implies the incomprehensible; being infinite, it implies the wonderful and the singular, as it consists of these three. And this is the reason why Theology chose the ternary number <note>The number three.</note> as the primary and most perfect, wishing to express God as the first form of all things, arguing from this that He is the origin of all forms and of Light. Furthermore, His greatness is explained through number; hence, mathematicians assign the triangle to Him, as this figure cannot be resolved into others, nor is it subject to any prior principles. Instead, the triangle is the beginning in the composition of all geometric figures and the end in their resolution, since the square, the pentagon, and all other figures proceed from it and return into it. It is, therefore, like the primary form, from which matter received its being, and without which it cannot exist. Furthermore, form is incapable of receiving "accidents" <term>accidents: in philosophy, these are non-essential qualities of a thing, like color or weight, which can change without the thing itself changing its nature</term>. From these points, it is clear how God—or the Mind itself (which participates in God)—is called by the Sages a number, or a circle, or a triangle, not because He truly is such a thing, but through a likeness and by way of parabolic speech. Therefore, if the symbol of my *Harmony* is of such a kind, it is indeed a philosophical one, just as I admit that Johannes’s symbol is a mathematical one; and this, I hope, is without injury either to his *Harmony* or to mine.

### *In the Defense against Analysis VI.*

Tones can be called dissonant intervals in one respect, but consonant in another; therefore, I have not denied the assertion of the ancients in this matter. For according to **Guido the Monk** <note>Guido of Arezzo (c. 991–1033), a foundational figure in Western music theory</note>, Consonance is, as it were, a "sounding-together" <note>original: *simul consonantia*</note>. Unless two voices sound at the same time, it cannot be a Consonance. Yet the tone is counted among the Consonances, of which he himself identifies eight. Furthermore, musical practice has taught me that a low string produces a sound with a **B-flat** <note>original: *B. molli*, literally "soft B"</note> and another with **B-natural** <note>original: *B. duro*, "hard B"</note>; and although that meeting of sounds may be somewhat rough and harsh, nevertheless, through a sudden leaning toward **identity** <note>the resolution of different notes into a shared harmonic state</note>, the agreement is made pleasant. Moreover, we see two tones of the same interval produce sounds that differ in number. Nor am I playing with ambiguity here (as you claim) since both the tone and the interval are sounds and harmonize with one another; for otherwise, a Tone would not be called a consonance, and thus one tone sounds identically with another tone. Nor do I argue that there is a difference in the *kind* of interval between one tone and another—since I have said that the tone of one string, corresponding to another by the identity of proportion, creates a unison symphony—but rather they differ in **number**. For this reason, I affirmed that it is a consonant interval in one respect, even though in another respect it may be dissonant. ## In the Defense, Analysis XII

I will briefly show the reader what I observed in my youth (contrary to the opinion of **John** <note>original: "Ioannis"; referring to Johannes Kepler, Fludd’s contemporary and rival, who also theorized about the Platonic solids but disagreed with Fludd's mystical interpretations.</note>) regarding those things, and how much profit I had drawn from them. Regarding the **regular bodies** <term>Platonic solids</term> <note>The five symmetrical 3D shapes (tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, and icosahedron) that the Greeks believed were the building blocks of the universe.</note>, we have noted—according to the opinion of the best mathematicians—that five of their shapes correspond to the parts of the world, namely to the heaven and the <margin>4.</margin> elements. We recognized that three of these are formed from triangular surfaces, the fourth from squares, and finally the fifth from flat pentagons. Indeed, beyond these specific shapes, no others are found by mathematicians in geometry, because no **solid angle** <term>vertex</term> <note>The point where the corners of the flat shapes meet to form a 3D corner. For a shape to be "solid" and not flat, the sum of the angles meeting at the point must be less than 360 degrees.</note> can equal or exceed four right surface angles (360 degrees), as is found more extensively in Book 11, Proposition 21 of Euclid’s *Elements*.

Therefore, the triangles that meet to form a [solid] angle must be either three, four, or five. For in an equilateral triangle, each angle certainly contains 60 degrees; from this it follows that three angles of this condition constitute 180 degrees, which is less than 4 right angles; consequently, four angles of this kind produce 240 degrees, and five likewise make 300 degrees. One must not proceed further in these angles, because six such angles produce 360 degrees, which is equivalent to four right angles—a thing which certainly does not suit the nature of a solid angle <note>If the angles added up to 360, the paper would stay flat and could not fold into a 3D shape.</note>.

From the meeting of these angles, therefore, three figures are produced, the first of which is the **Tetrahedron**, which is clearly a pyramid of four triangular bases.
