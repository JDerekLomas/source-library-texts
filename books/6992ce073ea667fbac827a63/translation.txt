# Methods for Painting Rocks
<note>original: "Hua shi fa." In Chinese aesthetics, rocks are not static objects but are seen as the structural framework of the world. Artists were taught to capture their "breath-resonance" (qi) by using specific stroke patterns.</note>

## Alum-head Texture Strokes
<note>original: "Fantou Cun." This technique involves creating clusters of small, rounded, blocky shapes that resemble crystals of potash alum (a mineral often used in traditional crafts). This method is traditionally used to depict the weathered, rounded peaks of the softer mountains found in Southern China, a style perfected by the 10th-century master Juran.</note>

## Large Axe-cut Texture Strokes
<note>original: "Da Fupi." These strokes are made by using the side of the brush to create broad, sweeping, and triangular marks. The name comes from their resemblance to the scars left by an axe chopping into wood. This technique is ideal for representing hard, jagged rock faces and cliffs, and is a hallmark of the "Northern School" of landscape painting.</note>

## Raindrop Texture Strokes
<note>original: "Yudian Cun." These are composed of tiny, vertical dabs of the brush, often compared to sesame seeds. By layering thousands of these small dots, the artist creates a sense of immense weight and the rugged, granular texture of sheer rock walls. This style is most famously associated with the monumental landscapes of the Song Dynasty master Fan Kuan.</note>

## Small Axe-cut Texture Strokes
<note>original: "Xiao Fupi." A more controlled and delicate variation of the axe-cut method. It utilizes the same side-brush technique but on a smaller scale, allowing the artist to define the intricate crags and shadows of smaller boulders or the detailed edges of a mountain range.</note>

<note>The term <term>Cun<gloss>texture strokes</gloss></term> refers to the various interior brushmarks used to give volume, texture, and light to the outlines of mountains and rocks. They are the essential vocabulary of the landscape painter.</note>

# Horse-tooth Hooks
<note>original: "Maya Gou." These are sharp, angular outlines used to define the primary structure of rocks. The name refers to the blocky, jagged appearance of the strokes, which resemble the large, flat teeth of a horse. This technique provides the "skeleton" of the rock before more subtle textures are applied.</note>

# Hemp-fiber Texture Strokes
<note>original: "Mapi Cun." Also known as "Hemp-skin" texture, this is perhaps the most influential technique in the Southern School of painting. These are long, slightly wavy, and overlapping lines that resemble the unraveled strands of a hemp rope. They are ideally suited for depicting the soft, rounded, and soil-rich mountains common in Southern China, creating a sense of gentle rhythmic flow.</note>

# Tree-bark Texture Strokes
<note>original: "Shupi Cun." This technique mimics the rough, cracked, and weathered surface of an old tree. The strokes are irregular and broken, used to represent rocks with deep fissures or a coarse, ancient quality. It provides a more rugged and rustic feel than the flowing hemp-fiber style.</note>

# Sesame-seed Texture Strokes
<note>original: "Zhima Cun." A variation of the granular dotting technique, these strokes consist of small, dense dabs of ink. While similar to the "Raindrop" strokes, these are often smaller and more scattered, mimicking the appearance of sesame seeds. They are used to build up density and volume, giving the viewer a sense of the gritty, solid mass of a mountain face.</note>

<term>Texture Strokes (Cunfa)</term>: The collective term for the brushwork techniques used to "flesh out" the internal surfaces of rocks and mountains, giving them three-dimensional volume and tactile quality.
<term>Horse-tooth Hooks (Maya Gou)</term>: A method of outlining rock edges using bold, tooth-like angles.

<term>Tree-bark Strokes (Shupi Cun)</term>: Craggy, irregular strokes that mimic weathered bark.
<term>Sesame-seed Strokes (Zhima Cun)</term>: Fine, granular dots used to represent the surface texture of dense stone.

## Long Axe-cut Texture Strokes
<note>original: "Chang Fupi." These are sharp, elongated vertical strokes that sweep down the rock face like the marks left by a heavy axe splitting wood. This technique is used to depict the sheer, angular surfaces of hard, crystalline mountain cliffs, emphasizing their monumental strength.</note>

## Rolling Cloud Texture Strokes
<note>original: "Juan Yun Cun." Characterized by rounded, swirling brushwork that resembles rising mist or curling clouds. This technique is famously associated with the Northern Song master Guo Xi and is used to render weathered, eroded rocks that appear organic, fluid, and full of movement.</note>

---

## Chisel-mark Texture Strokes
<note>original: "Zao Hen Cun." These consist of small, sharp, staccato strokes that mimic the indentations left by a stonecutter's chisel. This method is effective for showing the dense, pitted, and rugged surface of ancient stone or rocky outcrops.</note>

## Straight Texture and Rubbing
<note>original: "Zhi Cun Ca." This technique utilizes parallel vertical strokes combined with dry-brush rubbing. The combination allows the artist to define the steep verticality of a mountain face while suggesting a rough, weathered surface through the "rubbing" (*ca*) of the brush against the paper.</note>

<term>Texture Strokes (Cunfa)</term>: The specialized brushwork techniques used in Chinese landscape painting to provide "flesh" and texture to the "bones" (outlines) of rocks and mountains.

<term>Rubbing (Ca)</term>: A method where the brush is used with very little ink or water, often held at a slanted angle, to create a grainy or rough surface effect that complements the main texture lines.

### Flame-Style Branches
<gloss>Flame branches</gloss>
<note>This technique uses upward, flickering strokes that mimic the movement of fire, often used to depict deciduous trees in late autumn or early winter when their fine twigs appear sharp against the sky.</note>

### Sparrow-Claw Branches
<gloss>Sparrow claws</gloss>
<note>A classic method where small clusters of twigs radiate from a single point like the foot of a bird. This style is famously associated with the "Level Distance" landscapes of the Song Dynasty.</note>

---

### Clove-Style Branches
<gloss>Clove-style branching</gloss>
<note>original: "Dingxiang." These are short, blunt, or slightly rounded strokes resembling the buds of a clove flower, used to suggest the emergence of new growth or the dense clusters of certain flowering shrubs.</note>

### Trailing Branches
<gloss>Trailing branches</gloss>
<note>Sometimes called "Dragging" or "Drooping" branches, these long, downward-sweeping lines are used for willow trees or the heavy, weathered limbs of ancient pines.</note>

# Upward Mantis Style
<gloss>Upward-reaching mantis</gloss>
<note>original: "Yang Tanglang." This technique describes tree branches with sharp, angular crooks that resemble the articulated, raised forelegs of a praying mantis. It is used to give a tree a sense of structural strength and upward energy.</note>

# Drooping Silk Style
<gloss>Hanging silk threads</gloss>
<note>original: "Chui Si." This is the standard classical method for painting the long, swaying branches of the weeping willow. The brushwork must be fluid and continuous to capture the grace of silk threads blowing in the wind.</note>

---

# Downward Mantis Style
<gloss>Downward-reaching mantis</gloss>
<note>original: "Fu Tanglang." A variation of the "mantis" joint, where the angular branches point toward the ground. This style is often employed for the heavy, low-hanging limbs of ancient deciduous trees or those weighted by seasonal changes.</note>

# Clustered Needle Style
<gloss>Gathered needles</gloss>
<note>original: "Cuan Zhen." This is the foundational brushwork for pine trees. It involves "gathering" or clustering sharp, radiating lines around a central point to form the characteristic fan-like or circular needles of the pine.</note>

# Faults to Avoid in Bamboo Painting
<note>original: 畫竹所忌 (huà zhú suǒ jì). In Chinese painting manuals, these "taboos" are essential pedagogical tools, teaching students to avoid mechanical or unnatural shapes that disrupt the "Spirit Resonance" of the work.</note>

<term>Nail-head</term>
<note>original: 釘頭 (dīng tóu). This refers to a stroke fault where the beginning of a line—specifically at the joints of the bamboo—is too heavy, blunt, and square, resembling the flat head of a nail. It lacks the fluid, tapering energy required for a living stalk.</note>

<term>The character 'Small' (Xiǎo)</term>
<note>original: 小字 (xiǎo zì). This describes a leaf arrangement that too literally mimics the Chinese character for "small" (小). While bamboo leaves are often grouped in threes, the artist is cautioned against making them look like rigid calligraphy rather than organic foliage.</note>

<term>The character 'Individual' (Gè)</term>
<note>original: 个字 (gè zì). Similar to the "Small" character fault, this refers to leaf clusters that look exactly like the character "individual" or "piece" (个). A repetitive "ge" pattern makes the bamboo look mechanical and artificial.</note>

<term>Hourglass Drum</term>
<note>original: 杖鼓 (zhàng gǔ). This is a fault in painting the joints (nodes) of the bamboo. If the joint is drawn too narrow in the middle and flared at the ends, it resembles a traditional hourglass-shaped drum. This makes the bamboo look structurally weak and unnatural.</note>

<term>Side-pressure</term>
<note>original: 邊壓 (biān yā). A compositional error where the leaves or branches are awkwardly crowded or "pressed" against the edge of the frame or another element, stifling the sense of space and "breath" within the painting.</note>

<column-break/>

<note>The illustrations on this page act as a "negative model." By showing the "Nail-head" joint and the overly literal "Small" and "Individual" leaf patterns, the manual teaches the student that while <term>Ink Bamboo Painting</term> <note>original: 墨竹 (mòzhú)</note> is closely related to calligraphy, it must never lose its connection to the organic vitality of the natural world. The goal is to capture the *idea* of the character's structure without the stiffness of the printed word.</note>

## Further Faults in Bamboo Painting

<term>Drum Stand</term>
<note>original: 皷架 (gǔ jià). This refers to a compositional error where three or more bamboo stalks intersect or are arranged in a manner that resembles the tripod base of a traditional Chinese drum. In nature, bamboo stalks rarely converge so symmetrically; such a formation in a painting creates a rigid, artificial focal point that disrupts the organic rhythm of the grove.</note>

<column-break/>

<term>Aligned Joints</term>
<note>original: 對節 (duì jié). This fault occurs when the nodes—the ringed joints of the bamboo—of two or more adjacent stalks are painted at the exact same horizontal level. This creates a "ladder" effect that is visually unappealing and unnatural. A skilled painter will stagger the height of the nodes to suggest depth, perspective, and the spontaneous growth of the plants.</note>

<note>This page is part of a larger *Painting Manual* (畫譜 - huà pǔ), a genre of instructional book that became highly popular during the Ming and Qing dynasties for disseminating artistic techniques to amateurs and scholars alike.</note>

# On Drawing the Stalks
<term>Hua gan</term>
<note>original: 畫竿. This section details the "bones" of the bamboo. In Chinese aesthetics, the stalk represents the integrity and upright character of the scholar-gentleman.</note>

When drawing stalks, if you are only painting one or two, the <term>ink tone</term><note>original: 墨色 (mo se). This refers to the five shades of black achievable through water dilution, used to create depth and atmosphere.</note> may be applied according to your preference. However, if there are three or more stalks, those in the foreground must be dark, while those in the background should gradually become lighter. If the ink tone is uniform, it will be impossible to distinguish between front and back. 

Furthermore, although the stalks are painted segment by segment from the tip down to the root, the <term>brush intent</term><note>original: 筆意 (bi yi). A fundamental concept in Chinese art where the mental energy and movement of the artist continue even when the brush leaves the paper.</note> must remain continuous throughout. The segments at the tip should be short, gradually growing longer in the middle, and then becoming shorter again as they reach the base of the root. 

Every segment requires the ink tone to be even and steady. The movement of the brush must be straight and level, so that both sides of the stalk appear naturally rounded and upright. If the strokes are swollen, crooked, or if the ink tone is uneven—alternating between thick and thin, or dry and saturated—or if the gaps at the nodes are drawn inconsistently long or short, these are all violations of <term>artistic principles</term><note>original: 文法 (wen fa). While this word means "grammar" in modern Chinese, here it refers to the established "laws" or "syntax" of classical painting techniques.</note> and must never be committed. 

One often sees vulgar, amateurish painters using mats made of reed or locust bark, or even folded paper soaked in ink to "stamp" the stalks. These methods result in stalks that are the same thickness from root to tip; they look flat and entirely lack any sense of roundness. Such work is truly laughable. Students must be strictly careful not to imitate these shortcuts.

## 5. Tracing with Ink

When holding the brush, you must settle the mind and quiet your thoughts; the **intent must precede the brushstroke** <note>original: 意在筆先 (yi zai bi xian). A foundational principle in Chinese aesthetics where the artist must have a clear mental image and spiritual focus before the brush even touches the paper.</note>. The spirit and mind must be singular in focus—neither wandering nor falling into chaos.

Only then should you set the brush to paper. The stroke must be **rounded and vigorous, swift and sharp**. Yet, it must not be *too* fast; if it is too fast, you will lose the proper momentum and "posture" of the plant. Nor can it be too slow; if it is too slow, the ink will become sluggish and muddy. Furthermore, the stroke must not be too "fat"; if it is fat, it appears vulgar and crude. Nor should it be too thin; if it is thin, it will look withered and weak. 

The start and finish of every stroke must be precise, and the "coming and going"—the direction of the brush—must follow the natural flow or the necessary counter-flow of the plant’s growth. One must examine these movements carefully. 

For example:
*   When drawing **leaves**, seek **gentleness** within your strength and sharpness.
*   When drawing **stalks**, seek **uprightness** within their grace and charm.
*   When drawing **nodes**, ensure that even where there is a physical break in the ink, the parts remain **connected in spirit**.
*   When **inserting branches**, you must maintain **structural strength** <term>bone-strength</term><note>original: 骨力 (gu li). A term used to describe the internal structural integrity and "nerve" of a brushstroke, suggesting it has a skeleton supporting the ink.</note> even within the softness of the form.

Carefully observe the four seasons—the flourishing and the withered, the old and the young—and let the brush follow your will. Naturally, the branches and leaves will be lively, and the <term>breath of life</term><note>original: 生意 (sheng yi). Literally "life-idea," referring to the vital, creative energy that makes a depiction look truly alive rather than like a dead copy.</note> will be fully present. If one waits until the **application of color** to make the image look like bamboo, then it can no longer be considered a true painting.

# Yang Buzhi’s Treatise on Painting Plum Blossoms
<note>Yang Buzhi (1097–1169), also known as Yang Wujiu, was a famous Southern Song dynasty artist. He was renowned for his "ink plums," which favored elegant ink washes over the bright colors popular in the royal courts of his time.</note>

There are secrets to painting the plum. Above all, you must first **establish your intent**. When you set the brush to paper, move with **vigor and speed**, as if caught in a moment of madness or frenzy. Your hand should move like **streaks of lightning**; you must never pause or delay.

The branches should move in sweeping turns, some growing straight and others ending abruptly. Use the "Departing Phoenix" technique <note>original: "離鸞" (lí luán). A poetic name for a specific brush stroke that starts heavy and ends with a light, tapering flick, resembling a bird taking flight.</note> to sprinkle dark and light ink. Do not allow yourself to go back and fill in colors later. The roots should not have redundant bends, and one must avoid cluttering the tips of the flowers.

New branches are like **willows**, while old branches resemble **whips**. When painting the "bow-tips" and "deer-horns" <note>technical terms for specific branch configurations</note>, they must be as straight as a bowstring. A branch curved into a bow shape is called an "overturned fishing rod." 

Water sprouts <gloss>qitiao (氣條)</gloss> have no sepals; their base points straight toward the heavens. Withered sections should feature **protruding eyes** <note>referring to knots in the wood</note>, and supporting branches should appear to truly pierce through the composition.

***

### Rules of Composition

*   **Avoid the Cross:** Branches should not form a "plus" (+) shape <note>This is a common rule in Chinese painting to ensure the composition looks natural rather than geometric.</note>.
*   **Avoid Perfection:** Flowers should not be painted in full, identical bloom.
*   **Directional Flow:** Arranging branches to the left is easy, but directing them to the right is difficult.
*   **The Pinky’s Role:** To succeed, rely entirely on the small finger to guide the momentum of the brush and lead the placement of spots.
*   **Space and Spirit:** Leave "empty eyes" (gaps) along the branches; place the flowers within these spaces. When you add the flower’s companions, the **spirit of the plum** <gloss>huashen (花神)</gloss> will naturally complete itself.

***

### The Character of Age

Tender branches should be **lush with flowers** <note>The OCR reads "獸" (beast), but in the context of painting manuals and Yang Buzhi's original poem, this is almost certainly a transcription error for "繁" (lush/abundant).</note>, while old branches should be **sparse**. When the tree is neither young nor old, the "intent" of the blossoms should be lingering and continuous. 

Follow the laws of age and youth to distinguish between the growth of new and old years. Use the **Crane’s Knee** technique for bulging joints and **Dragon’s Scales** for the mottled spots of old bark.

<term>Crane’s Knee (鶴膝): A brush technique that creates a thickened, joint-like knot in the branch, suggesting the sturdy anatomy of a crane's leg.</term>
<term>Dragon’s Scales (龍鱗): Rough, circular brushwork used to depict the textured, peeling bark of an ancient tree.</term>

The branches should seem to "embrace" the main body of the tree, and the tips should appear unified and natural. For the calyx <note>the green outer part of the flower</note>, use **three distinct dots**, ensuring they are always connected to the stem.

The front sepals consist of five dots; the rear sepals are represented by circles. For withered branches, avoid "double eyes" <note>likely referring to small nodes or growth points that might appear too lively on dead wood</note>. Among all flowers, the plum is unique for its subtle fragrance and its "jade-like" countenance. A single blossom may stand alone at the highest peak. At the very top, place the tips of the branches; some should be sharp like the thorns of a wild pear <note>original: "刺棃" (cìlí)</note>, while others should resemble a horse-whip. The gnarled knots on the trunk, known as "leper-bumps" <note>original: "癩包" (làibāo), referring to the characteristic textured protuberances of old plum wood</note>, have many names. The quality of the blossoms follows the character of the tree. Do not make the trunk overly large. In general, the twists and turns are varied; by following this model, you will create a magnificent sight.

The plum tree possesses a trunk, branches, nodes, and roots; it has thorns and is often covered in moss. Sometimes it is planted within gardens and orchards, sometimes it grows atop rugged cliffs, and other times it is found clinging to fences. Because the locations where they grow are so diverse, the physical structure of their branches and bodies differs accordingly.

Furthermore, there are variations in the number of petals: five, four, or six. Generally, the five-petaled blossom is considered the "correct" or standard form. Those with four or six petals are known as **"Thorn Plums"** <note>original: "棘梅" (jíméi)</note>. These are considered the result of an imbalance in the "breath" of Nature <note>original: "造化" (zàohuà), referring to the creative force of the universe</note>—a lean toward either excess or deficiency.

Regarding the branches:
There are old and tender ones, curved and straight ones, sparse and dense ones, those that are perfectly balanced, and those that appear ancient.

Regarding the twig-tips:
There are those shaped like the "Dipper's Handle" <note>referring to the Big Dipper constellation</note>, those like iron whips, those like "crane knees" <note>original: "鶴膝" (hèxī), a term for knobby, swollen joints in the wood</note>, those like dragon horns or deer antlers, and those resembling the tips of a bow or a fishing rod.

Regarding their form:
They may be large or small, facing away or covering forward, slanted or upright, curved or straight.

Regarding the flowers:
There are "peppercorns" <note>tight, round buds</note>, "crab eyes" <note>tiny, emerging buds</note>, "smiling" flowers <note>half-open buds</note>, full blooms, fading blossoms, and falling petals.

Their forms are never uniform, and their transformations are limitless. I desire to use only a brush and a mere inch of ink—without relying on colors—to capture their essential spirit <note>original: "精神" (jīngshén)</note>.
