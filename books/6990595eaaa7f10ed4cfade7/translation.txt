# AUTHOR’S PREFACE TO THE SECOND EDITION

In revising *Tertium Organum* <note>Latin: "The Third Organon" or "Third Instrument of Thought," referring to a system of logic intended to succeed those of Aristotle and Bacon</note> for the second edition in English, my chief concern has been to coordinate its terminology with the more developed vocabulary used in my books written after the publication of the second Russian edition of *Tertium Organum*, from which the English translation was made.

Such a unity of terminology is all the more necessary because I am obliged to lead the reader into regions of thought and knowledge where boundaries have not been clearly established. In these regions, different authors—and often the same author in different works or during different periods of his career—have called the same thing by different names, or different things by the same name.

It must be admitted that language is a weak and inadequate vehicle even for expressing our usual understanding of things. This is even truer during those moments when our understanding unexpectedly expands and deepens, revealing an entire series of facts and relations for which we have neither words nor expressions. But quite aside from this, even in ordinary conditions of thinking and feeling, we are frequently at a loss for words and use one word at different times to describe different things.

On the other hand, it is no credit to an author to invent new words or to use old words with new meanings that have nothing in common with their accepted definitions—to create, in other words, a private terminology. I have always considered it necessary to write in the language that people commonly speak, and I have endeavored to do this, although in some cases it has been necessary to make additions to and corrections of that language for the sake of exactness and clarity.

In due time, I shall separately consider the subject of language and the methods of adapting it for the transmission of exact thought. For the present, I am referring only to the language used in *Tertium Organum*.

# TERTIUM ORGANUM

The first word demanding a more careful use is “consciousness.”

In conversational language and in everyday psychology—even in psychology purporting to be scientific—the word consciousness is often used as a term to designate the complex of all psychic functions in general, or for their separate manifestations. At present I do not have access to the necessary books—I abandoned them all in Petrograd <note>Now St. Petersburg, Russia</note> four years ago—but to the best of my recollection, Professor William James <note>A pioneering American psychologist and philosopher (1842–1910)</note> defined thought as “a moment of consciousness.”

From my standpoint, which I shall clarify in works now being prepared for the press, it is necessary to regard consciousness as distinct from the commonly understood psychic functions: thought, feeling, and sensation. Over and above all this, consciousness has several exactly definable forms or phases. In each one of these, thoughts, feelings, and sensations can function, producing different results in each. Thus, consciousness (whether it is this or something else) is a background upon which thoughts, feelings, and sensations reveal themselves. This background can be more or less bright. 

But just as thoughts, feelings, and sensations have their own separate life and can be regarded independently of this background, so can the background be regarded and studied independently of them. For the present, I shall not insist too strongly upon the idea of this "ground" as something separate in its substance from psychic functions. The practical result is the same if we say that thoughts, feelings, and sensations may have a different character, and that thoughts, feelings, and sensations of this or that character create this or that state of consciousness. It is important only to establish the fact that thoughts, feelings, and sensations—that is, psychic functions—are not consciousness itself. Rather, this or that state of consciousness is something pertaining to them, but separate from them, and in some cases capable of being separately observed.

In the early editions of *Tertium Organum*, I used the word consciousness in its generally accepted meaning: as a complex of psychic functions, or in the sense of their indication and contents. But as it will be necessary for me to use the word consciousness in its real and true meaning in my future works, I have tried in this revised text of *Tertium Organum* to substitute for the word consciousness (wherever it is used in the sense of a complex of psychic functions)...

By naming his book *Tertium Organum* <note>Latin: "The Third Tool" or "The Third Canon of Thought"</note>, Ouspensky reveals at once that astounding boldness which characterizes his thought throughout—a boldness which we are accustomed to associate with the Russian mind in all its phases. After establishing the objective method and making the reader aware of its principles, he suddenly alters his system of attack. From chapter VIII onward, he undertakes the study of the world order from the standpoint of subjectivity—of consciousness.

By a method both ingenious and new, he correlates the different grades of consciousness observable in nature—those of plants/animals, animals, and man—with the sense of space. He shows that as consciousness changes and develops, the sense of space changes and develops as well. That is to say, the dimensionality of the world depends on the development of consciousness. Man, having reached the third stage in that development, has a sense of three-dimensional space—and for no other reason.

Ouspensky concludes that nothing except consciousness unfolds or develops. Since there appears to be no limit to this development, he conceives of space as the multi-dimensional mirror of consciousness. He views time and motion as illusions; what appears to be time and motion is, in reality, only the movement of consciousness upon a higher space.

The problem of superior states of consciousness, in which "there shall be time no longer," is thus directly opened up. Since the remotest antiquity, the question of our relation to the true causes of our sensations has constituted the main subject of philosophical research. Humans have always felt that they should have some solution for this question, some answer for it. These answers have vacillated between two poles: from the full negation of the causes themselves—the assertion that the causes of sensations are contained within ourselves and not in anything outside of us—up to the recognition that we know these causes, that they are embodied in the phenomena of the outer world, and that these phenomena constitute the cause of sensations. # KANT’S SYSTEM

In modern philosophy, Kant’s system occupies a middle position in relation to this problem of the causes of sensations, sharing neither of these extreme views. Kant proved that the causes of our sensations are in the outside world, but that we cannot know these causes through any sensuous <note>original: "sensuous"; here meaning relating to the physical senses</note> approach—that is, by such means as we know phenomena <note>original: "phenomena"; observable events or things as they appear to our senses</note>—and that we cannot *know these causes, and shall never know them*.

Kant established the fact that everything known through the senses is known in terms of time and space, and that outside of time and space, we cannot know anything by way of the senses. He argued that time and space are necessary conditions of sensuous receptivity (that is, the ability to receive information through the five organs of sense). Moreover, and most importantly, he established the fact that extension in space and existence in time are not properties belonging to things themselves, but are simply the properties of our own sensuous receptivity. In reality, apart from our sensuous knowledge of them, things exist independently of time and space; however, we can never perceive them outside of time and space. By perceiving things and phenomena sensuously, we naturally impose upon them the conditions of time and space, which belong to our own form of perception.

Thus, space and time—which define everything we recognize by sensuous means—are in themselves just forms of our receptivity, categories of our intellect, and the prism through which we regard the world. In other words, space and time do not represent properties of the world, but rather properties of our knowledge of the world gained through our physical senses. From this it follows that the world, apart from our knowledge of it, has neither extension in space nor existence in time; these are properties which we add to it.

Cognitions <note>original: "cognitions"; the mental action or process of acquiring knowledge</note> of space and time arise in our intellect during its contact with the external world by means of the organs of sense, and they do not exist in the external world apart from our contact with it.

Space and time are categories of the intellect—that is, properties which we ascribe to the external world. # PHYSICAL AND METAPHYSICAL FACTS 29

There are things and phenomena in the world that undoubtedly exist in reality, but they are quite impossible to measure in terms of length, breadth, and thickness; they lie, as it were, outside of three-dimensional space.

By *really existing*, we understand that which produces a definite action, possesses certain functions, and appears to be the cause of something else.

That which does not exist cannot produce any action, has no function, and cannot be a cause.

But there are different modes of existence. There is *physical existence*, recognized by certain types of actions and functions, and there is *metaphysical existence* <note>Metaphysical: relating to things that are believed to be real but cannot be seen or measured physically, such as thoughts, ethics, or the soul.</note>, recognized by its own actions and functions.

A *house* exists, and the *idea of good and evil* exists. But they do not exist in the same way. The same method used to prove the existence of a house is not sufficient to prove the existence of an idea. A house is a *physical fact*; an idea is a *metaphysical fact*. Physical and metaphysical facts both exist, but they exist differently.

In order to prove the idea of a division between good and evil—that is, a metaphysical fact—I only have to prove *its possibility*. Once possibility is established, the idea exists as a force. But if I were to prove that a house—a physical fact—*might* exist, it does not mean at all that it exists in reality. If I prove that a man *could* own a house, it is no proof that he actually owns it.

Our relationship to an idea is quite different from our relationship to a house. It is possible, through physical effort, to destroy a house—to burn it or wreck it. The house will then cease to exist. But suppose you attempt to destroy an idea through effort. The more you try to contest, argue against, refute, or ridicule it, the more the idea is likely to spread, grow, and strengthen. Conversely, silence, oblivion, inaction, and "non-resistance" will exterminate, or at least weaken, an idea. However, silence and oblivion will not wreck a house or hurt a stone.

What can we learn about the fourth dimension by studying the geometric relations within our own space? What should be the relationship be between a three-dimensional body and one of four dimensions? A four-dimensional body can be understood as the result of moving a three-dimensional body in a direction that is not contained within itself. If we consider the very great difference between the point and the line, between the line and the surface, and between the surface and the solid—that is, the difference between the laws to which a line and a plane, or a plane and a surface, are subjected, and the difference of phenomena possible in a point, a line, or a surface—we will indeed come to understand how much of the new and inconceivable the fourth dimension holds for us.

We learn this last point first of all. And it is especially important, because it saves us from many deeply ingrained illusions which are very harmful to true knowledge.

# SPACE RELATIONS

We learn what cannot exist in four-dimensional space, and this permits us to set forth what *can* exist there. In his book, *The Fourth Dimension*, <note>Charles Howard Hinton (1853–1907), an English mathematician and writer known for his work on the fourth dimension.</note> Hinton makes an interesting statement concerning the method by which we may approach the problem of higher dimensions. He says:

> Our space itself bears within it relations through which we can establish relations to other (higher) spaces.
> 
> For within space are given the concepts of point and line, line and plane, which really involve the relation of space to a higher space.

Let us consider these relations within our space and see what conclusions we can derive from their investigation. We know that our geometry regards the line as a tracing of the movement of a point; the surface as a tracing of the movement of a line; and the solid as a tracing of the movement of a surface. On these premises, we put to ourselves this question: Is it not possible to regard the "four-dimensional body" as a tracing of the movement of a three-dimensional body?

But what is this movement, and in what direction?

The *point*, moving in space and leaving the tracing of its movement—a line—moves in a direction not contained in it, because in a point there is no direction whatsoever.

The *line*, moving in space and leaving the tracing of its movement—the surface—moves in a direction not contained in it because, moving in a direction already contained in it, a line will continue to be a line.

The *surface*, moving in space and leaving a tracing of its movement—the solid—moves also in a direction not contained in it. If it should move otherwise, it would remain always a surface. In order to leave a tracing of itself as a "solid," or three-dimensional figure, it must set off from itself and move in a direction which it does not possess within itself.

By analogy with all this, the solid—in order to leave as the tracing of its movement the four-dimensional figure (<term>hypersolid</term> <note>A geometric figure in four or more dimensions; the four-dimensional equivalent of a three-dimensional solid.</note>)—must move in a direction not confined within it. In other words, it must come out of itself, set off from itself, and move in a direction which is not present in it. Later on, it will be shown in what manner we shall understand this.

# PLANE PROJECTIONS OF SOLIDS

# WHAT IS TIME?

Motion cannot proceed out of time. Consequently, before speaking of what motion is, we must answer the question: what is time?

Time is the most formidable and difficult problem which confronts humanity.

Immanuel Kant <note>German philosopher (1724–1804) who argued that the mind organizes experience through innate structures.</note> regards time as he does space: as a subjective form of our receptivity. That is, he says that we create time ourselves, as a function of our receptive apparatus, for convenience in perceiving the outside world. Reality is continuous and constant, but in order to make the perception of it possible, we must dissever <note>original: "dissever"; to break or slice into parts.</note> it into separate moments. We imagine it as an infinite series of separate moments, out of which only one exists for us. In other words, we perceive reality as if through a narrow slit. What we are seeing through this slit we call the **present**; what we did see and now do not see—the **past**; and what we do not quite see but are expecting—the **future**.

Regarding each phenomenon as an *effect* of another, or others, and this in its turn as a *cause* of a third—that is, regarding all phenomena in functional interdependence one upon another—by this very act we are contemplating them in time. We do this because we picture to ourselves quite clearly and precisely first a cause, then an effect; first an action, then its function. We cannot contemplate them otherwise. Thus, we may say that the idea of time is bound up with the idea of causation and functional interdependence. Without time, causation cannot exist, just as without time, motion (or the absence of motion) cannot exist.

But our perception concerning our “being in time” is entangled and misty to the point of improbability.

# TIME AS THE FOURTH DIMENSION

Uncertainty exists only concerning the future; concerning the past, all agree that it has passed, that it does not exist now—*and that it was exactly as it has been*. In this last point lies the key to understanding the incorrectness of our views of the future. As a matter of fact, in reality, our relation both to the past and to the future is far more complicated than it seems to us. In the past, behind us, lies not only that which *really happened*, but *that which could have been*. In the same way, in the future lies not only that which *will be*, but *everything that may be*.

The past and the future are equally undetermined; they both exist in all their possibilities, and both exist simultaneously with the present.

By "time," we mean the distance separating events in the order of their succession and binding them into different wholes. This distance lies in a direction not contained in three-dimensional space; therefore, it must be the new dimension of space.

*This new dimension satisfies all possible requirements of the fourth dimension based on the preceding reasoning.*

It is incommensurable <note>original: "incommensurable"; meaning it cannot be measured by the same standards or units</note> with the dimensions of three-dimensional space, just as a "year" cannot be measured against the physical city of St. Petersburg. It is perpendicular to all directions of three-dimensional space and is not parallel to any of them.

As a deduction from all the preceding points, we may say that time (*as it is usually understood*) includes within itself *two ideas*: that of a certain space unknown to us (the fourth dimension), and that of *motion* through this space. Our constant mistake consists in the fact that we never see these two ideas within time, but always see only one. Usually, we see in time the idea of *motion*, but we cannot say from where, to where, in what direction, or upon what space that motion occurs. Attempts have been made before now to unite the idea of the fourth dimension with the idea of time. However, in those theories which attempted to combine the two, there always appeared the idea of some spatial element existing in time, and along with it, the theory admitted *motion upon that space*. Those who were constructing these theories evidently did not understand that by leaving in the possibility of motion, they were actually requiring a "new" time, because motion cannot proceed outside of time. As a result, time stays ahead of us, like our shadow, receding as we move toward it.

If we place the five fingers of one hand on the plane of the two-dimensional being, they will appear to him as five separate *phenomena* <note>original: "phenomena" — here meaning observable events or objects appearing in his world</note>.

Let us try to imagine what an enormous mental evolution he would have to undergo in order to understand that these five separate phenomena on his plane are actually the fingertips of the hand of a large, active, and intelligent being—a man.

It would be interesting in the highest degree to trace, step by step, how the plane-being would attain an understanding of our world, which lies in the region of what is to him the mysterious third dimension—existing partly in his past and partly in his future. First of all, in order to understand the world of three dimensions, he **must**:

<margin>1.</margin> **Cease to be two-dimensional.** He must become three-dimensional himself; in other words, he must feel an interest in the life of three-dimensional space. After having felt the interest of this life, he will, by doing so, transcend his plane and will never be in a position to return to it. By entering more and more into the circle of ideas and concepts that were entirely incomprehensible to him before, he will have already become not two-dimensional, but three-dimensional. 

<margin>2.</margin> But all along, the *plane-being will have been* essentially three-dimensional; that is, he *will have had* the third dimension without being conscious of it himself. To become three-dimensional, he must *be* three-dimensional. 

<margin>3.</margin> Then, as the ultimate goal, he can apply himself to liberating himself from the illusion of the two-dimensionality of both himself and the world, and to the apprehension of the three-dimensional world.

The impossibility of the mathematical definition of dimensions. Why does mathematics not perceive dimensions? The entire conditionality of representing dimensions through mathematical powers. Now that we have studied those "relations which our space itself bears within it," we shall return to the questions: *But what, in reality, do the dimensions of space represent—and why are there three of them?*

The fact that it is impossible to define *three-dimensionality* mathematically must appear most strange.

We are little conscious of this, and it seems like a paradox to us because we speak of the dimensions of space; but it remains a fact that mathematics *does not perceive the dimensions of space.*

The question arises: how can such a fine instrument of analysis as mathematics not feel dimensions, if they represent real properties of space?

Speaking of mathematics, it is necessary to recognize first of all, as a fundamental premise, that *every mathematical expression always corresponds to the relation of some realities.*

If this is not the case—if it is not true—then mathematics does not exist. This is its principal substance, its primary content. The problem of mathematics is to express the correlations of magnitudes. But these correlations must exist between something. It must be possible to substitute some reality in place of the algebraic *a*, *b*, and *c*. This is the ABC of all mathematics; *a*, *b*, and *c* are like credit bills <note>original: "credit bills"; here meaning banknotes or promissory notes</note>; they are only valid if there is something real behind them, and they are counterfeit if they represent no reality whatsoever.

# TERTIUM ORGANUM

"Dimensions" play a very strange role here. If we designate them by the algebraic symbols *a*, *b*, and *c*, they have the character of counterfeit credit bills. For these symbols *a*, *b*, and *c*, it is impossible to substitute any real magnitudes that are capable of expressing the actual correlations between dimensions.

Usually, dimensions are represented by powers: the first, the second, and the third. That is, if a line is called *a*, then a square (the sides of which are equal to this line) is called *a²*, and a cube (the face of which is equal to this square) is called *a³*.

Among other things, this gave Charles Hinton <note>Charles Howard Hinton (1853–1907) was a British mathematician and writer known for his work on the fourth dimension.</note> the foundation on which he constructed his theory of <term>tesseracts</term> <note>A tesseract is a four-dimensional analogue of a cube; just as a cube is to a square, a tesseract is to a cube.</note>, or four-dimensional solids—*a⁴*. But this is pure fantasy. First of all, this is because the representation of "dimensions" by powers is entirely conditional. It is possible to represent all powers on a single line. For example, take a segment of a line equal to five millimeters; then a segment equal to twenty-five millimeters will be the square of it (i.e., *a²*), and a segment of one hundred and twenty-five millimeters will be the cube (*a³*).

How shall we understand the fact that mathematics does not "feel" dimensions—that it is impossible to express the qualitative difference between dimensions mathematically?

It can be understood and explained by one thing only—namely, that this difference does not exist.

We truly know that all three dimensions are identical in substance. It is possible to regard each of the three dimensions as following the sequence *the first, the second, the third*, or to order them the other way around. This alone proves that dimensions are not mathematical magnitudes. All the real properties of a thing can be expressed mathematically as quantities—that is, numbers showing the relation of these properties to other properties.

But in the matter of dimensions, it is as if mathematics sees more than we do, or farther than we do. It looks through certain boundaries that stop us but do not stop it—and it sees that no realities whatever correspond to our human concepts of dimensions.

If the three dimensions *really* corresponded to three powers, then we would have the right to say that only these three powers refer to geometry, and that all the other higher powers, beginning with the fourth, lie beyond the realm of geometry.

# NON-EUCLIDEAN GEOMETRY

But even this is denied us. The representation of dimensions by powers <note>referring to mathematical powers like $x^2$ or $x^3$</note> is perfectly arbitrary. More accurately, geometry, from the standpoint of mathematics, is an artificial system for solving problems based on *conditional data*, deduced, probably, from the properties of our psyche.

The system of investigation of "higher space" Hinton <note>Charles Howard Hinton (1853–1907), a British mathematician and writer known for his work on the fourth dimension</note> calls *metageometry*, and with metageometry he connects the names of Lobachevsky, Gauss, and other investigators of non-Euclidean geometry. We shall now consider in what relation the questions touched upon by us stand to the theories of these scientists.

Hinton deduces his ideas from Kant and Lobachevsky. Others, on the contrary, place Kant’s ideas in opposition to those of Lobachevsky. Thus Roberto Bonola, in *Non-Euclidean Geometry*, declares that Lobachevsky’s conception of space is contrary to that of Kant. He says:

> The Kantian doctrine considered space as a subjective intuition, a necessary presupposition of every experience. Lobachevsky’s doctrine was rather allied to sensualism <note>The philosophical theory that all knowledge is derived from the senses</note> and the current empiricism, and compelled geometry to take its place again among the experienced sciences.*

Which of these views is true, and in what relation do Lobachevsky’s ideas stand to our problem? The correct answer to this question is: in no relation. Non-Euclidean geometry is not *metageometry*, and non-Euclidean geometry stands in the same relation to metageometry as Euclidean geometry itself.

The results of non-Euclidean geometry, which have submitted the fundamental axioms of Euclid to a revaluation, and which have found the most complete expression in the works of Bolyai, Gauss, and Lobachevsky, are embraced in the formula:

> *The axioms of a given geometry express the properties of a given space.*

# THIRD ORGANUM

2. Any figure may be transferred into another position without changing its properties.
3. Parallel lines do not meet.
(This last axiom is formulated differently by Euclid.)

In geometry on a sphere, or on a concave surface, the first two axioms alone are true, because the meridians which are separated at the equator meet at the poles.

In geometry on the surface of irregular curvatures, only the first axiom is true—the second, regarding the transference of figures, is impossible because a figure taken from one part of an irregular surface can change when transferred into another place. Also, the sum of the angles of a triangle can be either more or less than two right angles.

Therefore, axioms express the difference of properties of various kinds of surfaces.

A geometrical axiom is a law of a given surface.

But what is a surface?

Lobachevsky’s merit <note>Nikolai Lobachevsky (1792–1856) was a Russian mathematician known as the "Copernicus of Geometry" for his role in developing non-Euclidean geometry.</note> consists in the fact that he found it necessary to revise the fundamental concepts of geometry. But he never went so far as to re-evaluate these concepts from the standpoint of Immanuel Kant <note>Kant argued that space is not an external reality but an inherent framework of human perception.</note>. At the same time, his work is in no sense contradictory to Kant. *A surface* in the mind of Lobachevsky, as a geometrician, was only a means for the generalization of certain properties on which this or that geometrical system was constructed, or the generalization of the properties of certain given lines. About the reality or the unreality of a surface, he probably never thought.

Thus, on the one hand, Roberto Bonola <note>An Italian historian of mathematics (1874–1911).</note>, who ascribed to Lobachevsky views opposite to Kant—associating him with <term>sensualism</term> <note>The philosophical theory that all knowledge is derived from the senses.</note> and "current empiricism"—is quite wrong. On the other hand, it is not impossible to conceive that Charles Hinton <note>An English mathematician and writer (1853–1907) interested in the fourth dimension.</note> entirely subjectively ascribes to Gauss and Lobachevsky the inauguration of a new era in philosophy.

Non-Euclidean geometry, including that of Lobachevsky, has no relation to <term>metageometry</term> <note>The study of higher-dimensional spaces or the philosophical foundations of geometry.</note> whatever.

Lobachevsky does not go outside of the three-dimensional sphere. <term>Metageometry</term> regards the three-dimensional sphere as a section of higher space. Among mathematicians, Bernhard Riemann, who understood the relation of time to space, was nearest of all to this idea.

# METAGEOMETRY 77

A point in three-dimensional space is merely a cross-section of a metageometrical line. It is impossible to generalize metageometrical lines onto any surface whatsoever. This final point is perhaps the most important for defining the difference between standard geometries (Euclidean and non-Euclidean) and metageometry. It is impossible to view metageometrical lines as distances between points in our space, and it is impossible to represent them as forming any figures within our space.

The study of the possible properties of lines lying outside of our space—and the relationship of these lines and their angles to the lines, angles, surfaces, and solids of our geometry—forms the subject of metageometry.

The researchers of non-Euclidean geometry could not bring themselves to abandon the consideration of surfaces. There is something almost tragic in this. Consider the surfaces Eugenio Beltrami <note>Eugenio Beltrami (1835–1900) was an Italian mathematician known for his work in non-Euclidean geometry; he showed that Lobachevskian geometry could be realized on a surface of constant negative curvature called a pseudosphere.</note> invented in his investigations of non-Euclidean geometry—one of his surfaces resembles the surface of a ventilator, and another the inner surface of a funnel. But he could not decide to reject the surface entirely—to cast it aside once and for all and imagine that a line can be independent of a surface. That is to say, he could not imagine a series of lines which are parallel or nearly parallel that cannot be mapped onto any surface, or even within three-dimensional space.

Because of this, both he and many other geometers developing non-Euclidean geometry could not move beyond the three-dimensional world.

The field of mechanics recognizes *the line in time*—that is, a line that is impossible to imagine upon a surface or as a simple distance between two points in space. This line is taken into account in calculations regarding machines. However, geometry has never touched this line, dealing always with its cross-sections only.
