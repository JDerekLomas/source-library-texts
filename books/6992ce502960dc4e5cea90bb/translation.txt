# Phoenix

The <term>Phoenix (Feng 鳳)</term> is a divine bird. It is commonly called the King of Birds. Among the three hundred and sixty species of feathered creatures, the Phoenix is their leader. 

In appearance, it possesses the front of a <note>original: *Hong* 鴻</note> wild swan and the back of a <note>original: *Jun* 麇</note> water deer. It has the neck of a snake, the tail of a fish, the forehead of a stork, the <note>original: *Yuansi* 鴛思</note> brow of a mandarin duck, the patterns of a dragon, the back of a tortoise, the jaw of a swallow, and the beak of a rooster. It is adorned with all <note>The five traditional colors are red, yellow, blue/green, white, and black, representing the Five Elements.</note> five colors. 

It emerges from the <term>Land of Noble Men (Junzi zhi Guo 君子之國)</term> in the East; whenever it is seen, it signifies great peace and harmony throughout the world. The patterns upon its body are imbued with meaning: 
*   The markings on its head signify **Virtue** <note>original: *De* 德</note>.
*   The markings on its wings signify **Ritual Propriety** <note>original: *Li* 禮</note>.
*   The markings on its back signify **Righteousness** <note>original: *Yi* 義</note>.
*   The markings on its breast signify **Benevolence** <note>original: *Ren* 仁</note>.
*   The markings on its belly signify **Trustworthiness** <note>original: *Xin* 信</note>.

Its voice sounds like the playing of flute pipes. It is a creature of supreme purity: it will not peck at living insects, nor will it break living blades of grass. It does not dwell in flocks and does not travel in groups. It is never ensnared by nets or traps. It will perch on no tree except the <term>Wutong (Chinese Parasol Tree 梧桐)</term>, eat nothing but the <term>fruit of the bamboo (Zhushi 竹實)</term>, and drink from nothing but <term>Sweet Springs (Liquan 醴泉)</term>.

### The Luan Bird

The *Shuowen* <note>original: *Shuowen Jiezi*, the first dictionary of Chinese characters, compiled in the 2nd century</note> states that the Luan is the essence of divine spirits. It is red in color with plumage of five brilliant hues and possesses the form of a rooster. Its cry perfectly matches the five musical tones <note>referring to the traditional pentatonic scale: *Gong, Shang, Jiao, Zheng,* and *Yu*</note>. When songs of praise <note>original: *song sheng*; hymns celebrating a virtuous ruler</note> arise, the bird will appear.

One theory suggests that the "Green Phoenix" is in fact the Luan. The female is called the *He* <note>original: 和; meaning "Harmony"</note> and the male is called the *Luan*. Ancient lore says that the blood of a Luan can be made into a glue so strong that it can mend the broken strings of bows, crossbows, zithers, or lutes <note>original: *qin* and *se*; traditional plucked string instruments</note>.

Others say it is a creature second only to the Phoenix. When first born, it resembles a Phoenix, but as it matures, its five-colored plumage changes. During high antiquity, the Luan bird moved in accordance with the natural order. This bird would frequently perch upon the carriages of the virtuous; the male would sing in the front while the female responded from behind. Because later generations could not summon the actual bird, they fashioned the "Harmony-Luan" <note>original: *He-Luan*; these were ritual bells attached to the crossbar and shafts of imperial carriages</note> to symbolize them. This is why the bells are called *Luan*.

# The Peacock

<vocab>Peacock</vocab> <note>original: *Kongque*</note>

->[Woodcut Illustration: A peacock stands poised on a rock, its long, ornate tail feathers draping downward. Beside it, large peony blossoms—symbols of wealth and status—are in full bloom.]<-

The Peacock is a bird found in the southern frontier regions <note>original: *Nanman*; a historical term for the regions of Southern China and Southeast Asia</note>. It is a bird of considerable size, standing several feet tall, with a distinctive crest of feathers atop its head. Its most remarkable feature is its tail, which reaches several feet in length and is covered in brilliant, shimmering "eyes" of gold, green, and blue.

Natural histories state that when a Peacock sees a person dressed in vibrant, colorful silks, it will spread its tail into a magnificent fan to compete in beauty. It is a creature of great pride. It is also said that they consume poisonous snakes and insects; for this reason, while their feathers are prized for decoration and as markers of official rank in the imperial court, their flesh is considered toxic and should not be eaten.

There are two primary varieties: the "Green Peacock" and the "Golden Peacock." They are celebrated for their dignity and are often associated with prosperity and the removal of evil influences.

# The Peacock

The *Records of Diversified Matters* <note>original: *Bowu Zhi*; a 3rd-century encyclopedia of wonders and natural history</note> states that the peacock’s tail frequently changes color, appearing sometimes red and sometimes yellow; its hue is never fixed. When a person claps their hands near its tail, the bird will begin to dance. The tail features "golden-green" patterns <note>original: *jincui*; referring to the iridescent, metallic "eyes" that resemble the color of gold and kingfisher feathers</note>, which take five years to fully develop. These feathers grow in the early spring and begin to wither and drop after the third or fourth month. The female peacock lacks a crest, has a shorter tail, and does not possess these golden-green colors.

The peacock’s nature is quite jealous, and it takes great pride in its own tail. Even if it has been tamed and raised by humans for a long time, should it encounter a woman or a child wearing garments made of colorful silks or brocades, it will inevitably chase after them and peck at them in a fit of rivalry. Whenever it prepares to roost in the mountains, it first carefully selects a spot where its tail can rest safely. Because of this, those who wish to capture a peacock alive will wait for it to rain before attempting to seize it. This is because the bird so loves its tail that it refuses to fly or soar <note>original: *qianyang*; to rise high into the air</note> when its feathers are heavy with water, fearing they might be damaged.

The *Records of Southern Yue* <note>original: *Nan-Yue Zhi*; a historical geographical account of Southern China and Vietnam</note> claims that the peacock does not necessarily need to mate physically; it can become pregnant simply through the meeting of sounds or shadows <note>a traditional folk belief regarding the spiritual or spontaneous reproduction of auspicious animals</note>. It is also said to mate with snakes. The *Classic of Birds* <note>original: *Qin Jing*; the oldest Chinese treatise on ornithology</note> states: "The swan, upon seeing a snake, cries out and swells with fury; the peacock, upon seeing a snake, winds its neck and leaps."

### The Biyi Bird
<note>original: *Biyiniao*; literally "the bird with wings joined." This is a famous creature in Chinese mythology representing inseparable partnership.</note>

In the Kingdom of the Bound-Chests <note>original: *Jiexiongguo*; a legendary country mentioned in ancient texts like the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*, where people were said to have protruding or "knotted" breastbones</note>, there are Biyi birds. 

The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary and encyclopedia, used for centuries to explain the meanings of ritual and natural terms</note> states that in the south there are Biyi birds; they do not fly unless they are joined together, and they are called the *Jianjian* <note>The specific name for this bird in the *Erya* lexicon</note>. 

The commentary notes that they resemble wild ducks and are blue and red in color. They possess only one eye and one wing; they must find one another to be able to fly. 

When a ruler’s filial virtue <note>original: *xiaode*; the foundational Confucian virtue of devotion to parents and ancestors</note> extends even to the most distant and obscure regions, then these birds will arrive. <note>In traditional Chinese thought, the appearance of rare, miraculous animals was considered a "propitious omen," confirming that the Emperor was ruling in accordance with the Mandate of Heaven.</note>

# Jingwei

<term>Jingwei</term> <note>Literally "Refined Guardian" or "Spirit Guardian." In Chinese culture, this bird is the ultimate symbol of *jingcheng*—sincere, tireless perseverance in the face of impossible odds.</note>

On Mount Fajiu <note>A mythical mountain traditionally associated with the Zhangzi County region in modern Shanxi Province; it was said to be filled with lush mulberry trees</note>, there is a bird. 

Its appearance resembles a crow, but it is marked by a patterned head, a white beak, and red feet. It is named the **Jingwei**. 

The *Classic of Mountains and Seas* <note>The *Shanhaijing*, an ancient sourcebook of mythic geography and creatures from which this entry is derived</note> records that this bird was once Nüwa <note>Not to be confused with the creator goddess Nüwa; this refers to the daughter of the Flame Emperor</note>, the youngest daughter of the Flame Emperor <note>original: *Yandi*; a legendary ancestral sovereign of China, often associated with agriculture and fire</note>. 

While she was playing in the Eastern Sea, she tragically drowned and did not return. She then transformed into this bird. She is driven by a singular, eternal purpose: she constantly carries branches and stones from the Western Mountains in her beak and drops them into the Eastern Sea, vowing to fill the waters that claimed her life. 

Because of this eternal task, she is also known as the **Vow Bird** <note>original: *shinian*; also translated as the "Bird of the Oath"</note>, the **Bird of Resentment** <note>original: *yuanqin*</note>, or the **Bird of Ambition**.

## Jingwei

On Mount Fajiu <note>original: *Fajiu shan*; a mythical peak often identified with the mountains in modern Shanxi Province</note>, there is a bird. 

Its appearance is like that of a crow, but with a white head and a red beak. It is named the **Jingwei**. Its cry is a calling of its own name. 

This bird is the youngest daughter of Shennong <note>The "Divine Farmer," a legendary ruler and deity of Chinese agriculture and medicine</note>, whose name was Nüwa <note>Not to be confused with the creator goddess Nüwa; this refers specifically to the daughter of the Flame Emperor</note>. Long ago, she was traveling across the Eastern Sea when she tragically drowned and did not return. She then transformed into the **Jingwei**. She constantly gathers wood and stones from the Western Mountains in order to fill up the Eastern Sea <note>This act has become a famous Chinese idiom for indomitable will against impossible odds</note>.

*   Two years after birth, it sheds its fledgling feathers.
*   In its third year, it begins to breed and brood.
*   In its seventh year, it can fly as high as the <term>Milky Way</term> <note>original: *Yunhan* (雲漢); literally "The Cloud River."</note>.

*   Seven years later, it begins to <gloss>act in accordance with the seasons</gloss>.

### The Chicken

There are several varieties of chicken, including the Shu, Lu, Jing, and Yue breeds. The Yue chicken is small, while the Shu chicken is large. <gloss>The Lu chicken is even larger than the others.</gloss>

Ancient lore says that while a three-legged "crouching crow" resides within the sun, the chicken is a creature belonging to the West. Because the "Great Radiance" <note>the Sun</note> is born in the East, the chicken is governed by the number eight. <note>original: *ji ba zhi* (鷄八之). This refers to the *I Ching* (Book of Changes) where the chicken is assigned to the *Xun* trigram, which corresponds to the number eight in certain cosmological arrangements.</note>

The **Book of Changes** states: <term>*Xun*</term> <note>The trigram representing the "Gentle" or "Wind," which traditional Chinese biology associates with the chicken.</note> represents the chicken. When <term>*Dui*</term> <note>The trigram representing the "Joyous" or "Lake," associated with the West and sunset.</note> appears, *Xun* remains hidden; thus it is the nature of the chicken. This is because the chicken knows the proper time <note>of the dawn</note> and is naturally inclined to yield and brood.

The chicken is known for its habit of looking askance. For this reason, the Han dynasty poet **Wang Bao** wrote: "The fish glares, and the chicken glares." The writer **Wu Jun** also observed: "The chicken possesses the virtue of calling the flock; thus, when it discovers food, it notifies the others." <note>This "calling to eat" is considered one of the "five virtues" of the chicken, specifically the virtue of benevolence (*ren*).</note>

## The Parrot

The <term>parrot</term> <note>original: *yingwu* (鸚鵡)</note> possesses a tongue like that of a human and is capable of speech. It has green plumage and a red beak, and its physical form resembles that of a crow. 

Ancient accounts state that while the toes of most birds are arranged with three in the front and one in the rear, and their eyes blink by moving the lower eyelid upward, the parrot is unique: its four toes are divided equally <note>this refers to the zygodactyl foot structure, where two toes point forward and two point backward</note>, and both its eyelids move, just like human eyes. These birds are found throughout <term>Jiaozhou</term> and <term>Banan</term> <note>historical regions in what is now Northern Vietnam and Southern China</note>.

There is also another variety known as the <term>Qin Jiliao</term> <note>identified as the Common Hill Myna (*Gracula religiosa*), famous for its mimicry</note>. Its shape is similar to the parrot, but its color is black and the top of its head is slightly yellow. The feathers on its crown have a seam, appearing like a person’s parted hair. It possesses keen hearing, a clever mind, and a skillful tongue; there is no human speech it cannot master. It is also called the <term>Liaoying</term> <note>literally "the understood parrot"</note> and originates from Dufan Prefecture. In the Tang Dynasty, the poet <term>Bai Juyi</term> wrote a poem titled "The Song of the Qin Jiliao."

## The Yellow Bird

The **Yellow Bird** <note>original: *huangniao* 黃鳥</note> is the **Yellow Chestnut-Stayer** <note>original: *huangli-liu* 黃栗留; a name likely derived from its golden color and its habit of lingering in trees</note>. The **Hawk** is a bird of prey <term>zhìniǎo</term> <note>original: 摯鳥; a classical category for powerful, predatory birds such as eagles and hawks</note>. Another name for it is the **Shuangjiu** <term>shuǎngjiū</term> <note>original: 鷞鳩; an archaic name that historically associated the bird with a legendary official who governed the seasons</note>. # The Wild Goose

The **Wild Goose** is also known as the **Vermilion Bird** <note>original: 朱鳥; *Zhuniao*. Although this shares a name with the legendary Red Bird of the South, here it is used as a specific alias for the goose.</note>. During the solar term of **Frost Descent** <note>original: 霜降; *Shuangjiang*, occurring around October 23rd.</note>, they fly toward the south. When the ice thaws <note>original: 水泮; *shuipan*.</note>, they travel back to the north. Their nature is such that they loathe the heat; therefore, when the Central Plains begin to turn cold, they arrive from the north.

In the *Rites of Zhou* <note>original: 周官; *Zhouguan*. A foundational text on the administrative and ceremonial systems of the Zhou Dynasty (c. 1046–256 BCE).</note>, it is recorded that among the birds used for the **Six Ceremonial Gifts** <note>original: 六摯; *Liuzhi*. These were specific offerings presented to superiors to show respect and social rank.</note>, high officials held the wild goose as their token. 

When wild geese moor for the night on river islands or sandbars, they command **sentry geese** <note>original: 雁奴; *Yannu*, literally "goose slaves." These are designated members of the flock that stay awake to watch for danger while the others sleep.</note> to surround the group and maintain a vigilant watch. When they fly, they are said to carry reeds in their beaks to keep quiet and avoid **tethered arrows** <note>original: 矰繳; *Zengjiao*. These were hunting arrows with silk cords attached, used to bring down birds in flight.</note>. 

Because they possess a "Way" <note>original: 道; *Dao*.</note> for avoiding harm, they were chosen as ceremonial gifts. This selection was not merely because of their "principle of coming and going" <note>original: 去就之義; *Qujiu zhi yi*. This refers to the goose’s reputation for seasonal punctuality and loyalty, which was often compared to the conduct of a refined gentleman.</note>, but for their perceived wisdom.

The *Records of Diverse Matters* <note>original: 博物志; *Bowuzhi*. A 3rd-century collection of geography, mythology, and natural history.</note> states: "If one makes a bag from the down of a wild goose <note>original: 鴻毛; *Hongmao*.</note>, it can be used to cross a river without leaking."

<term>Six Ceremonial Gifts (六摯):</term> A system of ritual protocols where different animals or objects (such as lambs, geese, or pheasants) represented the status of the person presenting the gift.

<term>Frost Descent (霜降):</term> One of the 24 solar terms in the traditional East Asian calendar, marking the time when the first frosts appear and migratory birds head south.

# The Magpie

The magpie senses human joy. It feels a sense of shame regarding building its nest at the very tips of tree branches; in years with heavy winds, it will leave the tall trees to build its nest on the lower branches. It opens the entrance of its nest facing the <term>Heavenly One<note>Original: *Tian-yi* (天一); a celestial direction or star associated with auspiciousness</note></term> and with its back toward the <term>Year Star<note>Original: *Sui* (歲); referring to the direction of Tai-sui or Grand Duke Jupiter, which was avoided in traditional positioning</note></term>. It is said that they "pass the branch" to receive their eggs; for this reason, they are called the <term>Dry Magpie<note>Original: *Gan-que* (乾鵲); the character *Gan* can mean dry, but in this context, it often refers to the "Heavenly" trigram</note></term>. 

The philosopher *Zhuangzi* said: "Crows and magpies breed by passing the branch." Because they have few desires, the process is called <term>breeding<note>Original: *ru* (孺); here implying a peaceful or simple procreation</note></term>. 

Common folk lore says that there must be a "beam" within a magpie’s nest. Anyone who sees a magpie standing upon such a beam is destined for a life of nobility. 

The *Register of Primary Things of the Heavenly Mystery* <note>Original: *Tianxuan zhuwu bu* (天玄主物簿); an ancient text on the properties of things</note> states: "The magpie pecks at the seeds of the pagoda tree, which causes a jade-like stone to form within its brain, known as 'Magpie Jade.'" This particular magpie will no longer chatter or call throughout the rest of the year; although it builds a nest, it produces no offspring. The magpie is also known by the names "Flying Piebald" and "Divine Maiden."

## The **Filial Crow**

The crow is a filial bird <note>original: *xiàoniǎo* 孝鳥</note>. One of its names is the *Ya* <note>original: 鴉</note>, so called because its cry is a self-naming call. When a crow witnesses something strange, it will raise a clamor; therefore, when people hear the cawing of a crow, they spit <note>A traditional folk practice intended to ward off the bad luck or "ill omens" often associated with the bird's cry</note>. Its nature is to delight in wide-open spaces. It is said to conceive by the passing of saliva <note>A traditional biological misconception often found in ancient natural histories</note>.

Old accounts state that the nature of this bird is exceptionally long-lived. A folk saying suggests that after three deer have died, a single pine tree will fall; after three pine trees have died, a single crow will finally fall <note>This hyperbolic proverb emphasizes the bird's association with extreme longevity</note>. 

Commonly, people observe the flight of birds to predict the weather; if their wings seem heavy, they know that rain is imminent. This is because the bird is a creature of *Yang* <term>Yang: The "bright" or "masculine" cosmic principle, associated here with heat and dryness</term>; it senses the approaching *Yin* <term>Yin: The "dark" or "feminine" cosmic principle, associated here with moisture and cold</term> energy and becomes heavy. Thus, the common folk use this to divine the coming of rain.

The *Guangya* <note>An influential 3rd-century Chinese dictionary</note> says: "Those that are pure black and practice 'reverse feeding' <note>original: *fǎnbǔ* 反哺; the celebrated behavior where young crows bring food to their aging parents, serving as a primary metaphor for filial piety in Chinese culture</note> are called *Wu* (crows). Those that are small with white plumage beneath their bellies and do not practice reverse feeding are called *Ya* (jackdaws)." Crows with white necks that fly in flocks are called "Swallow-Crows"; these are the white-throated birds. The *Ya* crow is also known as the *Zhu*. This is what is meant in the *Classic of Poetry* <note>The *Shijing*, the oldest existing collection of Chinese poetry</note> when it says: "Flapping are those jackdaws" <note>original: 弁彼鸀斯; from the "Lesser Court Hymns" section of the Poetry Classic</note>.

# The **Bustard**

The **Bustard** <note>original: *Bǎo* 鴇. Specifically referring to the Great Bustard (*Otis tarda*), a heavy, ground-dwelling bird known for its shy nature and distinctive plumage.</note> resembles a wild goose but is distinguished by the fact that it lacks a hind toe. Its feathers are adorned with patterns similar to those of a leopard; for this reason, it is also known as the "Single Leopard" <note>original: *Dúbào* 獨豹</note>. When it encounters birds of prey <term>wùniǎo 鶩鳥 (predatory birds such as hawks or falcons)</term>, it is capable of ejecting its excrement to defend itself. If this strikes the feathers of the attacking bird, the feathers will entirely fall out.

The *Explication of Graphs and Analysis of Characters* <note>original: *Shuowen Jiezi* 説文解字; the first dictionary of Chinese characters, compiled in the 2nd century</note> states that its meat "is harvested in slices a foot long." Additionally, Jiao Gong’s *Forest of Changes* <note>original: *Jiaogong Yilin* 焦貢易林; an ancient Han dynasty work on divination and poetry</note> remarks: "The great leopard-bird of Mount Wen has a fat rump and much grease." This description is believed to refer to the bustard.

The scholar Lu Dian <note>A Song dynasty scholar (1042–1102) and author of the *Pi Ya*, an early encyclopedia of flora and fauna</note> notes that the bustard is lewd by nature and does not maintain a fixed mate. Because of this perceived behavior, the mistress of a brothel is commonly called an "Old Bustard" <note>original: *Lǎobǎo* 老鴇; a term that remains the standard Chinese word for a 'madame' in modern times</note>.

# The **Crested Myna**

The **Crested Myna** <term>Qúyù 鸜鵒; the Crested Myna bird (*Acridotheres cristatellus*)</term> resembles a shrike but possesses a crest <note>original: *zé* 幘; literally a "turban" or "cap," referring to the tuft of feathers on the bird's forehead</note>. # The **Sparrow**

The sparrow is a creature of excessive lust, bearing offspring throughout all four seasons. The young ones have yellow beaks and are so greedy for food that they are easily captured; however, the older birds are cunning and difficult to tame. By nature, they are unable to build their own nests, so they tunnel into the eaves of houses to dwell. For this reason, they are called **Tile Sparrows** <note>original: *wǎquè* 瓦雀</note>. 

An ancient saying claims that the sparrow’s eyesight grows dim at dusk. Consequently, a person who, upon the arrival of evening, can <gloss>not</gloss> see objects is said to have **Sparrow Blindness** <term>Sparrow Blindness: A traditional term for night blindness (nyctalopia), stemming from the folk belief that sparrows cannot see in the dark.</term>. 

There are many different varieties of this bird. There is the **Divine Sparrow**, the **Turkic Sparrow**, and the **Wormwood Sparrow**. The Wormwood Sparrow's body is slightly green in color, and it is commonly known as the **Grass Sparrow**.

In the Shandong region, it is generally known simply as the **Grey** <note>original: *cāng* 鶬</note>. Its common name is **Cuo-luo** <note>original: *cuò-luò* 銼落</note>; this name is used to describe the harshness and unpleasantness of the bird's cry. Names such as **Cang-luo** <note>original: *cāng-luǒ* 鶬頇</note> and **Grey Heron** <note>original: *cāng-lù*</note> are all intended to be onomatopoeic, mimicking the sound it makes. 

The bird is grey in color with long legs and flies in flocks. It possesses a preternatural awareness of the coming winter; when the frost is about to arrive, the bird senses it and begins to cry out. Within ten days of its cry, the frost will surely fall. 

The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary and encyclopedia, dating to the 3rd century BCE</note> states that the **Cang-mi-gua** <note>original: *cāng-mí-guā* 鶬麋鴰</note> is the **nine-headed bird**. Today, it is commonly called the **Ghost Carriage Bird** <note>original: *guǐchē niǎo* 鬼車鳥</note>. On overcast and cloudy days, it sometimes makes a sound like the creaking of a heavy carriage—hence its name. 

Folklore tells us that this bird originally had ten heads and the power to seize the "soul-breath" of humans. However, the **Celestial Dog** <term>Celestial Dog <note>original: *tiāngǒu* 天狗</note> <gloss>a mythical creature often blamed for eclipses and the disappearance of children</gloss></term> bit off one of its heads. To this day, the wound has never healed, and the bird drips blood incessantly. It is considered a terrible omen if the bird passes over a house...

## The Bifang <note>original: 畢方, *bìfāng*; a mythical bird from ancient Chinese folklore, often described as a harbinger of fire or a divine messenger</note>

On Mount Yizhang <note>A legendary peak mentioned in early geographical texts</note>, there is a bird whose form resembles a crane, but it possesses only one leg. It has red markings [across its plumage] and a white beak; it is named the **Bifang**. Its appearance is an omen of longevity <note>original: *shòu* 壽. Note: Most classical versions of the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* state that the Bifang portends "strange fires" (*èhuǒ* 訛火) in the local town; this version's use of "longevity" represents a distinct textual variation or a more auspicious interpretation</note>. 

The *Shangshu Shi* <note>Likely referring to a commentary or expanded chronicle related to the *Book of Documents*</note> records that during the reign of Emperor Wu of the Han <note>r. 141–87 BCE, a period famous for its interest in the supernatural and the collection of exotic tributes</note>, a one-legged crane was presented to the throne. Everyone at court regarded the creature as a strange and inexplicable anomaly. However, the scholar Dongfang Shuo <note>A renowned polymath and court advisor known for his immense knowledge of the esoteric and his witty personality</note> submitted a memorial to the Emperor, stating: "The *Classic of Mountains and Seas* <note>An ancient compendium of mythic geography and creatures</note> says this is the **Bifang** bird." Upon verifying the bird against the ancient descriptions, it was found to be exactly as he described.

# The Quail <note>original: *chún* 鶉; the Japanese quail (*Coturnix japonica*), a bird deeply embedded in Chinese seasonal lore and daily life</note>

<term>Quail</term> (*chún* 鶉), from the <term>Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms</term> (*Sāncái Túhuì*), section on <term>Birds and Beasts</term> (*Niǎoshòu*).

The **Quail** is a bird that does not perch in trees but prefers to dwell among the tall grasses of the fields. It is a creature that lacks a fixed nest, wandering according to the availability of food and the turning of the seasons <note>This lack of a "home" led the quail to become a symbol of the "mottled" or "ragged" clothing of wandering scholars and hermits in traditional Chinese iconography</note>. 

According to ancient natural histories, the Quail is the product of a seasonal transformation: it is believed that during the months of autumn, frogs transform into quails <note>original: *xiámǎ suǒ huà* 蝦蟆所化. In traditional Chinese biology, certain animals were thought to spontaneously transform into others—such as field mice into quails or frogs into birds—to explain their sudden appearance or disappearance during seasonal migrations</note>. 

The bird is recognized by its small, round body and its mottled, brownish plumage which provides excellent camouflage. While it appears humble, the Quail is known for its fierce and pugnacious spirit <note>Quail fighting was a significant cultural pastime during the Ming dynasty, similar to cricket fighting or cockfighting</note>. In medical texts of the era, its meat was highly regarded for its ability to supplement the "vital energy" (*qi*) and strengthen the spleen and stomach.

### The Quail-Lark <note>original: *yàn* 鷃; a term used for various small ground-dwelling birds like the meadow quail or the Eastern Skylark, characterized here by its humble habits</note>

# The Quail-Lark

The **Quail-Lark** <note>original: *yàn* 鷃; here specifically referring to its reputation in folklore and natural history</note> takes great pleasure in mating. While among most birds the male mounts the female, in this species alone, the female mounts the male. They produce offspring every single month. For this reason, **Western books** <note>original: *xīfāng zhī shū* 西方之書; a common reference to Buddhist scriptures originating from India/the West</note> consider this bird to be the <term>karmic retribution</term> <note>original: *shòuguǒ qíbào* 受果其報; the idea that one's current form is the "fruit" or result of past actions</note> for those who were lewd and lustful in their past lives. Indeed, sparrows, Quail-Larks, and Mandarin ducks are all considered the most lustful among all living creatures.

# The Falcon

The **falcon** <note>original: *gǔ* 鶻</note> possesses a "fist" <note>This refers to the bird's clenched talons used as a striking weapon in mid-air</note> that is as hard and as large as a pellet. It stoops from above to strike doves and pigeons for food. When a dove or pigeon is struck by this "fist" in mid-air, the falcon immediately rolls its body to catch the prey from beneath as it falls; it is even swifter than other **hawks and falcons** <note>original: *yīng sǔn* 鷹隼</note>. 

Ancient accounts say that the falcon possesses a **righteous nature** <note>original: *yì xìng* 義性; a term implying moral character or a sense of fair play often attributed to animals in classical Chinese natural history</note>. During the winter, it will capture a small bird that fits in its grasp. Throughout the night, it uses this bird to **warm its claws** <note>original: *yù qí zhuǎo* 燠其爪</note>, switching the bird between its left and right talons. At dawn, it releases the bird and allows it to fly away. If the bird flies toward the east, the falcon will not hunt in an easterly direction for the remainder of that day; the same rule applies to the north or south. This is because its righteous nature involves a cycle of **capture and release** <note>original: *qín zòng* 擒縱</note> in this manner.
