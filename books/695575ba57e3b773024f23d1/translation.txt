**W**HATEVER *progress Music has made up to our time, it seems that the mind has been less curious to deepen its true principles, even as the ear has become sensitive to the marvelous effects of this Art; so that one might say that reason has lost its rights there, while experience has acquired some authority.*

*The Writings that remain to us from the Ancients <note>Referring to Greek and Roman theorists like Pythagoras or Aristoxenus</note> show us clearly enough that reason alone provided them the means to discover the greater part of the properties of Music: However, although experience still makes us approve most of the Rules they have given us, we neglect today all the advantages that could be drawn from this reason, in favor of an experience of simple practice.*

*If experience can inform us about the different properties of Music, it is not, moreover, alone capable of making us discover the principle of these properties with all the precision that suits reason: The consequences drawn from it are often false, or at least leave us in a certain doubt, which it belongs only to reason to dispel. *Music is a science that must have certain rules; these rules must be drawn from an evident principle, and this principle can hardly be known to us without the help of Mathematics <note>The author refers to the "Quadrivium" tradition where music was studied alongside arithmetic, geometry, and astronomy.</note>: Thus I must confess that, notwithstanding all the experience I may have acquired in Music, having practiced it for a quite a long period of time, it is nevertheless only through the help of Mathematics that my ideas have become clear, and that light has succeeded a certain obscurity, which I did not perceive before. If I did not know how to distinguish the principle from the rule, soon this principle offered itself to me with as much simplicity as evidence; the consequences it then provided me made me recognize in them so many rules, which must consequently relate back to this principle; the true meaning of these rules, their correct application, their relationship, and the order they must maintain among themselves (the simplest always serving as an introduction to the less simple, and so on by degrees); finally the choice of terms <term>terms: the specific vocabulary used to describe musical intervals and ratios</term>; all this, I say, which I was ignorant of before, developed in my mind with such clarity and precision, that I could not help but agree that it would be desirable (as someone said to me one day when I was applauding the perfection of our modern Music) that the knowledge of the Musicians of this century should match the beauties of their Compositions. It is not enough, therefore, to feel the effects of a Science or an Art; one must also conceive of them in such a way that they can be made intelligible; and it is to this that I have principally applied myself in the body of this Work, which I have divided into four Books.*

**THE FIRST BOOK** contains an abridgment of the relationship of sounds, consonances, dissonances, and chords in general. The principle of <term>Harmony (the pleasing combination of simultaneous musical notes)</term> is discovered here within a single sound, and its most essential properties are explained. One sees here, for example, how through its first division this single sound engenders another, which is its <term>Octave (the interval between one musical pitch and another with double its frequency)</term>, and which seems to be at one with it. How it then appropriates this octave to form all chords; that all these chords are composed only of this principle, its <term>Third (an interval spanning three staff positions)</term>, its <term>Fifth (an interval spanning five staff positions)</term>, and its <term>Seventh (an interval spanning seven staff positions)</term>; and that it is from the power of the octave that all the diversity of which these chords are capable is born. One finds here several other properties, less interesting, in truth, for practice, but necessary, nonetheless, to lead us there; the whole being demonstrated in a quite simple manner.

**THE SECOND BOOK** concerns both theory and practice. The principle is then represented in the part of music called the <term>Bass (the lowest part of a musical composition)</term>, to which is added the epithet <term>Fundamental (the root pitch upon which a chord is built)</term>. All its properties, and those of the intervals, chords, and <term>Modes (scales or tonal systems)</term> which depend solely upon it, are explained there. We also speak of everything that can serve to make music perfect in its execution. To this end, we recall, when appropriate, the reasons from the preceding book, experience, and the authority of the best authors in this genre—without sparing them, however, when they may have been mistaken. For in the novelties found here, we endeavor to satisfy the <term>Scholars (the learned experts of music theory)</term> through reason; those who rely only on their ear, through experience; and those who have too much deference for the rules of their masters, by revealing to them the errors those rules may contain. Finally, we endeavor to prepare the reader to receive without constraint the rules prescribed here, which are deduced in order and at greater length in the following books.

One has not yet seen rules that teach Composition in the perfection in which it exists today; there is not even a skilled man in this genre who does not sincerely admit that he owes almost all his knowledge to his own experience alone; and when he wishes to provide it to others, he often finds himself constrained to add to his lessons this proverb familiar to Musicians: *Practice will teach the rest.* <note>original: "Cætera docebit usus." ### A

**ACCOMPANIMENT**. <term>Accompaniment: The art of playing chords over a bass line, usually on a keyboard or lute.</term> *See the fourth Book, page 363.*

**CHORD**. <note>original: "Accord"</note> There is only one single *Chord* from which all others originate; they can all be reduced to a division by **Thirds**, by means of **Inversion**. <term>Inversion: Rearranging the notes of a chord so that a note other than the root is at the bottom.</term> *Chapter VII of Book I, pages 102, 114, 126, 127 & 128.*

The principle of all *Chords* resides in a single sound. <note>This refers to the "corps sonore" or the fundamental pitch that contains its own harmonics.</note> *Pages 5, 127 & 133.*

There are only the **Perfect Chord** <note>The major or minor triad.</note> and that of the **Seventh**. *Pages 45, 61, 378 & 379.*

Nothing is easier than to practice **Consonant Chords**, and even **Dissonant** ones, by means of the fundamental rules of Harmony, from which one can derive every imaginable Melody, etc. By this means, one is never ignorant of the sounds that serve to make the *Chords* complete. *Pages 72 & 73.*

The **Chord of the Seventh** is always contained within Chords by **supposition**. <term>Supposition: An early harmonic theory where extra notes are added below a fundamental bass note.</term> *Pages 74 & 75.*

Every **Dissonant Chord** can only be composed of the union of **Consonances**. *Page 95.*

One must not only examine the effect produced by the Sounds of a *Chord* in relation to the **Bass**, but also the effect they produce among themselves, in order to draw just conclusions. *Page 96.*

One can give a **Consonant Chord** to all the Notes of a **Bass** that proceeds by **consonant Intervals**. *Page 395.*

*See* **Perfect**, **Rule**, **Consonance**, and **Octave**.

**ACUTE**. <note>original: "Aigu"</note> This is the true name that should be given to high Sounds, just as **Grave** is the name given to low Sounds.

**Acute** Sounds are contained within the **Grave**. <note>A reference to the overtone series where higher pitches are found within the vibration of a lower fundamental string.</note> *Pages 3 & 5.*

# TREATISE ON HARMONY

We shall leave the task of defining Sound to Physics <note>original: "la Physique"; in the 18th century, this referred to the study of natural laws and acoustics</note>; in Harmony, we distinguish it only as low and high, without pausing to consider its volume or its duration; and it is upon the relationship of high sounds to low sounds that all knowledge of Harmony must be founded.

Low sounds <note>original: "Sons graves"</note> are the deepest, such as those produced by male voices, and high sounds <note>original: "Sons aigus"</note> are the most elevated, such as those produced by female voices.

We call an *Interval* the distance there is from a low sound to a high sound, and from the different distances that can be found between one sound and another, different *intervals* are formed, whose degrees take their names from the numbers of Arithmetic. Thus, the first degree can only be named by *Unity*, from which we call two sounds on the same degree a *Unison*; consequently, the second degree is called a *Second*, the 3rd a *Third*, the 4th a *Fourth*, the 5th a *Fifth*, the 6th a *Sixth*, the 7th a *Seventh*, the 8th an *Octave*, etc., assuming that the first degree is always the lowest, and that the others are formed by raising the voice successively according to its natural degrees.

### On the Fifth & the Fourth.

The Sounds that form the *Fifth* <note>original: "Quinte"; the interval of five scale degrees, such as C to G</note> & the *Fourth* <note>original: "Quarte"; the interval of four scale degrees, such as C to F</note> are included within the divisions of the entire string, and consequently are generated from the fundamental Sound; however, with regard to intervals, in this case only the *Octave* & the *Fifth* are generated immediately from the fundamental Sound; for the *Fourth* is merely a consequence of the *Octave*; this *Fourth* arises only from the difference found between that *Octave* and the *Fifth*. If we were to raise 6 further to its *Octave*, we would have 8, 10, 12, which are in the same proportion as 4, 5, 6. This is why we cannot push this transposition of the low sound to its octave any further, since the perfect chord—which is composed of only three different sounds—can consequently produce in this manner only three different chords, of which it is the first and the fundamental.

Although the two chords derived from the *perfect* chord are consonant, they are called *imperfect*; not only to distinguish them from the one that is their principle, but also because their properties are different.

We may remark in passing the great power of the number 3, since the *Fifth* <note>The interval of a fifth</note>, which is the origin of all chords, takes its form from 3, and because the quantity of tuning numbers, primary consonances, and consonant chords subsists in this single number.

To provide an understanding of this *perfect* chord and its derivatives, we will infer the reasons for them within three triangles, along with the names of the Notes <note>original: "Nottes"</note> with which these chords are marked. The largest triangle will contain the *perfect chord* <term>accord parfait: a major triad consisting of a root, major third, and perfect fifth</term> as the principle and root of the other chords, which will be contained in the two smaller triangles. By further examining the numbers and the Notes at each corner of the large triangle, one will notice that whichever corner is taken as the base, a consonant chord will always be found there. One will find *C, E, G* <note>original: "Ut, Mi, Sol"</note> in each chord, the difference consisting only in the different placement of these three Notes or sounds; this is consistent with the inversion of numbers <term>renversement des nombres: the mathematical process of octave displacement</term>, since 8, which is the double of 4, always produces a *C*, just as 5 and 10 always produce an *E*.

# CHAPTER NINE.

## *Remarks on all the preceding Chords.*

One must be convinced by now that the difference between *perfect chords* <note>The "perfect chord" refers to what we now call a major or minor triad.</note> and *seventh chords* consists only in the different positioning of the *thirds*, or else in an *inversion* <term>renversement: the practice of rearranging the notes of a chord so that a note other than the root is at the bottom</term> of the order of these *thirds*; thus, these chords have never been distinguished by other names; because it is the *modulation* <note>In this context, "modulation" refers to the movement within a key or the establishment of a tonal center.</note> that obliges us to use certain sounds, upon which depends the order of the *thirds* that compose all chords. The *modulation* being thus once determined by the difference of the *third* which occupies the low position in one of the two *perfect chords*, one can no longer dispense with conforming the chords to the order of the sounds included in the *octave* of the fundamental sound of one of these *perfect chords*. But despite the strength of the *perfect chord* in *modulation*, the chord of the *seventh* from *Article III* is independent of it; it is the first of its kind, never changes regardless of the form the *perfect chord* takes, it alone is assigned to the *dominants*, and a conclusion <note>A musical cadence or ending.</note> cannot be perfectly felt without its help. It is the source of all *dissonances*; the *major third* which it takes from the *perfect chord* from which it derives forms all *major dissonances*; and the *minor third*, which is added to this *perfect chord* to compose this one, forms all *minor dissonances*. Furthermore, after having generated several chords by its *inversion*, or by the addition of a new low sound which supposes the one that is its foundation, it generates yet another equal quantity by yielding its foundation to another sound, without the place it must occupy being altered in any way: whereas the other *seventh chords* take from this first one everything they have that is perfect; they share only the *minor dissonance*, and it is the *modulation* that fixes the place they must occupy. From this it follows that all derivatives of this first *dissonant chord* are distinguished by a name that suits them alone; whereas the derivatives of other *seventh chords* have a common name, because they determine nothing, and they are determined by the *modulation*.

From everything we have remarked in the contents of this Chapter, we must conclude that there are in *harmony* only two chords, which are the *perfect* and that of the *seventh*, and that there are likewise only two *dissonances*, which are the *major* and the *minor*, as all that follows will clearly prove to us.

## CHAPTER ELEVEN.

->The manner of relating the ratios given for Divisions to Vibrations and the Multiplications of lengths.<-

**W**hen one takes the lengths resulting from each division of a proposed string—measuring from the numbered point to the end of the string on the right—and arranges them so that the Vibrations can be distinguished (assuming the strings have no difference between them other than their lengths), one will find that the ratios of the Vibrations will conform to those of the divisions. One may then divide a string into as many parts as necessary to obtain the ratios of the dissonances <note>In 18th-century theory, "dissonances" refers to intervals like seconds or sevenths that require resolution, as opposed to "consonances" like octaves or fifths.</note>, where the same conformity will always be found. See previously, Chapter III, Article VI, page 15.

To obtain the ratios of the lengths, one need only take the two lengths resulting from two different divisions and give each a common measure by means of a compass <note>A pair of dividers used for geometric measurement.</note>; one will find that each length contains the common measure as many times as the numbers marking the divisions contain units, with this difference: that the comparison will be inverted. Let me explain: if one compares the lengths resulting from the divisions marked by the numbers 2 and 3, one will find that string 2 will contain the measure three times, and string 3 will contain it only twice. Thus, we compare 2 to 3 on one hand, and 3 to 2 on the other. This would be the same thing if the first number of each ratio did not represent the lowest Sound to us; however, one need only invert any ratio imagined in the divisions in this manner to relate it to the lengths. But what appears very easy here in the comparison of two Sounds becomes more confusing as their number increases; because in Harmony, just as in a continuous quantity, the sounds or the middle terms must relate to each extreme. Consequently, we will not find in 4, 3, 2 what we find in 2, 3, 4, in that the Interval <term>Interval: the distance in pitch between two sounds</term> generated from the comparison of 2 to 3—which is the first here—is found instead to be the last on the other side. This is what obliges us to invert our Arithmetic proportion (as we have said elsewhere) by multiplying the two extremes by this means, and then one of the extremes by the other.

The Sounds, or rather the intervals assigned to the progression of the Fundamental Bass <note>The "Fundamental Bass" is a concept pioneered by Rameau, referring to an imaginary bass line consisting only of the roots of chords.</note>, must also accompany it throughout the parts that unite with it, but with a certain discretion that we do not impose upon the progression of the Bass which acts as their guide. # CHAPTER THREE.
## *On the nature and the property of the Octave.*

The Octave <note>The interval between one musical pitch and another with double its frequency; the most consonant interval after the unison.</note>, which contains all the sounds that can compose fundamental chords among themselves, can be added to them to increase their perfection. Without it, the Perfect Chord <note>A major or minor triad.</note> and its derivatives still exist; but with it, they become more brilliant, because the natural and inverted chords are heard at the same time. In pieces for four parts, one cannot dispense with using it, and in those for five parts, it accords perfectly with the sounds of the fundamental Seventh chord. In a word, it can always be joined to chords where there is only a minor dissonance; its progression, which must be diatonic <note>Moving by steps within a scale.</note> in the upper parts, easily submits to the rules; furthermore, it determines the modulation <note>The process of changing from one key to another.</note>, as we shall see in the following.

The **Tonic Note** is the name given to the one that terminates the perfect Cadence, in that it is with this note that one begins and ends, and it is within the span of its Octave that the entire modulation <note>In this context, the melodic movement or "key" of a piece</note> is determined.

This precursor sound to the Octave, which forms all major dissonances, is called the **Leading Note** <note>original: "Notte sensible"</note>, because one never hears one of these major dissonances without feeling that the tonic Note, or its Octave, must follow immediately after; this is why this name is quite suitable for the sound that makes one feel the one which is the object of the entire modulation.

## CHAPTER SIX.

->Of the Broken Cadence.<-
<note>original: "De la Cadence rompuë." Today commonly known as a Deceptive or Interrupted Cadence.</note>

IF we change the progression of one of the Sounds comprised in the first chord of a **perfect Cadence**, it is certain that we will interrupt its conclusion; it is from this interruption, caused by this change of progression, that the **Broken Cadence** takes its origin.

This **Cadence** does not differ much from the **perfect** one, since both are equally composed either of the same chords or of the same fundamental Bass; if the Chords are the same, the fundamental Bass—which in the **perfect Cadence** must descend by a Fifth—will rise diatonically <note>Moving by a step in the scale.</note> in this one; and if the fundamental Bass is the same in both Cadences, the perfect Accord that terminates the **perfect Cadence** will be changed here into a **Chord of the Sixth**; where it must be well noted that these changes only take place in the consonant Sounds, such as in the fundamental Sound, in its Octave, or in its Fifth, and not in those that form the dissonances; so that the **major Third**, the object of all major dissonances, will always rise by a semi-Tone, and the **Seventh**, the object of all minor dissonances, will descend by a Tone or a semi-Tone.
