<header>ON APOSTOLIC TRADITION.</header>

# PART TWO.
## Chapter One.
### On Apostolic Tradition in general.

<margin>What Tradition is.</margin>
After we have spoken in the previous part concerning Holy Scripture as the primary source and rule of faith: it now follows in order that we should discuss, briefly and succinctly, Apostolic Tradition as the other, secondary rule of the same faith.

<margin>Etymology.</margin>
Tradition is called *paradosis* <note>original: "παράδοσις"</note> by the Greeks, from the verb *paradidonai* <note>original: "παραδιδόναι"</note>, which means "to hand over": in Latin, however, "Tradition" takes its name from the act of handing down <note>original: "tradendo"</note>.

<margin>Definition.</margin>
It is defined in this manner: Tradition is doctrine poured from one person into another through the oracle of the living voice. In this sense, the Apostle says to Timothy: *The things that thou hast heard of me among many witnesses, the same commit thou to faithful men, who shall be able to teach others also.* II Timothy 2. And in II Thessalonians 2: *Hold the traditions which ye have been taught, whether by word, or our epistle.* In this passage, the name of Tradition is extended even to the Scriptures.

<margin>Division of traditions.</margin>
Furthermore, some Traditions are Divine, others are Apostolic, and others are Ecclesiastical.

<margin>Divine.</margin>
Those are called Divine which first proceeded from Christ himself and were left by the Apostles to their successors, such as the requirement that bread and wine are to be used in the Eucharist.

<margin>Apostolic.</margin>
Those are called Apostolic which were not indeed instituted immediately by Christ, but by the Apostles through the prompting of the Holy Spirit, and were derived down to posterity; such as the Fast of Lent and the sign of the Cross.

<margin>Ecclesiastical.</margin>
Finally, those are termed Ecclesiastical which were introduced gradually either by the Prelates of the Church or by the Christian people, and obtained the force of law by common consent: such as certain rites in the administration of the Sacraments, in Fasts, and in the celebration of Festivals.

<margin>What Apostolic Tradition is.</margin>
But we shall see to these categories later. Now we deal with Apostolic Traditions which pertain to faith and morals, which we, along with the Council of Trent, receive and venerate with an equal affection of piety and reverence as we do the written books.

<margin>Proof of Tradition.</margin>
The proof of Traditions rests chiefly upon these arguments.

<margin>1. From Scripture.</margin>
First, from Holy Scripture, which mentions Traditions and commands them to be observed. Besides the places already cited, one should look at I Corinthians 11: *Now I praise you, brethren, that ye remember me in all things, and keep my ordinances <note>The author uses the Latin "praecepta," often translated as "traditions" or "ordinances" in this context.</note>, as I delivered them to you.* And further down: *And the rest will I set in order when I come.* Where the Apostle openly indicates that he taught and ordained many things by the living voice which he had not committed to writing at that time.

<margin>2. From the Fathers.</margin>
Secondly, from the most ancient Fathers, who defended Traditions with unanimous consent.

<margin>Irenaeus.</margin>
Irenaeus, book 3, chapter 4: *What indeed* (he says) *if there should be a dispute concerning some minor question? Would it not be necessary to recur to the most ancient Churches, in which the Apostles held conversation, and to take from them what is certain and clear regarding the present question? For what if the Apostles had not even left us Scriptures? Would it not have been necessary to follow the order of tradition, which they handed down to those to whom they committed the Churches?*

<margin>Tertullian.</margin>
Tertullian, in the book *On the Soldier's Crown*, chapters 3 and 4, recounts many Traditions which he admits are not found in the Scriptures, and yet says are observed by everyone: *Of these* (he says) *and other such disciplines, if you demand a law from the Scriptures, you will find none: Tradition is held out to you as the originator, custom as the strengthener, and faith as the observer.*

# LETTER FROM BARON SPEDALIERI
## TO THE PUBLISHER

---

->Marseille, August 18, 1896.<-

*My very dear Friend,*

*I learn with pleasure that, during your stay in London, you went, upon my recommendation, to visit my friend Maitland <note>Edward Maitland (1824–1897), a prominent British humanitarian and mystic</note> and to take note of various manuscript works by our beloved master Eliphas Lévi <note>The pseudonym of Alphonse Louis Constant (1810–1875), a highly influential French occult author</note> which I had given to him, including, among others, the Great Secret <note>original: "le Grand Arcane"; a central work in Lévi's occult philosophy</note>. All are in the Master's own handwriting, except for the last one, the Great Secret, and here is why.*

*When, in June 1868, he had finished writing this work intended for publication and written entirely by his own hand, he sent it to me so that I might read it and, at the same time, make a copy of it. Now, this is what I did with great care, and it is this faithful copy that I gave to Maitland. You may therefore consider it as if it were the original.*

*Please accept, dear Friend, my fraternal greetings,*

->**Baron SPEDALIERI.**<-
