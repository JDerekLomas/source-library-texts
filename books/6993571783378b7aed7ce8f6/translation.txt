# Chapter 1. The Grief of Arjuna.

Dhritarashtra said,

“Tell me, Sanjaya, what did my people and the Pandavas do when they gathered at Kurukshetra <note>A vast plain near modern Delhi, traditionally the site of this Great War.</note>, eager for battle?”

Sanjaya replied,

“After Duryodhana saw the Pandava army drawn up for battle, he went to his teacher <note>original: 'Preceptor'; referring to Drona, the instructor in the arts of war</note> and spoke these words:”

“Look, Master! See the mighty army of the sons of Pandu, marshaled by your intelligent pupil, the son of Drupada. In this army are heroes equal to Bhima or Arjuna. There are also Yuyudhana, Virata, and Drupada; Dhrishtaketu, Chekitana, and the valiant prince of Kashi; Purujit, Kuntibhoja, and Shaibya, a mighty chief; Yudhamanyu the brave, and the daring Uttamauja; as well as the son of Subhadra <note>Abhimanyu</note>, and the sons of Krishna <note>This refers to Draupadi, whose name is often Draupadi-Krishna</note>, the daughter of Drupada—all of them great warriors. Be also acquainted with the names of the most distinguished leaders on our side. I will mention a few of my generals as examples. There is yourself, my teacher, and Bhishma, and Kripa, the victor in battle, and Ashvatthama, and Vikarna, and the son of Somadatta, along with many others who have risked their lives for my sake. They are all practiced in the use of arms and experienced in every mode of fighting. Our vast forces are commanded by Bhishma, while the smaller army of our enemies is led by Bhima. Let all the generals, according to their respective divisions, stand at their posts, and everyone must resolve to support Bhishma.”

The elderly chief <note>original: 'The ancient chief'; referring to Bhishma, the great-uncle of both the Kurus and the Pandavas</note>, then roaring with a voice like a lion, blew his conch shell <note>original: 'shell'; the conch or chank shell used as a ritual trumpet</note> to lift the spirits of the Kuru leader.

Immediately, countless conchs and other warlike instruments were struck up on all sides, creating a deafening clamor. At this time, Krishna <note>An incarnation of the Deity.</note> and Arjuna <note>The third son of Pandu and the favorite of Krishna.</note> were standing in a magnificent chariot drawn by white horses.

They also sounded their celestial conchs. The one blown by Krishna was named *Panchajanya*, and Arjuna’s was called *Devadatta*. Bhima, known for his terrible deeds, blew his massive conch, *Paundra*, and Yudhishthira, the royal son of Kunti, sounded *Anantavijaya*. Nakula and Sahadeva blew their conchs as well; one called *Sughosha*, the other *Manipushpaka*. The prince of Kashi of the mighty bow, Shikhandi, Dhrishtadyumna, Virata, Satyaki of the invincible arm, Drupada and the sons of his royal daughter Draupadi, with the son of Subhadra, and all the other chiefs and nobles, also blew their respective conchs. Their shrill, sounding voices pierced the hearts of the Kuru party and echoed with a terrifying noise from heaven to earth.

In the meantime, Arjuna, perceiving that the sons of Dhritarashtra stood ready to begin the fight and that weapons were beginning to fly, took up his bow and spoke these words to Krishna:

Arjuna said,

“I beg you, Krishna, drive my chariot and place it between the two armies. I want to see the men who stand here ready and anxious to commence this bloody fight. I want to see who I must face on this battlefield and who has gathered here to support the vindictive son of Dhritarashtra in this battle.”

Krishna, having been addressed this way by Arjuna, drove the chariot and stopped it in the middle of the space between the two armies. He then told Arjuna to look toward the ranks of the Kurus <note>original: Kŏŏrŏŏs</note> and see where the elderly Bhishma and Drona stood, along with all the chief nobles of their party. Arjuna looked at both armies and saw on either side nothing but grandfathers, uncles, cousins, teachers, sons, brothers, close relations, and dear friends. After he had gazed for a while and saw such friends prepared for the fight, he was overcome with deep pity and remorse, and he expressed his sorrow with the following words:

Arjuna said,

> “O Krishna! Seeing my own kin standing here, anxious for the fight, my limbs fail me and my face withers. My hair stands on end and my whole body trembles with horror! My bow, Gandiva <note>original: Gāndēēv</note>, slips from my hand, and my skin feels parched and dried up.
>

> 
> I am unable to stand; my mind is reeling, and I see bad omens on all sides. When I have destroyed my own kin, can I expect any happiness? I do not wish for victory, Krishna; I do not want power or pleasure. For what is power, or the joys of life, or even life itself, when those for whom we desire power, pleasure, and enjoyment have abandoned their lives and fortunes and stand here on the battlefield ready for war? Teachers, sons, and fathers; grandfathers and grandsons; uncles and nephews; cousins, kin, and friends! Even if they were to kill me, I do not wish to fight them—not even for rule over the three regions of the universe, let alone for this little earth! 
>
> What pleasure, Krishna, can we find in killing the sons of Dhritarashtra? If we destroy them, even though they are tyrants, sin will cling to us. Therefore, we should not kill such near relations. How, Krishna, can we be happy hereafter if we have murdered our own race? Even if those whose minds are corrupted by the lust for power see no sin in the destruction of their family or no crime in the murder of their friends, is that a reason why we—who abhor the sin of destroying our own blood—should not resolve to turn away from such a crime? 
>

Alas, that for the desire for the pleasures of power, we stand here ready to murder our own flesh and blood! I would rather suffer patiently while the sons of Dhritarashtra, weapons in hand, come upon me and kill me while I am unarmed and unresisting on the field.”

# Chapter 2: The Nature of the Soul and Speculative Doctrines.

Krishna, seeing him so overcome by pity, his eyes overflowing with tears and his heart oppressed by deep sorrow, addressed him with these words:

Krishna said:

“Where did this folly and unmanly weakness come from, Arjuna, while you stand here on the field of battle? It is disgraceful, contrary to your duty <note>original: "contrary to duty"—specifically the duty of a soldier</note>, and the very foundation of dishonor. 

<note>(7) Contrary to duty—Contrary to the duty of a soldier.</note>

Do not give in to such cowardice, for it does not suit a man like you. Abandon this pathetic weakness of your heart and stand up.”

Arjuna said:

“How, Krishna, can I resolve to fight with my arrows on this battlefield against men like Bhishma and Drona? Of all men, they are the most worthy of my respect. I would rather beg for my bread around the world than be the murderer of my teachers <note>original: "preceptors"</note>, to whom such deep reverence is due. If I were to destroy friends such as these, any possessions, wealth, or pleasures I gained would be polluted with their blood. We do not even know which would be better: for us to defeat them, or for them to defeat us. The very people I would not wish to live without—the sons and followers of Dhritarashtra—are standing right here, drawn up against us. My compassionate nature is overcome by the dread of sin.

Tell me truly what is best for me to do. I am your disciple; therefore, instruct me in my duty, for I have come to you for guidance. My understanding is clouded by my conflicting duties <note>original: "confounded by the dictates of my duty"</note>,

<note>(8) By the dictates of my duty—The specific duty of a soldier, which here conflicts with general moral principles against killing.</note>

and I see nothing that can relieve the grief that numbs my senses <note>original: "drieth up my faculties"</note>, even if I were to obtain an unrivaled kingdom on earth or dominion over the hosts of heaven.”

When Arjuna had spoken these words to Krishna and declared that he would not fight, he fell silent. Krishna, smiling, addressed the grieving prince as he stood between the two armies:

Krishna said:

“You grieve for those who do not deserve your sorrow, yet you speak with the words of wise men <note>original: "sentiments are those of the wise men"</note>.

<note>(9) The wise men—Pandits, or expounders of the law; or more generally, those who have attained 'Jnana' <note>original: "Gnān"</note>, or inspired wisdom through meditation.</note>

The wise grieve neither for the dead nor for the living. There was never a time when I did not exist, nor you, nor all these princes of the earth; nor shall any of us ever cease to be in the future. Just as the soul in this mortal body experiences infancy, youth, and old age, so will it find a similar transition in a future body. One who is firm in this belief is not disturbed by anything that may happen. The senses produce feelings of heat and cold, pleasure and pain; these sensations come and go, and are temporary and inconsistent. Bear them with patience, Arjuna <note>original: "son of Bharat"</note>; for the wise man who is not disturbed by these things, and to whom pain and pleasure are the same, is prepared for immortality. Something that is merely imaginary has no real existence, while that which is true can never cease to exist. Those who look into the fundamental principles of things see the true nature of both. 

Learn that the Spirit by which all things were formed is indestructible, and no one is able to destroy that which is eternal. These bodies, which house the souls inhabiting them—souls that are eternal, indestructible, and beyond all human comprehension—are said to be finite. Therefore, Arjuna, resolve to fight! Both the man who believes the soul can kill and the man who thinks the soul can be destroyed are...

...are equally deceived; for the soul neither kills, nor is it killed. It is not something of which a man may say, "it was," "it is about to be," or "it will be hereafter." It is without birth; it is ancient, constant, and eternal, and it cannot be destroyed even within this mortal body. How can a man who believes that the soul is incorruptible, eternal, inexhaustible, and without birth, think that he can either kill it or cause it to be killed? Just as a man throws away old clothes and puts on new ones, the soul, having left its old mortal bodies, enters into others that are new. 

No weapon can cut it, fire cannot burn it, water cannot rot it, and the wind cannot dry it away. It is indivisible, inconsumable, and incorruptible. It is eternal, universal, permanent, and immovable. It is invisible, inconceivable, and unalterable; therefore, believing it to be so, you should not grieve. 

But even if you believe the soul is subject to eternal birth and death, or that it dies along with the body, you still have no reason to mourn. Death is certain for all things that are born, and rebirth <note>original: "regeneration"</note> is certain for all things that die. Therefore, you should not grieve over what is inevitable. The state of beings before birth is unknown; their middle state—this life—is evident, but their future state after death cannot be discovered. Why then should you trouble yourself about these things? Some view the soul as a wonder; some speak of it and others hear of it with astonishment; yet no one truly knows it, even if they have heard it described. 

Since this spirit can never be destroyed in the mortal body it inhabits, it is unworthy of you to be troubled for all these mortal people. Consider the duties of your own caste <note>original: "tribe"</note>, and you will see it is wrong for you to hesitate. A soldier of the <term>Kshatriya</term> <note>original: "Kshăttrĕĕ"; the warrior and ruling caste in traditional Hindu society</note> caste has no higher duty than to fight. Just as you might wish, the door of heaven is opened before you. Only those soldiers favored by Heaven obtain such a glorious opportunity to fight as this. But if you will not perform the duty of your calling and fight this battle, you will abandon your duty and your honor, and you will be guilty of a crime. 

People will speak of your fame as infinite and inexhaustible. For one who has been respected in the world, disgrace is worse than death <note>original: "extended even beyond the dissolution of the body"</note>. The generals of the armies will think you fled the field out of fear, and you will become despised even by those who used to respect you. Your enemies will speak of you in shameful words, insulting your courage and abilities—what could be more terrible than that! If you are killed, you will gain heaven; if you are victorious, you will enjoy the world as your reward. Therefore, son of <term>Kunti</term> <note>original: "Kŏŏntēē"; Arjuna's mother</note>, stand up and resolve to fight. Treat pleasure and pain, gain and loss, victory and defeat the same, and then prepare for battle; if you do not, you will be deeply at fault. Apply your reason in this way on the battlefield.

This judgment I have shared is based on the speculative doctrines of the <term>Sankhya Shastra</term> <note>original spelling: "Sānkhyă sāstră"; a school of Hindu philosophy focusing on the analytical knowledge of the soul and nature</note>. Now, hear how it applies in practice; once you are equipped with this knowledge, you will be able to cast off the bonds of action (10).

(10 **The bonds of action.**—Hindus believe that every bodily action, whether good or evil, binds the soul to mortal birth. They believe an eternal release, which they call <term>Mukti</term> <note>original: "Mŏŏktĕĕ"</note>, can only be achieved by a total neglect of all earthly <note>original: "sublunary"</note> things, or—according to the doctrine of <term>Krishna</term>—by abandoning all hope for the rewards of our actions. They say such rewards can only provide a temporary stay in a heavenly place they call <term>Swarga</term> <note>original: "Swărg"</note>, because no man can achieve perfection through actions alone, due to the mixture of good and evil inherent in human nature.)

Even a very small amount of this duty saves a man from great fear. In this path, there is only one focused purpose; but the purposes of those who lack firm principles are endless and scattered in many directions.

Men of narrow minds, who delight in the debates of the <term>Vedas</term> <note>original: "Vēds"; the oldest and most sacred Hindu scriptures</note> and are tainted by worldly desires, prefer a temporary enjoyment of heaven over eternal union with the divine. While they claim there is no other reward, they speak flowery words focused on attaining worldly riches and pleasures, prescribing countless various ceremonies and promising rewards for the actions of this life. The resolve of those attached to wealth and enjoyment, whose reason is led astray by such teaching, is not based on mature consideration and meditation. The subjects of the <term>Vedas</term> deal with the three-fold nature of the material world (11).

(11 **The subjects of the Vedas deal with the three-fold nature.**—Commentators disagree on the meaning of this passage, but as the <term>Vedas</term> teach three distinct systems of religion, it likely refers to this fact.)

Be free from this three-fold nature; be free from double-mindedness and stand firm in the path of truth. Be free from care and trouble, and turn your mind toward spiritual things. To the enlightened person <note>original: "knowing divine"</note>, the entire <term>Vedas</term> are as useful as a small pond is when the whole land is flooded with water.

Let your motive be in the action itself, and not in the outcome. Do not be one whose reason for acting is the hope of a reward. Let

...do not let your life be spent in inaction. Rely on disciplined effort <note>original: "application"</note>, perform your duty, and abandon all thought of the consequences. Treat the outcome the same, whether it ends in good or evil; for such equanimity is called *Yoga* (12).

<note>(12 *Yoga*—There is no word in the Sanskrit <note>original: "Sănskrěět"</note> language that carries as many meanings as this. Its primary meaning is "junction" or "union." It is also used for physical or mental discipline; but in this work, it is generally used as a theological term to express the focused application of the mind to spiritual matters and the performance of religious rituals. The word *Yogi* <note>original: "Yōgēē"</note>, a devout person, is one of its derivatives. If the word "devotion" is understood as the performance of religious duties and the contemplation of God, it generally captures the sense of the original, as do the words "devout" and "devoted.")</note>

Physical action is far inferior to the disciplined application of wisdom. Seek refuge, then, in wisdom (13) alone; for those who are miserable and unhappy are only so because they are attached to the outcome of things.

<note>(13 Wisdom—Wherever "wisdom" is used in this translation, it refers to inspired wisdom or a direct knowledge of the Divine Nature. The original word is *Jnana* <note>original: "Gnān" or "Jnān"</note>.)</note>

People who are endowed with true wisdom do not dwell on good or evil in this world. Strive, then, to attain this discipline of your understanding, for such focus in one's affairs is a precious skill.

Wise people, who have abandoned all concern for the "fruit" or results produced by their actions, are freed from the chains of rebirth <note>original: "birth"</note> and reach the realms of eternal happiness.

When your reason overcomes the gloomy weakness of your heart, you will have attained all the knowledge that has been, or is worthy to be, taught. When your understanding is matured by study and fixed immovably in contemplation, then it shall obtain true wisdom.”

->Arjuna <note>original: "Ărjŏŏn"</note> said,<-

What, O Krishna <note>original: "Krěěshnă"</note>, characterizes the wise and steady person who is fixed in contemplation? What does such a sage say? Where does he live? How does he act?

->Krishna <note>original: "Krěěshnă"</note> said,<-

A person is said to be established in wisdom when they forsake every desire that enters the heart, and find happiness and contentment within themselves. Their mind is undisturbed in adversity; they are happy and contented in prosperity, and they are a stranger to anxiety, fear, and anger. Such a wise person is called a sage <note>original: "Mŏŏněě" (Muni)</note>. The wisdom of that person is established who is without worldly attachments in all things; and, having received good or evil, neither rejoices at the one nor is cast down by the other. Their wisdom is confirmed when, like the tortoise, they can draw in all their limbs and restrain them from their usual purposes. A hungry person loses interest in everything but satisfying their appetite; and once they become acquainted with the Supreme Being, they lose interest even in that. The unruly senses forcefully carry away the heart even of a wise person who strives to restrain them. The inspired person, trusting in me, may subdue them and be happy. The person who has their passions under control possesses true wisdom.

The person who dwells on the temptations of the senses develops an attachment to them; from this attachment, passion is created; from passion comes anger; from anger comes folly (14); from folly comes a corruption of memory; from the loss of memory comes the loss of reason; and from the loss of reason comes the loss of everything!

<note>(14 Folly—In the original, *Moha*, which refers to a confusion of the mental faculties arising from the material qualities of the world.)</note>

A person of a disciplined mind, who enjoys the objects of the senses with all their faculties obedient to their will—and who is free from pride and malice—obtains supreme happiness. In this happiness, they are granted an exemption from all their troubles; and with the mind thus at ease, wisdom soon flows to them from all sides. The person who does not attend to this is without wisdom or the power of contemplation. The person who is incapable of deep thought has no rest. What happiness can someone enjoy who has no rest? The heart that follows the dictates of moving passions carries away one's reason, just as a storm carries away a ship on a raging ocean. Therefore, the person who can restrain all their passions from their excessive desires is endowed with true wisdom. Such a person is awake during that "night" when all other things go to rest—the night of spiritual reality <note>original: "night of time"</note>. The contemplative sage <note>original: "Mŏŏněě"</note> sleeps during the "day" of the material world, when all other beings are awake.

# Chapter 3: Of Works

Arjuna said,

If, in your opinion, the use of the understanding is superior to the practice of deeds <gloss>(15 The practice of deeds: The performance of religious ceremonies and moral duties, called *Karma-Yoga*.)</gloss>, why then do you urge me to engage in an undertaking as dreadful as this?

You seem to confuse my reason with a mixture of conflicting sentiments; therefore, choose one path among them by which I may obtain happiness, and explain it to me.

Krishna said,

It has been observed by me before that in this world there are two systems: that of those who follow the *Sankhya*, or speculative science, which is the exercise of reason in contemplation; and the practical path, which is the exercise of moral and religious duties.

A person does not enjoy freedom from action simply by avoiding the start of what he has to do; nor does he obtain happiness from total inactivity. No one ever rests for a single moment in total inactivity. Every person is involuntarily urged to act by those principles which are inherent in his nature. The person who restrains his physical faculties but sits down with his mind focused on the objects of his senses is called a person with a lost soul and a practitioner of deceit. But the person is praised who, having subdued all his passions, performs all the functions of life with his active faculties while remaining unconcerned about the result. Perform your established duties: action is preferable to inaction. Even the maintenance of your mortal body could not succeed through inaction. This busy world is engaged in activity for many motives other than the worship of the Deity. Abandon then, O son of Kunti, all selfish motives, and perform your duty for Him alone.

When in ancient days Brahma <gloss>(16 Brahma: The Deity in his creative aspect.)</gloss>, the lord of creation, had formed mankind and, at the same time, established the way of worship, he spoke and said: "With this worship, pray for increase, and let it be that upon which you shall depend for the accomplishment of all your wishes. Through this, remember the Gods, so that the Gods may remember you. Remember one another, and you shall obtain supreme happiness. The Gods, being remembered in worship, will grant you the enjoyment of your wishes. He who enjoys what has been given to him by them, but offers not a portion back to them, is just like a thief. Those who eat only what is left over from the offerings shall be purified of all their transgressions. Those who prepare their food only for themselves eat the bread of sin. All living things are generated from the food which they eat. Food is generated from rain; rain from divine worship, and divine worship from good works. Know that good works come from Brahman <note>original: "Brăhm"; the ultimate reality or world-soul</note>, whose nature is incorruptible; therefore, the omnipresent Brahman is present in the worship."

The sinful mortal, who delights in the gratification of his passions and does not follow the wheel of life thus revolving in the world, lives in vain.

But the person who is self-delighted and self-satisfied, and who is happy within his own soul, has no further obligation <gloss>(17 Has no further obligation: Has no need to perform the ceremonial or ritualistic parts of religion.)</gloss>.

He has no personal interest either in what is done or what is not done; and there is not, in all created things, any object on which he must place his dependence. Therefore, always perform that which you have to do without thinking of the result; for the person who does what he must do without attachment obtains the Supreme.

Janaka and others have attained perfection <note>original: "(18)"; Janaka was a legendary king-philosopher who achieved enlightenment while fulfilling his royal duties</note> even through their works.

# Chapter 4: On the Renunciation of Action

Krĕĕshnă said,

"I formerly taught this eternal discipline <note>original: "discipline"; here referring to *Yoga*, a spiritual path or method</note> to Vĕĕvăswăt <note>the Sun-god</note>. Vĕĕvăswăt communicated it to Mănŏŏ <note>the first man and lawgiver</note>, and Mănŏŏ made it known to Eĕkshwākŏŏ <note>the first king of the Solar Dynasty</note>. Being passed down from one to another, it was studied by the Rājărshĕĕs <note>Royal Sages</note> until, at length, over the course of time, this mighty art was lost. It is the very same discipline that I have communicated to you today, because you are my servant and my friend. It is an ancient and supreme mystery."

Ărjŏŏn said,

"Seeing as your birth occurred long after the life of Eĕkshwākŏŏ, how am I to understand that you were the original teacher of this doctrine?"

Krĕĕshnă said,

"Both you and I have passed through many births. I know all of mine, but you do not know yours.

Although my nature is not subject to birth or decay, and I am the lord of all created beings, I still hold command over my own nature and manifest myself through my own power. Whenever there is a decline in virtue <note>original: "virtue"; here referring to *Dharma*, or moral order</note> and a rise in vice and injustice in the world, I manifest myself. I appear from age to age to preserve the just, to destroy the wicked, and to re-establish virtue.

O Ărjŏŏn, he who truly understands my divine birth and actions does not enter another mortal body upon leaving his current one; instead, he enters into me. Many who were free from attachment, fear, and anger—filled with my spirit and depending on me—have been purified by the power of wisdom and have entered into me. I assist all men who walk my path, just as they serve me.

Those who wish for success in their worldly works worship the Dēvătās (22).

<note>(22) **Worship the Dēvătās**: The word *Dēvătā* is synonymous with *Dev*, *Dew*, or *Deb*. These are the angels, or subordinate celestial beings; they represent the attributes of the Deity and everything in Heaven and Earth that poets have personified.</note>

Success achieved through worldly works in this life comes quickly.

I created mankind in four distinct types, differing in their inner principles and their duties <note>referring to the *Varna* or caste system based on temperament and profession</note>. Know me, then, to be the creator of mankind—yet I am uncreated and changeless.

Works do not affect me, nor do I have any desire for the fruits of my actions. He who believes me to be so is not bound by his works. The ancients, who longed for eternal salvation, performed their duties after discovering this truth. Therefore, perform your works just as the ancients did in former times. Even the learned are puzzled to determine what is 'action' and what is 'inaction.' I will tell you what 'work' is, so that by knowing it, you will be delivered from misfortune. It may be defined as action, improper action, and inaction. The path of action is obscure and difficult to understand.

He who can see inaction in action, and action in inaction, is wise among men. He is a perfect performer of all duties.

Wise men call him a Păndĕĕt <note>a scholar or wise man</note> whose every undertaking is free from selfish desire, and whose actions are consumed by the fire of wisdom. He abandons the desire for a reward for his actions; he is always contented and independent; and although he may be engaged in work, it is as if he does nothing at all. He is free from anxiety, with a disciplined mind and spirit, and is exempt from selfish perceptions. Because he performs only the necessary functions of the body, he commits no offense. He is pleased with whatever he may obtain by chance and has moved beyond the conflict of opposites <note>original: "duplicity"; referring to the dualities of life like pleasure and pain</note>."

# Chapter 5: On Renouncing the Fruits of Labor.

**Arjuna** <note>original: "Ărjŏŏn"</note> said,

"Now you speak, O **Krishna** <note>original: "Krĕĕshnă"</note>, of renouncing works, and then again of performing them. Tell me definitely which of the two is better."

**Krishna** said,

"Both the renunciation <note>original: "desertion"</note> and the practice of works are equally means to reach ultimate happiness; but of the two, the practice of works is superior to renunciation. The perpetual recluse, who neither desires nor complains, is worthy to be known. Such a person is free from conflict <note>original: "duplicity"</note> and is happily freed from the bonds of action. Only children, and not the learned, speak of the theoretical <note>original: "speculative"</note> and the practical doctrines as two separate things. They are actually one, for both achieve the very same end; the state reached by the followers of the one is also reached by the followers of the other. That person truly sees who sees that the theoretical and the practical paths are one.

To be a **Sannyasi** <term>Sănnyāsēē: a religious recluse or someone who has renounced the world</term>, or recluse, without dedicated practice, is to find only pain and trouble. Meanwhile, the **Muni** <term>Mŏŏnĕĕ: a sage or inspired saint</term>, who is actively employed in the practice of his duty, soon reaches **Brahman** <note>original: "Brăhm"; the ultimate reality or the Almighty</note>. The person who is employed in the practice of works, who has a purified soul, a disciplined spirit, and restrained passions, and whose soul is one with the universal soul, is not tainted by his actions. 

The disciplined man, who is acquainted with the true principles of things, thinks that he does nothing himself while he is seeing, hearing, touching, smelling, eating, moving, sleeping, breathing, talking, letting go, taking hold, or opening and closing his eyes. He understands that his faculties are merely occupied with their respective objects. The person who performs the duties of life and gives up all personal interest in them—placing them instead upon **Brahman**, the Supreme—is not stained by sin; he remains like the leaf of the lotus, which is unaffected by the water it floats upon. 

Practical men, who perform the duties of life using only their bodies, their minds, their intellect, and their senses, and who forsake the consequences for the sake of purifying their souls, obtain infinite happiness—even while they are active—because they have renounced the 'fruit' or reward of their actions. Conversely, the person who is not disciplined, being attached to the reward by the impulse of desire, remains bound in confinement. The person who has his passions under control and has mentally renounced all works, dwells at rest in the 'nine-gate city' of his body <note>original: "the nine-gate city of its abode." (26)</note>, neither acting himself nor causing others to act.

<margin>26. The body is described as having nine "gates" or passages for the senses: the eyes, nostrils, mouth, etc.</margin>

The Almighty creates neither the powers nor the deeds of mankind <note>original: "deeds of mankind" (27)</note>, nor the connection to the rewards of action; instead, nature prevails.

<margin>27. To understand this, one must know that the Hindus believe all actions, good or evil, arise from the inherent qualities and principles of our physical and mental constitutions.</margin>

The Almighty receives neither the vices nor the virtues of anyone. Mankind is led astray because their reason is obscured by ignorance. But when that ignorance in their souls is destroyed by the force of reason, their wisdom shines forth again like the glory of the sun and reveals the Deity. Those whose intellects are centered in him, whose souls are in him, whose faith is in him, and who find their refuge in him, are purified of all their offenses by wisdom. They go to a state from which they shall never return <note>referring to liberation from the cycle of rebirth</note>.

The learned see the Divine equally in a venerable **Brahmin** <term>Brāhmăn: a member of the highest priestly caste</term> perfected in knowledge, in an ox, in an elephant, in a dog, and even in an outcast <note>original: "him who eateth of the flesh of dogs"</note>. Those whose minds are fixed on this equality gain eternity even in this world. They put their trust in **Brahman**, the Eternal, because he is everywhere the same and free from fault.

The person who knows **Brahman**, trusts in **Brahman**, and whose mind is steady and free from folly, should neither rejoice in prosperity nor complain in adversity. He whose soul is unaffected by external impressions finds pleasure within his own mind. Such a person, whose soul is fixed on the study of **Brahman**, enjoys a happiness that never declines. The enjoyments that come from the senses are like the wombs of future pain. The wise person, who understands the beginning and the end of things, takes no delight in them. He who can withstand the internal violence produced by lust and anger during this mortal life is truly...

Krishna said,

He cannot be a Yogi who has not abandoned all personal motives <note>original: "intentions"</note> in his actions. 

Action is said to be the means by which a person who desires it may attain devotion; similarly, tranquility <note>original: "rest"</note> is the means for the one who has already attained devotion. When the deeply contemplative Sannyasi is no longer attached to the objects of the senses or to his works, then he is said to have attained devotion. He should lift himself up by his own effort; he should not allow his soul to be degraded. For the self is its own friend, and likewise, the self is its own enemy. The self is a friend to the person who has mastered their spirit with their spirit; but to the person who has no self-control <note>original: "hath no soul"</note>, the self acts like an enemy, delighting in hostility. 

The soul of the calm, self-conquered person remains the same—steadfast and composed—whether in heat or cold, pain or pleasure, honor or disgrace. The person whose mind is filled with divine wisdom and learning, who stands firm as if on a mountain peak <note>original: "upon the pinnacle"</note>, and has mastered his passions, is considered truly devout. To the Yogi, gold, iron, and stones are all the same. The person who maintains the same steady resolve whether among companions and friends, in the midst of enemies, among those who are indifferent or mediators, with those who love and those who hate, or in the company of saints or sinners, is highly distinguished.

The Yogi constantly disciplines the spirit in private. He lives as a recluse with a controlled mind and spirit, free from worldly hopes and possessiveness <note>original: "perception"</note>. He should place his seat firmly in a clean spot that is neither too high nor too low. He sits upon the sacred **Kusha** <note>original: "Kŏŏs"</note> grass, covered with a deerskin and a cloth. There, with the goal of restraining his passions, he should sit with his mind focused on a single object. In this practice of devotion for the purification of his soul, he should keep his head, neck, and body steady and motionless, fixing his gaze on the tip of his nose without looking anywhere else. The peaceful soul, freed from fear and committed to the path of a seeker of God <note>original: "one who followeth God"</note>, should control the mind and, fixing it on me, depend on me alone. The Yogi of a humble mind, who constantly disciplines his soul in this way, attains a supreme and spiritual <note>original: "incorporeal"</note> happiness in me.

This divine discipline, **Arjuna** <note>original: Ărjŏŏn</note>, cannot be reached by someone who eats too much or too little, nor by someone who sleeps too much or does not sleep at all. The discipline that removes suffering belongs to the one who is moderate in eating and recreation, whose actions are driven by moderate desires, and who is moderate in sleep. A person is called devout when their mind remains regulated within itself, and they are free from all lust and excessive desire. 

The Yogi of a disciplined mind, who is engaged in this practice of devotion, is compared to a lamp in a windless place, which does not flicker. He delights in his own soul; there, his mind—regulated by devotional service—is pleased to dwell, and there, with the help of the spirit, he perceives the soul. He becomes acquainted with that boundless pleasure which is far more satisfying to the intellect than anything arising from the senses. Once grounded in this, the mind does not stray from its principles. Having obtained this, he considers no other achievement to be as great; and while grounded in this, he is not moved even by the most severe pain. This freedom from the union with pain <note>original: "disunion from the conjunction of pain"</note> is known by the name **Yoga** <note>original: "Yōg"</note>, or spiritual union and devotion. 

It is attained through determination by the person who understands their own mind. Once he has abandoned every desire born from the imagination and has used his mind to subdue every impulse of the senses, he may gradually find peace. Having fixed his mind within himself through steady resolution, he should think of nothing else. Wherever the unsteady mind wanders, he should subdue it, bring it back, and center it within his own heart <note>original: "breast"</note>. 

Supreme happiness comes to the person whose mind is at peace, whose physical cravings and passions are subdued, and who—being free from sin—exists in God. The person who is thus constantly disciplining the soul and is free from sin enjoys eternal happiness, united with **Brahman** <note>original: "Brăhm"</note> the Supreme. The person whose mind is filled with this devotion, and who looks upon all things with equality, sees the Supreme Soul in all things, and all things in the Supreme Soul. To him who sees me in all things and sees all things in me, I am never lost, and he is never lost to me. The Yogi who believes in this unity and worships me as present in all things dwells in me in every way, even while he still lives. The person, O Arjuna, who judges pleasure or pain in others by the same standard he uses for himself <note>original: "from what passeth in his own breast"</note> is considered the highest Yogi."

# Chapter 12: Of Serving the Deity in His Visible and Invisible Forms.

Arjuna said:

Of those servants of yours who are always devoted in this way, which ones know their duty best? Those who worship you as you appear now, or those who serve you in your invisible and incorruptible nature?

Krishna said:

Those who have placed their minds on me, serving me with constant zeal and filled with steady faith, are considered the most devoted. Those who delight in the welfare of all living things and serve me in my incorruptible, inexpressible <note>original: "ineffable"</note>, and invisible form—which is omnipresent, incomprehensible, exalted, fixed, and immovable—with their passions and minds under control, remaining the same toward everything, shall also come to me. 

Those whose minds are focused on my invisible nature face a greater struggle, because an invisible path is difficult for embodied <note>original: "corporeal"</note> beings to find. But for those who prefer me, dedicating all their actions to me, and who—free from the worship of any others—contemplate and serve me alone, I quickly rescue them from the ocean of this mortal world, because their minds are attached to me. 

Place your heart on me, then, and fill your understanding with me; you will undoubtedly enter into me hereafter. But if you are unable to fix your mind steadily on me right away, try to reach me through the practice of constant devotion. If you are still unable even after practice, then perform your highest works for me; for by performing actions for my sake, you will attain perfection. But if you find yourself unable to do even this, then put your trust in me alone. Be humble in spirit and renounce the results <note>original: "fruit"</note> of every action. Knowledge is better than mere practice; meditation is superior to knowledge; and renouncing the results of action is even better than meditation—for peace <note>original: "happiness"</note> follows immediately from such renunciation.

That servant of mine is dear to me who is free from hatred, a friend to all living things, merciful, free from pride and selfishness, balanced in both pain and pleasure, patient when wronged, contented, always devoted, with controlled passions and firm resolve, and whose mind and understanding are fixed on me alone. He is also my beloved who does not cause fear in others, and who is not afraid of others; who is free from the pressures of worldly joy, impatience, and the fear of harm. 

That servant is dear to me who expects nothing, who is just and pure, impartial, free from mental distraction, and who has renounced every personal ambition <note>original: "enterprise"</note>. He is also worthy of my love who neither rejoices nor complains, who neither grieves nor desires, and who, as my servant, has let go of both good and bad luck. He is also my beloved servant who treats friend and foe the same; who is the same in honor and dishonor, in cold and heat, in pain and pleasure; who is unconcerned about how things turn out; to whom praise and blame are the same; who speaks little and is satisfied with whatever happens; who claims no particular home and has a steady mind. Those who seek this <term>Amrita</term> (101) of religion exactly as I have described, and who serve me faithfully above all others, are truly my dearest friends.

Arjuna <note>original: "Arjŏŏn"</note> said:

Krishna <note>original: "Krĕĕshnă"</note> said:

Understand that the word *Kshētră* <term>the field</term> refers to this body, and he who is acquainted with it is called *Kshētră-gnă* <term>the knower of the field</term>. Know that I am that Knower in every mortal frame. I consider the knowledge of the body and its Knower to be *Gnān*, or true wisdom.

Now hear what that *Kshētră* (the body) is, what it resembles, what its different parts are, where it comes from, who knows it, and what it produces. These things have been sung many times by the *Rĕĕshĕĕs* <note>original: "Rĕĕshĕĕs," meaning sages or seers</note> in various poetic meters and in verses containing divine precepts, including arguments and proofs.

This *Kshētră*, or body, then, is made up of the five *Māhābhōōt* (the great elements), *Ăhănkār* (self-consciousness), *Bŏŏdhĕĕ* (understanding), *Ăvyăktăm* (invisible spirit), the eleven *Eĕndrĕĕyă* (organs), and the five *Eĕndrĕĕyă-gōchăr* (faculties of the five senses); along with *Eechā* and *Dwēshă* (desire and hatred), *Sookh* and *Dookh* (pleasure and pain), *Chētănā* (sensibility), and *Dhrĕĕtĕĕ* (firmness).

Thus have I made known to you what that *Kshētră*, or body, is, and what its component parts are.

*Gnān*, or wisdom, consists of freedom from self-importance, hypocrisy, and causing harm; it is patience, honesty, respect for masters and teachers, purity, steadiness, self-control, and a lack of interest in the objects of the senses. It includes freedom from pride and a constant attention (102) to birth, death, old age <note>original: "decay"</note>, sickness, pain, and human flaws. It means being free from worldly attachments and from excessive focus (103) on children, wife, and home; maintaining a constant evenness of temper regardless of whether an event is desirable or not; and offering constant and unwavering worship to me alone. It involves seeking solitude <note>original: "worshipping in a private place"</note>, avoiding the busy society of men, constantly studying the superior spirit (104), and examining the benefits that come from knowing the first principle <note>original: "Tăttwă"</note>.

[The Supreme Spirit] contains all things; and without possessing qualities itself, it participates in every quality. Spirit <note>original: "Pŏŏrŏŏsh"</note> is the principle <note>original: "Hētŏŏ"</note> that operates in the sensation of pain and pleasure. The Spirit is that superior being who is called *Măhēswar* <note>the Great God</note>, the most high spirit, who in this body is the observer, the director, the protector, and the partaker.

Whoever understands the Spirit and Nature, together with the qualities <note>original: "Gŏŏn"</note>, to be exactly as I have described them, will not be subject to mortal birth again, regardless of the life they lead.

Some people, through meditation, behold the spirit within themselves with their own minds; others do so according to the discipline of the *Sānkhyă* <term>contemplative or rational doctrines</term>, and the discipline called *Kărmă-yōg* <term>the path of selfless action</term>. Others again, who are not acquainted with these paths but have heard of them from others, still follow them. Even these, who act based on the reports of others, pass beyond the gulf of death.

Know, O chief of the race of Bharat <note>a title for Arjuna, referring to his royal lineage</note>, that everything produced in nature—whether animate or inanimate—is produced from the union of the body <note>original: "Kshētră"</note> and the soul <note>original: "Kshētră-gnă"</note>, or matter and spirit. He who sees the Supreme Being equally in all things—remaining incorruptible even while the body decays—and realizes that God is the same in all things, does not harm his own soul and travels the journey toward immortality. He who realizes that all his actions are performed by Nature <note>original: "Prăkrĕĕtĕĕ"</note> also perceives that the soul <note>original: "Ătmă"</note> is not the actor in them. When he sees all the different species in nature contained within one single source, and spreading out from it into their vast variety, he then understands the Supreme Being <note>original: "Brāhm"</note>. This supreme spirit and incorruptible Being, even when it is in the body, neither acts nor is it affected, because its nature is without beginning and without qualities. Just as the all-pervading ether <note>original: "Ākās"</note>, because of its extreme fineness, passes everywhere without being affected, so the omnipresent spirit remains in the body without being affected by it. As a single sun illuminates the whole world, so does the spirit enlighten every body. Those who, with the eye of wisdom, perceive that the body and the spirit are distinct, and that there is an ultimate release from animal nature, go to the Supreme.

# Chapter 14: Of the Three Gunas or Qualities.

Krishna said,

I will now reveal to you a most sublime knowledge, superior to all others. Having learned this, all the *Munis* <note>original: "Mŏŏnĕĕs"; ancient sages or enlightened saints</note> have passed from this life to supreme perfection. They take sanctuary under this wisdom and, having arrived at a virtue similar to my own, they are not disturbed on the day of the confusion of all things <note>the periodic dissolution of the universe</note>, nor are they born again when the world is renewed.

The great *Brahm* <note>here referring to the "Great Nature" or primordial matter</note> is my womb. In it I place my seed <note>original: "fœtus"</note>, and from it comes the production of all nature. The great *Brahm* is the womb of all those various forms which are conceived in every natural womb, and I am the father who sows the seed.

There are three *Gunas* <note>original: "Gŏŏn"; qualities, attributes, or modes of being</note> arising from *Prakriti* <note>original: "Prăkrĕĕtĕĕ"; primordial nature</note>: *Sattva* (truth and goodness), *Rajas* (passion), and *Tamas* (darkness and inertia). Each of them confines the incorruptible spirit within the body. The *Sattva* quality, because of its purity, is clear and free from defect, and it entwines the soul with pleasant consequences and the fruit of wisdom. The *Rajas* quality is of a passionate nature, arising from the effects of worldly thirst, and it imprisons the soul with the consequences produced by action. The *Tamas* quality is the offspring of ignorance and the confounder of all the faculties of the mind; it imprisons the soul with intoxication, sloth, and idleness.

The *Sattva* quality prevails in happiness, the *Rajas* in action, and the *Tamas*, having possessed the soul, prevails in intoxication. When *Tamas* and *Rajas* have been overcome, then *Sattva* appears; when *Rajas* and *Sattva* are overcome, *Tamas* appears; and when *Tamas* and *Sattva* are overcome, *Rajas* appears. When *Jnana* <note>original: "Gnān"; wisdom or spiritual knowledge</note> becomes evident in this body at all its "gates" <note>the senses</note>, then it shall be known that the *Sattva* quality is prevalent within. The love of gain, industry, and the commencement of works, as well as intemperance and inordinate desire, are produced from the prevalence of the *Rajas* quality; while the tokens of the *Tamas* quality are gloominess, idleness, foolishness, and distraction of thought.

When the body is dissolved <note>at death</note> while the *Sattva* quality prevails, the soul proceeds to the regions of those immaculate beings who are acquainted with the Most High. When the body finds dissolution while the *Rajas* quality is predominant, the soul is born again among those who are attached to the fruits of their actions. Similarly, should the body be dissolved while the *Tamas* quality is prevalent, the spirit is conceived again in the wombs of irrational beings <note>animals or lower life forms</note>. The fruit of good works is called pure and holy; the fruit of the *Rajas* quality is pain; and the fruit of the *Tamas* quality is ignorance. From *Sattva* is produced wisdom, from *Rajas* covetousness, and from *Tamas* madness, distraction, and ignorance. Those of the *Sattva* quality mount on high, those of the *Rajas* stay in the middle, while those abject followers of the *Tamas* quality sink below.

When the observer perceives no other agent than these qualities, and discovers that there is a being superior to them, he at length finds my nature. When the soul has surpassed these three qualities, which coexist with the body, it is delivered from birth and death, old age and pain, and drinks of the water of immortality.

Arjun said,

By what signs is it known that a man has surpassed these three qualities? What is his practice? What are the means by which he overcomes them?

Krishna said,

He, O son of Pandu, who does not despise the light of wisdom, the attention to worldly things, or the distraction of thought when they come upon him, nor longs for them when they disappear; who, like one who is of no party <note>neutral or impartial</note>, sits unagitated by the three qualities; who, while the qualities are present, stands still and moves not; who is self-dependent and the same in ease and pain, and to whom iron, stone, and gold are all as one; firm alike in love and dislike, and the same whether praised or blamed; the same in honor and disgrace; the same toward friend and foe, and who forsakes all worldly enterprise—such a person has surmounted the influence of the qualities. And he, my servant, who serves me alone with due attention, having overcome the influence of the qualities, is prepared to be absorbed in *Brahm*, the Supreme. I am the emblem of the immortal and of the incorruptible; of the eternal, of justice, and of endless bliss.

# Chapter 15: Of Purushottama <note>original: "Pŏŏrŏŏshŏttămă"; the Supreme Being or "Highest Spirit"</note>.

Krishna said:

The incorruptible being is likened to the *Ashvattha* tree <note>original: "Ăswătthă"; the sacred fig tree (*Ficus religiosa*), often used as a metaphor for the universe</note>, whose roots are above and whose branches are below, and whose leaves are the *Vedas* <note>original: "Vēds"; the most ancient Hindu scriptures</note>. He who knows that tree is acquainted with the *Vedas*. Its branches grow from the three *Gunas* <note>original: "Gŏŏn"; the qualities of nature—goodness, passion, and inertia</note>; its lesser shoots are the objects of the organs of sense, spreading both high and low. The roots which spread abroad below, in the regions of mankind, are bound by action. Its true form is not perceived here, neither its beginning, nor its end, nor its exact likeness. 

When a man has cut down this *Ashvattha* tree—whose roots are so firmly fixed—with the strong axe of detachment <note>original: "disinterest"</note>, from that time he should seek the place from which there is no return for those who find it. I manifest that first *Purusha* <note>original: "Pŏŏrŏŏsh"; the primordial person or spirit</note> from whom the ancient progression of all things was produced.

Those who are free from pride and ignorance, who have prevailed over the faults arising from the consequences of action, and who have their minds constantly employed in watching over and restraining inordinate desires, are liberated. They are freed from "contrary causes" <note>the dualities of life</note>—whose consequences bring both pleasure and pain—and are no longer confused in their minds; they ascend to that place which endures forever. Neither the sun, nor the moon, nor fire enlightens that place from which there is no return, and which is the supreme mansion of my abode.

It is a portion of myself that, in this animal world, is the universal spirit of all things. It draws together the five organs and the mind, which is the sixth, so that it may obtain a body and leave it again. *Ishvara* <note>original: "Eĕswār"; the Supreme Lord</note>, having taken these under his charge, accompanies them from his own abode, just as the breeze carries fragrance from a flower. He presides over the organs of hearing, seeing, feeling, tasting, and smelling, together with the mind, and attends to their objects. 

The foolish do not see the spirit—attended by the *Gunas*—when it is departing, when it is present, or when it is enjoying. But those who are endowed with the eye of wisdom behold it. Those who industriously apply their minds in meditation may perceive it planted in their own breasts; however, those of unformed minds and weak judgment, despite their labor, do not find it.

Know that the light which proceeds from the sun and illuminates the whole world, and the light which is in the moon and in the fire, are mine. I pervade all things in nature and guard them with my beams. I am the moon, whose nature it is to give the quality of taste and relish, and to cherish the herbs and plants of the field. I am the fire residing in the bodies of all living things; there, joined with the two spirits called *Prana* and *Apana* <note>original: "Prān and Ōpān"; the breathing spirit and the spirit that acts in the bowels to expel waste</note>, I digest the food they eat, which is of four kinds <note>food that is either chewed, lapped with the tongue, sucked by the lips, or drunk</note>.

I penetrate into the hearts of all men; and from me proceed memory, knowledge, and the loss of both. I am to be known by all the *Vedas*; I am he who formed the *Vedanta* <note>original: "Vēdānt"; a philosophical system exploring the nature of God and reality, literally "the end of the Vedas"</note>, and I am he who knows the *Vedas*.

There are two kinds of *Purusha* in the world: the one corruptible, the other incorruptible. The corruptible *Purusha* is the body of all things in nature; the incorruptible is called *Kutastha* <note>original: "Kŏŏthăstă"; the unchangeable spirit, or "he who stands on the pinnacle"</note>. There is another *Purusha* most high: the *Paramatma* <note>original: "Părămātmă"; the supreme soul</note> or supreme soul, who inhabits the three regions of the world—the incorruptible *Ishvara*.

# Chapter 16: Of Divine and Evil Destiny.

Krishna <note>original: Krĕĕshnă</note> said,

The person who is born with a divine destiny is endowed with the following qualities: fearlessness, purity of heart, a constant focus on the discipline of understanding; charity, self-restraint, religious practice, study, penance, uprightness, non-violence, truthfulness, freedom from anger, renunciation, peacefulness, freedom from slander, universal compassion, freedom from greed, gentleness, modesty, discretion, dignity, patience, fortitude, purity, a lack of vengefulness, and freedom from vanity. 

In contrast, those who come into life under the influence of an evil destiny are marked by hypocrisy, pride, arrogance, anger, harshness of speech, and ignorance. The divine destiny leads to *Moksha* <term>original: Mōksh; liberation or eternal absorption into the divine nature</term>, while the evil destiny binds the soul to the cycle of mortal birth. Do not fear, Arjuna <note>original: Ărjŏŏn</note>, for you are born with a divine destiny before you. There are two types of nature prevailing in this world. I have already explained the nature of the divine destiny at length. Now, hear what the nature of the evil is.

Those who are born under the influence of an evil destiny do not know how to act rightly or how to refrain from doing wrong. Neither purity, truth, nor the practice of morality is found in them. They say the world has no beginning and no end, and is without a Lord <term>original: Eĕswăr; the Supreme Ruler or God</term>. They claim that all things are created merely by the union of the sexes and that lust is the only cause of existence. These lost souls, possessing little understanding, cling to this view and are born to perform dreadful and inhuman deeds that lead to the destruction of the world. They give themselves up to their insatiable physical appetites; they are hypocrites, overwhelmed by madness and intoxication. Because of their folly, they adopt false doctrines and continue to live impure lives. They hold onto their delusions even until the day of their death <note>original: "day of confusion"</note>, convinced in their own minds that the gratification of sensual appetites is the highest good. 

Bound by hundreds of ties of hope and placing all their trust in lust and anger, they use unjust means to accumulate wealth to satisfy their excessive desires. They think, “I acquired this today. I shall obtain that goal of my heart. I have this wealth, and I shall have that as well. I have already killed this enemy, and I will soon conquer others. I am the Lord <note>original: Eĕswăr</note>, and I enjoy life; I am perfect, I am powerful, and I am happy. I am rich and I am a leader among men; who else is there like me? I will host feasts, give presents, and be merry.” 

In this way, these ignorant men talk, and their minds go astray. Confused by various thoughts and schemes, they are caught in the net of folly. Being firmly attached to the gratification of their lusts, they eventually sink into the *Naraka* <term>original: Nărăk; a state of hell or spiritual darkness</term> of impurity. Being self-conceited, stubborn, and always chasing wealth and status, they perform religious rituals in name only and out of hypocrisy, rather than according to divine law. Placing all their trust in pride, power, ostentation, lust, and anger, they are consumed by slander and malice, and they hate me within themselves and within others. 

Therefore, I cast these cruel and hateful wretches—the lowest of humanity—down to the earth into the wombs of evil spirits and unclean beasts. Doomed to the wombs of *Asuras* <term>original: Ăsŏŏrs; demons or godless beings</term> from birth to birth, they fail to find me and eventually descend to the lowest regions. There are three gates to *Naraka* <note>Hell</note> which destroy the soul: lust, anger, and greed. A person should avoid these; for by being freed from these gates of sin, which arise from the influence of the *Tamas* <term>original: Tămă-Gŏŏn; the quality of darkness, ignorance, or inertia</term>, a person promotes his own happiness and at last travels the path of the Most High. 

He who abandons the teachings of the *Shastra* <term>original: Sāstră; sacred scripture or law</term> to follow the whims of his own desires achieves neither perfection, nor happiness, nor the realm of the Most High. Therefore, Arjuna, after you have familiarized yourself with the precepts of the *Shastra* to determine what is right and wrong to do, you should perform the work commanded by those sacred laws.

# Chapter 17: Of Faith Divided into Three Species.

Arjuna <note>original: Ărjŏŏn</note> said,

What is the guide for those men who, although they neglect the precepts of the Shastra <term>original: Sāstră; sacred scriptures or laws</term>, yet worship with faith? Is their nature defined by Sattva, Rajas, or Tamas <note>original: Sătwă, Răjă, or Tămă-Gŏŏn; the three qualities of goodness, passion, and darkness</note>?

Krishna <note>original: Krěěshnă</note> said,

The faith of mortals is of three kinds and is produced by their natural constitution. It is named after the three Gunas <term>original: Gŏŏn; the fundamental qualities of nature</term>: Sattvic, Rajasic, or Tamasic <note>original: Sātwăkēē, Rājăsēē, or Tāmăsēē</note>. Hear what these are. The faith of every person is a reflection of that which is produced from the Sattva-Guna. The mortal Purusha <term>original: Pŏŏrŏŏsh; the individual soul or person</term> is formed by faith; of whatever nature his faith may be, he becomes endowed with that same nature. 

Those who possess the disposition arising from the Sattva-Guna worship the Devas <term>original: Dēws; celestial beings or gods</term>; those of the Raja-Guna worship the Yakshas <term>nature spirits</term> and the Rakshasas <term>demons or giants</term>; and those of the Tama-Guna worship the departed spirits and the tribe of Bhutas <term>original: Bhŏŏts; ghosts or elemental spirits</term>. Those men who perform severe mortifications of the flesh that are not authorized by the Shastra are possessed by hypocrisy and pride, and are overwhelmed with lust, passion, and tyrannical strength. These fools torment the spirit that is within the body, and they torment me also, who dwells within them. Know that these are the resolutions of those who are born under the influence of an evil spirit.

There are three kinds of food which are dear to all men. Worship, zeal <note>113</note>, and charity are each also divided into three species. Hear what their distinctions are.

<note>113: Zeal, in the common meaning of the word, signifies the voluntary infliction of pain. The methods of doing this, as practiced to this day by the devotees of India, are as various as they are horrible and astonishing. Krishna, by pointing out what true zeal is, quietly condemns those extravagant mortifications of the flesh.</note>

The food that is dear to those of the Sattva-Guna is that which increases their length of days, their power, and their strength, and keeps them free from sickness, happy and contented. It is pleasing to the palate, nourishing, substantial, and agreeable to the body. It is neither too bitter, too sour, too salt, too hot, too pungent, too astringent, nor too inflammatory. 

The food that is craved by those of the Raja-Guna gives nothing but pain and misery. And the delight of those in whom the Tama-Guna prevails is food that was cooked the day before and is out of season; food that has lost its taste and has grown putrid; the leavings of others, and all things that are impure.

That worship which is directed by divine precept and is performed without the desire for reward, as a necessary duty and with an attentive mind, is of the Sattva-Guna.

The worship which is performed with a view toward the reward, and with hypocrisy, is of the Tama-Guna. <note>Note: Most translations assign hypocrisy and reward-seeking to the Raja-Guna, but the original text here attributes it to the Tama-Guna.</note>

The worship which is performed without regard to the precepts of the law, without the distribution of bread, without the usual invocations, without gifts to the Brahmins <note>original: Brāhmăns</note> at the conclusion, and without faith, is of the Raja-Guna.

Respect to the Devas, to Brahmins, to masters, and to learned men; chastity, honesty, the worship of the Deity, and a refusal to do injury, are called **bodily zeal**.

Gentleness, justice, kindness, and helpfulness of speech, and attention to one’s particular studies, are called **verbal zeal**.

Contentment of mind, mildness of temper, devotion, restraint of the passions, and a purity of soul, are called **mental zeal**.

This threefold zeal, being practiced with supreme faith and performed by men who do not long for the fruits of their actions, is of the Sattva-Guna.

The zeal which is shown through hypocrisy, for the sake of a reputation for sanctity, honor, and respect, is said to be of the Raja-Guna; it is inconstant and uncertain.

# Chapter 18: On Renouncing the Fruits of Action to Obtain Eternal Salvation.

Arjun said,

I wish much to understand the principle of *Sannyasa* <term>Sannyasa</term><note>original: Sănnyās; the formal renunciation of worldly life and possessions to focus on the divine</note>, and also of *Tyaga* <term>Tyaga</term><note>original: Tyāg; the inward act of giving up the desire for the rewards of one's actions</note>, each separately.

Krishna said,

The poets <note>original: bards</note> understand (114) that the word *Sannyasa* implies the renunciation of all actions driven by desire; and they call *Tyaga* the renunciation of the rewards <note>original: fruits</note> of every action.

<insert>(114) The poets understand, etc.—[Due to its length, this note is presented at the end.]</insert>

Certain philosophers have declared that all works should be avoided as if they were crimes; while others say that acts of worship, self-discipline <note>original: mortifications</note>, and charity should not be abandoned. Hear what my judgment is regarding the term *Tyaga*.

*Tyaga*, or renunciation, is declared to be of three types. But acts of worship, self-discipline, and charity are not to be abandoned: they are proper to be performed. Sacrifices, charity, and self-discipline are the purifiers of the wise <note>original: philosopher</note>. It is my final opinion and judgment that such works must absolutely be performed, while renouncing any attachment to their consequences and the hope for their rewards. Withdrawing from duties that one is appointed to perform is improper.

The renunciation of such duties through foolishness and mental distraction arises from the influence of the *Tama-Guna* <term>Tama-Guna</term><note>original: Tămă-Gōōn; the quality of darkness, ignorance, or inertia</note>.

The renunciation of a task because it is painful, or out of fear of physical discomfort, arises from the *Raja-Guna* <term>Raja-Guna</term><note>original: Răjă-Gōōn; the quality of passion, energy, and ego</note>; he who leaves undone what he ought to do for these reasons shall not obtain the spiritual benefit of renunciation.

The work which is performed because it is a duty and is considered necessary to be done—while renouncing all attachment to the results and the hope of a reward—is declared to belong to the *Sattva-Guna* <term>Sattva-Guna</term><note>original: Sătwă-Gōōn; the quality of goodness, purity, and light</note>.

The person who is possessed of the *Sattva-Guna* is thus a *Tyagi* <note>original: Tyāgēē</note>, or one who renounces the fruits of action. He has sound judgment and is free from all doubt; he does not complain in times of trouble, nor does he gloat in the success of his undertakings.

No physical <note>original: corporeal</note> being is able to completely refrain from working. He is properly called a *Tyagi* who renounces the rewards <note>original: fruit</note> of action.

The results of action are threefold: those that are desired, those that are undesired, and those that are a mixture of both. Those who do not give up their attachment to the results of their works obtain these consequences after death; but this is not the case for those who truly renounce the world, who are called *Sannyasis* <note>original: Sănnyāsēēs</note>.

Learn, O Arjun, that for the completion of every work, five factors <note>original: agents</note> (115) are necessary, as is further explained in the *Sankhya* and *Vedanta-Shastras* <term>Sankhya and Vedanta-Shastras</term><note>original: Sānkhyă and Vēdānt-Sāstrās; foundational systems of Hindu philosophy and scripture</note>: attention and oversight, the actor, various types of tools <note>original: implements</note>, many distinct methods <note>original: contrivances</note>, and lastly, the favor of Providence <note>divine will or fate</note>.

<insert>(115) Five factors, etc.—The five factors implied here are likely the soul as the supervisor; the mind as the actor or director; the sense organs as tools, etc.</insert>

Every work that a person undertakes—whether with their body, their speech, or their mind, and whether it is lawful or unlawful—involves these five factors in its performance. Therefore, he who, because of his own poor judgment, believes himself to be the only actor involved, is a misguided thinker and does not see the truth at all. He who has no pride in his character and whose judgment is not clouded—even if he were to destroy a whole world—neither kills, nor is he bound by the spiritual consequences of that act (116).

That **stubborn persistence** by which a person of low capacity does not abandon laziness <note>original: sloth</note>, fear, grief, depression, and intoxication, belongs to the **quality of darkness**.

The combatants thundered furiously at each other as they rolled across the field, spending their strength in mutual conflict.

The Asuras, finding themselves once again hard-pressed by the Suras, fled precipitately.
