# TO THE READER.
<note>original: "AD LECTOREM"</note>
## A ij
<note>This is a signature mark used by early printers to keep the pages in the correct order during binding.</note>

<image-desc>A large decorative historiated initial 'N' depicting a scholar or philosopher seated in a study, pointing to a book on a desk, with several other books visible on the floor and a window in the background.</image-desc>

I DO NOT DOUBT that the title of our book concerning *Occult Philosophy*, or *Magic*, will by its rarity entice many to read it. Among these, some of a weak mind and crooked opinion, and many who are malicious and ungrateful toward our genius, will approach. These men, in their rash ignorance, taking the name of "magic" in the worst sense, will—scarcely having seen the title—shout that we teach forbidden arts, sow the seeds of heresy, are a stumbling block to pious ears, a scandal to noble minds, and that I, being a magician, am a sorcerer <note>original: "maleficum," specifically one who performs harmful magic</note>, superstitious, and demonic. 

To these men, if I should respond that among learned men the word "magician" does not signify a sorcerer, nor one who is superstitious or demonic, but rather a wise man, a priest, and a prophet. The Sibyls <note>Ancient prophetesses of the Greek and Roman world</note> were magicians, and therefore prophesied most clearly concerning Christ. Furthermore, the Magi <note>The "Wise Men" from the East</note>, through the wonderful secrets of the world, recognized Christ the author of the world as being born, and were the very first of all to come and adore him. Thus, the name of magic itself is accepted by philosophers, praised by theologians, and not even unwelcome to the Gospel itself. 

I believe those censors of such stubborn arrogance would sooner forbid themselves the Sibyls, the holy Magi, and even the Gospel itself before they would receive the name of magic into their favor. They are so concerned for their own consciences that neither Apollo, nor all the Muses, nor an angel from heaven could rescue me from their curse. To these people, I now give this advice: let them not read our writings, nor understand them, nor remember them; for they are harmful, they are poisonous. The gate of Acheron <note>A river in the underworld; here meaning the entrance to Hell</note> is in this book; it speaks stones—let them beware lest it knock out their brains. 

But you, who come to read with an unbiased mind, if you apply as much prudent discretion as bees do when gathering honey, then read in safety. For I think you will receive no small amount of utility and a great deal of pleasure. If you find anything that does not please you, set it aside and do not use it; for I do not approve those things for you, but merely relate them. Do not, however, reject the rest on that account; for it happens to those inspecting the volumes of physicians that they read of poisons at the same time as antidotes and medicines. 

I confess, moreover, that magic itself teaches many superfluous things and curious wonders intended for ostentation; leave these aside as vain, yet do not remain ignorant of their causes. But those things which can be done for the utility of men—to avert evil events, to destroy sorcery, to cure diseases, to drive away phantoms, and to preserve the soundness of life, honor, and fortune, without offense to God or injury to religion—who would not judge those things to be as useful as they are necessary? But because I have warned you that I have written many things by relating them rather than by affirming them (for it seemed necessary to do so so that we might omit fewer things), we have furthermore followed many of the opinions of the Platonists and other Gentile <note>Non-Christian or Pagan</note> philosophers where they suggested an argument for our method of writing. Therefore, if I have erred anywhere, or if anything has been said too freely...

Forgive my youth, for I composed this work when I was less than a young man, so that I might excuse myself and say: "When I was a child, I spoke as a child, I understood as a child; but when I became a man, I put away childish things" <note>original: "dum eram paruulus... euacuaui quæ erāt paruuli" — a reference to 1 Corinthians 13:11</note>. Indeed, in my book *On the Vanity and Uncertainty of the Sciences* <note>original: "de Vanitate ac incertitudine Scientiarum" — Agrippa's later work where he famously attacked all human knowledge, including the magic he defends here</note>, I retracted much of this book. But here you might perhaps rebuke me again, saying: "Behold, you wrote this as a youth and retracted it as an old man; why then have you published it?" I confess that I began to write these books when I was very young, yet with the hope of eventually sending them out more corrected and enriched; for that reason, I first offered them for correction to Johannes Trithemius <note>A famous Benedictine abbot and polymath (1462–1516) who was a mentor to Agrippa in the study of "secret things" or the occult</note>, Abbot of Würzburg and formerly of Sponheim, a man most industrious in secret matters.

It happened afterwards, however, that the work was intercepted before I had put the finishing touches to it. Because the copies were corrupted, it was circulated in a mutilated and unpolished state, flying through the hands of many in Italy, France, and Germany. Already, some—I know not whether through impatience or impudence—wished to expose this unformed work to the printing press. Struck by this one danger, I decided to publish it myself, thinking there would be less peril if these books went forth from my own hand a little more refined, rather than being made public by the hands of others as mangled and disordered fragments. Furthermore, I deemed it no sin if I did not allow this specimen of my youth to perish.
