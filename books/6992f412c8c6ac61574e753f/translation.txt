# AN ALBUM ON THE ABORIGINES IN TAIWAN (FORMOSA)

As interest in the island of Taiwan <note>The original uses "Formosa," a name meaning "Beautiful Isle" given by Portuguese explorers in the 16th century.</note> grows, the arrival of a watercolor album focusing on the indigenous people is very timely. Although the artistic skill is modest—likely the work of an unknown craftsman—the album vividly portrays the living conditions in Taiwan from approximately one hundred years ago.

There are twelve paintings in this folded album, illustrating the life and customs of the island. The scenes depict:
1. Boys learning how to pound grain and students engaging in school recitations;
2. Workers putting the roof on a house;
3. A <term>sentinel: a soldier or guard whose job is to stand and keep watch</term> on duty;
4. Harvesting coconuts;
5. Hunting deer;
6. Hunting <term>bisons: in the context of Taiwan, this likely refers to the water buffalo</term>;
7. Crossing a stream;
8. Harvesting grain;
9. Storing the grain;
10. Catching fish;
11. Bathing a child; and
12. Planting <term>taro: a tropical plant with edible starchy roots that is a staple food for many indigenous cultures</term> and traveling on a cart.

Students of sociology and history will find this album to be an excellent visual study of the island's traditional way of life before it was transformed into a modern community.

# The Harvest of Palms and Papayas

This beautifully detailed illustration by the artist **Sun Lu** captures the skillful agricultural practices of indigenous villagers, focusing on the collection of two vital tropical crops. The scene is a testament to the community's agility and their intimate relationship with the fertile landscape of the island.

On the left side of the painting, several men demonstrate incredible physical prowess as they scale the tall, slender trunks of <term>betel nut palms<gloss>Areca catechu</gloss></term>. These trees, known for their swaying, narrow forms, produce the betel nut, which held deep social and ritual importance. The harvesters are shown at varying heights, expertly navigating the branchless trunks to reach the clusters of nuts at the very top.

The right side of the composition focuses on the harvest of the **papaya**. A man has climbed into the leafy canopy of a sturdy papaya tree to hand-pick the heavy, ripe fruit. Below him, two companions coordinate the effort, ready to receive the harvest. The artist has carefully rendered the distinct shape of the papaya leaves and the clusters of fruit, contrasting the sturdy, jagged form of the papaya tree with the elegant, vertical lines of the palms.

The background features the same rolling blue-green hills and misty horizons seen in previous entries of the <term>Sketches of Aboriginal Life<gloss>Fanshe Caifeng Tu</gloss></term>, grounding this specific labor within the broader geography of the village territory. The inclusion of a gnarled, ancient tree on the far right adds a sense of timelessness to this seasonal ritual of gathering food and resources.

# The Communal Deer Hunt

This dynamic scene captures a vital aspect of the traditional lifestyle of the indigenous peoples of Taiwan during the 18th century. While the previous page focused on the quiet labor of the harvest, here the artist—likely **Sun Lu**—portrays the energy and coordination required for a successful <term>communal hunt<gloss>a group effort where the whole community or a large group of men participated to secure meat and hides</gloss></term>.

The landscape is a sprawling, grassy field, rendered with soft green washes that suggest the fertile coastal plains or inland basins of the island. The focus of the hunt is the <term>Formosan Sika deer<gloss>Cervus nippon taouanus</gloss></term>, a species prized for its meat and its dappled hide, which was a major commodity in the regional trade networks of the era.

In the center-left, a small herd of spotted deer is shown in full flight, their bodies tensed in a desperate gallop. The artist skillfully illustrates the different stages of the pursuit:
*   **The Chase:** At the right, groups of hunters rush into the scene. They are armed with traditional weapons, including long spears and bows, and are dressed in light loincloths suitable for high-speed movement through the brush.
*   **The Capture:** In the upper right corner, the "hunting dogs"—essential companions in indigenous life—have successfully intercepted and brought down a deer, demonstrating the close partnership between the hunters and their animals.

The composition emphasizes movement and the vastness of the hunting grounds. By placing the hunters on the periphery and the fleeing prey in the center, the artist conveys the scale of the landscape and the strategic nature of the hunt, where men and dogs worked together to surround the herd. This record serves as a significant ethnographic document, preserved in the style of the <term>Sketches of Aboriginal Life<gloss>Fanshe Caifeng Tu</gloss></term>, capturing a moment of intense action in the seasonal cycle of the island's inhabitants.

# Catching Wild Water Buffalo

This vibrant illustration from the <term>Illustrations of the Customs and Manners of the Indigenous Tribes<gloss>referring to the *Fan She Cai Feng Tu*, a mid-18th century album commissioned by the Qing imperial inspector Liu-shi-qi to document the lives of the "Plains Indigenous Peoples" or *Pingpu* of Taiwan</gloss></term> depicts the thrilling pursuit of wild buffalo. 

Unlike the deer hunt which provided food and trade goods, the capture of <term>water buffalo<gloss>Bubalus bubalis</gloss></term> was often a precursor to their domestication. These powerful animals were essential for the heavy labor of tilling the wetlands and rice paddies as agriculture expanded across the island.

<column-break/>

The artist depicts a dynamic landscape where the hunt is in full swing:

*   **The Lasso Technique:** Three hunters are shown mounted on horseback—a detail that reflects the adoption of equestrian skills by some indigenous groups during the Qing dynasty. They are seen expertly wielding long poles or lassos to ensnare the buffalo. 
*   **The Struggle:** The central buffalo is captured in a moment of powerful resistance, its head lowered and its muscular body tensed against the pull of the hunters. The artist uses fine ink lines to convey the raw energy of the beast.
*   **The Landscape:** The setting is a lush, hilly terrain typical of the Taiwanese foothills, painted with light color washes that provide a sense of depth and atmosphere.

<note>Historical records suggest that wild water buffalo were not originally native to Taiwan but were introduced by the Dutch or Spanish in the 17th century. By the time this album was created, large herds had become feral, roaming the plains and providing a challenging target for indigenous hunters.</note>

Through this scene, we witness a pivotal intersection of traditional hunting culture and the shifting economic landscape of 18th-century Taiwan, where the wild was increasingly being brought under human control for the purposes of settled farming.
