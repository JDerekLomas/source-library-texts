#### Spotted Dove <gloss>Song Dynasty; Jiayou Era</gloss>
<note>The Spotted Dove (*Spilopelia chinensis*) has been documented in medical texts since the Jiayou period (1056–1063) of the Song Dynasty. It is known for its distinctive plumage and gentle cooing.</note>

<margin>Compendium of Materia Medica, Volume 49</margin>

**【Explication of Names】** Spotted Zhui <gloss>pronounced *zhui*</gloss>, Brocade Dove <gloss>referenced by Fan Wang</gloss>, Falcon Dove <gloss>referenced in the *Zuo Zhuan*</gloss>, Invoker Dove.
<note>Li Shizhen explains that the names of these birds are derived from their sounds, their appearance, or their historical roles in ritual and literature.</note>
<gloss>Shizhen says: "Jiu" (Dove) mimics its sound. "Ban" (Spotted) and "Jin" (Brocade) refer to the patterns on its feathers. "Zhui" is a specific name for doves with short tails. In ancient times, the ritual chefs prepared these birds for the master of ceremonies (known as the *shizhu* or "corpse-invoker") to place upon the sacrificial vessels; hence, they were called "Zhujiu" (Invoker Doves). These names all refer to the larger varieties of doves that possess spotted markings. Those that are smaller and lack spots are referred to as *zhui*, Chaff Doves, Mallow Doves, Thorn Doves, or Bush Doves. The young of the dove are called *yanjiu*, *yijiu*, *jidiao*, or *bidiao*. The various doves listed in Yang Xiong’s *Fangyan* are mixed together haphazardly and are not a reliable basis for classification.</gloss>

# Collected Explanations
<gloss>Chen Yuxi says:</gloss> Spotted doves are found everywhere. It is said that at the spring equinox, they transform into the yellow-brown "Wait-for-Summer" bird, and at the autumn equinox, they transform back into the spotted dove. There are those with spots, those without spots, those that are grey, and both large and small varieties. Although there are these different colors, their medicinal use is the same. I once raised them for several years and never saw them transform during the spring or autumn equinoxes.
<note>Chen Yuxi (987–1040) was a Song Dynasty scholar. Here he expresses scientific skepticism regarding the ancient folklore that certain birds transform into different species based on the seasons.</note>

<gloss>Li Shizhen says:</gloss> The screeching dove is small and is said to be able to transform into a hawk, but the claim that the spotted dove transforms into the yellow-brown bird is of unknown origin. Today, the screeching doves are small and grey, while the larger ones with spots like pear blossoms do not sing well. Only those with spots on the neck resembling pearls have a loud voice and sing well; they can be used as decoys to lure other doves into nets, which is very effective. The dove is simple-minded and filial, but clumsy at building nests—often laying just a few twigs, which frequently results in the eggs falling out. When rain is coming, the male drives the female away; when the weather clears, he calls for her to return. Thus it is said: "The dove is clever yet in danger; the dove is clumsy yet at peace." Some say the male calls for sunshine and the female calls for rain.
<note>Li Shizhen provides a detailed observation of the "pearl-necked" variety (likely the *Spilopelia chinensis*) and notes the bird's reputation in Chinese culture for being "clumsy" (*zhuo*) in its domestic habits but "filial" (*xiao*).</note>

### Collected Explanations
Li Shizhen says: The osprey belongs to the eagle family. It resembles a hawk but is earthy yellow in color. It has deep-set eyes and a habit of perching motionless for long periods while watching for prey. The male and female are devoted to one another, yet they maintain a respectful distance; they fly together when mating but inhabit separate places otherwise. It is capable of hovering gracefully over the water to catch and eat fish. People in the regions south of the Yangtze River <note>Jiangbiao</note> call it the "Fish-Eating Hawk." It also eats snakes. The *Classic of Poetry* says: "Guan-guan go the ospreys <note>original: "Suijiu"</note>, on the islet in the river"—this is that very bird. Its meat has a foul, fishy odor and is not fit for consumption. The scholar Lu Ji believed this bird to be a marabou stork; Yang Xiong believed it to be a white stork; and Mr. Huang believed it to be a cuckoo. All of them were mistaken. The *Classic of Birds* says that when a dove <note>original: "Jiu"</note> gives birth to three chicks, one will be an osprey <note>original: "Ejiu"</note>, specifically the Shijiu <note>often identified as a cuckoo or shrike</note>. Du Yu regarded the *Wangju* as the *Shijiu*, perhaps for this reason.

### Appended Formulas <gloss>One new formula</gloss>
<margin>Formula from the Tang Dynasty Monk Lin Daoren</margin>
**For Setting Broken Bones:** Use the "Bird of the Lower Cave," which is the Osprey. Take the bones and burn them, charring them while retaining their medicinal properties <note>original: "shaocunxing"—a technique where the material is charred on the outside but its nature is preserved inside</note>. Use with an ancient copper coin <note>likely as filings or infused</note>—do not use too much. If the injury is in the lower body, take on an empty stomach; if in the upper body, take after a meal. A dose of exactly one *qian* <note>approx. 3.75 grams</note> is extremely effective. One must first splint and bind the fracture securely before administering this medicine.

### Explanation of Names: Sparrow Hawk
<gloss>
Commentary on the *Classic of Odes*: *Yao* [Sparrowhawk] is pronounced *yin*; *Sun* [Falcon] is pronounced *sun*. Originally written as *Zhan* [Kestrel]. Li Shizhen says: The seal script character for Sparrowhawk (*Yao*) is a pictograph of the bird’s form.
</gloss>

It is said that "Yao" represents the sound the bird makes. As a bird of prey, it seizes its quarry like a falcon (*Sun*) shot from a bow; it is a falcon that strikes its prey. The name *Yao* also refers to its habit of striking from a distance <note>playing on the homophone "yao," meaning distant</note>. The *Commentary on the Classic of Odes* states: There are several types of falcons, and they are all commonly referred to as sparrowhawks. The sparrow hawk is said to transform into a cuckoo in the spring <note>a common folk belief in ancient China regarding the seasonal "metamorphosis" of birds</note>. The *Erya* <note>the oldest surviving Chinese dictionary</note> calls it the "Reed Owl." People of the Qi region call it the "Striker-Correct." Others call it the "Prominent Shoulder." The *Erya* notes: the sparrowhawk is the bird that carries off sparrows. In Buddhist scriptures, it is referred to as the *Alina* <note>a transliteration of a Sanskrit term</note>.

### Collected Explanations
Tao Hongjing <note>a 5th-century scholar and alchemist</note> says: The *Yao* is what the common people call the "Old Sparrowhawk." There is also the *Zhan* [Kestrel], which is similar but larger.

Li Shizhen says: The sparrowhawk resembles an eagle but is slightly smaller. Its tail functions like a rudder; it is extremely skilled at soaring to great heights and specializes in capturing quails and sparrows. There are several varieties. According to the *Classic of Birds* <note>original: "Qin Jing"</note>: Those skilled at wrestling are called *Yao*; those of a dark, mysterious color are called *Zhan*; those with strong bone structure are called *Yao*; those with clear vision are called *Yao*; those that fly with wide-spread wings are called *Yao*; and those that snatch prey are called *Yao*. 

The text further states: When a sparrowhawk produces three chicks, one of them will be a specific variety <note>likely referring to the male or a smaller, more aggressive subspecies</note> that is smaller than the typical sparrowhawk but is the most fierce and agile. It is capable of striking doves and pigeons and is known as the "Little Sparrowhawk" or the "Dragon-Shedder." The *Zhan* variety is blue-grey in color; it spreads its wings against the wind and rapidly shakes them to capture small birds. When it cries, it is loud...

### The Sparrowhawk’s Head

<margin>Preparation</margin> <gloss>Tao Hongjing <note>a famous 5th-century physician</note> says: Although there is no restriction on using male or female birds, they are more effective when used. They should be lightly toasted. For parts that should not be used [refer to specific guidelines]. Ancient recipes for treating ailments of the head and face include "Sparrowhawk Head Wine."</gloss>

<margin>Flavor and Nature</margin> Salty, neutral, and non-toxic. <gloss>Li Shizhen says: According to the records of Duan Chengshi, during the Tang Dynasty, Empress Zhang (the consort of Emperor Suzong) wielded great power. Whenever she presented wine, she would place the brain of a sparrowhawk inside, saying it would make a person intoxicated for a long time. It does possess a slight toxicity; however, for forgetfulness, one should use the sparrowhawk's head.</gloss>

<margin>Main Indications</margin> Migraines and "head-wind," dizziness, and epilepsy or seizure disorders. <gloss>From the *Miscellaneous Records*</gloss>

<margin>Appendix of Formulas</margin> <gloss>2 Formulas, Old</gloss> <gloss>For epilepsy and convulsions:</gloss> Three heads of the flying sparrowhawk and one *jin* <note>roughly 500 grams</note> of red lead <term>Minium; lead tetroxide</term>. Grind these into a powder and form into pills with honey, each the size of a parasol tree seed. Take twenty-seven pills with wine, three times a day. <gloss>From the *Prescriptions Worth a Thousand Gold*</gloss>

<margin>Formula</margin> <gloss>For severe vertigo and "swirling wind" dizziness:</gloss> One sparrowhawk head (toasted yellow), one *liang* <note>approx. 37 grams</note> each of bamboo shavings and white atractylodes, and half a *liang* of Sichuan pepper (toasted until the oils are removed). Grind into a powder and mix with honey to form pills.
