-> + Argument of the Drama *Ajax* + <-

**The** drama is one of **Sophocles’** literary productions; just like the *Antigonidai* <note>original: "ἀντιγονίδαι" (antigonidai) - likely referring to plays in the Antigone cycle</note>, the *Women of Mycenae* <note>original: "μυκηναίδες" (mykēnaides)</note>, the *Abduction of Helen* <note>original: "ελένης ἁρπαγή" (elenēs harpagē)</note>, and *Memnon*. For when **Achilles** had fallen in battle, the **Achaeans** <note>The Greek forces at Troy</note> decided to hold a contest <term>krisis</term> concerning his armor for those who had excelled most in valor. In the judging of the armor, **Odysseus** prevailed. But **Ajax**, failing to win the judgment, was driven to distraction and his mind was corrupted <note>In the play, this madness is a divine punishment from the goddess Athena</note>, so that he thought he was slaughtering the **Greeks** when he was actually destroying the herds of livestock. Some of the cattle he killed on the spot, while others he led back alive to his tent <term>skēnē</term>. Among these was a particularly large ram, which he believed to be **Odysseus**; he tied it up and whipped it. 

Because of this, the title "The Lash-Bearer" <note>original: "μαστιγοφόρος" (mastigophoros)</note> is attached to the play, or perhaps it is to distinguish him from the **Locrian** <note>Ajax the Lesser, another hero of the same name from Locris</note>. It is right to simply name him **Ajax** in the title, since there are indeed two Ajaxes. **Athena** finds **Odysseus** at the tent while he is scouting to see what **Ajax** might be doing. She reveals to him what has been done and calls **Ajax** out into the open while he is still in his madness, boasting as if he had destroyed his enemies. **Ajax** then goes back inside to continue whipping **Odysseus** <note>Actually the ram</note>. Meanwhile, the **Chorus** of sailors from **Salamis** <note>The island kingdom of Ajax</note> remains, with whom <unclear>this man</unclear> laments. 

When **Ajax** returns to his senses, he laments his own fate. **Tecmessa** <note>His captive wife</note> begs him to cease his anger, and he, pretending to have ceased, [leaves the scene]. Under the pretext of performing ritual purifications <term>katharsiōn</term>, he takes his own life. Following this, **Menelaus** and **Agamemnon** will not permit **Teucer** <note>Ajax's half-brother</note> to bury the body, but **Odysseus** resolves the situation by persuading them.
