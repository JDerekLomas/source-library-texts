## Milk Tree

The Milk Tree <note>original: *Nǎishù*. As noted previously, this refers to the *Codonopsis lanceolata*, or Milk Ginseng.</note> grows in Nan’an. It is a climbing plant with four leaves clustered at the ends of the stems. Green buds open into flowers shaped like purple flutes; they resemble the morning glory but have shorter petals. Beneath the bud, there is a green calyx. In autumn, it bears fruit containing seeds.

The sap within the vine is white and extremely thick, with a pungent odor. The root is yellowish-white with horizontal rings, similar to the high-quality <term>Ginseng</term> from Shangdang <note>Shangdang, in modern Shanxi province, was famous during the Tang and Ming dynasties for producing the finest medicinal roots.</note>. The root is fleshy and round with nodules about the size of a fist.

Mountain Sea Snail

Locals in Guangxin call the plant the "Mountain Sea Snail," a name inspired by the spiraled shape of its root. It is also known as "The Milk Lady." Its flavor is sweet and its nature is warm. Local people harvest the root to promote the production of breast milk. It is also found on Mount Heng in Hunan and is very easy to propagate. Folk healers call it "Bull’s Aconite" <note>original: *Niúfùzǐ*. *Aconite* usually refers to a potent medicinal root; here it suggests a powerful herbal substitute.</note> and use it to strengthen male <term>yang</term> <note>In traditional medicine, "yang" represents the active, warming, and masculine energy of the body.</note>.

> There is a "Milk Vine" similar to a trailing raspberry bush. Its leaves are long with pointed tips. When broken, it yields a white sap just like milk. After giving birth, women mash the vine into juice and mix it with rice to make a porridge; this naturally clears the milk ducts and stimulates flow.

## Local Green Wood Fragrance

Local Green Wood Fragrance <note>original: *Tǔ Qīngmùxiāng*. This is the root of the *Aristolochia debilis* plant, a species of birthwort. The name literally means "Local Green Wood Fragrance" to distinguish it from imported varieties of incense wood.</note> is found among the hillsides of Changsha. It is a climbing vine with slender stems. Its leaves and fruit are both identical to those of the "Horse Bell" plant <note>original: *Mǎdòulíng*, another name for *Aristolochia* species, named for the shape of the seed pods</note>. The roots are yellow, thin, and also possess a fragrant aroma.

Local folk doctors <term>folk doctors: *lǐyī*, referring to village healers or practitioners of regional medicine outside the imperial tradition</term> use this plant to clear "fire-toxin" and to unblock "stagnant qi" <note>In traditional Chinese medicine, "fire-toxin" refers to severe heat-related conditions like inflammation or infections, while "stagnant qi" refers to a blockage in the body's vital energy flow.</note>. 

The primary distinction is in the flowers: they are shaped like small tubes, narrow at the base and wide at the end, curving like the horn of an ox with the tip pointing upward. The color is a very deep, rich purple-black, revealing yellow stamens within. This is distinctly different from the flowers of the [standard] Horse Bell plant, which resemble the flowers of the Goji berry <note>original: *Gǒuqǐ*</note>.

## Xun Gu Feng

**Xun Gu Feng** <note>original: 尋骨風; *Aristolochia mollissima*</note> is found in Yuezhou, Hunan <note>Modern-day Yueyang, a city with a long history as a center of trade and herbal medicine along the Yangtze River</note>. 

It grows as a climbing vine. Its leaves resemble those of the **Rough Potato** <note>original: 蘿藦 *luómó*; *Metaplexis japonica*</note>; they are soft and thick, covered in a dense layer of fine hairs. The upper surface of the leaf is green, while the underside is white. 

In autumn, it bears fruit with six ridges, similar in appearance to the **Rangoon Creeper** fruit <note>original: 使君子 *shǐjūnzǐ*; *Quisqualis indica*, a plant commonly used in traditional medicine to treat parasites</note>. The fruit is a dark greenish-black color, and the seeds within are like beans.

Inner Wind Vine grows on the mountain slopes of **Hunan** <note>A province in south-central China known for its mountainous terrain and diverse medicinal flora.</note>. ## Iron Broom

The **Iron Broom** <note>Tiě Sàozhǒu</note> is produced in the mountains of **Jianchang** <note>A historical prefecture in modern-day Jiangxi Province, traditionally known for its production of medicinal herbs</note>. It is a vining plant with green stems that are soft, slender, and grow in a tangled, interwoven manner. The leaves reach several inches in length; they are rounded at the base with a distinct notch and taper to a point at the tip, appearing at wide intervals along the vine. The plant’s fine roots are hard, colored a dark purplish-ochre, and grow in dense, thick clusters. Folk doctors <term>俚醫 (lǐyī): local or rural healers whose practices often incorporated regional plants not found in formal pharmacopoeias</term> regard it as a medicinal agent for **invigorating the blood** <term>行血 (xíngxuè): a therapeutic method in Chinese medicine aimed at promoting blood flow to treat pain or stagnation</term> and **unblocking the joints** <note>original: 通骨節 (tōng gǔjié); referring to the relief of stiffness or arthritic symptoms</note>. The roots are prepared by decocting them in wine for consumption.

# Thorny Plowshare

The **Thorny Plowshare** <note>original: 刺梨頭, Cì Lítóu; scientifically known as *Persicaria perfoliata*, and often called "Mile-a-minute weed" in English due to its rapid growth</note> is also known as the **"Ground-Not-Passed,"** <note>original: 地不過, Dì-bù-guò</note> the **"Sudden Rope-Change,"** <note>original: 急改索, Jí-gǎi-suǒ</note> and the **"Blood-Receding Herb."** <note>original: 退血草, Tuì-xiě-cǎo</note> It is found in great abundance throughout **Jiangxi** and **Hunan** provinces.

It grows as a trailing vine with slender stems covered in fine, dense prickles. Both the stems and leaves resemble those of **buckwheat** <note>original: 蕎麥, Qiáomài</note>. It produces clusters of small, pinkish flowers that lack petals, followed by green fruits that are somewhat angular rather than perfectly round. At every junction where the stem branches, there is a single, circular leaf that surrounds the stem, resembling a **smartweed** <note>original: 蓼, Liǎo; refers to plants in the *Polygonaceae* family, characterized by swollen leaf joints</note>.

In **Jiangxi**, boatmen often collect and store this plant to prepare a medicinal bath. It is said that bathing in this infusion during the summer months prevents the development of **sores and boils** <term>瘡癤 (chuāng jiē): common skin infections or abscesses often associated with heat and humidity in traditional medicine</term>. 

In **Hunan**, folk doctors use it as a medicine to **circulate the blood and qi** <note>original: 行血氣, xíng xuè qì; a fundamental concept in Chinese medicine referring to the restoration of vital energy and blood flow to treat stagnation or pain</note> and to treat **strangury and turbid discharge** <term>淋濁 (lín zhuó): a category of urinary disorders characterized by painful, frequent, or cloudy urination</term>. 

According to my research, the **"Illustrated Classic of Materia Medica"** <note>original: 宋圖經, Sòng Tújīng; a reference to the 11th-century work *Bencao Tujing*</note> records a variety of **Yam** <note>original: 萆薢, Bìxiè; usually refers to *Dioscorea septemloba*</note> produced in the **Chengde Commandery** that has leaves like buckwheat and three-angled seeds. This is almost certainly the same plant described here. Its primary medicinal functions—**dispelling dampness** and **promoting urination**—are also very similar to those of the Yam.

## Bone-Penetrating Dissolver

The **Bone-Penetrating Dissolver** <note>original: 透骨消, Tòugǔxiāo</note> is produced in **Nan’an** <note>A region in modern-day Jiangxi province, known historically for its botanical diversity</note>. According to **Li Shizhen** <note>1518–1593, the most famous pharmacologist in Chinese history and author of the *Compendium of Materia Medica*</note>, the **Five-Poison Herb** <note>original: 五毒草, Wǔdúcǎo</note> and the **Red-Earth Utility** should be combined into a single entry. However, among the trailing vines, those that resemble **buckwheat** <note>original: 蕎麥, qiáomài</note> are not all of a single class. Since their colors and flavors are distinct and their names differ, they should be treated carefully; even though their uses in **external medicine** <note>original: 外科, wàikē; referring to treatments for the skin, bones, and wounds</note> for poultices and medicinal washes are generally similar, they must be distinguished when prepared as **oral decoctions** <note>original: 飲劑, yǐnjì</note>. This is because they affect different **meridians** <note>original: 經絡, jīngluò; the traditional pathways through which vital energy or "Qi" flows in the body</note>. Therefore, I have preserved both as separate entries to await further detailed investigation.

# Catfish Whisker

According to the **Materia Medica for Relief of Famine** <note>original: 救荒本草, Jiùhuāng běncǎo. The leaves resemble those of the **Madder-leaf Greenbrier** <note>original: 土茜, tǔqiàn.</note> but are larger; they also look like the leaves of the **China Root** <note>original: 金剛刺, jīngāngcì; *Smilax china*.</note> or the **Riparian Greenbrier** <note>original: 牛尾菜, niúwěicài; *Smilax riparia*.</note>. The foliage is **smooth and glossy**, lacking any roughness. It has a **sweet taste**. To prepare it, harvest the **tender shoots and leaves**, scald them in boiling water, and season with oil and salt for eating.

According to the **Simple Herbal Remedies** <note>original: 簡易草藥, Jiǎnyì cǎoyào.</note>, the **Diamond Vine** <note>original: 金剛藤, Jīngāng téng.</note> is the original name for the **Catfish Whisker**. The **Hubei Gazetteer** <note>original: 湖北志, Húběi zhì.</note> notes that the **Silver Carp Whisker** <note>original: 鰱魚鬚, Liányú xū.</note> is a vining plant. When the shoots first emerge from the soil, they are **purple in color**. The tips are **curled and coiled**, resembling the barbels of a fish. When **stir-fried with meat**, it is said to be truly wonderful.

# White Flower Vine

The vines curve and entwine in a dense, chaotic fashion. The leaves grow opposite one another at the nodes; similarly, the branches also bifurcate from the nodes. When new shoots emerge, they fork into two—one branch may produce leaves while the other produces flowers. Within this orderly symmetry, there is a subtle and pleasing variation. A branch that bears flowers must fork once more before producing opposite flowers. Likewise, a branch that bears leaves must also fork again to produce opposite leaves. Each flower is necessarily small, blooming in pairs from these bifurcating stems. <gloss>Based on what I observed last autumn during the fruiting stage: in short, the forks are stems that have not yet produced opposite seed pods.</gloss> Every time a leaf, a branch, or a flower emerges, it must fork, mirroring the way the **Two Poles** <note>original: 兩儀 (liǎngyí); the primary forces of Yin and Yang in Chinese cosmology</note> give rise to the **Four Images** <note>original: 四象 (sìxiàng)</note>, which in turn produce the **Eight Trigrams** <note>original: 八卦 (bāguà)</note> in an endless cycle of creation. 

The flower has five petals that encircle the center, forming a small hole in the middle that appears entirely empty. Neither the "heart" <note>the pistil</note> nor the "whiskers" <note>the stamens</note> are visible to the naked eye. However, these five petals actually originate from a single bud; if one peels it open, five stamens are found hidden inside, surrounding a single pistil. This pistil is the core that eventually matures into the horn-like seed pods, similar to the seeds of the **Metaplexis** <note>original: 芄蘭 (wánlán); a climbing vine mentioned in the *Classic of Poetry* known for its horn-shaped fruit.</note>. Because the petals curve and spiral to form a shape resembling the **Swastika symbol** <note>original: 卍字 (wànzì); used here as a botanical descriptor for the pinwheel-shaped corolla common in the Dogbane family.</note>, common people call it the "Swastika Flower," though they often mistakenly believe it has only four petals. It is also called the "Wheel Flower," which likewise describes its shape. The flower bud is supported by a base.

## Lalateng

Lalateng <note>original: 拉拉藤 (Lālāteng), commonly known today as Cleavers or Catchweed Bedstraw (*Galium aparine*).</note> is found everywhere. It is a creeping plant covered in bristly hairs that cling to a person’s clothing. Its length can reach several feet, becoming entangled like a mass of messy silk.

## Lalateng

**Lalateng** <note>original: 拉拉藤 (Lālāteng), commonly known today as Cleavers or Catchweed Bedstraw (*Galium aparine*).</note> is found everywhere. It is a creeping plant covered in bristly hairs that cling to a person’s clothing. Its length can reach several feet, becoming entangled like a mass of messy silk.

Five or six leaves grow together in a single cluster <note>This refers to a "whorled" leaf arrangement, where leaves radiate from a single point on the stem like the spokes of a wheel.</note>. In the spring, between the leaves and at the tips of the stems, it bears green fruits the size of millet. According to the **Materia Medica for the Relief of Famine** <note>original: 救荒本草 (Jiùhuāng Běncǎo), a 15th-century herbal focusing on edible wild plants for survival during crop failures.</note>, the appearance of **Lady's Bedstraw** <note>original: 蓬子菜 (Péngzicài), a related plant in the Galium genus.</note> is quite similar to this. In Yunnan, it is called **Eight Immortals Herb** <note>original: 八仙草 (Bāxiāncǎo).</note>, and it is used in local folk remedies.

<gloss>In the **Materia Medica of Southern Yunnan** <note>A regional pharmaceutical text by Lan Mao (1397–1470).</note>, Eight Immortals Herb is described as having a taste that is both bitter and acrid; its nature is slightly cold. It enters the **Lesser Yang** and **Greater Yin** meridians.</gloss> <note>These "meridians" are channels in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) through which vital energy (Qi) flows; specifically the Gallbladder/Triple Burner and Lung/Spleen systems.</note> It treats damp-heat <note>A TCM diagnostic pattern involving inflammation, heavy sensations, and feverishness.</note> in the **Spleen meridian**, exogenous heat <note>Heat originating from external environmental factors like wind or seasonal illness.</note> in all <insert>six channels, and effectively resolves toxins and reduces swelling.</insert>
