# <insert>The Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms</insert>

<note>The title *Sancai Tuhui* (三才圖會) literally translates to "The Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms." In traditional Chinese philosophy, the "Three Realms" or "Three Powers" are Heaven, Earth, and Humanity. This encyclopedia, published around 1609 by Wang Qi and his son Wang Siyi, was an ambitious attempt to categorize and illustrate all knowledge pertaining to these three spheres, ranging from star charts and maps to portraits of famous figures and depictions of exotic animals.</note>

The *Classic of Difficult Issues* <note>original: "Nan Jing" (難經), a foundational Han Dynasty medical text structured as 81 questions and answers addressing complex points of the "Yellow Emperor’s Internal Classic."</note> says: The lungs weigh three <term>catties</term><note>A *jin* (斤), a traditional unit of weight. In the context of ancient medical texts, these measurements were often symbolic or based on specific historical standards rather than modern metric weight.</note> and three <term>ounces</term><note>A *liang* (兩), one-sixteenth of a catty.</note>. They consist of six lobes and two "ears," totaling eight lobes. They primarily house the <term>Corporeal Soul</term><note>The *Po* (魄) is the "animal" or physical soul associated with the lungs and the metal element. It governs instinctive bodily functions and is said to return to the earth upon death.</note>. They hang down on four sides like a canopy and are attached to the third vertebra of the spine. Within them are twenty-four "cavities" <note>Literally "holes" or "voids" (空), referring to the internal passages or bronchioles.</note> arranged in rows which distribute the <term>Qi</term><note>The vital energy or life force that circulates through the body.</note> of the various organs. They serve as the "Splendid Canopy" <gloss>lung system</gloss> of the internal organs. <note>The "Splendid Canopy" (*Huagai* 華蓋) is an astronomical and imperial metaphor; as the highest organ in the chest, the lungs protect the other organs like a royal umbrella.</note>

Furthermore, the *Classic of Difficult Issues* says: The <term>throat</term><note>Referring specifically to the trachea or windpipe (*Houlong*).</note> weighs twelve ounces, is two inches wide, one foot two inches long, and consists of nine sections. The <term>gate of swallowing</term><note>The *Yanmen* (咽門), or pharynx/esophagus, which handles food rather than air.</note> weighs twelve ounces and is two and a half inches wide; the distance from it to the stomach is one foot six inches long. In the "Chapter on the Intestines and Stomach" <note>A section of the *Ling Shu* (Divine Pivot) portion of the Yellow Emperor's Internal Classic.</note>, the physician **Bo Gao** says: the gate of swallowing weighs ten ounces, though its width and length are the same <note>as the measurements previously mentioned</note>.

# Song of the Lung Organ

The Lung organ is situated foremost of all,
While the Large Intestine provides the proper path for its passage.
<gloss>Physically, the Lung is located above all other organs. In terms of function, it serves as the starting point for all the body’s "meridians" <note>Energy pathways</note>, which is why it is called "foremost." The Lung governs the body's Qi <note>Vital life force or breath</note>; the Large Intestine is the "yang" partner organ to the Lung, acting as the "Official of Transmission" responsible for circulating energy and overseeing the transformation and elimination of waste.</gloss>

*Dui* is its place among the Eight Trigrams,
Metal is its tie within the Five Phases.
<gloss>The Lung is associated with the direction of the West, represented by the *Dui* trigram <note>One of the eight symbols used in divination to represent fundamental principles of reality</note>. In the system of the Five Phases, it belongs to the element of Metal. Among the Five Phases—Wood, Water, Fire, Earth, and Metal—it is said that the others all rely on the movement or "rising" of Metal.</gloss>

The skin and the body hair correspond to its health.
<gloss>The Lung governs the "exterior" of the body, specifically the skin and hair; therefore, they are said to be in "correspondence" with the organ's condition.</gloss>

The *Po* soul and the spirits are linked together.
<gloss>The Liver is said to store the *Hun* <note>The "ethereal" or heavenly soul</note>, while the Lung—which receives its energy from the element of Wood in this specific cosmological cycle—stores the *Po* <note>The "corporeal" or earthly soul associated with physical instincts</note>. The Lung's Metal energy is received at the time of the "Tiger" [3-5 AM]. Detailed explanations of this can be found in the diagrams related to the "Forty Difficulties" <note>Refers to the *Nanjing*, or the *Classic of Difficult Issues*, a foundational medical text</note>.</gloss>

Through the nose, fragrant scents are discerned,
But congestion causes the Qi to boil and struggle.
<gloss>The medical classics state: "The Qi of the Lung opens into the nose." If the nose is clear and harmonious, one can distinguish between fragrances and foul odors. "Congestion" refers to a lack of flow in the breath. "Boiling and struggling" describes the effect when pathogenic or "evil" energy attacks the Lung's natural energy.</gloss>

# Too much speech results in a cough
<gloss>The Lung governs Qi <note>The body's vital breath or energy</note>. If one speaks excessively, the Qi is injured and loses its ability to be gathered and stored; it is from this state that illness arises.</gloss>

---

# Sores emerge when drenched with wine
<gloss>Alcohol is a substance of "damp-heat" <note>A traditional Chinese medicine concept describing a heavy, inflammatory internal environment</note>. Sores and skin eruptions arise from this damp-heat. Because the Lung governs the skin and body hair, excessive wine causes damp-heat to injure the Lung, leading to the development of sores across the skin. Thus, it is said that "sores float upon the wine."</gloss>

---

# In consumption, lustrous bones bring good fortune; withered bones make it hard to preserve life
<gloss>*Zhai* <note>original: 瘵 (zhài)</note> refers to a wasting disease or consumption. If the illness manifests with a white color that is lustrous and bright, this represents the element of Metal. If there is moisture, it represents Water. This indicates that Metal is still able to "generate" Water <note>In the Five Phases, Metal is the mother of Water; this implies the body's self-replenishing cycle is still functioning</note>, which is why it is called auspicious. If the bones appear white but are dry and without luster, this is also the color of Metal, but it is a sign of "standing on edge" <note>An idiom for a precarious situation</note>. A lack of luster means the body has lost its internal Water because it has been parched by Fire. When Fire comes to consume Metal, it is said that the consumption is difficult to cure.</gloss>

---

# The primary accumulation is the "Panting Breath" ailment; it arises in Spring and is blocked under the right ribs
<gloss>The Classic <note>likely referring to the *Nan Jing* or "Classic of Difficult Issues"</note> states: The "accumulation" <note>A localized internal blockage or mass</note> of the Lung is called *Xiben* <note>original: 息賁 (xī bèn), literally "Panting Breath" or "Breath Rush"</note>. It is located under the right ribs and is shaped like an inverted cup. It is contracted on the *Jia* and *Yi* days <note>Specific days in the traditional 10-day cycle associated with the element of Wood and the Spring season</note> of Spring. Why is this? Heart disease passes to the Lung; the Lung should then pass the imbalance to the Liver. However, the Liver is at its peak strength in the Spring. An organ at its peak will not accept a pathogen. The Lung tries to return the imbalance to the Heart, but the Heart cannot receive it. Therefore, the energy remains knotted and forms an "accumulation." Thus we know *Xiben* is contracted during the Spring.</gloss>

In the peak of winter, water freezes into the hardness of stone. Therefore, the pulse arrives deep, soft, and slippery. Thus, it is called "stone-like."
