# SECTION I.

### Chapter II.

Having first offered our prayers—lest we be said to "hunt with unwilling dogs," <note>original: "inuitis canibus... venari"; a Latin proverb meaning to attempt a task with people who have no interest or heart for it.</note> as the saying goes—the sum total of medical study consists in this: that we follow Nature herself, like the best and wisest parent, with close footsteps. This is clear from the prescription of the Coan, <note>Hippocrates, the "Father of Medicine," who was from the island of Cos.</note> for he predicts that if Nature is resistant, all efforts will be in vain. He offers a very elegant parable: *comparing Nature to the field, the teachings of the doctors to the seed, instruction to the sowing, the location to the air, and study to agriculture.* These comparisons, when weighed more closely, will reveal very pleasant and serious truths in their depths. Not departing from the parable, the following points should be considered:

*Whoever wishes to harvest happy medical fruits, by comparing the Nature of good fields with his own, can easily prophesy concerning good or bad hope.*

For it is the character of a good field to be rich, wealthy, luxurious, and pleasant; so too, let the NATURE of one initiated into Medicine be "rich" in medical love, "luxurious" in diligence, and "pleasant" in character. They declare a field to be of no value when it is rocky, muddy, stagnant, or thorny; likewise, in medical study, he will profit little or nothing who, in learning, is—

### CHAPTER III.

Once adorned with languages, the physician will profitably engage in the remaining disciplines necessary for his art. In order to be able to refute the arguments of adversaries, he must have knowledge of the art of **Logic** <note>original: "Dialecticae".</note>. In persuading the sick, as well as in discussing medical matters, **Rhetoric** is helpful. In matters of character, **Ethics** provides guidance. To find the correct doses of medications, **Arithmetic** provides instruction. **Astronomy** teaches the opportune time for applying pharmacy and indicating coming <term>crises<note>The "crisis" was considered the turning point of a disease, where the patient would either begin to recover or decline toward death.</note></term>, along with other signs. A succinct compendium of this art, as far as it pertains to medical use, has been diligently observed by notable physicians in their operations, not without benefit. That compendium follows here.

In those matters by which health is either preserved or recovered, a twin sense of "time" must be weighed: one of **NECESSITY**, the other of **CHOICE**.

In <term>bloodletting<note>original: "phlebotomia".</note></term>, the time of **NECESSITY** is when the disease allows for no delays due to its severity; as in the Plague, Quinsy <note>original: "Squinantia"; a severe inflammation of the throat or tonsils.</note>, Pleurisy, Phrensy <note>original: "Phrenesi"; inflammation of the brain or delirium.</note>, Apoplexy, and others of that kind, in which it is dangerous to wait for a "chosen" time because they kill very swiftly. Urgent Necessity does not recognize the "Time of the Law" <note>This refers to the standard astrological or seasonal rules usually followed in medieval medicine.</note>; therefore, at any time, at any hour, both by day and by night, the vein is cut. 

However, the "chosen" time for bloodletting is determined by three considerations, the first of which is the consideration of solar time. For most expert physicians command that the Sun be observed when letting blood; they choose a time of year declining more toward a temperate state, which they assert lasts from nearly the middle of Spring to the beginning of Summer. Averroes <note>A famous 12th-century Andalusian philosopher and physician whose medical encyclopedias were standard texts in Europe.</note> is a witness to this in the second book of his *Collectanea*, where he left this written: *The convenient time for bloodletting is Spring.* Summer-time prohibits bloodletting because of the weakness of the <term>vital power<note>original: "virtutis"; the natural strength or "virtue" of the body.</note></term> during that time and the dissipation of the spirits; however, if nature requires it, nothing prohibits performing a small one. Likewise, Winter-time is unfavorable for cutting a vein because of the coagulation and thickness of the blood which prevails then. The Autumnal constitution is also unfavorable, because of the heat of the summer immediately preceding it...
