# AMBOINSCH KRUID-BOEK.

## HET TIENDE BOEK.

### *Dealing with wild herbs of various kinds.*
### *Handelende van de Wilde Kruiden door malkander.*

### CHAPTER ONE.
### EERSTE HOOFDSTUK.

### *Cyperus rotundus. Teckee.* <note>The Round Cyperus or Nut Grass.</note>
### *The round Cyperus.*

In describing the wild herbs, we shall follow such an order as one would almost encounter if they were to travel through the region of Amboina <note>Modern Ambon Island, Indonesia</note> from one shore to the other.
For example, if one were to step out of the house, they would immediately encounter the grass and such herbs that grow around people's dwellings, in gardens, along public roads, and at the edges of cultivated fields.
Then, proceeding further, we shall find those that grow in the fields and hills, and gradually ascending to the peaks of the mountains and the valleys situated beneath them, we shall survey those which the Amboinese wilderness has hidden there. Then, in the eleventh book, suddenly descending to the other side of the shore, we shall arrive near the mouth of a major river, and we shall see such herbs that grow in marshes and damp places, indeed in the river water itself, and at the very edge of the sea, and consequently those that grow in the seawater itself; with these we shall determine and put an end to the terrestrial herbs of Amboina. In the subsequent book, we shall then proceed further into the sea itself, where we shall see what plants of a different nature it fosters and nourishes in its bosom.

Among the grassy herbs which we immediately tread upon with our feet when we go out, the first place is held by the *Cyperus*, which in these and mostly in all Oriental regions serves us as a common grass, inasmuch as it covers all the streets, deserted gardens, and low fields, although many other species of grasses are mixed in, some of which we shall describe in the following sections.

The Cyperus in its form corresponds mostly with that which the European Herbalists describe, but something peculiar is nevertheless discovered in this one, which will appear hereafter.

Following the common method, we divide it into three primary species, and we shall assign a specific Chapter to each: the first is round, the second long, and the third sweet.

*Cyperus rotundus* <note>Round Cyperus</note> is either scented or unscented. And the scented is again twofold: first, the bulbous, which is considered the female. Second, the flowering, and mostly destitute of bulbs, which is held to be the male, with certain spurious species subjoined to this chapter.

I. *Cyperus rotundus bulbosus*, or the legitimate one, is a grass bearing nothing but leaves, which are very thin, tender, and long, which are commonly compared with Leek leaves, neither wrinkled nor sharp, a straw-breadth wide, a span long, but in those which grow by common roads, shorter, and marked on the inner part by a deeply running and three-sided groove, which bulges out below with a sharp ridge; these leaves are placed eight, ten, or twelve together in a circle and sit upon a round stalk, which is a straw-thick and one or two inches high, and which is composed of their own sheaths.

Below it produces a small hard head, from which a few thin and fragile fibers hang on all sides; besides these, two usually thicker threads grow out to the sides, which support small bulbs, and from these again other thin but firm threads, blackish-brown like an iron wire, and from these at about a palm's breadth from the former.

---

*In order to describe the wild Herbs, we shall maintain such an order as one would roughly find if one crossed the land of Amboina from one shore to the other.*

*For example, as we set foot outside the house, we shall first meet the grass, and all such herbs that we see growing around people's dwellings, in the gardens, on the roads and edges of cultivated lands.*

*After that, stepping forward, we shall meet the field and hills with their herbs, and gradually climbing to the tops of the mountains, and the valleys lying beneath them, we shall see what the Amboinese wilderness has hidden there; after that, in the eleventh Book, suddenly descending again, we shall come out on the other side on the shore; near the mouth of a large River, and behold such herbs that grow in marshes and damp places, even submerged in the water, of which we [find] the front rampart of the Sea, and therefore grow in Seawater itself, with which we shall leave the Amboinese land-herbs and plants, and take our leave of them, to betake ourselves in the following Book a bit further onto the Sea, and see what kind of plants of another nature rest in her bosom.*

*Among the grass-types, which we must first tread upon as we go out, the Cyperus has the precedence, which in this country, and mostly in these Eastern Islands, serves us as common grass, covering all streets, abandoned gardens, and low fields: although many other sorts of grass run among them, of which we shall describe only some in the following.*

*The Cyperus corresponds in appearance mostly with that which the European Herbals describe, yet has something special, as shall appear in the sequel.*

*We divide it according to common custom into three main types, giving each a special Chapter, namely the first is round, the second long, and the third sweet.*

*The round Cyperus is either fragrant or not fragrant. The fragrant is again of two kinds, as a knobbed or glandular one, which is held for the female. II. Flowering and mostly without glands, which is held for the male, with some bastard-sorts to be found at the end of this Chapter.*

I. *Cyperus rotundus bulbosus* or *legitimate*, is a grass, bearing nothing but leaves, very narrow, tender, and long, which are commonly compared with Leek leaves; not at all rough nor sharp, a straw-breadth wide, a span long; but on the common roads shorter, on the inside with a deep continuous and three-sided gutter, which bulges out below with a sharp ridge. These leaves stand eight, ten or twelve together around a round stalk, a straw-thick, and an inch or two high, which is made of their sheaths.*

*At the bottom it has a hard little head, from which a few thin and brittle fibers hang around. Besides these, one or two thicker threads go to the sides, on which one finds small knobs, and from the same again other thin, yet stiff threads, black-brown, like a small iron wire, and on the same about a hand-*

from one bulb another bulb grows again, and so on to a fourth or fifth in a single row; they do not penetrate directly into the earth, but creep along obliquely. Those located just beneath the surface of the earth send out other small plants, so that an entire runner <note>stolo</note> a span <note>about 9 inches</note> in diameter is knit together by these firm threads.

These bulbs or glands constitute that aromatic part of this grass which is properly called *Cyperus*, being of unequal size and shape. For in Java, Bali, and other regions where the soil is rich and much Rice is cultivated, these roots reach the length of half a finger; some are as long and thick as the little finger. In these islands, however, the largest reach the size of hazelnuts, and when dry, they take on a pale reddish color. Their odor and flavor are pleasant and aromatic with a slight bitterness and astringency, filling the mouth with a grateful scent.

The remaining parts of these plants are odorless and tasteless, being merely grass-like. These bundles of leaves never produce a stalk or seed, in which respect they do not agree with the European *Cyperus* <note>Rumphius notes the tropical variety remains sterile in terms of seeds compared to European species</note>, but as they grow old they fall away and produce their plants through their copious lateral offspring. Indeed, the small bulbs or glands, when thrown away with the pulled-up roots, germinate everywhere they touch the earth; even the smallest bulb the size of a grain of wheat remaining in the ground sprouts anew, so that it is difficult to extirpate this plant once it has grown.

The hard threads do not pierce through the glands, although they are very firmly attached to them, so that the substance of the glands is uniform throughout.

### II. Flowering Cyperus

is considered the male of the *Cyperus*, or rather a *Cyprian grass* <term>Gramen cyperoides: a sedge-like grass</term>, for its root bears no glands, or very few. They consist of simple, brown, and curled fibers which are nonetheless fragrant if pulled out fresh, but they have no aromatic flavor; occasionally one or two small bulbs are seen on them, as proof that it is kin to the *Cyperus*.

The leaves are as in the former, but a little shorter, and the whole plant is lower, as it usually occurs in the streets and waysides. From the center of the leafy bundles grows a thin, straight, dark green, and smooth stalk, reed-like, three-sided, and rarely four-sided, half a foot high or a little more, and thicker than a sail-thread. At the summit it bears three narrow leaves placed directly in a triangle, shorter and narrower than those of the plant itself; between these others are located which are smaller. These support many chaff-like clusters <term>Corymbos: flat-topped flower clusters</term> extending one above the other, consisting of sharp cells and scales, mixed of green and brown color, in which the seed lies hidden, so that the scaly plumes constitute the flowers and seeds of this plant.

Two or three such flowering stalks sprout from a single root, which by no means creeps under the earth like the former, but multiplies by the fallen seed.

### III. Scentless Cyperus

is properly a shore-herb, larger in all its parts than the two former. Its roots are oblong glands like the gallbladders of fish, externally brown or reddish, and covered with the skins of dried leaves, a finger-joint long. The longest is located under the primary plant; to either side it sends out a thick thread, and at a distance of about six fingers similar glands are seen, but a little rounder than the former, sometimes two or three together. Besides those threads, other fibers grow out, both thick and thin, piercing the earth but bearing no glands, for the gland-bearing fibers always creep along obliquely.

Furthermore, each of these glands, since they are not located deep under the earth, bears at its top a leafy bundle which forms a thick stalk, a hand-breadth high, and which expands into ten or twelve leaves. These, however, are much longer than the former, two spans long or even longer, and a little wider, resembling a simple channel with very narrow edges and a sharp ridge underneath, never sending out flowers or seeds like the first species. The interior substance of the glands is harder and drier, almost destitute of aromatic odor and flavor, but is greatly bitter and astringent, for which reason they are neither collected nor preserved.

**Name.** In general, these roots are called *Cyperus*; by PLINY *Juncus angulosus* <note>Angled rush</note> and *triangularis* <note>Triangular rush</note>. But by CORNELIUS CELSUS, *Juncus quadratus* <note>Square rush</note>; perhaps because he saw them as four-sided in the Greek lands. This primary species is called *Cyperus rotundus* <note>Round Cyperus</note>, and the first variety of it is *Cyperus rotundus odoratus* <note>Scented round Cyperus</note>, or *Cyperus legitimus* <note>The true Cyperus</note>. In Malay, Javanese, and Balinese it is called *Teckee*, *Teckey*, and *Tickee*; on Ambon *Laupa*; in Chinese *Tauhyo*, which means "scented bean"; in Macassarese *Rucu*; and on Ternate *Tacki*. The second variety is called *Cyperus floridus* <note>Flowering Cyperus</note> or *Gramen Cyperoides* <note>Cyperus-like grass</note>, in Malay *Tecke lacki lacki* <note>Male Tecke</note>, because they consider it the male of this genus. The third variety is called *Cyperus inodorus* <note>Scentless Cyperus</note>, or *maritimus* <note>Maritime Cyperus</note>, in Malay *Tecke Pantey* <note>Beach Tecke</note>, and on Ambon *Laupa Layn*.

---

Above in the eighth book we noted that the ancients included many types of bulbous and knotty roots under the name *Cyperus*: for example, by the "lesser" or "common" *Cyperus* they mean the one just described. *Cyperus Babylonicus* is thought to have been *Galanga minor* <note>Lesser Galangal</note>, and perhaps also *Zedoaria* and *Zerumbet*. *Cyperus Indicus* <note>Indian Cyperus</note> is *Curcuma* <note>Turmeric</note>.

---

**Location.** The first and second varieties grow, as has been said, everywhere around human dwellings, such as in the streets, around yards, and especially in gardens on abandoned beds, as well as on the sandy banks of rivers. The largest and most beautiful are found on Java in cultivated lands that have lain fallow for some time, especially in rice fields that are somewhat elevated. The third variety grows on flat and coarse-sandy shores, creeping with its long roots into the pure sand and covering the edges of the seawater.

---

**Use.** Only the roots of the first variety are in use, namely its aromatic nodules <note>The small tubers</note>, which are cleaned of the adhering fibers; the brownish-black skins are scraped off slightly, then dried in the sun or in smoke, and then sold in spice boxes among other spices. For the Javanese and Balinese use them much in their scented ointment called *Bobori*. Two different and almost opposing qualities are found in this root: for a decoction <note>A medicinal tea made by boiling</note> of the fresh roots has a thinning, penetrating, and diuretic power; but the dried and powdered root is astringent, drying, and somewhat constipating. From this, its effects are found to be as follows.

The fresh roots, when crushed, are added to drinks and decoctions prepared for those with dropsy <note>Edema or swelling caused by fluid retention</note>, to carry the fluid out of the body. It also powerfully drives out urine, relieves gravel <note>Kidney stones or sediment</note>, and expels stones, especially when the herb *Pancaga* and the pointed roots or first sprouts of the Sedge-grass *Lala* are added to it.

A simple decoction of them also provokes the menses in women and expels the "white flow" <note>Leukorrhea</note>, so that it afterwards ceases.

Chinese doctors wash the dried roots in water, boil them, and give them to drink against asthma and whooping cough.

*Bontius* <note>Jacobus Bontius, a Dutch physician in Batavia</note> (Book 6, Chapter 43) testifies that *Cyperus rotundus* is used on Java in decoctions to drive urine and menses, and at the same time to dry up all superfluous watery humors, as in "cold" *Beriberi* and dropsy. In the hospital at Batavia at that time, a certain salt was also prepared from these roots, which had good effects on malignant and moist ulcers of the legs, consuming the superfluous flesh therein and then drying it up, just as is done in Europe with the dry powdered roots. In the ailments of horses, this powder is likewise mixed into their fodder, as is done on Ceylon and other places in old India, from which they recover.

In *Rochefort's* History of the Antilles (Chapter 11), it is read that round *Cyperus* grows in the Caribbean islands, very beautiful and large, and scented like Iris root; there it is given to women in labor to make the birth easy, and they consider it a proven remedy.

*Hodueg*, an Egyptian plant growing much in the Nile and described by *Prosper Alpinus*, is also a round *Cyperus*, bearing blackish roots or nodules the size of olives, many hanging together from a hair-like origin, which when scented have a sharp taste; but he says it bears an angled stalk, a foot and a half high, and upon it star-shaped extended leaves, from which certain ears sprout with grass-colored seeds, wherein it again...

...they grow, bearing seeds of a grassy color, in which matter it again differs from the Indian variety. Two other species, as far as I know, are not in use.

I have also observed that the powder of the dried roots, which can very easily be reduced to powder, brings the greatest relief to spreading ulcers, especially of the mouth, when it is sprinkled upon them and the mouth is rinsed with a decoction of it.

The grassy substance of the first two species is cut into small pieces to feed hens and roosters that are kept in cages, as many Indians <note>The author refers to the inhabitants of the East Indies, specifically the Malay Archipelago.</note> nourish their fighting cocks this way, mixing it with rice. If dogs are troubled by griping of the belly <note>Stomach cramps or colic.</note> and cannot find common dog-grass, they eat any kind of *Teckee* <note>A local name for Cyperus species.</note>, but especially the first species, by which they are cured through vomiting.

The fresh round roots of the *Cyperus* <note>Sedge</note> crushed with *Canarium* nuts, warmed in a leaf over a fire, and applied, will dissipate an incipient *Paronychia* <note>A whitlow or nail-bed infection</note>; but if it is already tending toward suppuration, it quickly matures and opens it. In the same manner, the following *Tabalissa* is employed.

The second species, or *Flowering Cyperus*, has two or three varieties, growing mostly in damp places. One indeed sends out a stalk a foot and a half high, which produces long and narrow leaves on all sides; and the vertical spikes are not so prickly, but more downy and extended. The second variety of it grows higher, almost like a *Sedge-grass* <note>original: "Caricis" / "snygras"</note>, under which it might also be classified, as can be examined in that chapter.

Its leaves are two feet long and somewhat sharp, mostly semi-concave, but the lower ridge is quite sharp. Its stem is three feet high, a straw's thickness, and triangular. At its summit are placed three long leaves, extended in a triangle.

Its spikes are brownish, hard, and prickly, composed of similar chaff-like seed.

If any white linens are dried over these seed-bearing crowns, they contract reddish spots. The roots of both are thin, reddish hairs, somewhat fragrant but faint.

The third variation, or fourth species of *Flowering Cyperus*, is similar to the former, for it likewise sends out long narrow leaves, a foot and a half to two feet long and clustered. Among these rises a peculiar stalk, two to two and a half feet high, triangular, sharp, having the thickness of sail-thread, yet firm. At the summit it produces no leaves placed in a triangle, in which matter it differs from all the preceding, but forms an extended umbel composed of many cross-shaped spikes, of which one is longer than the other, and their small stalks are rough and cutting to the touch.

From these hang many small grains, each placed on a particular stalk, mixed of a green and brown color like barley seed, and filled with similar seed. Four or five leaves together form a cluster at the bottom, where they are also somewhat concave.

*Toad Grass* <note>original: "Gramen Bufonum"</note>, in Malay *Rompot Codock*, in Balinese *Padangh Dancong*, by no means deserves to be referred to the species of *Cyperus*, but rather to the aquatic grasses.

It grows on the banks of waters and the muddy rice fields, where in Bali venomous toads dwell called *Dancong*, which are so poisonous that children have been killed by them, whose fingers were wounded by their bites; indeed, even snakes die if they are bitten by them while struggling. In Amboina, however, they are not found, where this grass does not grow in clay, but in another soil that is slightly damp.

Sheep and cows graze on the seeds, but they do not eat the grass itself, as it is too sharp and rush-like.

There are various species of *Cyperus* known to the ancients, as well as those detected primarily by the industry of more recent botanists; for besides differing in the length and roundness of the roots, they distinguish themselves by the width and narrowness of the leaves, as well as by the bitterness or sweetness of the taste and a greater or lesser fragrance of scent; concerning which see especially *Plukenet, Almagestum page 127*; *Tournefort, Institutiones Rei Herbariae page 527*; and *Sloane, Catalogus Plantarum Jamaicae page 35*; as well as *S. Dale, Pharmacologia in 4to page 281*.

## Cyperus longus. Teckee laut.
## The long Cyperus.

The second species of Cyperus is the long one, which in these Oriental islands has the following form, differing greatly from that shown in European books. It is a very long and creeping plant, having a long, thin, and perennial root that creeps not deeply beneath the sand; the stalk is as thick as a thumb <note>original: "pollicem"</note>, tough and rush-like, covered on the outside with brown skins and divided into many joints, spaced a thumb or two transverse fingers apart, where they send down very fine fibrils like little hairs. At every third or fourth joint, a short stalk rises upright, two or three inches high, as far as it is also submerged in the sand.

At its summit, it bears a leafy tuft composed of eight or twelve leaves, which are widest at their origin, channeled on the inner side, marked with a round back underneath, generally firm and bent backward, and ending in a sharp point, which is so prickly that one cannot easily walk barefoot among this plant. From the center of this cluster rises a firm and triangular stalk, scarcely two inches high, bearing at its top a short and compressed spike or a prickly head composed of many angular cells, which together form this head, about the size of a musket ball, but tapering gently to a point at the top. Each individual cell is the size of a grain of wheat, composed of simple brownish tips or scales, in which a chaff-like seed is hidden. Just below this head, three common small leaves are placed in a triangle, firm and stinging, and above these in the spaces are three other smaller leaves.

The root is aromatic, breathing a more pleasant scent than the round variety, especially when fresh, which gives off the odor of *Gladiolus odoratus* <note>The "fragrant sword-lily," likely a species of Iris or similar aromatic root</note> or *Tackary*, and furthermore it is somewhat salty, especially if it grows on the shore. When chewed in the mouth, it causes a slow burning in the throat, with cracks in the tongue and lips; however, this is much sharper in one plant than in another, for those that grow on the parched shores have a much sharper taste and produce more stinging leaves than those found in moist sand around the mouths of rivers.

On the sandy shores of *Assahoedy*, I found a very wild species of this, which instead of those seed-bearing heads bore huge heads armed with prickly leaves, as horrific as a Sea Urchin <note>Latin: *Echinus marinus*</note>, so that it could hardly be handled. Its leaves were like the common kind, but the roots had little scent.

---

**Name:** In Latin *Cyperus longus*; in Malay *Teckee laut*, by others *Tackary-laut*; in Ternate *Minjassal unting*.

**Location.** I have found this nowhere except on flat and sandy shores, especially those that project somewhat into the sea, where it creeps through the sand with its long roots, particularly at *Hitoelamma* on the coast of *Hitoe*, as well as in the *Amboina* bay on the *Mela* shore, and at the Bay of *Baguala*. On little *Carama* near *Loeham*, it grows at the mouths of certain rivers and at pools filled with salt water, where the soil is fine and sandy, and where, as has been said, it bears softer leaves and more aromatic roots.

**Use.** Only the long and brown roots of this plant, which creep under the sand, are in use in place of *Gladiolus odoratus* or *Tackary*; for when dug up, they are cut into pieces a palm long or shorter, and if they are naturally salty, they are immediately dried in the sun; but if they grow further from the sea, they are dipped in salt water and then dried in the sun. These pieces are mixed into all kinds of incense <note>original: "suffimigiis" / "reukwerken"</note>, just as is done with the *Tackari*. They should not be chewed in the mouth, for at first they provide a sweet or bland taste, but, as said, shortly thereafter they bite the tongue and lips vehemently, so that cracks follow.

The entire roots, ten or twelve fathoms <note>original: "orgyas" / "vadem"</note> long, are pulled out or eradicated with little effort, since they creep loosely under the earth; they are cleaned of the adhering fibrils and prickly leaves, woven into wreaths, once or twice...

# THE AMBOINESE HERBARIUM Book X. Chapter II.

...they are soaked once or twice in seawater, and dried again in the sun, and thus they are preserved for use as incense <note>original: "suffimigio" / "reukwerk"</note>.

When recently dried, these roots fill an entire room with their pleasant odor for several days.

I made mention just above of a certain grass that bore a prickly head, discovered on the shore of Assahudi <note>also spelled Assabudi in the Dutch text</note>; however, since I later obtained a larger variety of it in the year 1690, which differed too much from the common *Long Cyperus* <term>Cyperus longus: a type of sedge</term>, I shall hereafter provide its specific and subsequent description.

It appears to be a species of *Beach Sedge* <note>original: "Cyperi littorei"</note>, but sometimes it also grows somewhat further inland. It stands with an upright stalk, which is covered with long, very narrow, and stiff leaves, scarcely a shaft broad <note>the width of a quill or small arrow</note>, channeled toward the front, and ending in a very sharp point, six or seven inches long. Their edges are sharp and rough if they are stroked or rubbed backward. These leaves are placed in a circle around a large head, which is composed of stiff prickles like hog-bristles; some are six inches long, some half as long, and all are tangled together. Furthermore, the entire plant is so prickly that it cannot be touched or handled except from underneath.

This plant is found on the Orlanitii Islands, which lie to the west before Boëro and Tomahu. It reaches a height of three or four feet, sending out long, narrow, and sharp grass-like leaves on all sides. In the middle grows a head so large that a man can scarcely encompass it with his arms, composed of similar long and prickly leaves.

To the inhabitants, it serves no other use than for a certain clever pastime: when this head is cut off and placed on a flat beach, the blowing wind propels and agitates it, as if the Devil <note>original: "nikker" - a water-sprite or devil in Dutch folklore</note> were chasing it.

**Name.** In Malay it is called *Rompot bulu matta* <note>modern: Rumput bulu mata</note>, because its long spines somewhat resemble the long eyelashes of the Chinese. In Chinese it is *Tsjikiu*, that is, "rolling thorn," because it rolls with the wind; from which it may be concluded that it is also known in China. In Dutch it may be called *Wintboll* or "Wind-ball."

This plant, brought from the Tomahu Islands in the year 1691, showed that it is nothing other than a *Beach Sedge* creeping along the ground, bearing joints five or six inches long and as thick as the *Tsjavony* Rattan. At the nodes, it shoots long roots into the sand. Closely set against these joints are clustered leaves, a quill-pen broad and furrowed; when dry, however, they roll up and terminate in a stiff, long point. These "wind-balls" arise between the uppermost leaves, which are from seven to thirteen inches long. The heads are larger than those grown in Batavia <note>modern Jakarta</note>, for they bore bristles ten or eleven inches long, which at the base were no thicker than binding-thread, and at the top were jointed and rough like a horsetail <note>the plant Equisetum</note> if rubbed backward, exactly like the harmful sea-urchin *Echinometra setosa*.

The stalks are so tough that they cannot be broken, but the whole plant offers nothing aromatic. It grows on the sandy and uncultivated shores of Java and Sumatra, namely around Jambi, and is used nowhere except for the aforementioned game. Its small roots have a sharpness that somewhat bites the tongue. It also occurs on the sandy islands of Ceylon <note>modern Sri Lanka</note>, where it is even larger than the Amboinese variety, serving the same pastime.
