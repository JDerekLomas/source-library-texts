# Sugar Beet
<note>original: 菾菜, *tiáncài*. This refers to varieties of *Beta vulgaris*, encompassing both leaf beets (Swiss Chard) and early forms of the sugar beet.</note>

This plant was recorded as a "medium-grade" item in the *Supplementary Records* <note>original: 名醫別錄, *Míngyī Biélù*, a classic pharmacological text from the 3rd century.</note>. It is the same as the Chard vegetable <note>original: 莙達菜, *jūndácài*.</note>; in Hunan, it is called the "Sweet Vegetable." There is a variety with red stems that is unsuitable for eating; people grow it only for ornament. Note that the name *Junda* was first recorded in the *Jiayou Materia Medica* <note>An imperial botanical work compiled between 1057–1060.</note>. The great physician Li Shizhen grouped these together because the words "Beet" and "Sweet" <note>original: 菾 and 甜, both pronounced *tián*.</note> sound so similar. However, the descriptions provided by various authors claiming the leaves resemble *Shengma* <note>*Cimicifuga*, or Bugbane.</note> or *Shuodiao* <note>*Sambucus chinensis*, or Chinese Elder.</note> do not match this plant at all. For now, I will tentatively follow the existing classifications.

The flavor of this vegetable is sweet but "incorrect" <note>meaning an unappealing or cloying sweetness</note>, making it a very low-quality foodstuff. Because it is easy to plant and fertilize, only lazy gardeners tend to grow it. This stands in stark contrast to the *Commentary on the Tang Materia Medica*, which claims that when steamed with fish, it is "extraordinarily fragrant and delicious." Furthermore, it was once used in the summer months to make porridge with tea to relieve heat, though no one makes such porridge these days. The *True Materia Medica* states it treats "cold phlegm" <note>A traditional Chinese medicine term for thick, clear fluids associated with a "cold" constitution or digestive sluggishness.</note> trapped in the diaphragm and stomach. It must not be eaten in excess. Indeed, in places where truly fine vegetables are plentiful, this one is rightly rejected.

Among vegetables, those that are both sweet and salty have their essence focused entirely on the sweetness. If a zither or a lute played only a single, monotonous note, who could bear to listen to it? 

However, if a flavor is sweet and clear, or sweet and rich, there are still those who will crave it. Yet, if one indulges in such things for too long, "tooth-worms" <note>original: 齒蟲, *chǐchóng*; an old term for dental caries or cavities, once believed to be caused by literal worms</note> and "stomach-parasites" <note>original: 胃魷鼈, *wèiyóubiē*, literally "stomach squid-turtles," referring to various internal parasites or digestive ailments caused by poor diet</note> are born. Even the weevils that fly among the grains become pests <note>original: 蠱, *gǔ*, a term for parasitic or venomous entities created through stagnation</note> because the sweetness is unrestrained by any other flavor. 

When it comes to that which is sweet yet "turbid" and "incorrect" <note>original: 邪, *xié*, meaning deviant, off-flavor, or unhealthy</note>, then both the high-ranking literati and the humble gardeners despise it; the Beet Greens <note>original: 蕎菜, *qiáocài*; here referring back to the leaf beet/chard discussed previously</note> are exactly such a plant. 

There are many people in this world who try to please others with "sweetness" <note>i.e., flattery</note>. Some people are pleased by it, and some are not. Is this simply because tastes differ? Or is it because, like the turbid and "incorrect" sweetness of this vegetable, such flattery is fundamentally base and despised by people of character? Those who flatter, and those who love to be flattered, must surely be able to distinguish this for themselves.

# Taro

Taro <note>original: 芋, *yù*; *Colocasia esculenta*</note> is listed in the *Miscellaneous Records of Famous Physicians* <note>original: 別錄, *Biélù*</note> as a "middle-rank" substance. The varieties of taro are extremely numerous, and they differ significantly in both size and shape. In Hunan, there is a variety that produces flowers; these have a single petal and a single stamen, measuring three to four inches in length and possessing a yellow hue. Wild taro <note>likely *Alocasia odora* or similar toxic Araceae</note> is poisonous to humans and is found frequently in mountain ranges.

In the regions of Lingnan <note>Southern China</note>, Yunnan, and Sichuan, the names for taro are particularly diverse. According to the *Nanning Prefecture Gazette*, those suited for dry land are called **Big Taro** <note>original: 大芋, *dàyù*</note>, while those suited for damp earth are called **Noodle Taro** <note>original: 麵芋, *miànyù*; referring to its starchy texture</note>. Other varieties include the **Dry Taro**, **Dog-claw Taro**, **Water Taro**, and **Pu Taro**. 

According to the *Mengzi County Gazette*, there are varieties known as **Palm Taro**, **White Taro**, and **Hemp Taro**. The *Huitong County Gazette* lists the **Winter Taro**, **Water-Li Taro**, **Red-mouth Taro**, **Bullet Taro**, **Ginger Taro**, and the **Large-head Wind Taro**. The *Qiongshan County Gazette* records the **Mother-Hen Taro** and the **Eastern Taro**. The *Shicheng County Gazette* lists the **Green Bamboo Taro**, **Yellow Taro**, and **Foreign Taro**. Finally, the *Ruian County Gazette* mentions the **Child Taro** and the **Noodle Taro**. These varieties are so numerous they can hardly be counted in full.

In the *Treatise on the Surveyor and Weights and Measures* <note>original: 虞衡志, *Yúhéng Zhì*</note> by Pu Hai, it is claimed that the taro of Yunnan is the greatest in the world, though this may not be entirely accurate. Shen Pu remarked that Yunnan taro matures early and has a delicious flavor; its stalks can be used to prepare a fine soup. Su Yuju <note>The famous Song Dynasty poet Su Shi, also known as Su Dongpo</note>, in his poem *Ode to the Jade-Grain Porridge* <note>original: 玉糝羹, *yùsǎn gēng*</note>, praised it by saying its fragrance was "like ambergris" and its taste "like cow's milk." Shangu <note>The poet Huang Tingjian</note> also composed verses in its honor.

# Taro

It is said that yams <note>original: 薯蕷, *shǔyù*</note> grow in the summer heat but lack flavor, yet people mock the "crouching owl" <note>original: 蹲鴟, *zūnchī*; an ancient literary name for the taro, so named because its shape resembles a hunched owl</note> with similar disparagement. The poet Lu You <note>original: 放翁, *Fàngwēng*</note> once wrote: "Do not laugh at the 'crouching owl' for its lack of flavor; one relies on it to prop oneself up through years of famine." When an empty stomach rumbles like thunder, and one depends on the "jade extension" <note>original: 玉延, *yùyán*; a poetic name for the Chinese yam</note> or the "yellow solitary" <note>original: 黃獨, *huángdú*; the air potato</note> to survive, one cannot afford to be choosy. 

However, Lu You was merely composing a poem about the common taro. If one considers the **taro buds** <note>original: 芋奶, *yùnǎi*; literally 'taro milk,' referring to the small, tender secondary corms</note> of the Sanwu region <note>the Yangtze River Delta</note>, they are as smooth and tender as milk. When seasoned with cane syrup and swallowed, they slide down the throat of their own accord. How could such sweetness be considered inferior to the "jade extension" yam?

Furthermore, the *Complete Treatise on Agricultural Administration* <note>original: 農政全書, *Nóngzhèng Quánshū*</note> states that taro juice can be used to wash greasy clothes, leaving them as white as jade. In *Su Dongpo’s Miscellaneous Notes* <note>original: 東坡雜記, *Dōngpō Zhájì*</note>, it is mentioned that the people of Sichuan use taro mucilage when grafting flowers and fruits. Even the "leftover" properties of the plant serve the people's needs. 

Roasting taro in dry leaves is naturally a staple for mountain dwellers practicing "grain avoidance" <note>original: 辟穀, *bìgǔ*; a Daoist fasting technique</note>. Yet, the *Miscellaneous Records of the Cloud Immortals* <note>original: 雲仙雜記, *Yúnxiān Zhájì*</note> describes burning the finest charcoal and wrapping taro heads in "dragon brain" <note>original: 龍腦, *lóngnǎo*; borneol camphor</note> to roast them. The book *Simple Sustenance for the Mountain Dweller* <note>original: 山家清供, *Shānjiā Qīnggōng*</note> describes the **"Big Endurance Cake"** <note>original: 大耐糕, *Dànàigāo*</note>: take a large taro, remove the skin and core, blanch it with salted plum and licorice, and fill it with pine nuts and olive kernels. Such a dish surely transcends the simple "flavor of a late frost."

In the Tang dynasty, when Feng Guang was editing the *Selections of Refined Literature* <note>original: 文選, *Wénxuán*</note>, he explained the "crouching owl" by saying it was simply a "hairy radish." How could those who eat only meat ever recognize the products of the farm and garden? They are truly like those "facing a wall" <note>original: 面牆, *miànqiáng*; a Confucian metaphor for being stuck or ignorant due to a lack of study</note>.

## Illustrated Investigation of the Names and Realities of Plants: Volume 2

**The Farmer of Yulou** <note>The pen name of the author, Wu Qijun (1789–1847), a high-ranking official and celebrated botanist</note> **says:** In Yunnan <note>original: 滇, *Diān*</note>, there is a variety of taro with red roots and flowers. Its appearance belongs to the same family as the **giant taro** <term>original: 海芋, *hǎiyù*; *Alocasia macrorrhizos*</term> and the **jack-in-the-pulpit** <term>original: 南星, *nánxīng*; *Arisaema*</term>. One can cut the stems of its flowers, peel them, and roast them. When seasoned with the five flavors, it is comparable to **Chinese broccoli** <note>original: 芥藍, *jièlán*</note>. However, the whole root itself is inedible.

Indeed, the "crouching owl" <note>original: 蹲鴟, *zūnchī*; an ancient poetic name for the taro corm</note> has performed truly great service in saving the world from hunger. In the regions between the Zhang and Gong rivers <note>in Jiangxi province</note> and along the bends of the Xiao and Xiang rivers <note>in Hunan province</note>, taro fields are vast and numerous. Yet, those who have not seen this flowering variety are often astonished by its uniqueness; the timid might even fear it is as poisonous as the *zhen* bird <note>original: 鴆, *zhèn*; a mythical bird whose feathers were said to be so toxic they could poison wine</note>. 

The people of Yunnan eat their fill of the large corm, finding it delicious whether roasted or sliced into pieces. Furthermore, they alone have discovered that the flowering stems can be eaten, finding them even superior to daylilies or bean leaves. 

How abundant are the plants and trees of Yunnan! It is said that all things are born in the east and reach maturity in the west. Since Yunnan is situated in the southwest, it is frequently visited by the **Changhe winds** <note>original: 閶闔風, *chānghéfēng*; the winds of the "Gates of Heaven," associated with the west and the autumn harvest</note>. When things reach the autumn of their cycle, their vital essence gathers and rises. Therefore, the trees there bloom easily, and the herbs flourish readily. # Illustrated Investigation of Plant Names and Realities

### Chickweed

**Chickweed** <note>original: 繁縷, *fánlǚ*</note> is listed in the *Supplementary Records of Famous Physicians* <note>original: 別錄, *Biélù*; a 3rd-century pharmaceutical text</note> as a "lower-grade" herb. In the *Erya* <note>original: 爾雅; the oldest surviving Chinese dictionary</note>, it is referred to by the names *jiān*, *fù*, and *huò*; the commentary explains that these refer to what we now call Chickweed. Some call it **Chicken-Intestine Grass** <note>original: 雞腸草, *jīcháng cǎo*</note>. The *Tang Dynasty Herbal* followed this tradition without deviation.

**Li Shizhen** <note>The famous Ming dynasty pharmacologist (1518–1593)</note> believed this plant was actually **Goose-Intestine Grass** <note>original: 鵝兒腸, *é'ér cháng*</note> and not "Chicken-Intestine." Today, it is found in great abundance in extremely damp and shady places.

**The Farmer of Yulou** <note>The pseudonym of the author, Wu Qijun</note> says: When I first arrived in **Dian** <note>original: 滇; an ancient name for Yunnan Province</note>, I saw people selling "Goose-Intestine Vegetable" in the market. I found this very strange. I had thought of this plant as it exists in the regions of the **Yangtze and Xiang Rivers**, where it fills every gap in the masonry and chokes the ditches, its tangled threads and twisting vines so thick that they cannot be cleared away even with constant weeding. 

However, when I walked through my own garden in Yunnan, I could not find a single specimen. I ordered the gardeners to plant some in the garden beds, but even then it did not grow very lushly. Only then did I realize that in the Yunnan region, Chickweed is sold in markets precisely because it is **scarce** <note>original: 尠, *xiǎn*</note>. Li Shizhen believed it was a plant that propagated and grew with great ease, which is why it was nicknamed "Nourishing Grass"—but this is clearly not the case everywhere.

Beyond the city walls of Yunnan, the land is entirely composed of well-tended farm fields, and one finds no "miscellaneous" weeds or wild trees. Yet, the mountain flowers that can be worn in the hair or arranged in vases; the wild grasses that can be used for medicine or in ritual baths; and the roots, seeds, fruits, and vegetables that can be eaten or appreciated for their beauty—all of these are gathered by the locals and carried into the city markets. Therefore, without even stepping out of one's own courtyard, the botanical treasures of the four seasons are laid out for display upon one's **side tables** <note>original: 几案, *jī'àn*</note>.

## Fish Mint

**Fish Mint** <term>Jicai (蕺菜): *Houttuynia cordata*</term> is categorized as a "lower grade" herb in the *Collected Records of Famous Physicians* <note>original: "Bielu" (別錄)</note>. It is also known as **Fish-Smelling Grass** <term>Yuxingcao (魚腥草)</term>. Its flowers resemble the crabapple but are white in color; from the center, a long, thread-like spike protrudes. 

If one covers fish with its leaves, the fish will not spoil quickly. In Hunan, people boil the plant to make a drink during the summer to relieve heat stroke. According to the *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, dating back to the 3rd century BCE</note>, it is referred to simply as *Ji*. 

In his commentary, **Guo Pu** <note>A famous scholar of the Jin Dynasty</note> notes: "The herb resembles the **Winter Cherry** <term>Suanjiang (酸漿)</term>; its flowers are small and white with a yellow center." In the Jiangdong region, it is used to make **pickles** <term>Zu (葅)</term>. The *Comprehensive Treatises* <note>original: "Tongzhi" (通志)</note> considers this to be the same plant as the *Ji*.

The pronunciation of the characters *Ji* <term>蕺</term> and *Zhi* <term>職</term> are similar, and their appearances are also comparable. According to the *Spring and Autumn Annals of Wu and Yue*, the King of Yue once tasted feces <note>An act of strategic humiliation to prove loyalty to his captor</note>, which caused him to suffer from chronic bad breath. **Fan Li** <note>The King's legendary advisor</note> ordered the attendants to eat **Cen Grass** to mask the King's odor. A note clarifies that **Cen Grass** is actually **Fish Mint**. 

In years of famine, starving people dig up its roots for food. The *Essential Arts for the Common People* <note>original: "Qimin Yaoshu" (齊民要術), a 6th-century agricultural manual</note> describes a method for pickling Fish Mint. Today, however, it is no longer commonly eaten, and it is rarely used in formal medical prescriptions. It is primarily cultivated by local folk doctors in the Jiangxi and Hunan regions as an essential medicine for external surgeries <note>Typically used for treating boils, abscesses, or skin inflammations</note>. 

The *Gazetteer of Zunyi Prefecture* notes that **Side-Ear Root** <term>Ceergen (側耳根)</term> is the same as Fish Mint; in years of crop failure, the people dig up and eat the roots. According to the *Materia Medica*, its flavor is pungent. However, the *Gazetteer of Shanyin County* states that its flavor is bitter and warns that it "harms the *Yang* energy and depletes the marrow," suggesting it should only be used as a last resort to ward off death by starvation <note>literally: "slow the arrival of the ditches and valleys," a metaphor for death by the roadside during famine</note>.

# Rapeseed

**Rapeseed** <note>original: 芸薹菜, *Yuntaicai*</note> was first officially recorded in the *Materia Medica of the Tang Dynasty* <note>Compiled in 659 AD, this is considered the world's first state-sponsored pharmacopoeia.</note>. # Bracken

<note>original: 蕨, *jué*; the Bracken fern, *Pteridium aquilinum*. In Chinese culture, it is a symbol of the scholar-recluse, famously associated with the brothers Bo Yi and Shu Qi who ate only ferns rather than serve a new dynasty.</note>

There is also a **Water Bracken** that grows in aquatic environments; in northern regions, it is known as **Dragon-Whiskers Vegetable**.

According to the *Collected Studies of the Mountain Hall*, when the statesman Fan Wenzheng <note>original: 范文正公; Fan Zhongyan (989–1052 AD), a famous Song dynasty official known for his social reforms</note> was serving as a relief commissioner in the Jiang-Huai region, he presented the Emperor with **Dark-Dim Grass** <note>original: 烏昧草, *wū mèi cǎo*</note>, which the impoverished citizens were eating to survive. The *Anhui Gazetteer* later identified this "Dark-Dim Grass" as being none other than bracken.

Today, mountain dwellers in the regions of the Yangtze, Huai, Yunnan, and Guizhou all grind the roots of this plant to make food. The *Zunyi Prefecture Gazetteer* notes a variety called **Sweet Bracken**, whose roots resemble bamboo joints. These are dug up, washed, and pounded into a pulp known as **Bracken Curd**. This is mixed with water and the juice is collected, then filtered through palm fibers to remove the dregs. After sitting overnight, it settles into a paste called **Bracken Flour** <note>original: 蕨粉, *jué fěn*; a starch still used in Chinese cooking today</note>. This flour can be kneaded into cakes called **Bracken Ba** <note>original: 蕨巴; "ba" refers to a thick pancake or patty</note>. Yet, from what I have seen in the region of Qianzhong <note>Modern-day Guizhou province</note>, the laborers who climb steep paths carrying heavy burdens—lined up so closely that the crown of one's head nearly touches the heels of the person in front—all find it as sweet as candy.

> King Yan's <note>original: 偃王, *Yǎn Wáng*; a legendary ruler of Xu said to have been born without bones</note> beauty was originally boneless;
> Lady Gouyi <note>original: 鉤弋, *Gōu yì*; a consort of Emperor Wu of Han born with her hands clenched into fists</note> was born with her hands already made into fists.

Yang Chengzhai <note>The poet Yang Wanli (1127–1206 AD)</note> offered this advice in a poem: "Eat the arm of the bracken, but do not eat the fist" <note>A recommendation to eat the tender stalks but avoid the tightly curled, often fuzzy or bitter, "fiddlehead" tips</note>.

In the mountains of Yunnan and Sichuan, the local people preserve the bracken by drying it and then sell it; the stalks are nearly a *zhi* <note>An ancient unit of measure, approximately 8 to 10 inches</note> in length. In the ruins of the ancient Kingdom of Guzhu <note>The home of the legendary recluses Bo Yi and Shu Qi</note>, the bracken that grows is particularly plump. Because the word for "bracken" sounds exactly like the word for "extinction" <note>original: 蕨 (*jué*) sounds like 絕 (*jué*), meaning "to sever" or "to end"</note>, the people there have renamed the plant **"Auspicious"** <note>original: 吉, *jí*</note> to avoid the ill omen.

# Vetch
<note>original: 薇, *wēi*; specifically the Wild Pea or Common Vetch (*Vicia sativa*).</note>

The *Erya* <note>the oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, dating to the 3rd century BCE</note> describes the **vetch** as "the plant that hangs over water." Lu Ji’s *Commentaries on the Classic of Poetry* states that it is a trailing vine that resembles a garden pea. Xiang Anshi <note>a Southern Song dynasty scholar</note> believed it to be exactly the same as the "wild pea" variety that does not produce seeds.

The *Supplement to Materia Medica* <note>original: 本草拾遺, *Bencao Shiyi*; a medical text by Chen Zangqi, 8th century</note> was the first to formally record it. Ancient ritual texts mention it by various names such as "sheep's parsley" or "pig's vetch." During the Han dynasty, it was cultivated in official imperial gardens to supply the needs of ancestral temple sacrifices. Given this prestigious history, for Wang Anshi’s *Explanation of Characters* to claim that its name implies it is merely "food for the lowly" <note>original: 微者之食, *wēi zhě zhī shí*; a pun on the name *wēi* (vetch) and *wēi* (lowly/small)</note>—how could he be so mistaken!

Dietary habits have always differed between the North and South, and across different eras. For instance, the leaves of the Rehmannia plant <term>Rehmannia (*Dihuang*): a root commonly used in Chinese medicine, though its leaves are rarely eaten today</term> are consumed only by the people of Huaiqing. Would one then also call those "food for the lowly" simply because they are local or uncommon?

Regarding the name "Hanging over Water," commentaries state it grows by the water's edge. Textual critics argue that since the famous poem <note>refers to the *Classic of Poetry* poem "Gathering Vetch"</note> describes "climbing the mountain to gather vetch," and because the plant is named "Hanging over Water," it cannot be classified as an aquatic herb. 

However, looking at the *Qinru* <note>a local name for a vetch variety</note> found today along riverbanks, it grows particularly lush and thick. Its stems are weak and cannot stand upright on their own. Whether it clings to other plants on a mountain or hangs down by a marsh, what difference is there? The poet Du Fu wrote: "Today at South Lake I gather bracken and vetch." Just as there are both mountain and water varieties of bracken, the same holds true for the vetch.

The *Shuowen Jiezi* <note>an early 2nd-century dictionary</note> describes the vetch as resembling a small variety of bean leaf; in this description, both the physical form and the etymological meaning are fully satisfied. Chen Zangqi believed its leaves resembled duckweed, which also agrees with the appearance of the pea.

## **Chinese Milk Vetch**
<note>original: 翹揺, *qiáoyáo*; *Astragalus sinicus*. Often called "Purple Cloud Vetch" today.</note>

The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary/encyclopedia, dating back to at least the 3rd century BCE</note> refers to this plant as the **Pillar Man** <note>original: 柱夫, *zhùfū*</note> or the **Swaying Carriage** <note>original: 搖車, *yáochē*</note>. The commentary notes: it grows like a vine, has fine leaves and purple blossoms, and is edible. Today it is commonly called *Qiaoyao*. It was first officially recorded in the *Supplement to the Materia Medica* <note>original: 本草拾遺, *Bencao Shiyi*; an 8th-century medicinal text by Chen Zangqi</note>.

In the **Wu region** <note>the Yangtze Delta area, including modern Suzhou</note>, it is known as **Wild Broad Bean**. In **Jiangxi**, it is planted to fertilize the fields and is called **Red Flower Vegetable**; its seeds are sold by the *sheng* <note>a traditional unit of volume, approximately one liter</note>. In **Hubei**, it is also called the **Tip-Toe Flower** <note>original: 翹翹花, *qiáoqiáohuā*</note>. In the regions north and south of the **Huai River** and in the rural **Wu** countryside, people still eat it as a vegetable. 

Most high-ranking scholars are likely unaware of this plant. The great poet **Su Dongpo** <note>Su Shi (1037–1101), a famous Song Dynasty statesman and poet</note> once wished to have its seeds sent to Huangzhou; could it be that he had never seen the *Qiaoyao* swaying in the spring breeze among the ridges of the farm fields? Yet, his poetry says: 
*“The bean pods are round and small, the locust-like buds are fine and lush.”* 
He also wrote: 
*“This thing is uniquely charming,”* 
capturing the very essence of its branches, leaves, and flowers—it is like a painting within a poem. 

In the poetry of **Lu Fangweng** <note>Lu You (1125–1210), another prominent Song Dynasty poet</note>, he writes: 
*“This journey suddenly feels like the road to Ma Jin, waiting by the wind-stove to cook the **Little Nestling**.”*

## **Kohlrabi** <note>original: 甘藍, *gānlán*</note>

**Supplement to the Materia Medica** <note>original: 本草拾遺, *Běncǎo Shíyí*; a Tang dynasty medical text by Chen Zangqi</note> first recorded this plant, stating it is the "Indigo of the Western Lands" <note>original: 西土藍, *xītǔ lán*</note>. The **Complete Treatise on Agricultural Administration** <note>original: 農政全書, *Nóngzhèng Quánshū*; a Ming dynasty work by Xu Guangqi</note> notes that people in the North call it "Broken Indigo" <note>original: 擘藍, *bò lán*</note>. Note: This is the same as the "Pielan" <note>original: 撇藍</note> found in the Northern regions today. Its root <note>referring to the swollen, bulbous stem base</note> can grow quite large, weighing over ten catties <note>approximately 13 lbs / 6 kg</note>. It is suitable to be eaten raw or preserved in sauce, but it is not fit for stewing or boiling.

The **Shanxi Gazetteer** <note>original: 山西志, *Shānxī Zhì*</note> refers to it as the "King Turnip" <note>original: 王蔓菁, *wáng mánjīng*</note>. When shredded into long strips, it is as white and brilliant as shimmering silver. If these are soaked in freshly drawn well-water <note>original: 井華, *jǐnghuá*; the first water drawn from a well at dawn</note> and seasoned with vinegar and fermented meat paste, the flavor is crisp and delicious, providing a refreshing sensation to the throat. However, once it is placed in boiling water, it becomes pungent and soft, losing its texture so that it can no longer be chewed. Its leaves can be made into a fermented pickle <note>original: 虀, *jī*</note> called "Sour Yellow Vegetable," which is exceptionally tasty.

The **Materia Medica of Yunnan** <note>original: 滇本草, *Diān Běncǎo*</note> follows the convention of calling it "Yun-lan" <note>original: 芸藍</note>. It is used to treat <term>Spleen Deficiency</term> with excessive internal heat, phlegm accumulation in the chest, cold pains in the abdomen, and frequent nighttime urination. It also treats leprosy and various skin diseases; taking the remedy yields immediate results. Eaten raw, it quenches thirst. Roasted in embers, it treats bleeding from the large intestine. If burned to ash and ground into a powder, it treats "brain leak" <note>original: 腦漏, *nǎolòu*; chronic purulent nasal discharge or sinusitis</note> and nasal ulcers. He laments that a gentleman of character may find his career stalled even after receiving a meager late-life salary, while "wild amaranth" <note>a metaphor for common or opportunistic people</note> spreads and flourishes everywhere. The *Strange Records from Beyond the Southern Ranges* <note>original: "嶺表錄異" (*Lǐngbiǎo Lùyì*)</note> mentions an "eggplant tree" whose fruit is like a melon; I <note>Referring to the author, Li Shizhen</note> have personally seen this. The ash produced from burning the eggplant's calyx and root can be used to treat shivering and hiccups <note>The text uses "战唈" (*zhàn yì*), referring to convulsive sobbing or persistent hiccups</note>. The ash of the stems is also utilized in the production of gunpowder.

As eggplant varieties became numerous, they became highly suitable for the cooking pot. The *Eight Discourses on the Art of Living* <note>original: "遵生八牋" (*Zūnshēng Bājiān*), a Ming Dynasty manual on health and refined living</note> contains various methods for preparing them, such as steaming with sugar, pickling in vinegar and grains, lightly drying them, or preserving them in malt syrup; however, even these do not cover all the ways they can be prepared. 

The sweet variety known as the "water eggplant" can even be eaten as a fruit. Shangu <note>The pseudonym of the famous Song Dynasty poet Huang Tingjian</note> wrote a poem thanking someone for a gift of "Silver Eggplants," saying:

> "Your family's water eggplants are the color of white silver;
> They far surpass the bulky purple ones."

While the white variety is indeed superior to the purple, prior to the Tang Dynasty, the plant was only referred to as the "Kunlun Purple Melon" <note>original: "崑崙紫瓜" (*Kūnlún zǐguā*); "Kunlun" here refers to "dark-skinned" or "southern," suggesting its foreign origins</note>. The names "Bohai" <note>Named after the Bohai region</note> and "Foreign Eggplant" <note>original: "番茄" (*fānqié*); while this term means "tomato" in modern Chinese, here it refers to a non-native variety of eggplant introduced later</note> for the white variety likely appeared in later periods. 

Duan Chengshi <note>A Tang Dynasty scholar</note> stated that *Qié* was originally the name for a lotus stem. Today, we call this vegetable *Qié*, and its pronunciation is similar to the word *Jiā* <note>original: "伽"; a character often used in Chinese to transcribe Sanskrit sounds, such as in "Sangha" or "Yoga"</note>, though its exact linguistic origin is unknown. In popular stories, there are jokes regarding whether the character should be written with the components for "excellent" or "summon" beneath the grass radical. In the *Marrow of a Hundred Idlers* <note>original: "百懶髓" (*Bǎilǎnsuǐ*)</note>, it is recorded that Zhao Xixi, the Vice-Prefect of Shaoxing, once ordered his cook to prepare "Minced Meat Eggplant" <note>original: "燥子茄" (*zàozǐ qié*)</note>.

# Garland Chrysanthemum

**Illustrated Investigation of the Names and Natures of Plants, Volume 22**

The <term>Garland Chrysanthemum</term> <note>original: *tónghāo* (茼蒿); *Glebionis coronaria*. A popular leaf vegetable in East Asian cuisine, known for its aromatic foliage and daisy-like flowers.</note> was first formally recorded in the *Materia Medica of the Jiayou Era* <note>original: *Jiàyòu Běncǎo* (嘉祐本草); a mid-11th-century Chinese pharmacological text.</note>. It produces flowers similar to those of the chrysanthemum, which is why it is colloquially known as the "Chrysanthemum Vegetable."

The scholar Wang Ji <note>original: *Wāng Jī* (汪機); a famous Ming dynasty physician. The author implies that Wang, despite his expertise, lacked practical gardening experience.</note> did not recognize the Garland Chrysanthemum. It is likely he never even looked into a garden to see one. <term>Li Shizhen</term> <note>The most famous pharmacologist of the Ming Dynasty, author of the *Compendium of Materia Medica* (*Běncǎo Gāngmù*).</note> rebuked him for this, which was certainly appropriate.

However, the Garland Chrysanthemum does not actually carry the name "Crown Daisy" <note>original: *pénghāo* (蓬蒿); often confused with *tónghāo*.</note>. While the sounds of the words *peng* and *tong* are similar, their meanings do not overlap. The *Essential Formulas Worth a Thousand Gold Pieces* <note>original: *Qiānjīn Fāng* (千金方); a 7th-century medical classic by Sun Simiao.</note> classifies the Garland Chrysanthemum among vegetables. The wild "Crown Daisy" is thin, resembling pondweed; while it is edible, it is not a cultivated garden vegetable. Furthermore, what is called the "Large Crown Daisy" is actually <term>White Wormwood</term> <note>original: *báihāo* (白蒿); *Artemisia sieversiana*.</note>, which is a completely different species from this plant.

The leaves of this vegetable are similar to those of <term>Sweet Wormwood</term> <note>original: *qīnghāo* (青蒿); *Artemisia annua*.</note>, and their fragrances are also alike. It produces yellow flowers that look like scattered gold, blooming from spring through the heat of summer. As the plant ages, its appearance becomes even more ornate and beautiful; it can truly be considered a precursor to the "late-season integrity" <note>original: *wǎnjié* (晚節); an allusion to the chrysanthemum's symbolic association with moral endurance and scholars who remain upright in their old age.</note> of the autumn chrysanthemum.

## Basil

**Basil** <note>original: *Luole* (羅勒); Sweet Basil, *Ocimum basilicum*</note> was first recorded in the *Materia Medica of the Jiayou Period* <note>A Song Dynasty pharmacopoeia compiled circa 1057–1060 CE</note>; it is the same plant as **Orchid Fragrance** <note>original: *Lanxiang* (蘭香)</note>. Esoteric practitioners <note>original: *shujia* (術家); scholars of occult or alchemical arts</note> claim that if one burns sheep horns and horse hooves into ash and scatters it over damp ground, basil will spontaneously grow. The *Famine Relief Materia Medica* <note>A 15th-century guide to edible wild plants for use during crop failures</note> refers to it as "Fragrant Vegetable" <note>original: *Xiangcai* (香菜)</note> and notes that it is cultivated in the region between the Yi and Luo rivers.

The text *Casual Comments from a Poor Hut* <note>original: *Wengyou Xianping* (甕牖閒評); a collection of scholarly critiques</note> displayed an ignorance of basil when it criticized the *Origin of Matters and Things* <note>original: *Shiwu Jiyuan* (事物紀原)</note>. The latter work claimed that the name was changed to "Orchid Fragrance" to avoid the naming taboo of the ruler **Shi Le** <note>A 4th-century ruler of the Later Zhao; his personal name "Le" was homophonous with the second syllable of "Luole," making the original name disrespectful to utter</note>. The author of the *Casual Comments* argued this was incorrect, citing the ancient story of Duke Mu of Zheng dreaming of an orchid <note>A reference from the *Zuo Zhuan* where an orchid signifies a child of destiny</note> as evidence. However, this critique is flawed, as it mistakenly treats "Orchid Fragrance" (Basil) as if it were the same plant as the true "Orchid Herb" <note>original: *Lancao* (蘭草); usually referring to *Eupatorium* or fragrant thoroughwort</note>.

When I and **Boji** <note>A courtesy name for a contemporary scholar or colleague of the author</note> first began writing <note>original: *shebi* (涉筆); to put pen to paper</note>, we immediately fell into such errors. One should indeed broaden their knowledge and be willing to consult those of lower status; one should certainly not make reckless criticisms <note>original: *cihuang* (雌黃); literally "yellow arsenic/orpiment" used as "white-out" for yellow paper, an idiom for speaking or editing carelessly</note>.

## Spinach

<note>original: *Boleng* (菠薐); Spinach (*Spinacia oleracea*).</note> Spinach was first formally recorded in the *Jiayou Materia Medica* <note>An imperial pharmacopeia compiled during the Jiayou era (1056–1063) of the Song Dynasty.</note>. According to the *Record of Fine Conversations* <note>original: *Jiahualu* (嘉話錄), a Tang dynasty collection of anecdotes.</note>, the seeds were brought to China from the kingdom of Poling <note>interpreted as Nepal or a region in Persia; "Boleng" is a phonetic corruption of this place name</note>, and the name was later corrupted to *Boleng*. Its flavor is smooth and beneficial to the five internal organs. This vegetable is excellent in both color and taste. The imported coral brought by foreign ships is considered most precious when its color matches the stems of spinach; for this reason, spinach is also sometimes called "Coral Vegetable."

In the southern regions, it is available throughout all four seasons, though it is at its most tender and delicious during the early spring and early winter. The poet Su Dongpo <note>A famous Song Dynasty scholar and gourmet</note> wrote in a poem:

> The North suffers under a bitter cold that never ends,
> Yet beneath the snow, spinach stands like iron armor.
> Who knew my home in Sichuan was so rich in winter greens,
> Where frosted leaves and dewy buds grow even stronger in the cold?

Generally, the lands south of the Yangtze River are rich in winter vegetables, but those grown in cellars in the North have a particularly deep emerald color and a crispier texture. However, one occasionally finds spinach that is suddenly astringent to the point of being difficult to swallow; this is perhaps due to poor, thin soil rather than a lack of cultivation. In the North, during the third and fourth months of the lunar calendar, bunches of this vegetable can grow as tall as a human, becoming thick and sturdy without being fibrous. If blanched and then dried, it can be served in a soup bowl to guests, a preparation known as "Ten Thousand Years of Green" <note>original: *Wannianqing* (萬年青); a name usually reserved for evergreen perennials, here used as a poetic culinary term for preserved spinach</note>. In Fujian and Heilongjiang, the spinach leaves are thick and tough, shaped like arrowheads; these truly live up to the description of "iron armor."

# Grey Chenopodium

<note>original: *Huihuo* (灰藿); refers to the plant *Chenopodium album*, commonly known in the West as Lamb’s Quarters or Pigweed.</note>

### Vegetables, Volume 4

<term>Huihuo</term> <note>The name literally translates to "Grey Bean-leaf," though it is not a legume; the "grey" refers to the silvery, mealy powder found on the underside of its leaves and stems.</note>

The illustration for this entry depicts the <term>Huihuo</term> in its entirety, showcasing the height of pre-modern Chinese botanical art. The drawing highlights a robust taproot system and broad leaves with distinct serrated margins. At the crown of the plant, the elongated terminal flower spikes are visible, which is the primary feature used by the author, Wu Qijun, to classify the species.

In the tradition of Chinese botany, this plant is listed under the "Vegetables" category <note>original: *Shulei* (蔬類)</note>. While often considered a common weed today, it has historically been valued as a resilient food source. Its leaves are tender and edible when young, frequently gathered from the edges of fields and gardens during the spring months.
