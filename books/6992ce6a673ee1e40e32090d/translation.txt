# Common Carpesium <note>original: "天名精" (Tianmingjing). Known scientifically as *Carpesium abrotanoides*, this plant is a member of the daisy family and has been used in traditional medicine for centuries to treat inflammation and parasites.</note>

The Common Carpesium grows in plains, along rivers, and in marshes. Today, it is found throughout the regions between the Yangtze and Huai rivers. During the summer and autumn, it sprouts stalks that closely resemble mint. Its flowers are a purplish-white, and its leaves are similar to those of Chinese cabbage <note>original: "菘菜" (Songcai)</note> but smaller. For this reason, people of the south call it **Earth Cabbage** <note>original: "地菘" (Disong)</note>.

Because its fragrance is like that of an orchid, it is also known as the **Toad Orchid** <note>original: "蟾蜍蘭" (Chanchulan)</note>. Its form resembles the indigo plant, leading to the name **Frog Indigo** <note>original: "蝦蟆藍" (Xiamalan)</note>. Due to its sweet yet pungent flavor, it is called **Wheat-Ginger** <note>original: "麥句薑" (Maijujiang)</note>.

Another name for it is **Pig’s Head** <note>original: "豕首" (Shishou)</note>. This is the same plant referred to in the *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, dating back to the 3rd century BCE, which categorized plants and animals.</note> as the **Rabbit’s Hide** <note>original: "莵薖" (Tuke)</note> and "Pig’s Head." In the Jiangdong region, people use this plant to light the way for fireflies and silkworm chrysalises.

The herb should be harvested in the fifth month of the lunar calendar. While it is known as "Earth Cabbage," in the "Lower Category" <note>Referring to the *Shennong Ben Cao Jing*, where plants were categorized into Upper, Middle, and Lower classes based on their toxicity and medicinal strength.</note> of herbal classifications, there is also an entry for "Earth Cabbage Stalk." It is variously called **Tianmenjing**, **Stinking Stickweed** <note>original: "豨薟" (Xixian). Though listed here as a synonym, it is often treated as a distinct medicinal plant (Siegesbeckia) in later texts.</note>, or **Guanwei**.

Its medicinal nature is cold and non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat blood stasis and persistent nosebleeds. It promotes urination, relieves numbness and joint pain, clears accumulated heat from the stomach, and treats scrofula <note>original: "瘻" (Lou). A swelling of the lymph nodes, historically often associated with tuberculosis.</note>.

# Cassia Seed

Cassia Seed <note>original: "決明子" (Juemingzi). The seeds of *Senna tora* or *Cassia obtusifolia*.</note> grows in the rivers and marshes of <term>Longmen</term> <note>A geographical region often associated with the Yellow River valley.</note>; today, it can be found everywhere. People plant it in their gardens and fields. In early summer, the sprouts emerge, reaching a height of about three or four feet. The roots have a purple tint, and the leaves resemble <term>clover</term> <note>original: "苜蓿" (muxu).</note>. In the sixth and seventh months, it produces yellow-white flowers. Its seeds form in clusters resembling those of the <term>green ginger-bean</term> <note>original: "青蘘豆" (qingrangdou), likely referring to a type of pulse with similar pod structures.</note>, but they are pointed.

The seeds are harvested on the tenth day of the tenth month and dried in the shade for one hundred days. According to the <term>Erya</term> <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, dating back to at least the 3rd century BCE.</note>, the names <term>Jishang</term> and <term>Yuanhua</term> are explained as the medicinal herb Cassia. <term>Guo Pu</term> <note>A famous scholar and commentator of the Jin Dynasty (276–324 AD).</note> notes in his commentary: "The leaves are yellow and pointed, the flowers are red, and the fruit is like the <term>Cornelian cherry</term> <note>original: "山茱萸" (shanzhuyu).</note>." However, in the region west of the pass <note>Guanxi.</note>, the plant called Jishang is quite different from this species.

There is another variety called <term>Horseshoe Cassia</term> <note>original: "馬蹄決明" (matie jueming).</note>. Its leaves are like those of the <term>cowpea</term> <note>original: "江豆" (jiangdou).</note>, and its seeds are shaped like a horse's hoof, which is how it earned its name.

The flavor is salty and bitter; its nature is slightly cold and non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat <term>green blindness</term> <note>original: "青盲" (qingmang). An ancient term for internal eye disorders where the eye looks normal but vision is lost, similar to modern glaucoma.</note>, excessive tissue growth in the eyes <note>original: "淫膚" (yinfu), referring to a pterygium or similar growth.</note>, redness, and white films or opacities on the eye. It relieves acute eye pain and tearing, and it is also used to treat a blue or bluish-black tint to the lips.

# Madder Root

**Madder Root** <note>original: "茜根" (Qiangen). Also written as 蒨 (Qian).</note> grows in the valleys of Mount Qiao. Nowadays, it is found in most nearby locations. It is the plant used for dyeing fabric a crimson color. 

<term>Xu Shen</term> <note>The famous Han dynasty scholar (c. 58–147 AD) who compiled the *Shuowen Jiezi*, the first dictionary to analyze Chinese characters.</note>, in his work *Explicating Graphs and Analyzing Characters*, claimed that this plant was born from human blood. Its leaves resemble those of the jujube tree, but they are pointed at the tip and wider at the base. They grow in whorls of three to five at the nodes of the stem. The plant's vines creep and spread over other grasses and trees, and the roots are purple in color.

<term>Lu Ji</term> <note>A 3rd-century scholar and poet known for his "Commentary on the Plants, Insects, Fish, and Birds in the Book of Odes" (*Mao Shi Caomu Shu*).</note> noted in his *Commentary on Plants and Trees* that: "The names *Rulu* and *Maosou* both refer to the Madder plant. People in the State of Qi call it *Qian*, while those in Xuzhou call it *Niuman* <note>original: "牛蔓", meaning "Cow-creeper."</note>."

The roots should be gathered during the second and third months <note>of the lunar calendar</note> and dried in the sun. Nowadays, gardeners often cultivate it in organized planting beds. For this reason, the *Biographies of Money-makers* <note>A chapter in Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian* (Shiji) that discusses the economy of the Han dynasty.</note> states: "A person possessing one thousand <term>picul</term> <note>original: "石" (Dan), a unit of weight roughly equivalent to 60kg in this period.</note> of <term>Gardenia</term> <note>original: "巵" (Zhi), another important dye plant.</note> and Madder is as wealthy as a 'family of a thousand chariots' <note>A metaphor for a high-ranking noble or a very wealthy household.</note>." This speaks to the immense profit that can be harvested from the land.

It is also known as:
*   **Earth Blood** <note>original: "地血" (Dixue).</note>
*   **Rulu** <note>original: "茹藘".</note>
*   **Maosou** <note>original: "茅蒐".</note>
*   **Qian** <note>original: "蒨".</note>

**Properties and Usage:**
Its flavor is bitter, its nature is cold, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat "wind-dampness" numbness <note>original: "風痺" (Fengbi), a condition characterized by joint pain and restricted movement.</note> and jaundice. It supplements the internal organs, stops bleeding, and treats internal hemorrhaging, blood in the stool, bladder deficiency, and injuries from falls. It is also used against <term>Gu-poison</term> <note>A legendary form of venom or sorcery involving toxic insects.</note>. If taken over a long period, it benefits the <term>Essence and Qi</term> and lightens the body. It can be used to dye fabric a deep red.

# Five-Flavor Berry

The <term>Five-Flavor Berry</term> <note>original: "五味子" (Wuweizi), Schisandra chinensis</note> grows in the valleys of Mount Qi, as well as in Dai Commandery, Hedong, and the various prefectures of Shaanxi, where it is especially abundant. It is also found in the regions between Hangzhou and Shaoxing.

In early spring, the seedlings emerge and send out red vines that climb upon tall trees, reaching lengths of six or seven feet. The leaves are pointed and round, resembling those of an apricot tree. In the third or fourth lunar months, the plant blooms with yellowish-white flowers similar to small lotuses. In the seventh month, it bears fruit about the size of a garden pea; these are green when young and turn reddish-purple when ripe. 

The <term>Erya</term> <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary/lexicon, dating back to at least the 3rd century BCE.</note> refers to it as <term>Wei</term> <note>original: "菋"</note> or <term>Zhi</term> <note>original: "荎"</note>, and the commentary explains these are the Five-Flavor Berry. It grows as a vine with clusters of berries at the ends of the stems. 

Today, there are several varieties which are generally similar, though the ones with a sweet taste are considered superior. They are harvested in the eighth month and dried in the shade for medicinal use. One account states that the small, wrinkled, bubbly berries covered in a layer of white "salty frost" are best. Those in which the flavors of sour, salty, bitter, pungent, and sweet are all fully present are the genuine variety.

# Broom Cypress Seed
<note>Botanical name: *Kochia scoparia*. This plant is famous in East Asia for its dual use: the seeds are a medicinal staple, and the dried stalks are traditionally tied together to make household brooms.</note>

Broom Cypress Seed grows in the marshlands and fields of Jingzhou <note>A historical region encompassing parts of modern-day Hubei and Hunan provinces.</note>, as well as in the regions surrounding Shuchuan and Langzhong <note>Areas in modern-day Sichuan province.</note>. When the plant first emerges, it spreads low across the ground to a height of five or six inches. Its roots are shaped like those of wormwood, with reddish stems and green leaves that closely resemble Fineleaf Schizonepeta <term>Schizonepeta (Jingjie): An aromatic herb in the mint family frequently used in traditional formulas.</term>. In the seventh lunar month, yellow flowers bloom; these produce the fruit known as "Earth Skin" <note>original: 地膚 (*Difu*)</note>. By the eighth month, the stalks become dry and sturdy and are ready to be harvested [for broom-making].

The leaves have a bitter taste, are "cold" in nature, and are non-toxic. They are primarily used to treat <term>diarrhea</term> of the large intestine and to stop "red and white dysentery" <note>A traditional classification of dysentery: "red" referring to blood in the stool and "white" referring to mucus.</note>. They harmonize the <term>qi</term> <note>The body's vital energy or functional balance.</note> and act as an astringent for the stomach and intestines. They are also used to treat fresh malignant sores and toxic swellings. The leaves are harvested in the third, fourth, or fifth lunar months. Other names for this plant include "Ground Mallow" <note>original: 地葵 (*Dikui*)</note> and "Ground Wheat" <note>original: 地麥 (*Dimai*)</note>.

## Thousand-Year Vine

The Thousand-Year Vine grows in the mountain valleys of <term>Mount Tai</term> <note>A mountain of great historical and ceremonial importance in eastern China.</note>. It grows as a climbing woody vine, spreading itself over trees. Its leaves resemble those of a grape plant, though they are smaller. In the fourth month of the lunar calendar, if one plucks the stem, the sap is white and fuzzy <note>This likely refers to a milky exudate or the presence of fine hairs on the new growth</note>. In the fifth month it blossoms, and in the seventh month it bears fruit. The seeds are gathered in the eighth month; they are a deep blue-black color with a slight tint of red. Only in the winter do the leaves wither.

This is the very plant referred to as the <term>Gelei</term> <note>Wild Vine</note> in the *Classic of Poetry* <note>The *Shijing*, the oldest existing collection of Chinese poetry, dating from the 11th to 7th centuries BC.</note>. The explanations provided by <term>Tao Hongjing</term> <note>A famous 5th-century Daoist and alchemist known as "The Recluse of Huayang."</note> and <term>Chen Zangqi</term> <note>An 8th-century pharmacologist noted for documenting medicines omitted from earlier works.</note> are the most accurate.

Another name for it is the *Lei* <gloss>Bitter</gloss>. Its flavor is sweet, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to supplement the five internal organs, replenish <term>Qi</term> <note>The vital life force or energy in traditional Chinese medicine.</note>, mend tendons and bones, and promote the growth of muscle. It is also used to eliminate various forms of <term>Bi</term> <note>A medical category referring to "blockage" or "obstruction" syndromes, typically manifesting as joint pain, numbness, or heaviness caused by environmental factors like cold or dampness.</note>.

# Jingtian (Stonecrop)

Jingtian grows in the mountain valleys of **Mount Tai** <note>original: "Taishan," a sacred peak in Shandong province known for its diverse flora</note>. Nowadays, it can be found everywhere in both the north and south. Many people cultivate it in their central courtyards or plant it in pots to be placed upon their rooftops. It is said that this plant can ward off fire, and thus it is commonly called the **"Fire-Careful Grass"** <note>original: "Shenhuo cao"</note>.

In the spring, it sprouts seedlings with leaves that resemble **purslane** <note>original: "machi," referring to Portulaca oleracea</note> but are larger and grow in distinct layers. The stems are extremely fragile and brittle. In mid-summer, it blooms with tiny, delicate purple flowers. After autumn, the plant withers and dies, although some varieties possess perennial roots that survive the winter.

The flowers, stems, and leaves should be harvested on the **fourth day of the fourth lunar month** and the **seventh day of the seventh lunar month**. These should be dried in the shade for medicinal use.

Jingtian is primarily used to treat skin ulcers, toxic swellings, and **pediatric wind-rash** <note>original: "yingru fenglai," likely referring to childhood eczema or hives</note> in cases where the eruptions remain beneath the skin. 

**Preparation and Application:**
*   Take five large <term>liang</term> <note>a traditional unit of weight, roughly equivalent to 31 grams</note> of fresh seedlings and leaves.
*   Mix with three large <term>liang</term> of salt.
*   Grind the ingredients together and squeeze to extract the juice.
*   Apply the juice to the affected area by rubbing it with warm hands twice daily.

This method is effective for all manner of **heat-toxin skin inflammations** <note>original: "dan chuang," often referring to erysipelas or red, painful skin sores</note>.

The plant is known by many names:
*   **Fire-Abstinence** <note>original: "Jiehuo"</note>
*   **Fire-Mother** <note>original: "Huomu"</note>
*   **Fire-Rescue** <note>original: "Jiuhuo"</note>
*   **Flash-Fire** <note>original: "Chuhuo"</note>
*   **Careful-with-Fire** <note>original: "Shenhuo"</note>

Its flavor is bitter and sour, and it is entirely non-toxic.

## Yin Chen (Capillary Wormwood)

**Yin Chen Hao** <note>original: 茵陳蒿; Latin: *Artemisia capillaris*</note> originates from **Mount Tai** <note>Taishan, a sacred mountain in Shandong province</note>. Its flavor is **bitter**, and its nature is **slightly cold**. It is primarily used to treat **wind-dampness** <term>fengbi</term> <note>joint pain and numbness attributed to "wind" and "dampness" in the body</note>, **accumulated heat**, and **jaundice** <note>original: 黃疸 (huangdan)</note>. # Ladybell Root (Shashen)

Ladybell Root grows in the river valleys of Henei <note>Modern-day Henan Province</note> and in the districts of Yuanju and Banyang’s Xu Mountain. ## Xu Changqing

<note>Scientific name: *Cynanchum paniculatum*. This herb is a member of the milkweed family and has been used in Chinese medicine for centuries to treat pain and neutralize toxins.</note>

Xu Changqing grows in the mountain valleys of Mount Tai <note>A sacred mountain in Shandong Province</note> and in Longxi <note>An ancient region in present-day Gansu Province</note>. # Cloud Fruit (Yunshi)

<note>Scientific name: *Caesalpinia decapetala*. This is a woody, deciduous climbing shrub found throughout East and South Asia, often recognized by its hooked thorns and bright yellow flowers.</note>

**Cloud Fruit** grows in the river valleys of **Hejian** <note>A historical region in present-day Hebei Province</note>. It reaches a height of five to six feet. Its leaves resemble those of the **locust tree** <note>original: 槐 (Huai), *Sophora japonica*</note>, but they are narrower and longer. The branches are covered in thorns.

### Harvesting and Preparation
The fruit is traditionally harvested in the tenth month and dried in the sun for use. Nowadays, the sprouts are collected in the third and fourth months, and the fruit is collected in the fifth and sixth months. If the fruit is left past its season, it will wither and fall off. It is frequently used in medicinal formulas for treating **carbuncles** <note>original: 癰 (Yong); painful, pus-filled inflammations of the skin and deeper tissues</note>.

It is also known by the following names:
*   **Fushi** (Supporting Fruit)
*   **Yunying** (Cloud Petal)
*   **Tiandou** (Heavenly Bean)

**Nature:** Its flavor is bitter and acrid; its character is warm and non-toxic.

*   **Diarrhea and dysentery**
*   **Chronic intestinal disorders** <note>original: 腸癖 (Changpi); refers to long-term digestive ailments or bloody stools</note>
*   **Parasites and Gu-poisoning** <note>original: 蠱毒 (Gudu); "Gu" refers to a legendary form of sorcery involving toxins from venomous creatures, but in a medical context, it often refers to severe parasitic infections or abdominal distension</note>
*   Expelling **evil influences** and **congested Qi** <note>In traditional medicine, "evil" (Xie) refers to external pathogens like wind or cold, while "congested Qi" refers to stagnant energy causing discomfort</note>
*   Relieving pain
*   Eliminating **chills and fever**

# Cocklebur

Cocklebur grows in the river valleys of Anlu and the fields of Lu'an <note>Anlu and Lu'an are historical regions in modern-day Hubei and Anhui provinces.</note>; today, it can be found everywhere.

I respectfully note that the authors of the *Classic of Poetry* <note>The oldest existing collection of Chinese poetry (11th to 7th centuries BCE).</note> referred to this plant as the "Curled Ear" <note>original: 卷耳 (juan'er)</note>. The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary.</note> calls it the "Dark Ear" <note>original: 蒼耳 (cang'er)</note>, while the *Guangya* <note>An early 3rd-century dictionary.</note> calls it *Xie'er*. These names are all derived from the physical appearance of its fruit.

The commentary by Lu Ji <note>A prominent 3rd-century scholar and poet.</note> states: 
"The leaves are a pale bluish-white and resemble <term>coriander</term> <note>original: 胡荽 (husui)</note>. It has white flowers and thin stems, growing as a spreading vine. It can be boiled and eaten as a leafy vegetable; it is slippery in texture and has little flavor. In the fourth month, it produces seeds that look exactly like a woman's earrings; thus, some people today call it the 'Earring Herb.'"

Zheng Kangcheng <note>Zheng Xuan (127–200 CE), a highly influential Confucian commentator.</note> identified this plant as "White Coriander." The people of the Youzhou region <note>A historical province covering parts of modern Beijing, Hebei, and Liaoning.</note> call it "Sparrow’s Ear." Another name is "Sheep-Brings-It" <note>original: 羊負來 (yangfulai). This colorful name refers to how the prickly burrs cling to the wool of passing sheep, allowing the plant to spread.</note>. It is commonly known as "Taoist’s Head."

The fruit should be harvested when it is ripe. Other names for this plant include "Foreign Cocklebur" <note>original: 胡葈 (huxi)</note>, "Earth Mallow" <note>original: 地葵 (dikui)</note>, *Shi*, and "Constant Thinking" <note>original: 常思 (changsi)</note>.

**Properties and Usage:**
The flavor is **bitter** and its nature is **slightly cold**; it is **slightly toxic**. It is primarily used to treat "wind" headaches <note>In traditional medicine, "wind" is an external pathogen that causes sudden symptoms like headaches or chills.</note> and pain caused by cold. It treats <term>generalized numbness</term> <note>original: 周痹 (zhoubi)</note> brought on by wind and dampness, as well as cramping and pain in the four limbs. It is also used for "bad flesh" and "dead muscle" <note>This refers to skin ulcers, necrotic tissue, or various skin growths.</note>. If taken over a long period, it <term>augments the vital energy</term> <note>original: 益氣 (yiqi). This refers to the cultivation of 'Qi', the fundamental life force in traditional Chinese thought.</note>.

<term>Kudzu Root</term> <note>original: 葛根 (Gegan). # Snake Gourd <note>original: 栝樓 (Gualou). Known scientifically as *Trichosanthes kirilowii*. It remains a staple in traditional Chinese medicine today, where its fruit, seeds, and roots are used for different therapeutic purposes.</note>

The **Snake Gourd** grows in the valleys of <term>Hongnong</term> <note>A historical commandery centered around modern-day Lingbao, Henan Province.</note> and in the shaded southern slopes of mountains. Today, it can be found almost everywhere. Its fruit is known as the **Yellow Melon**; this is the same plant referred to as the <term>Guoluo</term> <note>original: 果臝. An ancient name for the plant found in the *Classic of Poetry* (Shi Jing).</note> in the ancient songs. Its root is also called **White Medicine**.

The plant has a yellow skin and white flesh. In the third or fourth month of the lunar calendar, it sprouts and produces trailing vines. The leaves resemble those of a sweet melon but are lobed or "forked." In the seventh month, it produces delicate flowers that look like bottle gourd blossoms but are pale yellow. The fruit grows beneath the flower and is about the size of a fist. It is green when young, turning a reddish-yellow when it ripens in the ninth month.

The roots are harvested in the second and eighth months. The outer skin is removed, and they are dried in the sun for thirty days. While some fruits are perfectly round and others are pointed and long, their medicinal effects are the same. Regarding the root, those that have grown deep into the soil over many years are of the highest quality. However, one must be cautious: those growing in <term>saline-alkali soil</term> are toxic.

According to careful records, the **Snake Gourd** is primarily used to treat <term>Wasting-Thirst</term> <note>original: 消渴 (Xiao Ke). A traditional diagnosis characterized by excessive thirst and hunger, similar to modern diabetes.</note>. It is also known by the names **Earth Gourd**, **Fruit Vine**, **Heavenly Gourd**, and **Marsh Maiden**. The fruit—the "Yellow Melon"—has a bitter taste and a "cold" nature. It is non-toxic. It is used to supplement those suffering from exhaustion and labor, to relieve dry mouth, and to moisten the heart and lungs. It is also excellent for treating <gloss>wrinkles on the hands and face, vomiting blood, and "intestinal wind"</gloss> <note>original: 腸風 (Chang Feng). A condition involving blood in the stool, often associated with chronic digestive issues.</note>, as well as bloody discharge and "red and white" dysentery. It is a wonderful medicine for these uses.

## Rice Paper Plant <note>original: 通草 (Tongcao). In historical Chinese medicine, this name was used for two different plants: the Akebia vine (*Akebia quinata*) and the Rice Paper Plant (*Tetrapanax papyrifer*). The text here blends descriptions of both, as they were believed to share the medicinal property of "opening passages" in the body.</note>

Rice Paper Plant, also known as Mutong <note>original: 木通, literally "woody passage." Today this name specifically refers to Akebia.</note>, grows in the valleys of Shicheng Mountain and in Shanyang. Today, it is also found in Zelu, Hanzhong, the Jiang-Huai region, and the prefectures of Hunan. It grows as a creeping vine, as thick as a finger. The largest stems reach three inches in diameter. Every node produces two or three branches, and at the end of each branch grow five leaves. # Black Figwort

Black Figwort grows in Hejian and Yuanju <note>Hejian is in modern Hebei province; Yuanju is in modern Shandong.</note>; today, it is found everywhere. In the second month, the sprouts emerge. The leaves resemble those of sesame, and also look like those of the scholar tree and willow. The slender stems are bluish-purple. In the seventh month, it blossoms with bluish-green flowers, and in the eighth month, it bears black seeds. There is also a variety with white flowers. Some have square, large, purplish-red stems covered in fine hairs, with nodes like those of bamboo, reaching five or six feet in height. The leaves are as large as a palm, pointed and long with edges serrated like a saw. Its roots are pointed and long; they are bluish-white when fresh but turn purplish-black when dried. Fresh roots are moist and oily; a single plant can produce five to seven roots. They are harvested in the third, eighth, and ninth months and dried in the sun; some say they should be steamed before being sun-dried.

It is also known as "Double Tier," "Mysterious Tier," "Deer’s Herb," "Correct Horse," "Salty Mother," and "Upright Herb." <note>original names: 重臺 (Zhongtai), 玄臺 (Xuantai), 鹿 (Lu), 正馬 (Zhengma), 咸 (Xian), 端 (Duan)</note> Its flavor is bitter and salty; its nature is slightly cold and non-toxic. It is used to treat "Head Wind" <note>A traditional term for chronic headaches or dizziness attributed to external pathogens.</note> and heat-toxins. It supplements "deficiency and exhaustion" <term>Xu-lao: a state of physical wasting or chronic fatigue</term>, and treats heart palpitations, irritability, and lack of energy. It is also used for "bone-steaming" <term>Gu-zheng: a sensation of heat radiating from within the bones, often seen in chronic infections</term>, "corpse-transmission" <term>Chuan-shi: a historical term for infectious wasting diseases such as tuberculosis</term>, and "evil qi." It stops forgetfulness and disperses swollen toxins.

# Dang Gui

<note>original: 當歸. *Angelica sinensis*, often called "Female Ginseng" due to its importance in women's health.</note>

Dang Gui grows in the river valleys of Longxi <note>A historical region in modern-day Gansu Province.</note>. Today, it is also found throughout the various prefectures of Sichuan, Shaanxi, as well as in Jiangning Prefecture and Chuzhou. Those harvested from the Sichuan region are considered superior. 

In the spring, the sprouts emerge with green leaves that have three leaflets. In the seventh or eighth month, it blossoms with flowers that resemble those of dill <note>original: 蒔蘿 (shìluó)</note>, possessing a light purple color. The roots are blackish-yellow. One should harvest the roots in the second and eighth months and dry them in the shade.

There are two varieties of sprouts. Both generally resemble Chuanxiong <term>Szechuan Lovage (Ligusticum chuanxiong)</term>, though they differ in the size of their leaves. The stems and stalks are much shorter and lower than those of Chuanxiong.

The roots are also categorized into two types:
*   The large-leaved variety is named **Horse-Tail Dang Gui** <note>original: 馬尾當歸 (mǎwěi dāngguī); referring to roots with long, flowing rootlets.</note>.
*   The fine-leaved variety is named **Silkworm-Head Dang Gui** <note>original: 蠶頭當歸 (cántóu dāngguī); referring to a thick, knobby root head.</note>.

Generally, those with thick, fleshy roots that are not withered are considered the best. Those growing wild in the mountains are called *Bi* <note>original: 薜</note> or *Shanqi* <note>original: 山蘄</note>. Those found on level ground are called *Qin* <note>original: 芹; a term often used for celery or water dropwort</note>. The large, coarse ones found in the mountains are specifically called Dang Gui or *Gan Gui* <note>original: 乾歸</note>.

**Medicinal Properties:**
The flavor is sweet and pungent; its nature is greatly warm and non-toxic. It is used to treat:
*   All "wind" disorders <note>In traditional medicine, "wind" refers to conditions ranging from the common cold to tremors or numbness.</note>.
*   All blood-related conditions.
*   All forms of physical exhaustion or "taxation" <note>original: 勞 (láo); refers to chronic fatigue or depletion of vital energy.</note>.
*   It breaks up "evil" blood <term>stagnant or bruised blood</term> and nourishes new blood.
*   It is the primary treatment for abdominal masses <term>Zhengpi; hard masses or chronic digestive obstructions</term>.

# Ephedra
<note>original: 麻黃 (máhuáng). Known as one of the most important herbs in the Chinese pharmacopeia, used primarily to induce sweating and treat respiratory conditions.</note>

Ephedra grows in the lands of Jin <note>the region of modern-day Shanxi province</note> and Hedong <note>literally "East of the River," referring to the area east of the Yellow River</note>. Today, it is found in many places near the capital. Those plants found in Xingyang and Zhongmu <note>cities in modern Henan province</note> are considered superior.

The sprouts emerge in spring. By the fifth month of summer, they reach a height of about one foot. Yellow flowers bloom at the tips of the stems, and the plant bears fruit resembling lily petals but smaller. The fruit also looks like the seeds of the soap pod tree. The flavor is sweet with a slight scent of ephedra. The fruit has a red outer skin and a black kernel inside. The root is a purplish-red color.

Common lore states there are two types, male and female. The female plant blooms during the first and fourth months and bears fruit in the sixth month. The male plant has no flowers and does not fruit. The stems are harvested after the Start of Autumn <note>Liqiu, one of the 24 solar terms, usually occurring in early August</note> and are dried in the shade.

For current medicinal use, it is also known by the names *Beixiang* <note>Humble Minister</note>, *Longsha* <note>Dragon Sand</note>, and *Beiyan* <note>Humble Salt</note>. The flavor is bitter, the nature is warm, and it is non-toxic. It acts to open the nine orifices <note>the eyes, ears, nostrils, mouth, and two lower orifices</note>, regulates the blood vessels, and opens the pores of the skin. It expels wind <note>a "pathogen" in traditional medicine associated with sudden onset illnesses like the common cold</note>, breaks down <term>Zhengpi</term> <note>chronic abdominal masses or hardened accumulations</note>, and expels pathogenic qi from the five viscera. It is used to reduce fever and protect against <term>mountain miasma</term> <note>toxic vapors traditionally believed to cause illness in damp, mountainous regions</note>.

## Fritillaria
<note>original: 貝母 (Beimu). The name literally translates to "Mother of Shells," referring to the white, scaly bulbs of the plant which resemble small cowrie shells.</note>

**Fritillaria** grows in the lands of Jin <note>a historical region primarily in modern Shanxi province</note>. Today, it is also found throughout the prefectures of Hezhong, Jiangling, Ying, Shou, Sui, Fu, Gong, Run, and Chu. The root has segments that are yellowish-white and resemble a cluster of small cowrie shells; for this reason, it is named **Shell Mother**. 

In the second month of the year, the plant sprouts. Its stems are slender and green, and its leaves are also green, resembling those of buckwheat. These leaves emerge alongside the sprout. In the seventh month, it produces bluish-green flowers shaped like a "drum flower" <note>likely referring to the bell-shaped flowers of the bindweed or morning glory</note>. In the eighth month, the roots are harvested and dried in the sun.

There are several varieties of this plant. The *Odes of Bei* <note>a section of the *Classic of Poetry*, the oldest existing collection of Chinese poetry</note> mentions gathering the *Qi* <gloss>pronounced "zha"</gloss>, which the commentator Lu Ji <note>261–303 CE; a prominent scholar and writer</note> explains is actually the Fritillaria. He notes that its leaves are like those of the **Snake Gourd** <term>original: 栝樓 (Gualou); Trichosanthes kirilowii</term> but are finer and smaller. The "offspring" bulbs are found beneath the main root and look like small taro tubers; they are pure white and are joined together on four sides with distinct segments. The varieties found in nearby regions today are exactly like this.

Guo Pu <note>276–324 CE; a famous scholar and commentator</note>, in his annotations to the *Erya* <note>the first Chinese dictionary, dating back to the 3rd century BCE</note>, states that it has white flowers and leaves like chives, though this particular variety is rarely seen. This medicine is also used to treat poisoning from parasites. Its alternative names include **Hollow Herb**, **Medicine Fruit**, **Bitter Flower**, and **Bitter Vegetable**. <gloss>Another name is Business Herb, and another is Diligent Mother. Its flavor is semi-bitter, its nature is slightly cold, and it is non-toxic. It dissolves abdominal masses, eases palpitations and fears of the heart, stops coughing, and treats malignant sores.</gloss>

# Dahurian Angelica
<note>original: 白芷 (Baizhi). Known scientifically as *Angelica dahurica*, this root is prized in traditional medicine for its fragrance and its ability to treat "wind" related ailments.</note>

*   **Fragrance** <note>original: 芳香 (Fangxiang)</note>

## Dog’s Spine

## Page Thirty-Three

**Shilongrui** <note>original: 石龍芮 (Shilongrui); *Ranunculus sceleratus*, commonly known as Celery-leaved Buttercup or Cursed Crowfoot.</note> grows along the banks of rivers and marshes near the stones of **Mount Tai** <note>original: 泰山 (Taishan); the most sacred of the Five Great Mountains of China, located in Shandong.</note>. **Tao Yinju** <note>original: 陶隱居 (Tao Yinju); the pseudonym of Tao Hongjing (456–536 AD), a renowned Daoist physician and alchemist who organized the Materia Medica.</note> says that it can be found everywhere along the common roads, though nowadays it is only said to come from **Yanzhou** <note>original: 兗州; a historical prefecture in what is now western Shandong province.</note>.

It grows in clusters of several stems, which are a bluish-purple color. Each stem bears three leaves; these leaves are fuzzy, short, and small, with many deep notches or serrations. Its seeds are like those of the **Tingli** <note>original: 葶藶; *Descurainia sophia* or *Lepidium apetalum*, common medicinal seeds in the mustard family.</note> but are yellow in color. The seeds should be gathered in the fifth month, while the skin should be harvested in the second and eighth months and dried in the shade for use. It is capable of expelling various **Wind** <note>In traditional Chinese medicine, "Wind" (feng) refers to a category of pathogens that cause symptoms like dizziness, tremors, or wandering pains.</note> conditions and removing heat and dryness from the Heart.

**Su Gong** <note>original: 蘇恭; a Tang Dynasty scholar and lead editor of the *Revised Materia Medica* (Xinxiu Bencao).</note> says: Its common name is **Shuidong** <note>original: 水董 (Shuidong); literally "Water Dong."</note>. Its sprouts are like those of **Aconite** <note>original: 附子 (Fuzi); the daughter root of *Aconitum carmichaelii*.</note> and its fruit is like a **Mulberry** <note>original: 桑椹 (Sangshen).</note>. It grows in low-lying, damp areas. However, this [description by Su] refers to the *Shuidong* plant and is not the true **Shilongrui**.

This plant is also known by the names **Luguneng**, **Dizhen** <note>original: 地椹; "Earth Mulberry."</note>, **Shineng**, **Penggen**, and **Tiandou** <note>original: 天豆; "Heavenly Bean."</note>. It is bitter in taste and non-toxic. It can expel various Wind ailments and primarily serves to remove heat and dryness from the Heart.

# Cogon Grass Root

Cogon Grass Root <note>original: 茅根 (Maogen); *Imperata cylindrica*</note> grows in the valleys and fields of the Chu region <note>referring to the historical region encompassing modern Hubei and Hunan provinces</note>; today it is found everywhere. 

In spring, the sprouts spread across the ground like needles; popularly they are called **Cogon Needles** <note>original: 茅針 (maozhen)</note>. These are edible and are considered very beneficial for children. In summer, the plant produces white flowers that are soft and fuzzy in appearance, which then wither by autumn. Its roots are exceptionally pure white and are also quite sweet and delicious. The roots are harvested for medicinal use in the sixth month <note>of the lunar calendar</note>.

People today take the **Cogon Needles**, pressing them to apply to <term>metal wounds<gloss>wounds caused by blades, swords, or other metal weapons</gloss></term> or using them to plug <term>nasal floods<gloss>severe nosebleeds</gloss></term>. They are remarkably effective for sudden <term>blood in the stool<gloss>hematochezia</gloss></term> and <term>bloody urine<gloss>hematuria</gloss></term>. 

There is also the **Tube Grass** <note>original: 管 (guan)</note>, which belongs to the same category as Cogon grass. It is also known by the following names:
*   **Orchid Root** <note>Langen</note>
*   **Madder-like Root** <note>Rugen</note>
*   **Earth Tube** <note>Diguan</note>
*   **Earth Sinew** <note>Dijin</note>
*   **Jiandu**

Its flavor is sweet, its nature is cold, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to clear the **blood vessels** and treat <term>urinary difficulty<gloss>painful, dripping, or hesitant urination</gloss></term>. It also treats wounds from knives or arrows, stopping bleeding and relieving pain. The root is primarily used to treat **irregular menstruation** in women.
