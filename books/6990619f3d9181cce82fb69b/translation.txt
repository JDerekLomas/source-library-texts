...part of them, they were afflicted in the same way as the others for a period of two years.

On Friday, February 13, 1590—the eve of Saint Valentine’s Day and the thirty-second year of Queen Elizabeth's reign—Gilbert Pickering of Titchmarsh Grove, an Esquire in the County of Northampton, visited Warboys. Being the uncle of the children and having heard how strangely they were tormented and troubled, he went both to see them and to comfort their parents. Arriving at the house where they lived, he found them all at that moment as healthy as any children could be. 

About half an hour later, Mr. Pickering was informed that a Mistress Andley, a Mr. Whittle of Saint Ives, and others had gone to the house of Mother Samuel <note>Mother Samuel was the nickname for Alice Samuel, the primary woman accused in the Warboys witch trials</note> to persuade her to come visit the children. Because they were gone a long time, it seemed to Mr. Pickering that they were unable to bring her, even though Mother Samuel had often said she would visit the children whenever it pleased their parents to send for her. She had previously claimed she would even risk her life in water up to her chin or lose <note>original: "leese"; to lose</note> some of her best blood to do them any good. 

But now, it seemed, her mind was completely changed. The suspected reason was that all the children (as previously mentioned) cried out against her during their fits, saying that she had bewitched them. She also feared that the common practice of <term>scratching</term> <note>Scratching was a folk belief that a victim of witchcraft could break a spell by scratching the suspected witch until they drew blood</note> would be used on her. In reality, nothing could have been further from their intentions at that time; both the parents and Mr. Pickering had sought advice from respected clergymen <note>original: "good Diuines"</note> regarding the illegality of such a practice. 

Therefore, Mr. Pickering went to Mother Samuel’s house, both to see her and to persuade her that if she were the cause of the children's trouble, the matter might be corrected. Arriving at the house, he found Mr. Whittle, Mistress Andley, and the others still trying to persuade her to visit the children, but she loudly and utterly refused to go.

Upon this, Mr. Pickering told Mother Samuel that he had the authority to bring her, and if she would not go with him willingly of her own accord, he would force and compel her whether she wanted to or not. He then did exactly that, taking her along with her daughter, Agnes Samuel, and a woman named Cicely Burder. All three were suspected of being witches, or at the very least, of being in a conspiracy with Mother Samuel. As they were going toward Mr. Throckmorton’s house, Mr. Whittle, Mistress Andley, and the others walked on ahead.

Mr. Pickering covered Mother Samuel’s hand with his own hand to see what the child would do during this extraordinary fit <note>original: "passion"; at this time, "passion" often referred to a violent physical fit or a state of being overcome by emotion or illness</note>. But the child would not scratch his hand; instead, she felt back and forth upon the bed for that which she missed. If by any means she could manage to touch Mother Samuel’s hand—even with just one of her fingers—she would scratch that hand only and no other. Indeed, sometimes while the said Mr. Pickering covered Mother Samuel’s hand with his own, the child would put one of her fingers between Mr. Pickering’s fingers and scratch Mother Samuel’s hand, which lay underneath <note>original: "nethermost"</note>, while all her other fingers lay still on Mr. Pickering’s hand without moving.

During this fit, if Mother Samuel’s hand was hidden or withdrawn from the child at any time, she would mourn and show clear <note>original: "apparent"</note> signs of distress, as if she had been treated with great discourtesy. Therefore, the Judge recited the charm and commanded him to speak the words; but he refused to do so and said, "I will not speak them." The Judge persuaded him and pleaded with him, so much so that the Judge—the more to encourage the said Samuel—openly spoke the charm himself. Doctor Dorington and others then present also spoke it (at the Judge's request), yet Samuel still refused. Many good and godly prayers were openly made to God, both by the Judge and others, for the child’s relief, at which the child seemed not to be moved at all. 

Then the Judge commanded Samuel to pray to God for the comfort of the child, which he did. However, when he named God or Jesus Christ, the child's head, shoulders, and arms were violently shaken and appeared more troubled than before. Then the Judge said that if he would not speak the words of the charm, the court would hold him guilty of the crimes of which he was accused. And so at last, with much difficulty <note>original: "much adoe"</note>, the said Samuel said in a loud voice in the hearing of everyone present: 

> "As I am a witch and consented to the death of Lady Cromwell, I charge the devil to allow Mistress Jane to come out of her fit right now."

No sooner were these words spoken by the old witch than Mistress Jane, in her usual manner, wiped her eyes and came out of her fit. Then, seeing her father, she knelt down and asked for his blessing, and showed respect to her uncles who stood near her—to whom she had previously shown no sign of recognition. Wondering, she said, "O Lord, father, where am I?" For it seemed she had neither seen nor heard anything before, nor did she know how she had been brought into such a presence. 

Then the Judge said, "You all see she is now well, but not by the music of David's harp," referring to the passage of Scripture where King Saul, being tormented by an evil spirit, received comfort and help when David played his harp before him <note>Reference to 1 Samuel 16:23</note>.

After these confessions were read and delivered to the Jury, it pleased God—for the further proof and downfall of these wicked people—to bring forward more witnesses who were unexpected at that time. These witnesses spoke of things they knew personally and things they had heard from others. 

The first witness was Robert Poulter, the Vicar and Curate of Brampton, who stated publicly that one of his parishioners named John Langley—who was at that very moment lying dangerously ill in his bed—had told him a story. Langley said that one day, while at the Sign of the Crown <note>A well-known inn in Huntingdon.</note> in Huntingdon, he had overheard Mother Samuel. He had warned Master Knowles of Brampton not to give her any food <note>original: "meate"</note> because she was an old witch. 

Immediately afterward, that afternoon, as he traveled from Huntingdon to Brampton on a good horse, the horse suddenly died in the field. Within two days, by God's providence, Langley himself narrowly escaped death two or three times in very dangerous circumstances. Although God did not allow the devil to take control of his body at that time, he soon after lost many cattle—judged by everyone to be healthy and sound—which were valued at twenty marks <note>A mark was a unit of currency worth 13 shillings and 4 pence. Twenty marks was a significant sum, roughly £13 6s 8d.</note>. He himself, not long after, was physically afflicted in an extraordinary way. And on the very night of the day of the Assizes <note>A periodic court session held in English counties.</note>, it later proved true that this same John Langley died.

On the following Friday, the tenth day after those encounters, one of his two-year-old animals died. On the next Sunday, another animal of the same group and age also died. ...the bewitching of the said cattle, in the manner and form as has been declared.

Then the Jailer of Huntingdon gave this evidence: that one of his men, finding Mother Samuel very unruly while she was a prisoner, had at one time chained her to a bedpost. For this reason, not long after, his man fell sick and was in all respects afflicted <note>original: "handled"</note> just as the previously mentioned children were: with the heaving up and down of his body, and the shaking of his arms, legs, and head. He had more strength during his fits than any two men combined, and he cried out against Mother Samuel, saying that she was bewitching him. Remaining in this extraordinary state of sickness, he died about five or six days later.

The said Jailer also said that, not long after the death of his servant, one of his sons fell sick and was for the most part afflicted just as his servant had been. It was so apparent that he was bewitched that the Jailer went into the prison and brought Mother Samuel to his son’s bedside. He held her there until his son had scratched her, whereupon his son immediately recovered <note>original: "amended." In witchcraft lore, "scratching" a witch to draw blood was believed to break their spell over the victim.</note>.

To bring the matter to a conclusion, the Jury of life and death, in the afternoon, found all the indictments to be true bills <note>original: "Billa vera"—a legal term meaning the grand jury found enough evidence for a trial.</note>. When old Father Samuel heard this, he said to his wife in the hearing of many: "May a plague from God fall upon you! For you are the one who has brought us all to this, and we can thank you for it."

Then the Judge proceeded to sentencing and asked old Father Samuel what he had to say for himself, and why a judgment of death should not be pronounced against him. He answered that he had nothing to say, except "the Lord have mercy on me." 

Then the Judge asked old Mother Samuel what she had to say for herself to delay <note>original: "stay"</note> judgment. She answered that she was pregnant. This caused everyone present to burst into laughter—and she laughed more than any of them, because she thought that, for this reason, no judgment would be given <note>Under English law, a pregnant woman could "plead her belly" to postpone execution until after the child was born.</note>. She was nearly eighty years old, so the Judge urged her to abandon that answer. However, she would not be persuaded to give it up until, at last, a jury of women <note>A "Jury of Matrons" was specifically gathered to physically examine a female defendant claiming pregnancy.</note> was empaneled and sworn to examine <note>original: "search"</note> her.
