### ON THE PROMETHEUS BOUND.

<note>The following Greek verse is from Hesiod's *Theogony*, line 522</note>
...with painful bonds, having driven them through the middle of a pillar.
<note>original: "δεσμοῖς ἀργαλέοισι, μέσον διὰ κίον' ἐλάσσας."</note>

It appears that [the author] interpreted these words in such a way that he thought a wedge was driven through the middle of Prometheus himself. He repeated this idea in his lost play *Prometheus Unbound*, as is known from these verses translated into Latin by Cicero:
> *Inserting these wedges with cruel craftsmanship,*
> *he broke through my limbs; by this wretched skill*
> *I am pierced through and inhabit this fortress of the Furies.*
<note>Cicero translated fragments of Aeschylus into Latin in his work *Tusculan Disputations*.</note>

But Hesiod intended his words to be understood as "having driven the bonds through the middle of the pillar" <note>original: "τὰ δεσμὰ διὰ μέσον κίονα ἐλάσσας"</note>—meaning that the manacles were fixed into the middle of the column to which Prometheus was bound. As for why no mention was made of blood flowing from the wound, Schütz <note>Christian Gottfried Schütz (1747–1832), a noted German classical scholar</note> thought the reason was that, according to Homer (*Iliad* V. 340), the gods have *ichor* <note>The ethereal fluid that flows in the veins of the gods instead of blood</note> instead of blood. In my view, there was simply no reason here to add anything about blood. However, I would not go so far as to claim that no blood was shown to the eyes of the spectators. Certainly, it is mentioned in *Prometheus Unbound*, where Prometheus says:
->fawning upon our blood with its feathered tail.<-
<note>Referring to the eagle that preyed upon Prometheus's liver.</note>

The common reading indeed provides a very elegant <term>trimeter: A line of poetry consisting of three metrical units, common in Greek drama.</term>:
->to the gods, except Zeus at least; but he always secretly...<-

Furthermore, in Parisinus B, there is a note: <gloss>read "one," namely "Zeus."</gloss> But if it can hardly be doubted that "Zeus" was inserted into the verse from an interpretation, it appears one must write **except for one at least** <note>original: δίχα γοῦν ἑνός</note>. Thus in *Agamemnon* line 1386: "taught late, you shall **at least** learn wisdom." Sophocles in *Oedipus Rex* 408: "Even if you are a tyrant, the right to **at least** speak back equally must be made equal." And he uses it thus quite often.
