# Introduction.

->§. 1.<-

Anyone who observes the growth of plants even to a limited extent will easily notice that certain external parts of them sometimes transform and transition into the shape of the adjacent parts, either entirely or to a greater or lesser degree. <note>This introduces the concept of metamorphosis, a central theme in 18th-century natural philosophy.</note>

->§. 2.<-

Thus, for example, a simple flower usually changes into a "double" flower <note>original: "gefüllte"; referring to flowers with extra layers of petals</note> when, instead of the stamens <term>Staubfäden: the pollen-bearing stalks</term> and anthers <term>Staubbeutel: the pollen sacs at the tip of the stamen</term>, petals develop. These petals are either perfectly identical in shape and color to the other leaves of the corolla <term>Krone: the inner envelope of a flower, composed of petals</term>, or they still bear visible signs of their origin.

Now, when we observe that it is possible for the plant to take a step backward in this manner and reverse the order of growth, we are made all the more attentive to the regular path of nature; thus, we learn the laws of transformation <note>German: "Umwandlung"</note> by which nature produces one part through another and presents the most varied forms through the modification of a single organ.

->§. 4.<-

This effect, through which one and the same organ appears to us in manifold changes, has been called the *Metamorphosis of Plants*.

### §. 5.

This *metamorphosis* <note>The transformation or structural change in plants.</note> reveals itself to us in three *ways*: *regular*, *irregular*, and *accidental*.

### §. 6.

The *regular metamorphosis* may also be called *progressive*: for it is this type which can be observed acting step-by-step from the first seed-leaves <note>The cotyledons, or the first leaves to appear from a germinating seed.</note> to the final formation of the fruit. Through the transformation of one form into another, it ascends—as if upon a spiritual ladder—to that pinnacle of nature: reproduction through two sexes. It is this process which I have observed attentively for several years, and which I undertake to explain in the present essay. For this reason, in the following *demonstration* <note>A formal presentation or proof of scientific principles.</note>, we shall consider the plant only insofar as it is *annual* <term>Einjährig: a plant that completes its life cycle, from germination to the production of seeds, within one growing season and then dies.</term>, and progresses irresistibly forward from the seed-grain toward fertilization.

### §. 7.

We could also call irregular metamorphosis the **retrogressive** metamorphosis. ## On the Seed-Leaves.

### §. 10.

**A**s we have undertaken to observe the successive stages of plant growth, we direct our attention to the plant at the very moment it develops from the seed. At this stage <note>Goethe uses the word "epoch" to describe a distinct phase of biological development</note>, we can easily and precisely recognize the parts which belong to it directly. The plant leaves its husks more or less behind in the earth—which we shall not investigate at this time—and in many cases, once the root has fastened itself into the soil, it brings forth into the light the first organs of its upward growth, which were already present, hidden beneath the seed-coat <term>seed-coat: the protective outer layer of a seed, also known as the testa</term>.

### §. 16.

The **cotyledons** <note>The "seed leaves" or the first leaves to appear from a germinating seed.</note> are usually doubled, and we find a remark to be made here which will seem even more important to us in the future. Namely, the leaves of this first **node** <note>The point on a stem where leaves emerge.</note> are often *paired* even when the subsequent leaves of the stem stand *alternately*; thus, we see here a convergence and union of parts which Nature subsequently separates and moves away from one another. It is even more remarkable when the cotyledons appear as many small leaves gathered around a single axis, and the stem, developing gradually from their center, produces the following leaves individually around itself. This case can be observed very precisely in the growth of **pine species** <note>original: "Pinusarten"</note>. Here, a wreath of needles forms, as it were, a **calyx** <note>The protective outer circle of a flower; here used metaphorically to describe the whorl of needles.</note>, and we shall have to remember the present case in the future when encountering similar phenomena.

### §. 20.

Yet the further development spreads irresistibly from node to node through the leaf, as its midrib <note>original: "Mittelrippe"</note> lengthens and the secondary ribs arising from it extend more or less toward the sides. These varying proportions of the ribs to one another are the primary cause of the manifold leaf shapes. In a sequence of several leaves, the midrib pushes forward, the fan-like simple leaf is torn and divided, and a highly complex leaf, rivaling a branch, is developed.

### §. 82.

### §. 113.

If we consider a plant insofar as it manifests its **vital force**, we see this happen in a twofold manner: first, through *growth*, as it produces stems and leaves; and second, through *reproduction*, which is completed in the structure of the flower and fruit. If we examine growth more closely, we see that as the plant continues from node to node, from leaf to leaf, as it buds, a reproduction is likewise occurring. This differs from reproduction through flower and fruit—which happens *all at once*—in that it is *successive*, revealing itself in a sequence of individual developments. This budding force, manifesting itself little by little, is most closely related to that which develops a great reproduction all at once. Under different circumstances, one can compel a plant to *bud* continually; conversely, one can accelerate the flowering process. The former happens when cruder sap <note>original: "rohere Säfte"; referring to the nutrient-rich fluids drawn from the soil</note> flows into the plant in greater measure; the latter occurs when the more spiritual <note>original: "geistigeren"; in this context, it refers to refined or "essential" forces rather than supernatural ones</note> forces predominate within it.
