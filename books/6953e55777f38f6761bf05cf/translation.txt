# Andreas Vesalius’s Seven Books on the Structure of the Human Body
->Basel. 1543.<-

First edition of this fine book. The woodcuts are by Johan Calcar, a pupil of Titian. <note>Reference to Jacques Charles Brunet’s "Manuel du libraire et de l'amateur de livres" (Manual for the Bookseller and Book-Lover), 1864 edition, Volume 5, page 1151.</note>

Johan Stephan <insert>van</insert> Calcar or Calcar was born circa 1500 in a town of that name in the Duchy of Cleve, and died about 1546. Vasari <note>referring to Giorgio Vasari’s "Lives of the Most Excellent Painters, Sculptors, and Architects," Florence 1857 edition, 10 volumes in octavo, volume 13, page 150</note> speaks of him with high praise as a Fleming <note>a person from Flanders, modern-day Belgium</note> who had so completely caught the Italian style that his works would never have been ascribed to a Fleming. As a painter he was a successful imitator of his master Titian.

His portrait of Andreas Vesalius in this volume is so well painted that I <unclear>could not withhold it a</unclear> place in my "Examples of the Engraved Portraiture of the 16th Century", 1872, in which it appears at page 58.

An original, or at least an old portrait in oils of the <insert>same</insert> Vesalius, agreeing with this print, and ascribed to Titian is in the Hunterian Museum in Glasgow University.

The title page of this volume, representing Vesalius in his lecture-room, is also a work of very great interest. It is probable that many of the heads, as well as that of the Professor, are portraits. With the exception of this title, the <term>blocks</term> <note>woodblocks used for printing illustrations</note> appear to have been used again in the edition of the book which was published at Basel, by the same publisher Johannes Oporinus in 1555. The title-block in that edition is evidently a copy, and by a very inferior hand, as may be seen by comparing, in the two, the head of Vesalius; the title-tablet, and the tablet at the foot of the page; the candle-stick and articles on the table near the Professor; the boy climbing on the side; and the man and dog on the other. This latter group has in the later edition, been increased but not improved by the addition of a goat.
