# The Kingdom of Great Ryukyu

->**[Illustration of a Ryukyuan Man]**<-
<note>The accompanying woodcut depicts a man from the Great Ryukyu Kingdom wearing a robe with a geometric lattice pattern. He is barefoot, bearded, and wears circular earrings, reflecting the Ming dynasty's ethnographic view of the islanders' appearance.</note>

The **Kingdom of Great Ryukyu** <note>original: 大琉球國 (Da Liuqiu Guo). Located in the modern-day Okinawa Islands.</note> is situated five hundred *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance; 500 *li* is approximately 150 miles or 250 kilometers.</note> by water to the east of **Jian’an** <note>original: 建安. A historical prefecture in Fujian province, China, which served as a primary point of departure for missions to Ryukyu.</note>. Their King resides among many mountain settlements <note>original: 山峒 (shandong). This can refer to mountain caves or, more likely, fortified mountain villages or districts.</note>.

There are lesser kings known as **Commanders** <term>Budui (部隊): Historically a term for military units, but here used as a title for local chieftains or district leaders under the principal King.</term> who do not provide military aid to one another in times of conflict. They send tribute to the **Imperial Court** <note>original: 國朝 (Guochao). A respectful reference to the reigning Ming Dynasty.</note> at irregular intervals. Both the princes of the kingdom and the sons of their **ministers** <note>original: 陪臣 (peichen). Literally "subordinate officials," referring to the ministers of a vassal state in relation to the Chinese Emperor.</note> all enter the **Imperial Academy** <note>original: 太學 (Taixue). The highest rank of educational institution in traditional China, located in the capital.</note> to engage in study.

<summary>This page describes the Kingdom of Great Ryukyu (Okinawa), noting its geographic location relative to Fujian, its internal political structure of local chieftains, and its tributary relationship with the Ming Dynasty, specifically highlighting the education of Ryukyuan elites in China.</summary>

# The Kingdom of Japan

->**[Illustration of a Japanese man]**<-

The **Kingdom of Japan** <note>original: 日本國 (Ribenguo)</note> is the same as the **Kingdom of Wa** <note>original: 倭國 (Woguo). In China, they are called the **Wokou** <term>Wokou (倭寇): Literally "dwarf pirates." This term refers to the maritime raiders who plagued the coasts of China and Korea. While the term implies Japanese origin, these groups were often diverse, including both Japanese sailors and Chinese outlaws.</term>.

### The Kingdom of Gentlemen

The **Kingdom of Gentlemen** <note>original: 君子國 (Junziguo)</note> is located to the north of **Shebi** <note>original: 奢比 (Shebi). In Chinese mythology, Shebi refers to a deity or a legendary land located in the east; here it serves as a geographic marker for this mythical nation.</note>. 

Its people wear traditional **caps and sashes** <note>original: 衣冠 (Yiguan). This term specifically refers to the formal dress of the Chinese gentry and scholar-officials, signifying a high level of civilization and adherence to ritual.</note> and **carry swords** <note>original: 帶劍 (Daijian)</note> at their waists. Although they eat the flesh of beasts, they are accompanied by **two large tigers** <note>original: 二大虎 (Er dahu)</note> that constantly remain by their side.

These people are naturally **fond of yielding and do not engage in conflict** <note>original: 好讓不爭 (Hao rang bu zheng). "Yielding" or "deferring" is a core Confucian virtue where one puts the needs and status of others before one's own to maintain social harmony.</note>. Because of their profound virtue, they cause even the **tigers and leopards** to understand the principles of **integrity and yielding** <term>Lianrang (廉讓): A compound of "Lian" (integrity/modesty) and "Rang" (yielding/deferring). It describes a person who is incorruptible and avoids self-assertion.</term>.

<summary>This page describes the mythical Kingdom of Gentlemen, a land where the inhabitants embody the highest Confucian virtues of politeness and self-restraint. Their moral influence is so powerful that it civilizes the wild tigers that live alongside them.</summary>

<keywords>Kingdom of Gentlemen, Junziguo, Shebi, Confucian virtue, Ming Dynasty mythology, Sancai Tuhui, Tigers, Integrity, Yielding</keywords>

### The Kingdom of Fusang

The **Kingdom of Fusang** <note>original: 扶桑國 (Fusangguo). A legendary land in the far east. While some modern scholars have playfully speculated on a connection to the Americas, historically it was associated with the ends of the known world or, later, with Japan.</note> is located to the east of the **Kingdom of Greater Han** <note>original: 大漢國 (Dahanguo). In this geographical context, this refers to a semi-mythical region described in early Chinese histories as being located far to the northeast of China, beyond the known borders.</note>. 

The inhabitants construct **houses made of wooden planks** <note>original: 板屋 (Banyu)</note>, and they have **no inner or outer city walls** <note>original: 城郭 (Chengguo). In traditional Chinese thought, the presence of city walls was a hallmark of civilization and defense; their absence here implies a land of peace or a different social order.</note>. 

During the reign of **Emperor Wu of Song** <note>original: 宋武帝 (Song Wudi), who reigned 420–422 CE during the Southern and Northern Dynasties period</note>, a person from **Jibin** <note>original: 罽賓 (Jibin). An ancient kingdom usually identified with the region of Kashmir or Gandhara in Central/South Asia</note> arrived in that country. 

The people of that land **raise deer as cattle** <note>original: 養鹿為牛 (Yang lu wei niu). This is a distinctive cultural marker in Chinese records, noting that the deer served the same domestic purpose as cows in China.</note> and **take their milk** <note>original: 取乳 (Qu ru)</note> for nourishment.

<summary>This page describes the Kingdom of Fusang, a legendary land in the far east, focusing on its architecture and its unique practice of domesticating deer for milk. It cites a historical contact between a traveler from Jibin (Kashmir) and the kingdom during the 5th century.</summary>
