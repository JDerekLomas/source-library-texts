### Wild Crow Toon
<note>original: 野鴉椿 (Yeyachun); scientifically known as *Euscaphis japonica*. The name "Wild Crow Toon" likely refers to the contrast between its red fruit pods and its shiny black, eye-like seeds.</note>

The **Wild Crow Toon** grows upon the hills and mounds of Changsha. It grows in dense clusters, reaching heights of over ten feet. Its green branches feature opposite nodes, from which small branchlets emerge. The leaves are arranged in dense, opposite pairs, resembling those of the **Chinese Toon** <note>original: 椿 (Chun); *Toona sinensis*</note> but are shorter and more rounded; they also resemble **Sandalwood** leaves but possess sharp, fine teeth and sparse veins. The reddish-brown roots extend outward with a slight covering of short, beard-like fibers. 

<gloss>Folk healers</gloss> consider this a medicine for **releasing the exterior** <term>releasing the exterior (達表, dabiao): a traditional medical term for treating the early stages of illness—like a common cold—by stimulating circulation or sweating to push pathogens out of the body</term>. In autumn, it bears red fruit with husks that resemble the **Red Paulownia flower** but are slightly harder. When these husks burst open, the seeds cling to the edges of the shell much like the seeds of the **Chinese Parasol Tree** <note>original: 梧桐子 (Wutongzi); *Firmiana simplex*</note>. Seen from a distance, they look like black seeds stuck onto flower petals. 

According to the *Tang Materia Medica* <note>original: 唐本草 (Tang Bencao); the world's first official pharmacopoeia, commissioned by the Tang Dynasty in 659 CE</note>, the appearance of the **Jiazi Wood** is extremely similar to this plant; it also notes that the seeds are like **pepper seeds** <note>original: 椒目 (Jiaomu); the black seeds of the Sichuan pepper plant</note> located within the petals. Perhaps those "scorched red" parts described in the old texts are actually the flowers? This observation is included here for further investigation.

# Cone-bearing Walnut Tree
<note>original: 化香樹 (Huaxiangshu); scientifically known as *Platycarya strobilacea*. The name literally translates to "Transforming Fragrance Tree," though it is most distinct for its cone-like fruit.</note>

The **Cone-bearing Walnut Tree** grows within the deep mountain ranges of Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Hunan, and Guangdong provinces. It is a substantial tree, reaching heights of twenty to thirty feet. 

The leaves are similar in shape to those of the **Chinese Scholar Tree** <note>original: 槐 (Huai); *Sophora japonica*</note> but come to a more distinct point at the tip. They also bear a resemblance to the foliage of the **Chinese Ash**, featuring fine, serrated teeth along the leaf margins. 

In the spring, the tree produces elongated flowers that appear like clusters of delicate stamens. It bears fruit about an inch long, which looks exactly like miniature **pine cones** <note>original: 松毬 (Songqiu)</note>. These "cones" are yellow-green in color and contain many tiny seeds. 

<gloss>Local herbalists</gloss> gather these seeds to prepare a medicinal wash for **clearing sores and toxins** <term>clearing sores and toxins (洗瘡毒, xi chuangdu): a traditional external treatment where an herbal infusion is used to disinfect and heal skin infections or ulcers</term>. Additionally, the leaves possess a unique cleansing property; when soaked in water, the resulting liquid can be used to **wash away grime** <note>This refers to the natural saponins in the leaves, which allow them to act as a traditional substitute for soap.</note>.

<summary>This page describes the Cone-bearing Walnut Tree (Platycarya strobilacea), detailing its physical resemblance to the ash and scholar trees and its distinctive cone-like fruit. It highlights the tree's dual utility as a medicinal wash for skin ailments and a natural cleansing agent.</summary>

# Cone-bearing Walnut Tree
<note>original: 化香樹 (Huaxiangshu); *Platycarya strobilacea*</note>

The **Cone-bearing Walnut Tree** is found everywhere throughout the province of **Hunan**. It grows to a height of over ten feet. Its leaves slightly resemble those of the **Chinese Toon** <note>original: 椿 (Chun); *Toona sinensis*</note>, featuring rounded teeth along the edges like an **oak leaf**, though they are thinner in substance. 

The tree bears fruit that resembles **pine cones**; the scales are flat and thin, and the seeds reside within these scales, looking similar to **hollyhock seeds**. When one breaks open the cone, it releases a **sharp, powerful fragrance**. Local people gather the fruit specifically to use it for **dyeing materials black**. 

According to the *Supplement to the Materia Medica* <note>original: 本草拾遺 (Bencao Shiyi); an 8th-century medical text by Chen Zangqi</note>, the tree known as the **Bili** <note>original: 必栗</note> has a flavor that is acrid, its nature is warm, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat **"ghostly qi"** <term>ghostly qi (鬼氣, guiqi): a traditional diagnosis for sudden, severe ailments or spiritual disturbances believed to be caused by malevolent influences</term>. It can be boiled for internal consumption or burned as incense to kill insects and parasites. If the leaves are crushed and placed in upstream water, the fish downstream will all float to the surface with their gills exposed <note>This indicates the presence of natural piscicides (fish toxins) in the plant</note>. 

Another name for it is the **Transforming Wood Fragrance** <note>original: 化木香 (Huamuxiang)</note>, or **Zhan Fragrance**. The leaves are like those of the Toon and it grows in high mountains. The wood is suitable for making **book scrolls**, as it prevents **silverfish** <note>original: 白魚 (baiyu); *Lepisma saccharinum*</note> from damaging the paper. 

Furthermore, the *Materia Medica of the Southern Seas* <note>original: 海藥本草 (Haiyao Bencao); a 10th-century work focusing on imported and coastal drugs</note> states that it primarily treats **"ghost-infusion"** <term>ghost-infusion (鬼疰, guizhu): a historical term for chronic, wasting diseases or infectious conditions like tuberculosis</term> and pains in the heart region, and it cuts off all "evil qi." If the leaves fall into the water, the fish will die suddenly. 

Upon examining the shape and characteristics of the **Cone-bearing Walnut Tree**, it matches these historical descriptions quite closely, and the names are also very similar. However, the primary modern use of this tree is in **dye shops**, where the cones are soaked and dried in such quantities that they fill overflowing baskets and crates. Since the *Supplement to the Materia Medica* failed to mention its utility as a dye, I have some doubts [as to whether they are identical]. <gloss>Folk doctors</gloss> use this plant as a medicine to **smooth the Qi and disperse phlegm**.

# Native Magnolia
<note>original: 土厚朴 (Tu Houpu); typically referring to *Magnolia officinalis* or similar local species used for their medicinal bark</note>

<note>This entry is authored by **Wu Qijun**, a prominent 19th-century scholar and official who compiled this extensive botanical work to reconcile local plant names with historical medicinal texts.</note>

The **Native Magnolia** is a tree known for its thick, aromatic bark. It is categorized here among the **Woody Plants** <note>original: 木類 (Mu lei)</note> due to its significant height and timber-like qualities.

As depicted in the accompanying illustration, the tree produces large, broad, egg-shaped leaves with prominent veins that branch out toward the edges. These leaves tend to cluster at the ends of the woody stems. While it is related to the official **Magnolia Bark** <note>original: 厚朴 (Houpu); *Magnolia officinalis*</note> frequently cited in the **Materia Medica** <note>original: 本草 (Bencao); a general term for historical Chinese pharmacological texts</note>, the prefix "Native" or "Local" <note>original: 土 (Tu)</note> indicates that this specific variety is sourced from local regions—often the southern or mountainous provinces—rather than the standardized commercial sources.

The tree is highly valued by local herbalists. Like its counterparts, its bark is harvested, dried, and used in decoctions to treat ailments related to the "stagnation of breath" or **qi** <term>qi (氣): in traditional medicine, the vital energy or life force that must flow freely through the body to maintain health</term>. The fragrance of the wood and bark is a defining characteristic used for identification in the field.

## Native Magnolia
<note>original: 土厚朴 (Tu Houpu); specifically refers to a regional variety of Magnolia, likely *Magnolia officinalis* or a closely related species used for its medicinal bark.</note>

The **Native Magnolia** grows in **Jianchang** <note>A historical prefecture in modern-day Jiangxi Province</note>. It is also a large tree. 

The leaves grow in opposite pairs on thick stalks, reaching nearly a foot in length; the undersides and edges of the leaves are white and quite brittle. At the tips of the branches, the young leaves are curled in a shape resembling a **wooden brush** <note>original: 木筆 (mubi); a common name for magnolia buds because their shape resembles the tip of a Chinese calligraphy brush</note>. 

The plant possesses a pungent taste and a fragrant aroma. The local people use it as a substitute for the official **Magnolia Bark** <note>original: 厚朴 (Houpu); the standardized medicinal bark used in Chinese pharmacology</note>, and it is found to be equally effective.

### Embroidery Needle
<note>original: 繡花鍼 (Xiuhuazhen); literally "Embroidery Needle." This name refers to the plant's sharp, needle-like branch tips.</note>

The **Embroidery Needle** is found in both **Jiangxi** and **Hunan**. It is a small tree with slender stems that grow in opposite pairs. The branches are jagged and irregular, and the leaves also grow in opposite pairs attached to the branches.

These leaves resemble those of the **pomegranate** flower but are slightly smaller; the top surface is a deep, rich green, while the underside is a pale bluish-green. They are glossy, smooth, and soft to the touch, with only a single, straight vein running through the center. Leaves near the stem are as small as a fingernail, and those at the very tips of the branches are also quite tiny.

About an inch <term>cun; a traditional unit of length, roughly 3.3 cm</term> from the tip of the branch, there are no leaves at all; instead, the tip is as thin as a needle. In the spring and summer, these tips are soft, but as the plant ages in the autumn, they become hard and sharp.

In **Jiangxi**, some people call this plant "Bird-Doesn't-Step" <note>original: 雀不踏 (Quebuta); a folk name indicating that the thorns are so sharp even a bird would not dare perch there.</note>. Folk doctors use it as a medicine to supplement *Qi* <term>the vital life force or energy in traditional Chinese medicine</term> and blood.

The *Compendium of Materia Medica* <note>The famous 16th-century pharmaceutical encyclopedia by Li Shizhen.</note> records "Magpie-Doesn't-Step" <note>original: 鵲不踏 (Quebuta)</note> as an alternative name for the **Angelica Tree** <note>original: 楤木 (Songmu); Aralia elata.</note>. However, it is not recognized that in the South, any tree or herb that possesses thorns is commonly called "Bird-Doesn't-Step." Therefore, this cannot be used as a definitive or fixed name for the plant.

To prepare them, gather the flowers and scald <term>zha; to quickly boil or blanch in hot water</term> them until cooked. # Red Medicine-Seed
<note>original: 赤藥子 (Chiyaozi). This plant is likely *Casearia glomerata*, a species of tree found in southern China, though historical identifications vary.</note>

The tree reaches a height of one to three <term>zhang</term> <note>A traditional unit of length; one zhang was roughly 3.2 meters or 10.5 feet during the Qing dynasty, making this tree about 10–30 feet tall</note>. They are yellow-green in color with light ochre veins and have horizontal wrinkles across the surface.

In winter, the tree bears fruit. Initially, the berries look like those of the Sichuan pepper <note>original: 椒 (jiao)</note> but are smaller, gathering in dense, fragmented clusters. ## Purple Bauhinia

The **Purple Bauhinia** <note>original: 紫荆花 (Zijinhua). Here, the author describes a variety found in Changsha, likely referring to the Chinese Redbud (*Cercis chinensis*).</note> grows among the hills and slopes of Changsha. *   Carbuncles and abscesses <note>original: 癰疽 (yongju)</note>
*   Throat blockages
*   "Flying corpse" syndromes <note>original: 飛尸 (feishi); a traditional term for sudden, severe ailments believed to be caused by malevolent influences or infectious pathogens.</note>
*   *Gu* poison <note>original: 蠱毒 (gudu); a legendary form of venomous magic or parasites.</note>
*   Toxic swellings and chronic sores
*   Bites from serpents, vipers, and rabid dogs.

For these conditions, one should boil the plant to make a juice for internal consumption. The juice can also be used to wash sores and swellings to remove stagnant blood and promote the growth of new skin. Another name for it is the "Purple Thorn Tree." It resembles the yellow chaste-tree, but its leaves are smaller and lack notches or lobes. It is **not** the "Thorn of the Tian Family." <note>This refers to a famous legend where a Redbud tree withered when three brothers (the Tian family) decided to split their inheritance and cut the tree down, only to revive when they resolved to stay together.</note> By autumn, the seeds ripen; they are a true purple and round like small pearls. It grows among the forests and marshes of the Jiangdong region. Its shape is extremely similar [to the Redbud], and the conditions it treats are the same.

Furthermore, according to the *Supplement to the Dream Pool Essays* <note>original: 補筆談 (Bu Bitan) by the polymath Shen Kuo.</note>, the author claimed that the entry for "Purple Bauhinia" in the *Supplement to the Materia Medica* was an error. The original book already clearly stated that this was "not the Tian family's thorn," making the distinction quite plain.

## Chinese Fringe Flower <term>Jihua (*Loropetalum chinense*)</term>

The Chinese Fringe Flower is also called the **Paper Shred Flower** <note>original: *Zhimohua* (紙末花)</note>. It is found in abundance on the hills and ridges of Jiangxi and Hunan. It grows in clusters with slender stems. Its leaves resemble those of the elm, but are smaller, thick, rough to the touch, and lack serrated edges. In spring, it opens into delicate white flowers, each over an inch long, appearing like strips of shredded white paper. Each blossom consists of dozens of these strands, which hang down in a cascading tangle. Wherever the **Mountain-Glowing Red** <note>The *Yingshanhong* or Azalea (*Rhododendron simsii*)</note> is found, this plant is also present. The red and the white dazzle alongside one another, as intense as fire and as white as rush-flowers <note>The author uses the idiom *ruhuo rutu* (如火如荼), literally "like fire and like rushes," to describe the striking visual contrast between the red azaleas and white fringe flowers.</note>. If its leaves are chewed into a pulp and applied to cuts or puncture wounds, they can **stop bleeding**. The *Gazetteer of Poyang County* records the name as *Ji* (檵), though the original source for this is unknown. The local pronunciation is *Jiji*, while the name "Paper Shred" is given because of its physical shape.

## Water-Watching Sandalwood
<note>original: *Wangshuitan*. As noted previously, while "Sandalwood" (*tan*) appears in the name, this refers to the quality of the wood rather than the fragrant *Santalum* species.</note>

**Water-Watching Sandalwood** grows on **Mount Lu** <note>A mountain in Jiangxi Province, famous for its mist and botanical diversity.</note>. This is simply part of the <term>laws of nature</term> <note>original: *wuli*. Hundreds of these pods are clustered together to form a spherical shape, which is approximately the size of **lotus seeds** <note>the text compares the clustered fruit head to the seed pod of an aquatic lotus</note>.

**Vocabulary and Terms**

*   **Dry Lotus** (*Han Lian*): A name given to this land-based tree because its fruit clusters resemble the seed-heads of the water-dwelling lotus.
*   **Illustrated Investigation of Plant Names and Realities** (*Zhiwu Mingshi Tukao*): A monumental botanical work compiled by the Qing Dynasty official Wu Qijun, published posthumously in 1848.
*   **Nanchang**: A major city where the author observed these specimens.
*   **Paper Mulberry Leaves** (*Chu ye*): Used here as a botanical reference point for the size and texture of the leaves.
*   **Cylindrical Capsules** (*Tong zi*): A descriptive term for the elongated, tube-like shape of the individual fruits.
