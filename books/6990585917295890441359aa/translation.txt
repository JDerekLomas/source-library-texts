§. 2. All those who have written even somewhat plausibly on this subject establish that sound consists in the air, and that the air is, as it were, its vehicle, by which it is carried in every direction from its source. Indeed, the matter cannot be otherwise, since nothing exists except air that surrounds our ears and is capable of producing a change within them. For although it might be objected that the mechanism of hearing is perhaps arranged in the same way as smell and sight—senses which are excited not by the air itself, but by actual "effluvia" <note>original: "effluviis"; these were thought to be tiny particles or flows emitted by objects</note> sent out from the object—nevertheless, it is demonstrated by means of the air pump <note>original: "antliae pneumaticae"; a device used in 18th-century physics to create a vacuum</note> that if a sound-producing instrument is placed in a space void of air, so that it has absolutely no communication with the air, no sound at all can be perceived, no matter how close you approach. However, as soon as air is allowed to enter, the sound is heard again. From this it follows that the air and the change produced in it by the sound-emitting instrument are the true and immediate cause of sound.

§. 3. To make it clear what this change and modification of the air is that excites the sensation of sound, it is useful to consider a specific case in which sound is produced and to investigate the resulting effect in the air. For this reason, let us look at a stretched string <note>original: "chordam tensam"</note> which, when plucked, emits a sound. By plucking the string, nothing else is produced except a trembling motion <note>original: "motus tremulus"; what we would now call vibration</note>, by which the string wanders very rapidly back and forth beyond its position of rest within its own boundaries. In thicker strings, this motion is easily perceived even by the eyes; in thinner ones, even if it cannot be seen, there is no doubt that it is present. Furthermore, anyone who touches a ringing bell with their hand will feel it trembling all over.

§. 21. Furthermore, I shall not pursue this division of consonances any further, since I am about to introduce another much more suitable and useful division, which is made into **complete** and **incomplete** consonances. I call a consonance **complete** if no sound can be added to it without the consonance itself simultaneously being referred to a higher degree; or, in other words, its exponent becomes more complex. A consonance of this kind is one consisting of the sounds 1 : 2 : 3 : 6, the exponent of which is 6. For if any new sound is added, the exponent will become larger. On the other hand, an **incomplete** consonance is one to which one or more sounds may be added without multiplying the exponent: for example, the exponent of this consonance 1 : 2 : 3 does not become larger even if the sound 6 is added, for which reason I call it incomplete.

§. 22. From the preceding sections, it is understood that any number denoting a simple sound is a divisor <note>A number that divides another without a remainder.</note> of the exponent of the consonance <note>The "exponent" is the least common multiple of the numbers representing the vibration ratios of the sounds in a chord.</note>. Therefore, if all the divisors of an exponent are taken, and as many simple sounds are expressed by them, a **complete consonance** of that exponent will be obtained; for besides these numbers, there will be no other that divides this exponent. Thus, a consonance consisting of the sounds **1 : 2 : 3 : 4 : 6 : 12** will be complete, because these numbers alone are the divisors of the exponent of this consonance, which is 12, and no other number besides these divides the number 12.

§. 27. In the second way, a consonance becomes simpler when the remaining sounds share a common <term>divisor</term>; in this case, they must be divided by this common factor before the <term>exponent</term><note>The "exponent" here refers to a mathematical value used to rank the complexity or "sweetness" of a musical interval.</note> is determined. §. 28. This occurs in the consonance 3:6:8:9:12, which has an exponent of 72.
