# The History of Textual Loss

It is not that books do not **fill the rafters** or lie scattered on the ground; yet, while the books themselves may survive, cases where the individual chapters are lost are numerous. I have often sighed over this and deeply reflected on the reasons why.

In the early Han dynasty, there were no formal records of the ancient classics. The way of observing the heavens is fully encompassed here. When the legendary Emperor Yao commanded the brothers Xi and He <note>In Chinese mythology, Xi and He were the official astronomers and astrologers appointed by Emperor Yao to observe the movements of the sun, moon, and stars to establish a calendar for the people.</note>, he specifically used the phrase "calculate and represent." Thus, the "representation"—the model or instrument—is born from the "calculation"; it is not the representation that creates the calculation. Consider the relationship of weights and scales <term>Quanheng: a metaphor for standards and measures; "Quan" is the weight, "Heng" is the scale's beam</term>. Does one set the weight because of the scale, or set the scale because of the weight? One does not need a gathering of scholars to debate this; the answer is perfectly clear.

Suppose one were to rely solely on instruments while discarding the underlying calculations; this would be like holding the weight but breaking the scale. Even if an armillary sphere <term>Jixuan: a complex ancient astronomical instrument consisting of a system of rings representing the celestial sphere</term> is ingenious and a sundial <term>Gui</term> is precise, if there is no theoretical observation to guide them, of what use are they? ## General Overview

In ancient times, there were eight schools of thought regarding the heavens. The first is called the <term>Celestial Sphere: Huntian; a theory viewing the universe like an egg, with the earth as the yolk and the heavens as the shell</term>, which is what is recorded in Zhang Heng’s *Spiritual Constitution*. The second is called <term>Infinite Space: Xuanye; a theory suggesting the heavens have no physical form and stars float in a vacuum</term>, <note>The OCR reads "Yi Ye" (宜夜), likely a transcription error or variant for "Xuan Ye" (宣夜), the famous school of infinite space.</note> though its lineage of teaching has been lost. The third is called the <term>Canopy Heaven: Gaitian; a theory where the sky is a hemispherical cover over a flat or slightly curved earth</term>, as recorded in the *Gnomon of Zhou*. The fourth is called the <term>Lofty Heaven: Xuantian</term>, as described by Yao Xin. The fifth is called the <term>Vaulted Heaven: Qiongtian</term>, as proposed by Lu Cong. The sixth is called the <term>Fixed Heaven: Antian</term>, as described by Yu Xi. The seventh is called the <term>Square Heaven: Fangtian</term>, argued by Wang Chong. The eighth is called the <term>Four Heavens: Sitian</term>, a fable of the <term>Xianhu: Zoroastrian or Central Asian foreigners</term>. 

Of these, the Celestial Sphere school alone is the most rational. Therefore, <insert>the Tang dynasty astronomer</insert> Li Chunfeng exclusively chose the *Spiritual Constitution* to open his work, the *Divination of the Yi Si Year*. 

The text states: In ancient times, the former kings sought to trace the paths of the heavens to determine the spiritual tracks of the stars. Seeking the thread of the fundamental origin, they first used the model of the celestial sphere as their standard. From this, they corrected their instruments and established measurements, so that the <term>Great Pole: Huangji; the celestial north pole or the ultimate axis of the universe</term> was properly established, and the celestial movements could be properly examined. By establishing these standards and examining these movements, they charted the constant laws of the heavens. Sages have no fixed mind; they develop their understanding based on these observations. Thus, the *Spiritual Constitution* was created. It says: Before the <term>Great Prime: Taisu; the state of primordial matter and the beginning of form</term>, all was dark and clear...
