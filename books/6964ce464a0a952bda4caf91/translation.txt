### PAGE 8

**Latin Inscription (Frontispiece):**
> NARIVS MANLIVS BOETHIVS VIR CLARISSIMVS ET INLVSTRIS EX PRAEFECTVS PRAETORIO PRAEFECTVS VRBIS ET COMES CONSVL ORDINARIVS ET PATRICIVS

**Translation:**
Narius Manlius Boethius, a man most distinguished [*Vir Clarissimus*] and illustrious, former Praetorian Prefect, Prefect of the City, and Count, Ordinary Consul, and Patrician.

**Scholarly Notes:**
*   **NARIVS MANLIVS BOETHIVS:** Proper noun. Historically identifying the father of Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius (the author).
*   **VIR CLARISSIMVS ET INLVSTRIS:** These are formal late-Roman rank designations. *Vir Clarissimus* (v.c.) was the standard title for a member of the senatorial order; *Illustris* was a higher grade of the same.
*   **EX PRAEFECTVS PRAETORIO:** The OCR reads "EX PRAEFECTVS." In classical epigraphy, *ex* denotes a former holder of the office (*ex-prefect*). Note that *praefectus* here retains the nominative case despite the preposition *ex*, a common feature in later Latin titles.
*   **CONSVL ORDINARIVS:** The "Ordinary Consul," referring to those who took office on the Kalends of January and gave their name to the year, as opposed to *suffect* consuls.

### PAGE 9

**Latin Epigraphs (Title Page):**

> *Quantumlibet igitur sæviant mali, sapienti tamen corona non decidet, non arescet.*

**Translation:**
To whatever extent the wicked may therefore rage, the crown of the wise man shall nonetheless not fall, nor shall it wither.

**Scholarly Notes:**
*   **Source:** Boethius, *De Consolatione Philosophiae*, Book IV, Prosa 3. 
*   **sæviant:** Present subjunctive, expressing a concession ("though they may rage").

> *Melioribus animum conformaveris, nihil opus est judice præmium deferente, tu te ipse excellentioribus addidisti ; studium ad pejora deflexeris, extra ne quæsieris ultorem, tu te ipse in deteriora trusisti.*

**Translation:**
[If] you have conformed your mind to better things, there is no need for a judge to bestow a reward; you have, by your own hand, joined yourself to more excellent things. [If] you have turned your zeal toward worse things, seek no avenger from without; you have, by your own hand, thrust yourself into lower conditions.

**Scholarly Notes:**
*   **Source:** Boethius, *De Consolatione Philosophiae*, Book IV, Prosa 4.
*   **conformaveris / deflexeris:** Future perfect indicatives (or perfect subjunctives) used here in a conditional sense.
*   **tu te ipse:** A double emphatic construction ("you yourself [have added] your own self"), emphasizing the Stoic and Neoplatonic concept of self-determined moral status.

**PAGE 17**

[Header: Proem. | Original Page xi]

...attended by a throng of senators, and the acclamations of the multitude. Boethius himself, amid the general applause, delivered the public speech in the King’s honour usual on such occasions. Within a year he was a solitary prisoner at Pavia, stripped of honours, wealth, and friends, with death hanging over him, and a terror worse than death, in the fear lest those dearest to him should be involved in the worst results of his downfall. It is in this situation that the opening of the ‘Consolation of Philosophy’ brings Boethius before us. He represents himself as seated in his prison distraught with grief, indignant at the injustice of his misfortunes, and seeking relief for his melancholy in writing verses descriptive of his condition. Suddenly there appears to him the Divine figure of Philosophy, in the guise of a woman of superhuman dignity and beauty, who by a succession of discourses convinces him of the vanity of regret for the lost gifts of fortune, raises his mind once more to the contemplation of the true good, and makes clear to him the mystery of the world’s moral government.

### Scholarly Notes and Translations of Latin Terms

[1] **Narius Manlius Boethius**: Likely an OCR error or variant for *Nar. Manlius Boethius*. The father of the philosopher was *Flavius Nar. [Nartius] Manlius Boethius*, Consul in 487 A.D.

[2] ***mappa circensis***: Translated literally as "circus cloth." In Roman tradition, this was a white linen cloth dropped by the presiding magistrate (in this case, the Consul) to signal the start of the chariot races.

[3] **Peiper**: Refers to Rudolf Peiper, whose 1871 Teubner edition (*Boetii De consolatione philosophiae libri quinque*) was the standard critical text for late 19th-century scholars.

[4] ***Magister Officiorum***: Translated as "Master of Offices." This was one of the most senior administrative roles in the late Roman and Ostrogothic bureaucracy, acting as a sort of prime minister or head of the civil service and palace guard.

### PAGE 23
**BOOK I.**  
**THE SORROWS OF BOETHIUS.**

**SUMMARY.**

Boethius’ lament (*Song I*). — Ch. I. Philosophy appears to Boethius, banishes the Muses of Poetry, and herself laments (*Song II*) the disordered state of his mind. — Ch. II. Boethius is struck dumb with astonishment. Philosophy wipes away the tears that have clouded his vision. — Ch. III. Boethius recognizes his nurse, Philosophy. In response to his wondering inquiries, she explains her presence and reminds him of the persecutions to which Philosophy has often been subjected by an ignorant world. — Ch. IV. Philosophy commands Boethius to disclose his grief. He recounts the history of his unjust accusation and downfall. He concludes with a prayer (*Song V*) that the moral order in human affairs be restored. — Ch. V. Philosophy admits the justice of his defense, but grieves for the disturbance of his mind. She intends to soothe his spirit with gentle remedies. — Ch. VI. Philosophy examines Boethius' mental state through questioning and identifies three causes of his soul's malady: (1) He has forgotten his true nature; (2) he knows not the end of the universe; (3) he knows not how the world is governed.

**[METRUM I: *Carmina qui quondam studio florente peregi*]**

**BOETHIUS’ COMPLAINT.**

I, who once composed rhythmic measures with flourishing zeal,  
Am now compelled, alas, to begin mournful songs in tears.  
Behold, the Muses, their hair disheveled, dictate what I must write,  
And elegies of true sorrow moisten my face.  
These at least could not be overcome by fear;  
They accompany me as comrades on my journey.  
They were once the glory of my happy and green youth;  
Now they provide solace in the fates of a wretched old man.  
For old age has come unawares, accelerated by evils,  
And grief has commanded its own season to inhabit me.  
Premature white hairs are scattered upon my head,  
And my skin trembles, loose upon an exhausted frame.

### PAGE 28
**[PROSA I: *Haec dum mecum tacitus ipse reputarem*]**

**I.**

While I was silently turning these things over within myself and recording my tearful complaint with the aid of my pen, there appeared to me, standing above my head, a woman of an exceedingly venerable countenance. Her eyes were glowing with fire and keen beyond the usual power of men; her complexion was full of life, her strength inexhaustible, though she was so full of years that she was clearly not believed to be of our age. 

Her stature was of an ambiguous measure. For at one moment she contained herself within the common human height, while at another she seemed to strike the very heavens with the crown of her head; and when she raised her head higher, she even penetrated the sky, frustrating the gaze of those looking upon her. Her garments were woven of the finest threads and indissoluble material, of sophisticated workmanship; these she had woven with her own hands, as I later learned from her own speaking.

**[PROSA I CONTINUED]**

The appearance of these garments was shadowed by a certain neglect and the gloom of antiquity, much like the smoky statues of ancestors. On the lowest hem was read the Greek letter **Π** [Pi], and on the uppermost, the letter **Θ** [Theta] was perceived.* And between the two letters, steps were seen marked out like a ladder, by which there might be an ascent from the lower element to the higher. Yet the hands of certain violent men had torn this same garment and had carried away such fragments as each was able to seize.† In her right hand she carried books, and in her left she bore a scepter. 

When she saw the Muses of Poetry standing near my bed and suggesting words to my weeping, she was moved for a moment, and with eyes flashing sternly, she cried: "Who has permitted these stage-prostitutes to approach this sick man? They possess no remedies for his pains, but rather nourish them with sweet poisons. These are they who kill the fruitful harvest of reason with the sterile thorns of the passions; they accustom the minds of men to disease, they do not liberate them."

**SONG III: THE MISTS DISPELLED**

Then was the night dispelled and the darkness left me; my former sight returned to my eyes. Just as when the swift *Caurus* [Northwest wind] gathers the rain-clouds and the sky stands thick with watery tempests; the sun is hidden and the stars do not yet peer through the night. But if *Boreas* [North wind], sent from the Thracian cavern, strikes the storm and opens the clear day, out shines the sudden light and strikes the wondering eyes with its rays.

**Liber I, Prosa 3 (cont.)**

**Latin Context:**

**II**

For a short time she kept her eyes fixed on the ground, as if withdrawn into the august sanctuary of her mind; then she began thus:
"All the care of mortal men, which is labored at in a multiplicity of pursuits, proceeds by different paths but strives to reach one and the same end: Happiness (*beatitudo*). This is the Good which, once attained, leaves nothing further to be desired. It is the supreme of all goods, containing within itself all that is good; for if anything were lacking to it, it could not be the supreme good, since there would remain something outside it that could be wished for. It is clear, then, that happiness is a state made perfect by the concentration of all goods. Toward this, as we have said, all men strive, though by various paths. For the desire for the...

--- Page 117 ---
...true Good is naturally implanted in the minds of men, but error leads them astray toward false goods. Some, believing it the highest good to lack nothing, labor to abound in riches; others, judging that the Good is that which is most worthy of respect, seek to be honored by their fellow citizens through the acquisition of dignities. Some place the highest good in supreme power; these either wish to rule themselves or seek to attach themselves to those in power. Those who judge glory to be the most excellent thing hasten to spread the fame of their name through the arts of war or peace. Many measure the fruit of the Good by joy and gladness; these believe it most happy to abandon themselves to pleasure. 
There are also those who exchange these ends and causes with one another; for instance, they desire riches for the sake of power and pleasure, or they seek power for the sake of money or fame. It is toward these things, then, and others of this kind, that the aim of human actions and desires is directed—as, for example, nobility and popularity, which seem to provide a certain renown; or a wife and children, which are sought for the sake of delight. As for friendship, its most sacred kind is counted as a matter of virtue, not of fortune; but other kinds are sought for the sake of power or interest. Furthermore, it is evident that bodily goods are related to these same ends; for strength and stature seem to provide power; beauty and swiftness provide celebrity; and health provides pleasure. Through all these, it is clearly only Happiness that is sought. For whatever a man seeks above all else, he judges to be the supreme good. But we have defined the supreme good as Happiness; therefore, the state which each man desires above all others is judged by him to be Happiness.
You have now before you a map, as it were, of human happiness: riches, honors, power, glory, pleasure.

--- Page 119 ---
Now Epicurus, considering these things, concluded that pleasure was the highest good, because all other pursuits seemed to bring some delight to the mind. But I return to the studies of men: the human mind seeks to regain its own proper Good, though its memory is clouded, but like a drunken man, it does not know by what path to return home. For do those seem to err who strive to lack nothing? Certainly not. There is nothing which can so effectively realize happiness as a state fortified with all goods, self-sufficient, and needing nothing from without. Do those err who judge that what is best is also most worthy of reverence? Not at all; for that cannot be mean or contemptible which almost all the efforts of mortals labor to obtain. Is power not to be reckoned among goods? Why, is that to be considered weak or strengthless which is clearly more efficacious than all else? Or is fame to be held of no account? It cannot be ignored...

That these two are different will easily be clear if one considers the efficacy of each. For Providence is the Divine Reason itself, seated in the Supreme Ruler of all, which disposes all things; but Fate is the inherent disposition in moving things, through which Providence binds all things together in their orders. For Providence embraces all things together, however different, however infinite; but Fate sets in motion individual things distributed by places, forms, and times. 

Therefore, this unfolding of temporal order, unified in the foresight of the Divine Mind, is Providence; but that same unity, distributed and unfolded in time, is called Fate. Although these are different, one depends upon the other; for the order of Fate proceeds from the simplicity of Providence. For just as a craftsman, perceiving the form of the thing to be made in his mind, carries out the effect of his work and leads through temporal moments that which he held simply and instantly in his mind—so God disposes all things to be done through Providence, simply and stably, but administers those very things he has disposed through Fate in a manifold and temporal way.

[[vocabulary: fatum (fate), providentia (providence), fati series (order of destiny), artifex (artificer/craftsman)]]

Whether, therefore, Fate is carried out by certain Divine spirits as the ministers of Providence, or by some soul, or by the service of all nature, or by the celestial motions of the stars, or by the power of angels, or by the various cunning of demons, or by any or all of these—the series of Fate is woven. This, at least, is certain: that Providence is the immovable and simple form of things to be done, while Fate is the shifting bond and temporal order of those things which the Divine Simplicity has disposed to be done. 

Thus it is that all things which are subject to Fate are also subject to Providence, to which Fate itself is subordinate. But some things, which are established under Providence, transcend the chain of Fate. These are the things which, fixed stably in proximity to the primary Divinity, are beyond the order of the mobility of Fate. For as among several circles revolving around the same center, the innermost approaches the simplicity of the middle point and acts as a sort of pivot for the others which revolve around it; but the outermost, whirled in a wider circuit, the further it departs from the indivisible center of the point, the more it is spread out through broader spaces—

—while, on the other hand, if anything joins and unites itself to that center, it is gathered into simplicity and ceases to be scattered or to flow away. And if it cleaves to the stability of the Supreme Mind, being beyond movement, it also transcends the necessity of Fate. Therefore, as reasoning is to the intellect, as that which is generated is to that which is, as time is to eternity, as the circle is to the center—so is the moving sequence of Fate to the stable simplicity of Providence. 

That sequence moves the heaven and the stars, tempers the elements among themselves in turn, and transforms them by alternate change. It renews all things that are born and die through the similar progressions of offspring and seeds. This same sequence binds the actions and fortunes of men by an indissoluble bond of causes; and since it proceeds from the origins of immovable Providence, it is necessary that these causes also be immutable.

### BOOK V.

### ON THE COEXISTENCE OF CHANCE, 
### FREE WILL, AND DIVINE 
### FOREKNOWLEDGE.

#### SUMMARY.

[[meta: This summary corresponds to the transition from Book IV to Book V.]]

**CH. I.** Boethius inquires whether anything may be truly attributed to chance (*casus*). Philosophy, following the Peripatetic definition of Aristotle (*Physics* II, 4), explains that chance is not the absence of cause, but the unexpected convergence of independent causes in actions undertaken for a specific purpose. This convergence is governed by an inescapable chain of order proceeding from Providence.—**CH. II.** The question of whether human freedom (*liberum arbitrium*) can exist within this causal chain. Philosophy asserts that every rational nature possesses free choice, for without judgment there is no reason. However, this freedom is greater in divine minds and diminished when entangled in earthly desires.—**CH. III.** The conflict between God’s certain foreknowledge (*praescientia*) and human freedom. Boethius argues that if God sees all things eternally, then all things must happen of necessity, rendering rewards and punishments unjust.

[[vocabulary: Boethius, Aristotle, casus (chance), providentia (providence), liberum arbitrium (free will)]]

--- Page 244 ---
[[original page: 222]]

-> *The Consolation of Philosophy.* <-

If human actions are necessitated by divine foresight, then the soul’s merit is void, and the very act of prayer becomes a futility. God would then appear as the author of both virtue and vice.—**CH. IV.** Philosophy explains that the difficulty lies in the limitation of human reason (*ratio*), which struggles to grasp the nature of divine simplicity. She notes that knowledge of a thing depends not on the nature of the object itself, but on the capacity of the one who knows. Sense, imagination, and reason all perceive the same man differently; intelligence (*intelligentia*) transcends them all.—**CH. V.** A discussion on the hierarchy of cognition. As human reason is superior to the imagination and senses, it should not submit to their limitations. We must strive to rise toward the heights of the supreme intelligence, where the eye of God perceives all things as a present reality.—**CH. VI.** The definition of Eternity (*Aeternitas*): the total, simultaneous, and perfect possession of unending life. God, being eternal, views all temporal changes as if in an ever-present "now." This foreknowledge does not impose necessity upon future events any more than our seeing an event in the present imposes necessity upon its occurrence. A distinction is drawn between *absolute necessity* (e.g., that the sun must rise) and *conditional necessity* (e.g., that a man must be walking if he is perceived to be walking).

[[vocabulary: praescientia, ratio, intelligentia, aeternitas, necessitas (necessity)]]

--- Page 245 ---
[[original page: 223]]

-> *Summary.* <-

Events which occur through the freedom of the will are known to God as necessary in a conditional sense—because they are present to His sight—but remain free in their own nature. Thus, the integrity of the will remains, and the moral order of rewards and punishments is preserved. Man is subject to a profound responsibility, for all things are done before the eyes of a Judge who sees all.

### EPILOGUE.

WITHIN a short time of writing ‘The Consolation of Philosophy,’ Boethius died by a cruel death. As to the manner of his death there is some uncertainty. According to one account, he was cut down by the swords of the soldiers before the very judgment-seat of Theodoric; according to another, a cord was first fastened round his forehead, and tightened till ‘his eyes started’; he was then killed with a club.
