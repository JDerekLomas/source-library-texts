### SKETCH OF LIFE.

Dionysius was born<sup>1</sup> in Athens in 5 A.D., nine years after the birth of Jesus Christ. He was educated first at Athens and then at Heliopolis in Egypt. There, at the age of twenty-five, he witnessed—along with Apollophanes—the miraculous darkness<sup>2</sup> of the sun which occurred at the time of the Crucifixion in 30 A.D.<sup>3</sup> Having returned to Athens, he married a woman of noble birth named Damaris and was elected one of the nine Archons <note>original: "Archons"; the chief magistrates or high-ranking officials of Athens</note> of the city. Having filled the office of Archon without reproach, he was elected a member of the Areopagus, a court of justice consisting of fifty-one citizens of Athens who were distinguished for their birth, wealth, intelligence, and character. 

In 49 A.D.,<sup>4</sup> nineteen years after the Crucifixion and in the ninth year of the reign of Emperor Claudius, St. Paul was brought before the court of the Areopagus as a "proclaimer of strange gods" because he was preaching about Jesus and the Resurrection. The Court of the Areopagus was the guardian of the religion of Athens; it had previously condemned Socrates to death on the grounds that, by teaching that one God was supreme, he dishonored the gods of Greece. The Athenians, however, were so ashamed of their treatment of that great philosopher that no person afterward was subjected to the same fate for the same cause. Hence, St. Paul escaped. 

Nor was this all. Dionysius, along with other illustrious men, stayed close to Paul and embraced the Christian Faith. The primary cause of his conversion was learning from St. Paul that the darkness he had observed in Egypt happened at the same time <note>original: "coeval"</note> as the Crucifixion. He was baptized on October 3rd, 49 A.D., in the forty-fourth year of his age. St. Paul left Athens soon after, accompanied by Dionysius, who was his companion for three years.<sup>6</sup> During this time, Dionysius was prepared for the Diaconate <note>the office of a deacon</note> and formed that intimate friendship with Timothy which lasted until death. Dionysius was present with Hierotheus, St. Paul, St. Peter, St. James, St. John, and other Apostles at the funeral rites of the Blessed Virgin, who died at Jerusalem in 54 A.D.,<sup>7</sup> twenty-four years after the Ascension, in the seventy-second year of her age. # INTRODUCTION.

In his letter to Demophilus, Dionysius suggests that he had admitted Demophilus to the order of the *Therapeutae* <note>original: "Therapeutæ"; a term for an early ascetic Christian community or "servants of God"</note>. It was the priest’s function alone to admit members to that order. Dionysius appears to have been a member of a College of Clergy in Athens, whose duty was to provide instruction to candidates for baptism, to study higher theology, and to share such information as was needed by the Church at large <margin>See the *Celestial Hierarchy*, chapter 13, near the end.</margin>. Dionysius of Corinth says that he took the oversight <note>original: "τὴν ἐπισκοπὴν" (ten episkopen); the office of a bishop or overseer</note> of the Parish of Athens <margin>Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*, chapter 23.</margin>. He also wrote letters to individuals who sought explanations of statements contained in his circulated works. 

He appears to have traveled to other centers. He was absent from Athens when Demophilus was guilty of the act of insubordination which prompted his instructive letter of rebuke <margin>See Letters.</margin>. That same letter alludes to his visit to Crete, where he was the guest of Carpus, who afterwards became Bishop of the Troad <note>See 2 Timothy 4:13</note>. Dionysius was ordained Bishop of Athens by St. Paul, succeeding Hierotheus, who most probably accompanied St. Paul on his first journey to Spain in A.D. 59—the sixth year of Nero’s reign. 

Before his martyrdom, St. Paul sent for Dionysius to come to Rome to take charge of the congregations Paul had planted in Gaul <note>Modern-day France</note>. Dionysius arrived too late to see the Apostle alive, but Clement <note>Pope Clement I</note> confirmed the commission. Dionysius, along with Rusticus, Eleutherius, and others, departed for Gaul. They arrived first at Arles, where Regulus was appointed Bishop to succeed Trophimus <margin>2 Timothy 4:20.</margin>, who had returned to Asia due to illness. 

Dionysius and his two companions ultimately established themselves in *Lutetia Parisiorum* <note>The Roman name for Paris</note>, which was then the center of trade between Britain and the Mediterranean. He remained there as a center of operations until A.D. 96, when he returned to Athens to meet St. John after his release from the island of Patmos. St. John and Dionysius met at Ephesus to "speak face to face," and it is probable that the last chapter was then added to St. John’s Gospel. The tenth letter of Dionysius clearly implies that St. John’s Gospel was already in circulation and had been read by Dionysius. The last chapter was added to correct a prevalent impression in the Church that St. John would not die. 

Dionysius remained in Asia for two years. Before his return to Gaul, St. Timothy died during the reign of Nerva (A.D. 97–98), as affirmed by Polycrates, a second-century writer and Bishop of Ephesus. It is probable that Dionysius revised and completed his works during that time. Upon his return to Gaul, Dionysius presented a copy of the *Divine Names* to Eugenius Marcellus, treating him as a "second Timothy" in pious memory of the first <margin>Volume 2, Antiquarian Edition, page 431.</margin>.

# INTRODUCTION.

Dionysius acquired a commanding influence in Spain, Germany, and Britain (in all the Western regions <note>original Greek: πᾶσι τοῖς Ἑσπερίοις μέρεσι</note>). The Druid priests <margin>pages 335-9</margin> made desperate efforts to thwart him. His influence became known in Imperial Rome. Nearly the whole of the last years of the Emperor Trajan <margin>See Trajan and Hadrian in *Smith's Dictionary of Greek and Roman Biography*</margin> were spent in the East for the subjugation of the Parthian and other kingdoms. In A.D. 115, he left Syria for the Parthian expedition. In August, A.D. 117, he died at Selinus in Cilicia on his return to Rome. 

Hadrian was proclaimed Emperor at Antioch on August 11th, 117. He at once arranged matters in the East and arrived at Rome in 118 A.D., where, by concessions to the Senate and gifts to the people, he consolidated his position. In A.D. 119, he began his tour of the provinces by passing through Gaul on his way to Britain. He then sent the Prefect of Gaul to allure Dionysius from Christianity to Paganism. If the Prefect succeeded, he was to bring Dionysius with honor into the Imperial presence. If he failed, he was to cut off his head. 

Dionysius rejected the Imperial honors and chose the crown of martyrdom. With his two companions—united in life and not divided in death—he joyfully sealed his testimony in his own blood and "departed to Christ" <note>original Latin: *migravit ad Christum*</note> on October 9th, A.D. 119, <margin>page 341</margin> in the seventieth year of his Christian service and in the one hundred and fourteenth year of his natural life. The hill on which he suffered is therefore called Montmartre <note>literally "Mount of the Martyrs"</note>. 

In order to terrorize Dionysius and his companions, Lisbius—the first convert <note>original: "first-fruits"</note> of Gaul—along with his repentant wife, suffered martyrdom a few days previously. Because of this, the noble house of Montmorency <margin>*The History of the House of Montmorency* by M. Desarmeaux, Vol. I, p. 12. Paris, 1764. Antwerp Edition, 2nd Vol., 317.</margin> bears the title "First Christian Baron" <note>original French: *Premier Baron Chretien*</note> as his representatives to this day. It is, perhaps, more than a coincidence that Monseigneur Darboy, the Archbishop of Paris—who had written so nobly and bravely in defense of the writings of Dionysius and had translated them into French—suffered death as Archbishop in the same city, if not on the same mount, on May 24th, 1871.

The Eastern Church, naturally, commemorates the Baptism of Dionysius on the 3rd of October. The Western Church, just as naturally, commemorates his martyrdom on the 9th of the same month.
