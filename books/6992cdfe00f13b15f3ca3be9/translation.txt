# The Eight Principles of the Character *Yong*
<note>The "Eight Principles of the Character *Yong*" (永字八法) is the most famous pedagogical tool in calligraphy. It teaches that by mastering the character for "eternity" (永), a student masters the eight essential strokes required for all other characters.</note>

## The Side (The Dot)
<term>側 (Ce): "Side." In calligraphy, this refers to the **dot** stroke.</term>

The **Side** stroke must not be made with a flat brush; one should slant the brush toward the right to execute it. 
**Question:** Why is this called "Side" rather than simply a "Dot"? 
**The Treatise says:** It is because the <term>brush tip (筆鋒)</term> must look toward the right. By judging the "dangerous" or precarious momentum of the stroke and slanting the brush accordingly, we call it the "Side."
<note>original: 勢險 (shi xian). In calligraphy, "danger" refers to a sense of dynamic tension and precarious balance that gives a stroke life and energy.</note>

## The Bridle (The Horizontal Stroke)
<term>勒 (Le): "Bridle" or "To Rein In." This refers to the **horizontal** stroke.</term>

The **Bridle** stroke must not be made with a "lying" or limp brush. The middle should be slightly raised while the two ends remain lower; one must use the <term>heart of the brush (筆心)</term> to apply pressure. 
**Question:** Why is this called a "Bridle" rather than a "Stroke"? 
**The Treatise says:** A "Bridle" suggests reining in and driving the brush forward. By catching the empty space of the stroke and achieving a sense of <term>astringency (澁)</term>, the effort is successful. 
<note>original: 勁澁 (jin she). "Astringency" or "friction" is a prized quality in calligraphy where the brush feels as if it is meeting resistance from the paper, preventing the stroke from looking slick or oily.</note>

## The Crossbow (The Vertical Stroke)
<term>努 (Nu): "Crossbow" or "Tension." This refers to the **vertical** stroke.</term>

The **Crossbow** stroke must not be perfectly straight; if it is too straight, it will lack power. 
**Question:** A stroke is usually a gathering of ink in the center; why is this called "Tension"? 
**The Treatise says:** "Tension" refers to the momentum of the brush as it moves with a slow, deliberate friction. It should have a subtle, bowed strength—like a crossbow under tension.

## The Kick (The Hook)
<term>趯 (Ti): "Kick." This refers to the **hook** at the end of a stroke.</term>

The **Kick** emerges from the **Crossbow** (vertical) stroke. Using the "slippery" tip of the brush, one pauses slightly with a "thwarting" motion, then borrows the existing momentum to "kick" the brush upward. 
**Question:** In most characters, the exit of the brush tip is called a "Pick" (挑); why is it here called a "Kick"? 
**The Treatise says:** This is because it is the <term>closing stroke (殺筆)</term> following the vertical stroke, moving in a diagonal direction.
<note>original: 殺筆 (sha bi). Literally the "killing stroke," this refers to the decisive, final movement that completes the energy of a specific stroke sequence.</note>

# The Whip
<term>策 (Ce): "Whip." This refers to the **rising** stroke that moves diagonally upward from left to right.</term>

The **Whip** stroke should be high at both ends and lower in the middle; one must lift it using the <term>heart of the brush</term> <note>original: 筆心 (bi xin). The central, longest bristles of the brush that provide the core structure of a stroke.</note>.
**Question:** The **Whip** is sometimes called a "broken stroke" or a "different horizontal." Why is it called a **Whip** here?
**The Treatise says:** The logic of the **Whip** is fundamentally different from a standard horizontal stroke. One must turn the brush upward to reveal the <term>tip</term> <note>original: 鋒 (feng). The very point of the brush, essential for precision and "spirit" in calligraphy.</note>, then lift it lightly as it advances. For this reason, it is called the **Whip**.

# The Slant
<term>掠 (Lue): "Slant" or "To Brush Past." This refers to the **long left-falling** stroke.</term>

To **Slant** means to brush past toward the left. This term is borrowed from the concept of <term>brush momentum</term> <note>original: 筆勢 (bi shi). The dynamic energy and directional flow of the brush as it moves across the paper.</note>.
**Question:** The **Slant** is also known as a "Falling stroke" or "Spreading." Why is it called a **Slant** here?
**The Treatise says:** The **Slant** requires a precise rhythm between slowness and speed. The brush follows the hand’s movement, and the tip naturally emerges from the left. The goal is to achieve a finish that is both forceful and sharp; if the stroke is released without a clear target, the movement lacks purpose. Therefore, the essential technique of the **Slant** must be strictly maintained.

# The Peck
<term>啄 (Zhuo): "Peck." This refers to the **short left-falling** stroke.</term>

The **Peck** is like a bird pecking at an object. One must hold the brush upright and press down; speed is of the essence. It is also referred to as the "Lying Brush."

How could there be no traditional transmission? <note>original: 雲 (Yún), 唐 (Táng). The top "rain" radical of Cloud and the top of the name Tang are often written as an identical horizontal flow; the "transmission" refers to the secret of the final stroke.</note>

# **Treatise on the Method of Cursive Writing**

The various forms of <term>Cursive Script</term> <note>original: 草書 (cǎoshū); a rapid, flowing style of Chinese calligraphy where characters are simplified and strokes are connected.</note> are like people in different states: sitting, lying down, walking, standing, bowing in greeting, yielding, or even quarreling in anger. They resemble one who boards a boat or leaps upon a horse, or the movements of singing, dancing, clapping, and leaping. Every transformation and variation contains its own inherent logic and principle, which must never be ignored.

When first applying the brush, one must use a <term>Hidden Tip</term> <note>original: 藏鋒 (cángfēng); a technique where the brush tip is tucked into the start of the stroke to create a rounded, powerful appearance</note> and rotate the wrist. The movement should be slow at the beginning and fast toward the end. The momentum and shape of the characters should be like **scorpions and snakes** <note>original: 蠆蛇 (chàshé); a metaphor for strokes that are sinuous, fluid, and yet possess a sharp, "stinging" energy</note>, with the internal intent hooked and connected so that the energy is never interrupted. Simplicity and brevity are to be valued above all else; do not prize redundancy or cluttered strokes.

As for the edges, slants, rises, and falls, these are established according to the natural momentum of the brush. Generally, one achieves subtle beauty through the arrangement of spacing and structure. If a stroke is made in error, it must not be traced over or corrected, for fear of losing the original "brush momentum."

If there are dots to be placed, they must be "thrown" down from a distance in the air. Their downward momentum should be like a **rock falling from a high peak**. The application of the brush should be like **releasing an arrow**; an arrow must not be slow, for if it is slow, it will not penetrate the target. Although these methods are applied to the traces of cursive script, one must also constantly incorporate the spirit of <term>Seal Script</term> <note>original: 篆勢 (zhuànshì)</note>, <term>Eight-parts Script</term> <note>original: 八分 (bāfēn); a refined version of Clerical script</note>, <term>Cursive-Clerical</term> <note>original: 章草 (zhāngcǎo); an early, rhythmic form of cursive</note>, and <term>Ancient Clerical Script</term> <note>original: 古隸 (gǔlì)</note>. These various styles should be blended together to stimulate and expand one's creative intent.

## **The Force and Form of Wang Xizhi’s Cursive Script**
<note>Wang Xizhi (303–361 AD) is traditionally celebrated as the "Sage of Calligraphy." This section describes his *Cursive Script Momentum* (Caoshu Shi), a poetic treatise on how the writing should move and feel.</note>

Its speed is like a startled snake losing its path; its advance is like swirling water lingering in a pool. When slow, it moves like a walking goose; when swift, it is as sharp as a magpie's flight. Long strokes are pulled out like a pheasant crouching; dots are like a rabbit leaping. Now pausing, now drawing out, the brush acts according to its own will.

Whether massive or delicate, the writing follows its own form to create wondrous variety. It is like clouds gathering and water dispersing; like the whirling wind and the flash of lightning.
