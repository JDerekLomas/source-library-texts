Master Kong says that the timing of the <term>Five Elements</term> <note>Wuxing (五行): Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water.</note> is like that of the <term>Four Seasons</term>. It is said that by harmonizing with and following the timing of the Five Elements, all tasks and labors will be successfully completed. 

The <term>Liyun</term> <note>original: "禮運"; the "Transmissions of Rites," a chapter in the *Book of Rites*.</note> says that the Five Elements are sown throughout the Four Seasons. Generally, the Four Seasons represent "Energy" <note>Qi (氣)</note>, while the Five Elements represent "Phenomena" <note>Xiang (象); the visible manifestations or forms of that energy</note>. 

The Four Seasons are each divided into 92 days and 25/80ths <note>The OCR reads 92, though mathematically 91 days and 25/80ths (91.3125) is required to equal a solar year of 365.25 days when multiplied by four, as noted on the previous page.</note> to constitute the standard duration of one season. Within this, the Five Elements are assigned: 
* Wood is paired with Spring, 
* Fire is paired with Summer, 
* Metal is paired with Autumn, 
* Water is paired with Winter. 

As for Earth, it flourishes for a portion of all four seasons, specifically for eighteen days and a fraction at the end of each season.

Hu Dan <note>A Song Dynasty scholar</note> says: "On Earth, the Five Elements exist as physical substances; in Heaven, they exist as Time." Because we follow their timing and manage them accordingly: 
* In <term>Mid-Spring</term> <note>Zhongchun (仲春); the second month of the lunar spring</note>, we cut "Yang wood" <note>wood from the sunny side of a mountain or hardy timber</note>.
* In <term>Mid-Summer</term>, we cut "Yin wood" <note>wood from the shaded side or softer timber</note>. 
This is how we manage the timing of Wood. 

In the final month of Spring, we bring out the fires, and in the final month of Autumn, we store the fires; this is how we manage the timing of Fire. 

The <term>Minister of Works</term> <note>Sikong (司空); an ancient official responsible for land management and waterworks</note> inspects the slopes and wetlands to manage the timing of Earth. 

In Autumn, we repair the embankments, and in Spring, we clear the canals; this is how we manage the timing of Water. 

Furthermore, the "Virtue" of Spring resides in Wood; thus, distributing charity and bestowing favors follows the timing of Wood. The remaining elements and seasons follow this same logic.

### Introduction to the Map of the Mountains and Seas

The *World Map of the Mountains and Seas* by the Scholar of the Mountains <note>original: "利山人" (Li Sharen), a respectful name for the Jesuit missionary Matteo Ricci</note> features three outer rings representing the celestial sphere. These rings record the distances across the heavens, the varying lengths of days and nights, and the numerical differences in the <term>gnomon shadows<gloss>the shadow cast by a vertical pole used to measure the sun's altitude to determine seasons and latitude</gloss></term> between winter and summer, as well as a general overview of celestial degrees.

Regarding the regions situated within and beyond the North and South Poles that bind the mid-heavens, there are three or four distinct types of lands: those with shadows, those without shadows, and those with circular shadows <note>This refers to the different ways shadows behave in the tropics (where the sun can be directly overhead, leaving no shadow), the temperate zones, and the polar circles (where the sun may circle the horizon)</note>. These phenomena all describe the relative distance of the sun’s orb. 

These details are published alongside this map and are now compiled into this illustration. They are further detailed within the divisions of the year and the seasonal climates listed under the four seasons.

The central circle of this *Map of the Mountains and Seas* is the terrestrial globe <note>original: "地球" (Diqiu), a term popularized by Ricci to describe the Earth as a sphere</note>. Within it, the Great Earth is contained, encompassing everything. It briefly records the mountains, rivers, local customs, and products of the "ten thousand nations" <note>A traditional Chinese term for all the countries of the world</note>. The world is collectively divided into five major continents <note>Ricci introduced the Chinese to the concept of the five continents: Asia, Europe, Libya (Africa), America, and Magallanica</note>. This map provides a general outline of these lands for study and reference.

# The Course of the Heavenly Han

The Heavenly Han <note>original: "天漢", one of several classical names for the Milky Way</note> arises in the East, passing between the asterisms of the **Winnowing Basket** <term>Ji</term> and the **Tail** <term>Wei</term>. It is called the **Heavenly River** <note>original: "天河"</note> and also the **Ford of Han** <note>original: "漢津"</note>. At this point, it divides into two paths. 

Its southern path passes through [the asterisms of] **Fu Yue** <note>named for a legendary Shang dynasty minister</note>, the **Fish Pool** <note>Yu Yuan</note>, the **Celestial Key** <note>Tian Yue</note>, the **Celestial Market Crown** <note>Tian Bian</note>, and the **River Drum** <note>He Gu</note>. 

Its northern path passes through the **Winnowing Basket** <note>Ji</note>, pierces through the **Turtle** <note>Bie</note>, and descends. Next, it entwines the **Southern Dipper** <note>Nan Dou</note>, the **Legs** <note>Kui</note>, and the **Left Flag** <note>Zuo Qi</note>, reaching the **Celestial Ford** <note>Tianjin</note>, where it descends to merge once more with the southern path. 

The river then travels toward the southwest, dividing again to flank the **Gourd** <note>Pao Gua</note>. It entwines the **Celestial Human** <note>Ren Xing</note>, the **Pestle** <note>Chu</note>, **Zao Fu** <note>a legendary charioteer</note>, the **Flying Serpent** <note>Teng She</note>, **Wang Liang** <note>another master charioteer</note>, the **Auxiliary Road** <note>Fu Lu</note>, and the northern end of the **Flying Gallery** <note>Ge Dao</note>. 

Proceeding further, it moves through the **Great Mausoleum** <note>Da Ling</note>, the **Celestial Boat** <note>Tian Chuan</note>, and the **Rolled Tongue** <note>Juan She</note>. Moving southward, it entwines the **Five Chariots** <note>Wu Che</note>, passes to the south of the **Northern River** <note>Bei He</note>, and enters the **Eastern Well** <note>Dong Jing</note> and the **Water Level** <note>Shui Wei</note>. Continuing its course toward the southeast, it entwines the **Southern River** <note>He Nan</note>, the **Watchtower Mound** <note>Que Qiu</note>, the **Celestial Dog** <note>Tian Gou</note>, the **Celestial Record** <note>Tian Ji</note>, and the **Celestial Millet** <note>Tian Ji</note>. Finally, it vanishes to the south of the **Seven Stars** <note>Qi Xing</note>. 

**Zhang Heng** <note>A renowned polymath and astronomer of the Han Dynasty (78–139 CE)</note> stated: "The Ford of Han is the essence of the element Metal."

### Page 5

The **Canon of Yao** <note>original: "Yao Dian"; a foundational chapter in the *Book of Documents*</note> states: "Calculate and delineate the sun, the moon, and the stars, and so respectfully deliver the seasons to the people." 

The commentary explains: "That which the sun and moon meet is called 'calculation and delineation.' Its specific divisions and markers refer to the sun and moon intersecting and meeting within the **Twelve Stations** <term>Shi'er ci: Twelve equal divisions of the celestial horizontal circle, used to track the positions of the sun, moon, and the planet Jupiter</term>."

The **Commentary of Zuo** <note>original: "Zuo Zhuan"; one of the earliest Chinese narrative histories</note> says: "The meeting of the sun and moon is called a **Celestial Point** <term>Chen: In this astronomical context, it refers specifically to the longitudinal position of the solar-lunar conjunction</term>."

The commentary on the **Monthly Ordinances** <note>original: "Yue Ling"; a ritual calendar and guide to natural cycles found in the *Book of Rites*</note> says: "In the movements of the sun and the moon, there are twelve meetings within a single year." 

This is because the **Celestial Circuit** <term>Zhoutian: The full rotation of the heavens</term> consists of 365 and 1/4 degrees <note>Ancient Chinese astronomy defined a degree (*du*) as the distance the sun travels in one day; thus, the circle was 365.25 degrees rather than 360</note>. The sun travels slowly, passing through one degree every day and completing one full circuit of the heavens in one year. The moon travels quickly, passing through thirteen degrees every day and completing one circuit of the heavens in a single month. It must then travel a further twenty-nine and a half degrees <note>This refers to the extra distance the moon must cover to "catch up" with the sun, which has also moved forward during the month</note> before it finally meets with the sun again.

# The Path of the Sun and the Changing Seasons

The **Middle Path** <term>Zhonglu: The celestial equator, or the sun's path during the equinoxes</term> is situated ninety-one and a half degrees and a fraction away from both the North and South Poles. On the day of the **Spring Equinox** <term>Chunfen: One of the 24 solar terms, marking the moment the sun crosses the celestial equator</term>, the sun travels along this Middle Path. From the Spring Equinox until the **Summer Solstice**, the sun gradually moves toward the **North Path** <term>Beilu: The sun's northernmost track, corresponding to the Tropic of Cancer</term>; the Summer Solstice marks the sun's trajectory at this northern limit. 

From the Summer Solstice until the **Autumn Equinox**, the sun turns back from the North Path toward the Middle Path; the Autumn Equinox marks the sun's trajectory upon returning to the center. From the Autumn Equinox until the **Winter Solstice**, the sun gradually moves toward the **South Path** <term>Nanlu: The sun's southernmost track, corresponding to the Tropic of Capricorn</term>; the Winter Solstice marks the sun's trajectory at this southern limit. From the Winter Solstice back to the Spring Equinox, the sun turns from the South Path and returns once more to the Middle Path.

<note>Traditional Chinese astronomy divided the sky into "paths" or "circuits" to explain the sun's shifting position. The "Middle Path" equates to the Equator, while the North and South paths represent the sun's position at the solstices.</note>

The varying length of the days—long or short—is due to the relative rising and falling of the Earth <note>This refers to the perceived vertical shift of the sun's path relative to the observer's horizon throughout the year</note>. At the Spring and Autumn Equinoxes, the Earth's position is balanced in the center of the heavens. On these days, the sun rises in the **Mao** direction <note>original: 卯; the direction of true East</note> and sets in the **You** direction <note>original: 酉; the direction of true West</note>. Because the sun travels the Middle Path at these times, the lengths of day and night are perfectly equal.

From the Spring Equinox to the Summer Solstice, the "Yang" Sun rises higher while the Earth effectively "descends" <note>In this cosmological model, the sun's path moves "upward" toward the North Pole</note>. During this period, the sun rises in the **Yin** direction <note>original: 寅; East-Northeast</note> and sets in the **Xu** direction <note>original: 戌; West-Northwest</note>. Because the sun is traveling the North Path, the days are at their longest.

Conversely, from the Autumn Equinox to the Winter Solstice, the "Yang" Sun descends while the Earth effectively "rises." During this time, the sun rises in the **Chen** direction <note>original: 辰; East-Southeast</note> and sets in the **Shen** direction <note>original: 申; West-Southwest</note>. Because the sun is traveling the South Path, the days are at their shortest.

<note>The directions mentioned (Mao, You, Yin, Xu, Chen, Shen) are part of the Twelve Earthly Branches, a system used in ancient China to mark both directions on a compass and hours of the day.</note>

## Discussion on Eclipses of the Sun and Moon

In the section titled "Intersection of the Tenth Month" in the *Classic of Poetry*, it is recorded that "there was an eclipse of the Sun." <note>original: "日有食之" (rì yǒu shí zhī). This refers to one of the earliest recorded solar eclipses in Chinese history, dated to 735 BCE.</note> At the end and beginning of the lunar month, the Sun and Moon meet; if they share the same degree of longitude and follow the same latitudinal path, then the Moon covers the Sun, and for this reason, the Sun is eclipsed. <note>The text describes a conjunction during a New Moon (晦朔), where the Moon passes directly between the Earth and the Sun.</note> At the time of the Full Moon, when the Sun and Moon are in opposition, if they share the same degree and the same path, the Moon confronts the Sun and becomes eclipsed.

According to my assessment, the theory of the Moon covering the Sun causing a solar eclipse is easy to understand. However, the theory of the Moon confronting the Sun to cause a lunar eclipse is difficult to explain.

Earlier Confucian scholars claimed that the Sun’s essence is fundamentally Yin at its core. <note>In this context, Yin represents darkness or opacity within the otherwise bright Yang essence of the Sun.</note> Because it is Yin, there is an "obstructed spot" within it. At the Full Moon, when the two bodies are at opposite degrees and paths, the Moon and Sun confront one another. The Moon is then struck by the rays from that "obstructed spot" within the Sun, and thus it is eclipsed. <note>original: "閼處" (è chù). This archaic theory suggested that a dark "core" of the Sun projected a shadow onto the Moon.</note> This was the view of Master Hengqu <note>Referring to the Neo-Confucian philosopher Zhang Zai (1020–1077 CE).</note> and serves as the basis for the standard commentaries on the *Classic of Poetry*. 

Yet, this theory is particularly dubious. The light of the Sun shines outward, leaving no place unilluminated. Even if there were darkness within its interior, that darkness would remain contained inside. How could it possibly project outward to "shoot" the Moon and cause it to lose its brightness?

Only the theory of Zhang Heng <note>A polymath and astronomer of the Han Dynasty (78–139 CE) who correctly identified that the Moon is illuminated by the Sun and that lunar eclipses are caused by the Earth's shadow.</note> seems easy to understand. Heng stated that the point directly opposite the Sun is a void as large as...

## Discussion on Eclipses of the Sun and Moon (Continued)

Where the light of the Sun and Moon does not reach is called the <term>Dark Void</term>. <note>original: "àn xū" (暗虛). Traditional Chinese astronomy used this term to describe the region of darkness directly opposite the Sun where lunar eclipses occur.</note> When the Dark Void encounters the Moon, a lunar eclipse occurs; when it encounters a star, that star vanishes. Currently, <term>astronomers</term> <note>original: "lìjiā" (曆家), literally "calendar experts," who were the professional astronomers of the imperial court.</note> observe that when the Full Moon travels along the <term>Ecliptic</term>, <note>original: "huángdào" (黃道), the apparent path of the Sun across the sky.</note> it encounters this Dark Void. Because the encounter with the Dark Void can be at the edge or the center, and can be shallow or deep, lunar eclipses vary in their direction—occurring to the north or south—and in their magnitude.

In my assessment, the theory of the Dark Void cannot be easily disputed. However, if one says that its size is only as large as the Sun, I fear its size is actually greater than that. Consider that some lunar eclipses last for two or three <term>double-hours</term>. <note>original: "shíchén" (時辰). # The Calculation of the Lunar Cycle

Calendars, as mathematical systems, accumulate fractions to form degrees. However, seeking accuracy over long spans of time easily leads to errors. To understand the essentials of timekeeping, it is better to look to the cycle of the **Dark Moon** and **New Moon** <term>Huishuo</term> <note>original: *huishuo* (晦朔). *Hui* is the last day of the lunar month when the moon is completely dark; *shuo* is the first day of the new month when the sun and moon are in conjunction.</note>. 

The position where the Sun resides is the place where the Moon meets it. Calculating from the day of the New Moon, the Moon travels thirteen and seven-nineteenths degrees every day. By the time it reaches the Dark Moon and meets the Sun again, the positions of the **Quarter** and **Full Moons** <term>Xianwang</term> <note>original: *xianwang* (弦望). Referring to the first/third quarters and the full moon phases.</note> can all be derived through calculation. Even if the result is not perfectly exact, it will not be far off.

The movements of both the Sun and Moon involve **Surplus and Deficit** <term>Yingsuo</term> <note>original: *yingsuo* (盈縮). These terms describe the varying orbital speeds of celestial bodies—specifically acceleration (surplus) and deceleration (deficit) relative to their average motion.</note>. If the Sun is in "surplus" (moving faster) and the Moon is in "deficit" (moving slower), the New Moon conjunction occurs after the mean midpoint. If the Moon is in surplus and the Sun is in deficit, the New Moon occurs before the mean midpoint. Therefore, it is said: "Even if not perfectly precise, it is not far off."

The meetings of the Sun and Moon define the **Twelve Stations** <term>Shi'er Ci</term> <note>original: *shi'er ci* (十二次). These are twelve divisions of the celestial sphere along the equator or ecliptic, used to track the positions of the sun, moon, and Jupiter.</note>. The coordination of these twelve stations is what the twelve New Moons record. Although these twelve cycles of dark and new moons are said to constitute a year, there are always remaining fractions. 

This is because the Sun travels for 365 days and 25 units <term>Ke</term> <note>original: *ke* (刻). A traditional Chinese unit of time where 100 *ke* typically make up one 24-hour day; thus 25 *ke* is exactly 0.25 days.</note> to complete its circuit of the heavens. The Moon, however, travels for 29 days and slightly more than 53 units before it meets the Sun again. In total, twelve cycles of dark and new moons are completed in 354 days and 37 units. In relation to the sun, there are periods of brightness and darkness—the dark moon and the new moon—which resemble the trigram **Kun** <note>the Receptive or Earth, representing total Yin and the absence of light</note>. When the crescent moon first emerges at the position of **Geng** <note>the 7th Celestial Stem</note>, it takes the form of **Zhen** <note>the Arousing or Thunder, symbolizing the first spark of light/Yang</note>. 

**Dui** <note>the Joyous or Lake</note> represents the first quarter moon at the position of **Ding** <note>the 4th Celestial Stem</note>. **Qian** <note>the Creative or Heaven</note> reaches its fullness at the positions of **Jia** and **Ren** <note>the 1st and 9th Celestial Stems, representing the peak of Yang</note>. Then, the moon begins its retreat as **Xun** <note>the Gentle or Wind</note> at the position of **Xin** <note>the 8th Celestial Stem</note>. It diminishes further as **Gen** <note>the Keeping Still or Mountain</note> at the position of **Bing** <note>the 3rd Celestial Stem</note>. Finally, the light of the moon vanishes as **Kun** <note>Earth</note> at the positions of **Yi** and **Gui** <note>the 2nd and 10th Celestial Stems</note>, returning once more to the state of **Kun**. 

This constitutes the cycle of "waxing and waning, growth and decay" <note>original: "xiaoxi yingxu," a fundamental concept in Chinese philosophy describing the rhythmic increase and decrease of Yin and Yang</note> within a single month. Terrestrial methods <note>referring to systems of geomancy or divination</note> follow this logic to calculate the transformations of the <term>Eight Trigrams</term> <note>the foundational symbols of the I Ching</note>. By assigning these to specific positions, they govern the directions of the <term>twelve degrees</term> <note>the divisions of the compass or time based on the Twelve Earthly Branches</note> and pair them with the positions of the <term>Five Elements</term> <note>Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, and Water</note>. All these systems are fundamentally rooted in this lunar progression.
