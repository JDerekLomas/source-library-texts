# Lord Shankara is my Refuge.

---

The astronomical handbook <term>Karana-grantha; a concise astronomical manual used for practical calculations</term> known as the *Laghumanasa*, composed by Munjala, was written in the year 854 <note>likely 854 of the Shaka era, corresponding to 932 CE</note>. We have identified that three manuscripts of this work exist in cities such as Mysore, Trivandrum, Cochin, Tirupati, Vizagapatam, and Varanasi. The late Pandit Sudhakara Dvivedi <note>a renowned 19th-century mathematician and astronomer</note> recorded some biographical details of Munjala in his own work, *Ganaka-tarangini* (written in Shaka 1814 / 1892 CE). Now, the entire text, accompanied by the commentary of Paramesvara <note>a famous 15th-century astronomer from Kerala</note> composed around Shaka 1353 (1431 CE), has been edited and printed by us. Consequently, it is now easy to understand everything propounded in this entire volume.

**Special Features of this Book:**

(a) When a circle is divided into four parts, the sine and cosine <term>Bhuja and Koti; the horizontal and vertical components of an arc</term> become equal at the end of the odd quadrants.
(b) In the year 854 Shaka, the precession of the equinoxes <term>Ayanamsha; the angular distance between the tropical and sidereal zodiacs</term> was calculated to be 6 degrees and 50 minutes.
(c) The conceptualization of the two verses beginning with "The cosine of the Sun minus the Moon's apogee..." and "In the results of the Moon and its motion..." are derived from the *Vateshvara-siddhanta*, as noted by Yalluvaraya in his commentary on the *Laghumanasa* titled *Kalpavalli*.

### Chapter One
### On the Mean Motions of Planets

->I, the best of the twice-born <note>A term for the Brahmin caste</note> of the Bharadvaja lineage, famous as the Sun in the city of Prakasha, shall speak of this other "Small Mind-Center," which provides a short, unprecedented, and accurate method for calculation. || 1 ||<-

> Having bowed to the Crowned One <note>The Sun God or Vishnu</note>, who is dawn-colored and by whom this entire universe is pervaded, I am composing this commentary on the "Small Mind-Center."

The connection of the words is as follows: I, the best of the twice-born of the Bharadvaja clan, famous like the Sun in the town named Prakasha, shall speak of another "Small Mind-Center" distinct from the "Great Mind-Center" <note>referring to Munjala's larger, lost work, the Brhadmanasa</note>. "Unprecedented-accurate" means that such accuracy did not exist previously. "Short" and "unprecedented-accurate" combined means a short yet unprecedentedly accurate method. This refers to a scientific treatise containing such a method. || 1 ||

->Having determined the weekday, the solar transit, the lunar day, the constant positions <term>Dhruva: fixed astronomical parameters used as a starting point for calculations</term>, and the lunar apogee at the beginning of the month of Chaitra, and having determined the others at the start of the solar year, one should calculate from that point for the rest of one's life. || 2 ||<-

He explains that one should calculate the positions of the Sun and other planets after establishing the "constants" for the desired year. At the beginning of Chaitra <note>The first month of the Hindu lunar calendar</note>, specifically at midday on the first lunar day of the bright fortnight, one must know the weekday, the solar transit, the lunar day, the mean position of the Sun, the mean position of the Moon, and the lunar apogee, along with the constant Shaka era years <note>The Shaka era began in 78 CE; it is the standard epoch for Indian astronomy</note>. Furthermore, one must know the others—the mean positions of Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn, the "fast-apogees" <term>Shighrocca: the point used to calculate the anomaly for inner planets</term> of Mercury and Venus, and the ascending node (Rahu)—at the start of the solar year (the end of the Sun's mean motion cycle). One should then calculate these for the duration of one's life. The phrase "with the constants" should be applied to all of them. The "constants" are the Shaka years. If one asks how these are to be understood, it is said: they are understood through the rules to be described hereafter, based on the constants established by the Teacher. We shall show those constant values here:

# Chapter on Mean Motions: The Laghumanasa

> The precession of the equinoxes is six degrees and fifty minutes; furthermore, one minute is added for each subsequent year. The Sun, combined with this value, marks the beginning of the Northern Equinox.

The degrees of the precession of the equinoxes <note>original: "ayanacalana"</note> are 6° 50'. Again, one minute should be added to this for each passing year. This precession value must be added to the planet's true longitude to understand its true motion in the north and south directions. The Sun joined with this value is designated as the "Beginning of the Northern Equinox." This means that this specific value should be taken as the Sun's position at the vernal equinox. Since these constants <note>original: "dhruva" - fixed starting points or epochs</note> were established on a Saturday, the determination of the weekday for subsequent dates begins from the following Sunday. When there are many such constants, the total number of elapsed days <note>term: "dyugana" - the count of days since an epoch</note> is used to find the desired weekday by adding increments of one, two, etc. Now, for the sake of computational brevity, other constants are recorded here, based on those taught by the Teacher:

> In the Shaka year measured by the Moon (1), the Gods (33), and the Moon (1) <note>Shaka 1131, or 1209 CE</note>, at the start of the month of Chaitra, on Sunday at noon:
> The Sun's signs are 0, degrees are 11, and minutes are 18; the seconds are 44.
> For the Moon, the values are 11, 26, and 55 respectively.
> For the Moon's Apogee <note>original: "candroccha"</note>, the values are 11, 0, and 22. Then, at the end of the Sun's revolution:
> For Mars, the values are 10, 6, and 38. Then for Mercury <note>original: "candraputra"</note>:
> The values are 11, 22, and 0. For Jupiter, the values are 2, 24, and 33.
> For Venus <note>original: "bhrguputra"</note>, the values are 2, 16, and 53. Then for Saturn <note>original: "suryatanaya"</note>:
> The values are 0, 5, and <unclear>5</unclear>5. For Rahu <note>the ascending lunar node</note>, the values are 0, 15, and 59.

Here, the "Transit Tithis" <note>lunar days associated with the Sun's entry into a sign</note> are calculated using the mean position of the Sun. Because this set of constants is established for a Sunday, the determination of the weekday for calculations will proceed from the following Monday. Thus, having established the constants for a desired year, one should calculate the positions of the Sun and other planets using them. // 2 //

> The number of years elapsed since the epoch should be multiplied by ten and increased by one-eighth of itself.
> Add the Transit Tithis, then place the result below and subtract one-sixtieth of itself. // 3 //

> Subtract the remainder left after dividing by thirty, and add the number of lunar days elapsed since the start of Chaitra.
> Subtract the seasons passed in the current year multiplied by three; the result is the count of days from the epoch's weekday. // 4 //

Now he describes the method for calculating the total elapsed days <note>dyugana</note>: "From the epoch..." etc. In the year for which the constants were established, take the number of years that have passed since then. Multiply them by ten. Then, set that value down separately, divide it by eight, take only the whole number result, and add it to the first quantity. Then, add the "Transit Tithis" mentioned in the constants to that sum. Again, set this result down separately below...

Again, placing it below, divide the bottom-most of those three quantities by sixty. Taking only the whole-number results, subtract them from the middle quantity. Divide that middle quantity by twenty; taking the whole-number results, set them aside somewhere. Subtract the remainder from the top-most quantity. To that quantity, add all the lunar days <note>tithis</note> starting from the first day of the bright fortnight of the month of Chaitra in the current year. Then, from that quantity, subtract three times the "Fixed Years" <note>dhruvābda: a constant or epochal year-count used in the system</note> and the elapsed seasons of the current year. That quantity is called the **Day-Count** <term>Dyugaṇa: the total number of days elapsed from a fixed epoch</term>. When that is divided by seven, the remainder indicates the day of the week starting from the "Fixed Day" <note>dhruvavāsara</note>. By adding or subtracting this Day-Count, the desired weekday is determined. However, if at the start of the year the triple-years cannot be subtracted, then one should subtract the quantity (including the Chaitra lunar days) from the triple-years minus the remainder of the years divided by thirty. In that case, the remainder is known as the **Negative Day-Count** <term>Ṛṇadyugaṇa</term>. Subtracting that from seven, the weekday is determined from the remainder. This occurs when the Fixed Year count is one or two. || 3 || 4 ||

Now he describes the calculation of the mean position of the Sun: "The Day-Count..." etc. Having placed the Day-Count for the desired day, and placing it again separately below, add ten times the Fixed Years to the lower one. Divide that by seventy <note>original: "khāga", where kha=0 and aga=7, using the Katapayadi system</note>. Subtract the resulting degrees from the upper quantity. Multiply the remainder by sixty and divide again by seventy; the resulting minutes <note>liptā</note> should be subtracted from the upper portion after converting one degree into minutes. Again, subtract eight times the Fixed Years from the upper quantity, and add minutes equal to one-eighth of the Fixed Years to the total minutes. The result will be the degrees. This means the degrees are in the upper position and the minutes are in the lower. Again and again, raising the upper degrees by twenty, add the "Fixed Constant" <note>dhruva</note>. That becomes the **Mean Sun**. If the eight-fold years cannot be subtracted, subtract the solar degrees and minutes from the eight-fold years, and subtract that remainder from the Fixed Constant. To that remainder, add the minutes from one-eighth of the years; that is the Mean Sun. In the case of a Negative Day-Count, subtract the Negative Day-Count from the ten-fold years; add the result of the division by seventy to the Negative Day-Count, add the eight-fold years to that as well, and then subtract that from the Fixed Constant. Add the minutes from one-eighth of the years to the remainder. That is the Sun. || 5 ||

**The number of elapsed days multiplied by thirteen, minus the number of years multiplied by three and the number of days; then add the result of dividing that by sixty-eight, and multiply by twenty-four; this gives the Moon’s position starting from degrees. || 6 ||**

He explains the method for bringing forth the mean position of the Moon—*The number of elapsed days multiplied by thirteen* etc. Having set down the <term>dyugana</term> <note>the total count of days elapsed since a fixed epoch</note>, multiply it by thirteen (the "Vishvas"). Set this value down separately below it. From that lower value, subtract the <term>dhruvabdas</term> <note>fixed "anchor years" used in the calculation</note> multiplied by three, and also subtract the simple day-count. Divide the remainder by sixty-eight (the "eight-limbs"). Take the resulting degrees, and from the remainder, multiply by sixty and divide again by the same divisor to get the <term>liptas</term> <note>minutes of arc</note>. Add these degrees and minutes to the first value (the day-count multiplied by thirteen). Then multiply the result by twenty-four (the "Jinas") and add the anchor years to that same value. This becomes the <term>Shashi</term> <note>the Moon</note> expressed in degrees. Again, as before, reduce the degrees by thirty to find the signs and add the fixed constant. In the case of the "Karna" day-count, subtract the day-count from the day-count multiplied by thirteen, then add the years multiplied by three, divide by sixty-eight, and add the resulting fraction to the day-count multiplied by thirteen; then subtract those path-values from the constant and add the years multiplied by twenty-four. That becomes the Moon. || 6 ||

**The Moon’s highest point in degrees is the day-count minus twice the years, divided by nine; add the years multiplied by forty, and subtract the minutes equal to one-eighth of the years. || 7 ||**

Subtract twice the anchor years from the day-count and divide the remainder by nine; the result is the degrees. From the remainder, multiply by sixty and divide by nine to get the <term>kala</term> <note>minutes of arc</note>. To those degrees, add the anchor years multiplied by forty (the "sky-Vedas"). From that, subtract the minutes equal to one-eighth of the years <unclear>the measure of the years...</unclear>. The result is the degrees of the Moon’s apogee. Again and again, reduce those degrees by thirty and add the constant. That becomes the <term>Chandratunga</term> <note>the Moon's apogee or highest point</note>. If twice the years is greater than the day-count: subtract the day-count from twice the years, divide by nine to get degrees and minutes, subtract those from the constant, and add the years multiplied by forty. Then subtract the minutes equal to one-eighth of the years. That is the apogee. In the case of the "Karna" day-count, add twice the years to the day-count, divide to get degrees and minutes, subtract from the constant, add the years multiplied by forty, and subtract the minutes equal to one-eighth of the years. That is the apogee. Or rather, one may calculate the Sun and Moon's apogee without the single operation of the elapsed years. || 7 ||

# [Chapter on the Mean Motion of Planets] — With the Parameśvara Commentary

> From the Sun combined with the epoch-constant, multiplied by two; and from that multiplied by sixteen <note>original: "nṛpa" (kings), a numerical code for 16</note>, divided by five hundred and five <note>original: "iṣukhepu" (arrows-void-arrows), a numerical code for 505</note>; added to seven times [the Sun] divided by forty-six <note>original: "ṛtuveda" (seasons-vedas), a numerical code for 46</note> and four times the Sun. || 8 ||

Now he describes the method for bringing the mean positions of Mars and others from the mean Sun: "From the Sun combined with the epoch-constant..." etc. Having placed the Sun's position—consisting of minutes, degrees, and signs—at noon on the desired day, one should place the years elapsed since the epoch <term>dhruvābda: the number of years passed since a fixed astronomical starting point</term> above the sign position. This is called the "Sun combined with the epoch-constant." However, between the start of the lunar year (Chaitra) and the half-year mark, the epoch-years are reduced by one, because the calculation of the constants for Mars and others is based on the solar year's beginning. 

Having set down this "Sun combined with the epoch-constant," divide it by two and place the resulting years in one spot. Then, multiply the remaining year by twelve, add it to the signs below, and divide those signs by two; place the resulting signs below the previously calculated years. Again, multiply the remaining signs by thirty, add them to the degrees, divide those degrees by two, and place the resulting degrees below the previously calculated signs. Finally, multiply the remaining degrees by sixty, add them to the minutes, divide those minutes by two, and place the resulting minutes below the previously calculated degrees.

Furthermore, take the "Sun combined with the epoch-constant" again and multiply the minutes, degrees, signs, and years separately by sixteen <note>original: "nṛpa" (kings)</note>. Carry the minutes to degrees by dividing by sixty, carry the degrees to signs by dividing by thirty, and carry the signs to years by dividing by twelve. Then, divide those years by five hundred and five <note>original: "iṣukhepu"</note> and add the resulting years to the previously calculated years. The remainders represent the current portions of the lunar day, half-day, or yoga. "Their beginning and end are found by their own motions"—the knowledge of the start and end times of these periods is known through the Rule of Three <term>trairashika; mathematical proportion</term> based on their respective daily motions. The elapsed minutes of the current lunar day or half-day, multiplied by sixty and divided by the difference between the daily motions of the Sun and Moon, gives the elapsed ghatikas <note>A unit of time equal to 24 minutes</note> of the current lunar day or half-day. Similarly, the remaining minutes to be traversed give the remaining ghatikas. For the current asterism, the elapsed or remaining minutes multiplied by sixty and divided by the Moon's true daily motion gives the elapsed or remaining ghatikas. For the current yoga, the elapsed or remaining minutes multiplied by sixty and divided by the sum of the motions of the Sun and Moon gives the elapsed or remaining ghatikas of the current yoga. In all cases, the motion is expressed in minutes. Thus, the beginning and end are known from the Rule of Three. "By proportion" means by the Rule of Three. The asterisms for other planets are also understood just like the Moon's. As it was said: "From the planets, the asterisms..." || 4 ||

# Chapter on Eclipses: The Laghumanasa

By reasoning: When two planets are moving in opposite directions (one direct and one retrograde), the sum of their daily motions is used. When they are moving in the same direction (both direct or both retrograde), the difference between their specific motions is used. By dividing the minutes of arc separating the two planets by this [combined or difference] motion, the days [until or since conjunction] are found. The remainder, multiplied by sixty, gives the results in *nadis* <note>A unit of time equal to 24 minutes; 60 nadis make one day.</note>. This represents the time interval between the desired time and the actual moment of conjunction. Here, the motion must be expressed in minutes of arc. Using these results and the rule of three, one should calculate the planets to be equal at the moment of conjunction.

This is what is being said: At the time of the *parvan* <note>The new or full moon day</note>, one should calculate the mean positions of the Sun and Moon at midday. Having found those mean positions, one should apply the corrections for terrestrial longitude and the "equation of the center" <term>bhujavivara: the correction for the equation of time due to the eccentricity of the orbit</term> to make them "true" (corrected) positions. These then become the true Sun and Moon. Again, take the difference between them in minutes, multiply by sixty, and divide by the difference of their true daily motions in minutes. The result is the time of conjunction in *ghatikas* <note>Synonymous with nadis; 24-minute increments</note> and so on. This means the time of the end of the *parvan* relative to midday.

Furthermore, multiply the true motions of the Sun and Moon separately by those *nadis*, divide by sixty, and the resulting minutes should be added to or subtracted from the midday Sun and Moon. If the conjunction is yet to come, add them; if the conjunction has already passed, subtract them. At that point, the Sun and Moon become equal. One should set aside these conjunction *ghatikas* in one place. In the case of a lunar eclipse, however, the *nadis* are to be calculated from the difference between the Moon and the Sun increased by six signs <note>180 degrees, representing opposition</note>. In this same way, one should perform the calculation of the time of conjunction and the equalization for other planets as well.

Regarding the *bhujavivara* correction: multiply one-sixth of the planet's mean daily motion by the minutes of the Sun's "arm-result" <note>The result of the sine of the solar anomaly</note>, and divide by 3610. The resulting minutes should be added to or subtracted from the planet's mean position according to the Sun's position. This is demonstrated by us as follows:

> "The minutes of daily motion multiplied by one-sixth of the Sun's arm-result,
> divided by 3610, are the minutes to be added or subtracted according to the Sun's arm."

The Teacher <note>Referring to Munjala, the author of the original verses</note> did not explicitly teach this because the difference it makes is not very large. || 2 ||

-> The units 7400 divided by the Sun's divisor are the Sun's disk; <-

Now he describes the calculation of the minutes of the diameter of the disks of the Sun and others: "The Sun's..." Divide 7400 by the Sun's true divisor. The result is the Sun's disk in minutes and so on. This means the minutes of the diameter of the solar disk. Dividing 3100 by the Moon's "slow-true" divisor, the result is the Moon's disk. The term "slow-divisor" <term>mandahara</term> is used to specify the divisor used in the second correction process, to exclude the divisor used for the first correction. || 3 ||

One should calculate the versed sine <term>utkramajya: 1 - cos(x)</term> of the "distance from the peak" <term>koti: the angular distance from the nearest tropical point</term> of the planet (including the precession of the equinoxes <term>sayana: "with precession"</term>) using the versed sine method. Multiply that value (in degrees) by one-fourth of the planet's own latitude (in minutes). Divide this separately by the rising time of the sign the planet is in and by the rising time of the seventh sign. The results in degrees and minutes should be applied respectively to the planet's longitude (already adjusted by the first correction) and to the planet plus six signs (already adjusted by the first correction). If both the latitude and the cosine-distance are simultaneously negative or both positive, the result should be added to the planet and to the planet plus six signs. If one is positive and the other is negative, the result should be subtracted from the planet and from the planet plus six signs. Thus, the planet adjusted by both visibility corrections becomes the "Rising Point" of the planet. The planet plus six signs adjusted by both corrections becomes the "Setting Point" of the planet. Having thus applied the two visibility corrections to the true longitude of the planet and the planet plus six signs as appropriate, one should determine the beginning of its combustion <note>maudhya: the period when a planet is too close to the sun to be seen</note> and other states. || 2 ||

# Chapter on the Visibility of Planets

->**Multiply three hundred by twelve, sixteen, thirteen, eleven, eight, and fifteen.**<-
->**Divide by the rising time of the sign; the Sun, increased or decreased by this, is the "Sun of Setting" relative to the Moon and others. || 2 ||**<-

Now, he describes the calculation of the "Sun of Setting" <note>Astarka: the specific solar position at which a planet is lost in the Sun's light</note> to understand the rising and setting of planets: "Twelve" <note>original: *Surya*</note>, "Sixteen" <note>original: *Ashti*</note>, "Thirteen" <note>original: *Vishve*</note>, "Eleven" <note>original: *Rudra*</note>, "Eight" (as commonly known), and "Fifteen" <note>original: *Vishaya*</note>. These are the degrees <note>of separation from the Sun</note> for the Moon and the other planets. Multiply three hundred <note>original: *khakha-agni*, literally "sky-sky-fire" representing 300</note> by these values and divide by the "rising time of the eastern sign" <note>Pragbhodayas: the time it takes for a zodiac sign to rise at a specific latitude</note>—either the rising time in *vinadikas* <note>a unit of time equal to 24 seconds</note> of the sign where the planet is situated, or the *vinadikas* of the seventh sign from it, as applicable. 

The resulting value <note>1</note> in degrees and minutes should be applied to the true Sun of that time in two ways: add it to one and subtract it from the other. These two solar positions are known as the "Suns of Setting." [The phrase "rising time of the eastern sign" is used because the divisor depends on the local horizon at that moment.] 

Regarding the division of the divisor: When the planet is visible or about to be seen in the eastern hemisphere <note>Prāk-kapāla</note>, the divisor is the rising time in *vinadikas* <note>2</note> of the sign where the planet—processed by the two visibility corrections <note>Drik-karma: calculations to adjust for the planet's latitude and the observer's location</note>—is located. However, when the planet is visible or about to be seen in the western hemisphere <note>Paścāt-kapāla</note>, the divisor is the rising time of the seventh sign from the planet's position. In that case, when the planet is increased by six signs <note>180 degrees</note>, the "Sun of Setting" must also be increased by six signs.

This is what is meant: When a planet visible in the eastern hemisphere goes to its disappearance <note>heliacal setting</note>, or when a planet to be seen in the eastern hemisphere goes to its rising <note>heliacal rising</note>, one should calculate the two "Suns of Setting" using the degrees mentioned (twelve, sixteen, etc.) and the rising *vinadikas* of the sign where the planet sits after the two visibility corrections. When the planet (corrected by the two visibility adjustments) moves within the interval between those two "Suns of Setting," that planet is "set" <note>invisible</note>. This applies to the eastern hemisphere. 

Conversely, when a planet visible in the western hemisphere goes to its setting, or one to be seen in the western hemisphere goes to its rising, one should add six signs to the planet's position, apply the two visibility corrections, and calculate the two "Suns of Setting" using the setting *vinadikas* <note>the rising time of the opposite sign</note> and the planet's specific degrees. Then, add half a circle <note>3</note> (six signs) to those two "Suns of Setting." When the planet (corrected by the two visibility adjustments and increased by six signs) <note>4</note> is <note>5</note> within the interval of those two "Suns of Setting" (also increased by six signs), then that planet goes to its setting. This means it becomes impossible to see. If it moves outside the "Suns of Setting," it has reached its rising. Thus, the time of rising and setting should be known <note>6</note> through the "Suns of Setting" and the planet's latitude at that time. Indeed, the purpose is served by even a single "Sun of Setting." || 3 ||

# [Section on Miscellaneous Calculations] Laghumanasam

Now, another method for the half-visibility correction <note>original: "drkkarma-arddham"</note> — regarding latitude <note>original: "vikshepa"</note>. One should calculate the true deflection <note>original: "valana"</note> of the planet at its rising or setting. Multiply the digits of that deflection by the minutes of the planet's latitude and divide by 900 <note>original: "khakha-anga", where kha=0, kha=0, anga=6, but here interpreted in context as 900</note>. The resulting degrees should be applied to the true longitude of the planet, or to the longitude plus six signs (180 degrees). If the latitude and deflection are in different directions, add the result; if they are in the same direction, subtract it. When this is done, the planet is corrected for equatorial deflection. The phrase "difference between the setting sun" means the planet, once corrected by deflection, reaches the point of difference relative to the setting sun. Some read this without the specific verse. Others desire the deflection correction in addition to the two visibility corrections. That is incorrect. Since the deflection process itself accomplishes both visibility corrections, one should perform either the deflection process or the two visibility corrections. If the planet is in the eastern hemisphere, perform this at rising; if in the western hemisphere, at setting. [End of section on Miscellaneous Calculations].

->**The solar degrees for the setting and rising of Agastya are 77 and 97.**<-
->**Subtracted from or added to eight times the local equinoctial shadow. ॥ 4 ॥**<-

Now, he explains how to know the time of the rising and setting of the sage Agastya <note>The star Canopus, highly significant in Indian astronomy and mythology</note> — "Agastya" etc. When the true longitude of the sun, converted into degrees, equals 77 degrees minus eight times the digits of the local equinoctial shadow <note>The shadow of a gnomon at noon on the equinox, used to determine latitude</note>, that is the time of Agastya's setting. Again, when the true longitude of the sun equals 97 degrees plus eight times the digits of the local equinoctial shadow, that is the time of Agastya's rising. This time is shown because of its auspicious nature. ॥ 4 ॥

->**By the difference between the square of six and the degrees of ascensional difference,**<-
->**Divide the latitude multiplied by the equinoctial shadow;**<-
->**The result corrects the ascensional difference to its true value. ॥ 5 ॥**<-

Now he describes the correction for ascensional difference <note>original: "chara-samskara"</note> — "Charatana" etc. Set aside the minutes of ascensional difference for the Moon and Sun (with the precession of the equinoxes added). Divide them by 49; the result is the "degrees of ascensional difference." The "square of six" is 36. Divide the minutes of the planet's own latitude (multiplied by the digits of the equinoctial shadow) by the difference between the degrees of ascensional difference and 36. The result is the correction for ascensional difference in minutes of time. When applied, the local ascensional difference becomes "true" <note>accurate for the planet's specific latitude</note>. This means: if both the latitude and the ascensional difference are both positive or both negative, the sum of the correction minutes and the ascensional difference becomes the true ascensional difference.

# [Chapter on Miscellaneous Topics]
## Accompanied by the Parameshvara Commentary

When the <term>ascensional difference</term> <note>the difference between the rising of a celestial body on the equator versus its actual position</note> and the <term>celestial latitude</term> <note>the angular distance of a planet north or south of the ecliptic</note> have opposite signs—one being positive and the other negative—the difference between the correction for the ascensional difference and the minutes of the ascensional difference becomes the **True Ascensional Difference**. In this manner, one should calculate the true ascensional difference for the Moon and other planets. Using this correction, the previously mentioned <term>visibility correction</term> <note>Sanskrit: drkkarma; calculations to determine when a planet actually appears on the horizon</note> is performed. || 5 ||

-> When the difference in minutes between the day-lengths of the Sun and Moon <-
-> is less than the equinoctial shadow, the Vyatipata occurs. <-
-> The Vaidhrita occurs similarly between day and night. <- || 6 ||

Now, he explains the method for knowing the time of the *Vyatipata* <note>a specific inauspicious astronomical alignment</note>: "When the difference..." As long as the difference in minutes between the day-lengths of the Sun and Moon is less than the <term>equinoctial shadow</term> <note>Sanskrit: palabha; the shadow of a vertical gnomon at noon on the equinox</note> measured in digits, the affliction known as **Vyatipata** exists. Regarding **Vaidhrita** between day and night: having calculated the day-length for one (Sun or Moon) and the night-length for the other, as long as the difference in minutes between those day and night lengths is less than the digits of the equinoctial shadow, the affliction known as **Vaidhrita** exists. 

Here, the term "equinoctial shadow" refers to the shadow of the equinox diminished by one-eighth. Therefore, the state of being "less than" should be understood relative to that value. Furthermore, *Vyatipata* occurs only when, of the two day-lengths (Sun and Moon), one is progressively increasing while the other is progressively decreasing. Otherwise, the *Vyatipata* affliction does not occur. Similarly, for the *Vaidhrita* affliction, it must be understood that one (day or night length) must be increasing while the other is decreasing; otherwise, there is an absence of *Vaidhrita*.

As it is said: "Equality occurs only through the logic of progressive increase and decrease." And by the rule "The affliction occurs when it is less than the limit," equality is established by the calculation of the equinoctial shadow diminished by one-eighth. || 6 ||

-> From the Moon-Ascendant at that time, with both visibility corrections applied, <-
-> find the Moon's elapsed day-time; from that, find the shadow like the Sun's. <- || 7 ||

Now he describes the calculation of the Moon's shadow: "With both..." One should perform the two types of visibility corrections for the Moon at the time of observation. That Moon, with both visibility corrections applied, is the "Moon at that time"; then, one should also calculate the rising <term>Ascendant</term> <note>the point of the zodiac rising on the eastern horizon</note> for that same time. Having added the <term>precession of the equinoxes</term> <note>Sanskrit: ayana; the slow shift of celestial coordinates over time</note> to that Ascendant and that Moon, one should calculate the time interval in <term>ghatikas</term> <note>units of 24 minutes</note> between those two tropical positions using the methods taught in the *Chapter on the Three Problems* <note>Sanskrit: Triprasnadhyaya; a standard section of astronomical texts dealing with time, place, and direction</note>. Those minutes constitute the **Moon's elapsed day-time**. From that day-length, the shadow is determined just like the Sun's shadow, using the Moon's elapsed day-time and the Moon's own true day-length. This is what is meant: having calculated the Moon's true ascensional difference from the remaining degrees of the <term>declination</term>, and having derived the measures of half-day and half-night from that ascensional difference, one then finds the Moon's elapsed day-time...
