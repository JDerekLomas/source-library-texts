# E P I S T O L A .
<note>Latin: "Epistle" or "Letter of Dedication"</note>

<image-desc>The initial paragraph of the text is printed over a faint, decorative woodcut or engraving featuring celestial clouds and ornate scrollwork motifs.</image-desc>

*Born of the royal blood of the Chinese Emperors, they call him Confucius <note>The Latinized form of Kong Fuzi, or "Master Kong"</note>; by the unanimous consent of the Chinese, he is regarded as the wisest of all who have ever flourished among them, and the Master and Oracle of both Moral and Political Philosophy.*

*Just as three thousand disciples once hung upon his every word, so now the vastest empire is governed by his sayings; by them the laws are established, the customs of the Nation and civil duties are arranged, and finally, the sole hope and method of obtaining the highest honors of the Republic and the Magistracies is contained in the thorough learning of his doctrine. All the Chinese marvelously revere his memory, his books, and his very name; even the Emperors themselves do not disdain to come as worshipers to those houses where he once taught (which are preserved as shrines of wisdom) and to his schools. And truly, not without merit; for it was from such a great Master that they learned to adore and fear the Supreme Emperor of Heaven <note>original: "Summum caeli Imperatorem," referring to the Chinese concept of Shangdi or Tian</note>—as he himself called Him—the moderator and judge of all Kingdoms and Empires; to rule the peoples subject to them with great equity and charity; to foster the arts; and finally, to govern the Chinese world, flourishing for so many years now in peace and war, with the most holy institutions and laws.*

*Here, therefore, that very Confucius presents himself to you,* G R E A T  K I N G <note>Refers to King Louis XIV of France</note>, *carried as if by your cares and Royal liberality into the lands of Gaul <note>original: "Gallias," meaning France</note>; he approaches, prostrate at the feet of Your Majesty, openly to admire your wisdom. He is ready to confess that his own wisdom—even if boasted of with incredible fame and esteem among his own people—nevertheless yields to Yours no less than the stars yield to the Sun.*

*He will stand fast, I believe, at the first approach and sight of you, and fixed at once by admiration and joy, he will realize he has found at last*

<meta>catchword: at last</meta>

<summary>This dedication introduces Confucius to King Louis XIV, describing the philosopher as the foundational authority for Chinese law, morality, and governance. It frames the translation of his works as a tribute to the French King's own superior wisdom.</summary>

...at last he would declare that Prince to be the one whom he had hitherto burned with such great zeal to see, though in vain. For when that distinguished man <note>Likely referring to Confucius, given the context of the Chinese term used below</note> had sketched in his books an extraordinary Emperor—the kind he had formed in his mind—and could find no one among the ancestral Princes of the Empire who truly equaled his desires, in whom all those royal endowments united, and who reflected that form and idea of a most perfect Prince, complete in every part, he then broke out into these words: **Tâi Kî Gîn** <note>original: "Tâi Kî Gîn"; likely a transliteration of a Chinese phrase meaning "The Great Sage" or "The Man of the Great Ultimate"</note> *This is the Man to be expected, who shall come one day, and, endowed with a certain divine and admirable wisdom, shall show himself to be such a one in whom neither our own nor the public's prayers can find anything lacking.*

Would he not, if only he were to return to life and behold **Yᴏᴜ, Gʀᴇᴀᴛ Kɪɴɢ**, recognize that You are the very one whom he had foreseen in his mind? Overwhelmed with incredible joy, would he not exclaim that his prayer had been granted? Would he not propose Your wisdom in governing a most vast Kingdom to all kings, and seek examples from Your character and laws from Your utterances? Finally, would he not wish for Your piety, mercy, justice, and that steady serenity and presence of mind and countenance—even amidst such a great mass of the highest affairs and business—joined with such Majesty, to be the norm and rule for all Princes?

# E P I S T O L A .

<note>Text continues from the previous page</note>

*...foreign doctrines, which he repeatedly declared were born for the destruction of peoples and the ruin of Monarchies, he might utterly overthrow. Hence that saying of his, even today most celebrated among the Chinese <note>original: "Sinas"</note>:* Cum hu y tuon, <note>original: "Oppugna heretica dogmata"</note> *Attack heretical doctrines. How much joy, therefore, would it bring to a man so loving of piety, if indeed he had been able to reach these most happy times of the law of grace, to see that care of yours, O King, for protecting and enlarging Religion, for extirpating heresy, and for propagating piety? With what praises would he extol You, when he beheld heresy—that most foul enemy of the ancestral faith and of a most flourishing kingdom—trampled and crushed; the edicts, by which it seemed to draw its life, repealed; its temples cast down, its very name buried; so many thousands of souls led so sweetly, so bravely, and so fortunately from their former errors to the truth, and from destruction to salvation; and finally, all of France <note>original: "Galliam"</note> seen as most Christian under a King who is Greatest and truly Most Christian?*

*Truly, he would not only marvel at and proclaim the other miracles of your France: not the many fortresses, fortified by all the protections of both art and nature, partly cast down and captured by You, and partly built and erected; not the most powerful and numerous fleets with which you have brought terror to Asia and Africa; not the many victories won over enemies, upon which you placed a most glorious crown as a trophy of public peace; not those sights in which you unfold your Royal splendor and magnificence, the Palaces; not the rivers carried across mountains; the seas opened and joined; not the many gymnasiums and seminaries of arts and sciences. All these things, I say, would not strike so much admiration into that most wise Philosopher <note>A reference to Confucius</note> as this one triumph of Religion over Heresy, under Your leadership and auspices...*
