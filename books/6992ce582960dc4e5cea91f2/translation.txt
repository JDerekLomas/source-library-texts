## The Qilin
<note>original: "麒麟" (Qilin); often translated as "Chinese Unicorn," though it is a chimera. It is a symbol of benevolence, prosperity, and the arrival of a wise ruler.</note>

The <term>Record of Rites by the Elder Dai</term> <note>original: "大戴禮" (Da Dai Li); a collection of ritual texts from the Han Dynasty.</note> states that there are three hundred and sixty species of furred creatures, and the Qilin is the leader among them. The <term>Shuowen Jiezi</term> <note>original: "說文"; the first Chinese dictionary to analyze characters, compiled by Xu Shen.</note> notes: the female is called *Qi* and the male is called *Lin*.

When the male cries out, it is called "Wandering toward the Sage"; the female's cry is called "Returning to Harmony." Their cry in the spring is called "Supporting the Young," and their cry in the autumn is called "Nurturing Peace."

According to the <term>Amulet of the Essence of Feelings in the Spring and Autumn Annals</term>: <note>original: "春秋感精符" (Chunqiu Ganjing Fu); an apocryphal text from the Han Dynasty that links natural phenomena to the conduct of rulers.</note> "When a King does not harm the unborn in the womb and does not break the eggs in the nest, then the Qilin will appear in the outskirts of the city."

<term>Sun Qingzi</term> <note>original: "孫卿子"; another name for the philosopher Xunzi (c. 310 – c. 235 BCE).</note> said: "When a ruler loves life and hates killing, the Qilin will wander in the wild."

Some say: The *Lin* has a horn, while the *Qi* resembles the *Lin* but lacks a horn.

Song Jun <note>a Han Dynasty commentator</note> said: "The Qilin is blue and yellow in color."

The <term>Garden of Stories</term> <note>original: "說苑" (Shuoyuan); a collection of anecdotes and moral parables compiled by Liu Xiang.</note> says: "The Qilin has the body of a <term>river deer</term>, <note>original: "麇" (jūn); the water deer or hydropote, often used in classical texts to describe the Qilin's delicate frame.</note> the tail of an ox, the feet of a horse, and round hooves. It has a single horn, and there is flesh on the tip of the horn." <note>The "flesh on the horn" signifies that while the Qilin has the potential for defense, it is so benevolent that its weapon is soft and harmless.</note>

# The Suanni
<note>original: "狻猊" (suanni). While the Suanni is known in broader mythology as one of the "Nine Sons of the Dragon," this text uses the term as a historical synonym for the lion.</note>

The **Suanni** resembles a tiger or a cat and is said to eat tigers and leopards; it is, in fact, the **lion** <note>original: "即獅子也" (ji shizi ye)</note>. Generally, those with tiger-like markings are called **Pimiao** <note>original: "貔貓" (pimiao); an ancient term for a predatory feline or leopard-like cat</note>.

Furthermore, the commentaries in the <term>Records of the Western Regions</term> <note>original: "西域傳註" (Xiyu Chuan Zhu); historical accounts of Central and Western Asia, the regions through which lions were first introduced to China as tribute</note> describe the lion as resembling a tiger, being pure yellow in color, and having a smooth tail. The hair at the tip of the tail is said to be as large as a **bushel** <note>original: "大如斗" (da ru dou); a "dou" is a traditional Chinese measuring bucket, emphasizing the lion's bushy tail tuft</note>.

The <term>Classic of Poetry</term> <note>original: "詩" (Shi/Shijing)</note> also mentions that the joys of the land of **Han** <note>original: "韓土" (Hantu); an ancient state during the Zhou Dynasty</note> include both "cats" and "tigers." The <term>Book of Zhou</term> <note>original: "周書" (Zhou Shu)</note> records that during a royal hunt, **King Wu** captured twenty-two tigers. Based on these accounts, the **Suanni** is classified as being of the same kind as the tiger.

# The Xiezhi
<note>original: "獬豸" (Xièzhì). The Xiezhi is a legendary "righteous beast" in East Asian mythology. It is famously known as the "justice beast" because of its innate ability to distinguish between truth and lies, and between the innocent and the guilty.</note>

<note>This illustration is a quintessential woodcut from the Ming dynasty. It depicts the **Xiezhi** as a creature with a scaly body, lion-like paws, and a head resembling a dragon. It is shown standing on a rocky outcrop beneath a leafy tree, surrounded by stylized clouds that emphasize its supernatural nature.</note>

The **Xiezhi** is traditionally described as a "law-abiding beast" <note>original: "任法獸" (renfashou)</note>. It is characterized by its single horn, which it uses to strike those who are in the wrong. In historical Chinese culture, this creature was highly revered by officials; its image was often embroidered on the "patch" of a judge's or censor's official robes to symbolize their commitment to impartiality and the discovery of the truth. 

When the **Xiezhi** sees two people fighting, it will use its horn to gore the one who is unjust. When it hears people arguing, it will bite the one who is dishonest. Because of its legendary discernment, it has remained a symbol of the law for centuries.

## The Xiezhi

On Mount Dongwang <note>original: "東望山" (Dongwangshan), literally "East-Watching Mountain."</note> there lives the **Xiezhi**, a divine beast. It has existed since the era of Emperor Yao <note>A legendary sage-king of ancient China (c. 2300 BCE) who is a symbol of the perfect, moral ruler.</note>. This creature has the ability to "touch the wicked" <note>This refers to the beast's legendary habit of goring or striking those who are guilty or unjust with its horn.</note>. In appearance, it is like a sheep with a single horn and four legs. It is said that when a ruler's legal cases and litigations are fair and impartial, the creature will appear.

The traditions of the Censorate <note>original: "御史臺" (Yushitai), the high-level supervisory agency in imperial China responsible for investigating corruption and maintaining law.</note> state that the "legal cap" <note>original: "法冠" (faguan)</note> worn by the Censors is also known by the name "**Xiezhi**." It is a divine sheep and possesses one horn. The King of Chu <note>A ruler of the ancient southern state of Chu during the Zhou Dynasty.</note> once famously captured one.

# The Sea Horse

The **Sea Horse** <note>original: "海馬" (Haima). In this historical context, this refers to a mythical aquatic horse rather than the small fish known today.</note>. The *Classic of Mountains and Seas* <note>original: "山海經" (Shanhaijing)</note> says that within the North Sea there is a beast shaped like a horse that is also an excellent runner. For this reason, those who incorporate the Sea Horse into the patterns of their official robes <note>original: "服色" (fuse), referring to the decorative motifs on bureaucratic or military uniforms</note> do so to symbolize this exceptional speed. Some say the **Taotu** <note>original: "騊駼", a legendary wild horse of the northern steppes</note> is also a horse renowned for its running ability and is, in fact, the same creature as the Sea Horse.

Furthermore, the *Book of Sui* <note>original: "隋書" (Suishu), the official historical record of the Sui Dynasty</note> records that in the Western Regions, the **Tuyuhun** people <note>original: "土谷渾", a nomadic kingdom</note> lived near **Qinghai** <note>original: "青海", literally "Blue Sea," referring to Qinghai Lake</note>. Within this "sea" is a small mountain. It was their custom to wait until the winter ice froze over and then release their mares onto the island. It was said that by doing so, they could obtain **dragon-seed** offspring <note>original: "龍種" (longzhong), referring to horses of divine or supernatural lineage</note>. Legend has it they once obtained a Persian horse and released it into the "sea," where it sired a **piebald colt** <note>original: "驄駒" (congju)</note> capable of traveling a thousand miles in a single day. Consequently, people at that time referred to these remarkable steeds as "Sea Horses."

<term>Classic of Mountains and Seas</term>: An ancient Chinese compilation of mythic geography and beasts, serving as a primary source for legendary zoology. <term>Tuyuhun</term>: A powerful nomadic state established by the Xianbei people, located in the modern-day Qinghai and Gansu provinces. <term>Dragon-seed</term>: A poetic term for horses believed to have been fathered by dragons or water spirits, representing the highest quality of steed available to the empire.

It explains the creature's significance as a symbol on official dress and its legendary origin as the "dragon-seed" offspring of horses bred on island retreats.</summary>

# The Zouyu

<vocab>Zouyu, Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms, Birds and Beasts</vocab>

<note>The Zouyu (騶虞) is a legendary "benevolent beast" in Chinese tradition. Though it possesses the fearsome physical traits of a large feline, it is defined by its extreme compassion and non-violence.</note>

[Image Description: A woodcut illustration depicts the mythical beast **Zouyu**. It is shown as a large, powerful animal with the body of a tiger, covered in dark stripes. Its most distinctive feature is a tail that is significantly longer than its entire body. The creature is depicted walking calmly through a rocky landscape beneath the overhanging branches of a pine tree, a traditional symbol of longevity and moral uprightness.]

<note>According to classical sources like the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* (Shanhaijing), the Zouyu is white with black markings and is capable of traveling a thousand miles in a single day. It is hailed as a "righteous animal" because it refuses to eat any living creature, subsisting only on things that have died naturally, and is said to walk so gently that it never treads upon living blades of grass. Historically, its appearance was reported to the emperor as a high-level auspicious omen, signaling that the ruler's virtue had achieved a state of perfect harmony with nature.</note>

# The Rhinoceros

The Rhinoceros <note>original: 犀 (Xī)</note> is found beyond the southern frontiers. Its body is shaped like an ox, resembling a water buffalo but with the head of a pig, a large belly, and short, squat legs. Each foot has three hooves, and the animal is black in color.

It possesses three horns: one on the crown of the head, one on the forehead, and one on the nose. The horn on the nose is known as the "food horn" <note>original: 食角 (shí jiǎo)</note>; it is small and not oval in shape. The animal has a fondness for eating thorns.

There are also rhinoceroses that possess only a single horn. The front legs of the rhinoceros are straight, so the animal often rests by leaning against a tree; if the tree should happen to fall, the creature is unable to stand up again. <note>This mirrors a common medieval myth—also found in European bestiaries regarding elephants—that exotic beasts lacked joints in their legs and could not right themselves if they fell.</note>

# The Tiger

Tigers begin to mate on the day of the Great Cold <note>The last of the 24 solar terms, occurring in late January</note> and give birth seven months later. Their nature is exceedingly fierce; even when pursued, they will linger and look back as they retreat. If a tiger is seriously wounded, it departs with a series of roars. By counting these roars, one can determine the distance of its retreat: generally, each roar signifies a distance of one *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of measurement, roughly 500 meters</note>. 

When pouncing on prey, the tiger does not exceed three leaps; if it fails to catch its target within those three attempts, it abandons the hunt. When eating, it stops once it reaches the ears of the carcass, for it is believed that touching the ears would violate its own name <note>This likely refers to a linguistic taboo or a folk belief that the tiger’s identity or "spirit" is concentrated in its name, which shares a mystical connection with the ears (er) of its prey</note>. Those tigers that have previously harmed humans always have notched ears, looking much like the teeth of a saw. 

At night, the tiger sees by emitting a beam of light from one eye while using the other eye to observe objects. Hunters lie in wait to shoot them; if successful, the beam of light falls to the ground and can be gathered up, appearing like a white stone. The tiger also possesses mystical arts, such as the ability to draw signs upon the ground to perform divination <note>original: 畫地以卜 (huà dì yǐ bǔ)</note>. A tiger with white fur is called a <term>Biao|彪</term>, and one that is black is called a <term>Shu|虪</term>.

# The Rabbit

The rabbit’s mouth has a cleft, and it is said to give birth by spitting out its young; therefore, it is called *Tu* <note>original: 兔 (tù); a pun on the word 吐 (tǔ), which means "to spit" or "to vomit." Among creatures that chew their food, most possess nine orifices and are born from a womb; however, the rabbit alone—whether male or female—is said to have only eight orifices.

Accordingly, the writings of Master Tao <note>original: 陶氏 (Táo Shì); referring to the famous Taoist scholar and physician Tao Hongjing.</note> state that a rabbit conceives by licking the "male bristles" <note>original: 雄毫 (xióngháo); referring to the male's fur.</note> of its mate, and after five months, she bears her young. Furthermore, local folk traditions suggest that rabbits conceive by gazing at the <term>Gazing-Rabbit<note>original: 顧兔 (Gùtù); a poetic name for the legendary rabbit that lives in the moon.</note></term> and absorbing its celestial essence. Consequently, people divine the abundance or scarcity of rabbits by observing the brightness or dimness of the autumn moon. This "Gazing-Rabbit" is the creature that resides within the very center of the moon.

Generally, wild beasts have dens, but only the rabbit has fur covering the soles of its feet, which is known as <term>Erect-Hair<note>original: 建毛 (Jiànmáo)</note></term>. By nature, the rabbit is cunning and skilled at running. In the places where a rabbit dwells, its burrow is consistently made with three openings <note>This is the origin of the Chinese idiom "the wily rabbit has three burrows," which praises the animal's foresight in always having multiple escape routes.</note>.

It is not particularly swift or majestic, which is why it is often referred to as the "lame donkey" <note>original: "蹇驢" (jiǎn lǘ). One ancient name for this animal is *wei* <note>original: "衞" (wèi)</note>; this naming convention is similar to how the great tortoise is sometimes referred to by the name *cai* <note>original: "蔡" (cài)</note>. 

As a rule, the donkey brays at noon and again at the start of the fifth watch <note>The fifth watch refers to the dawn period, roughly between 3:00 AM and 5:00 AM</note>. Its temperament and physical build make it naturally suited for turning millstones and carrying heavy burdens on its back.

According to the *Shuowen Jiezi* <term>*Shuowen Jiezi* (說文解字): The first Chinese dictionary to analyze the structure of characters, compiled during the Han Dynasty</term>, another name for the donkey is *yu* <note>original: "騟" (yú)</note>.

When a male horse and a female donkey mate, their offspring is called a *jueti* <note>original: "駃騠" (juétí); historically this refers to a hinny, though the term was often used for various hardy crossbreeds</note>. It is said that only three days after its birth, the *jueti* is already capable of leaping over its mother, illustrating that its vitality and speed far exceed those of an ordinary donkey. Another name for this creature is *teng* <note>original: "騰" (téng), meaning "to gallop" or "to soar"</note>.

# The Dog

There are three types of dogs: first, the **hunting dog**; second, the **barking dog**; and third, the **edible dog**. The edible dog is similar to the "beef cattle" <note>original: "菜牛" (càiniú) - cattle raised specifically for meat rather than labor</note> of today. Because their nature is to be protective, they are raised to guard the homestead. Regarding the preparation of meat: while pork is best suited for roasting, dog meat is best suited for stews. For this reason, the *Book of Rites* <note>A foundational Confucian classic detailing the social norms and ceremonial regulations of the Zhou dynasty</note> contains references to "dog stew."

The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary and encyclopedia, dating back to at least the 3rd century BCE</note> states: When a dog gives birth to a litter of three, they are called *biao*; a litter of two is called *shi*; a litter of one is called *zhan*. The term *shi* implies a multitude, while *biao* refers to a huddle or cluster. A dog that has not yet grown its coarse guard hairs is called a *gou* <note>original: "狗" - while this is now the common word for "dog," it historically referred specifically to puppies or young dogs</note>. A dog with a long muzzle is called a *xian*, and one with a short muzzle is called a *he*. The long-muzzled *xian* is skilled at hunting, whereas the short-muzzled dog is skilled at barking for protection. Another name for the dog is the *mang* <note>original: "尨" - often used to describe a shaggy or multi-colored dog</note>. Among the most excellent dogs of antiquity, the state of Han possessed the "Lu" and the state of Song possessed the "Que." The "Lu" was solid black, and the "Que" was black and white.

# The Pig

The pig <note>original: 豕 (shǐ)</note> is also known as the swine <note>original: 彘 (zhì)</note>. The nature of the pig is such that it is capable in water; for this reason, the places where pigs are herded must always be at the intersection of water and grass. A pig with four white hooves is called a *gai* <note>original: 豥</note>. Regarding the restless nature of pigs, the *Explaining the Trigrams* <note>original: 說卦 (Shuōguà); a classical commentary on the I Ching or Book of Changes</note> states that the <term>Trigram Kan <note>original: 坎 (Kǎn); the trigram representing the element of Water</note></term> corresponds to the pig. The nature of *Kan* is to tend downwards; similarly, a pig is able to lower its head and specializes in dwelling in lowly and filthy places. It is an animal associated with the Water element, which is why *Kan* is represented by the pig.

The offspring of a pig is called a *tun* <note>original: 豚; commonly used today to mean pork or piglet</note>. If a litter produces three offspring, they are called *zong*; if two are born, they are called *shi*; if only one is born, it is called *te*. The female pig is called a *ba*.

The *Explaining Graphs and Analyzing Characters* <note>original: 說文 (Shuōwén Jiézì); a Han Dynasty dictionary</note> also refers to a small piglet as a *gu*. At three months old, it is called a *zong*; at six months, it is called a *jian*. Others say that at one year old it is a *zong*; at two years, when they are able to grapple and clash with one another, they are called *pa*; and at three years, when their shoulders reach the same height, they are called *ba*. Although these accounts differ from the *Approaching the Refined* <note>original: 爾雅 (Ěryǎ); the oldest surviving Chinese encyclopedia</note>, they are all names for young pigs, and thus they are recorded here in detail.

# The Pangolin

The pangolin has four feet and resembles a <term>crocodilian</term><note>original: 鼉 (tuó); specifically the Chinese alligator, which was well known to scholars of this period.</note> but is shorter and smaller in stature. Its body is shaped like an otter's and is covered entirely in overlapping scales. It lives in earthen burrows; it is actually a type of land mammal and not a species of fish. However, because the color and appearance of its scales resemble those of a carp, it is also called the "scaly carp." <note>original: 鯪鯉 (línglǐ). This name reflects the traditional "Scale" (鱗) category in Chinese taxonomy which grouped scaly mammals with fish.</note> 

Country folk call it the "mountain-penetrator" <note>original: 穿山甲 (chuānshānjiǎ); this remains the standard Chinese name for the pangolin today.</note> because its large tail enables it to tunnel through the earth. It is capable of moving both on land and in water. 

When it emerges onto a bank, it opens its scales and lies perfectly still as if it were dead. This lures ants to crawl into the gaps between its scales. Once the scales are full of ants, the pangolin suddenly snaps them shut and enters the water. There, it opens its scales again, causing the ants to float to the surface, where the pangolin promptly catches and eats them. For this reason, it is said to be able to control ant populations.

# The Sheep

Sheep are social by nature; whenever they form a flock, one male acts as the leader, and the entire group follows his direction. They also possess a disposition to constantly move forward. Anatomically, their intestines are coiled nine times. They are prone to skin diseases <note>original: 瘯 (cù). Likely referring to mange or scabies.</note>; if one becomes ill, they immediately contaminate one another.

The young are called <term>lambs</term><note>original: 羔 (gāo). This term is used for both lambs and kids.</note>. Specialized names are given based on the month of their birth: a lamb born in the fifth month is called a <term>ling</term><note>羚</note>; one born in the sixth month is called a <term>di</term><note>羝</note>; and one born in the seventh month is called a <term>qian</term><note>牽</note>. Those that have not yet reached a full year of age are called <term>zhu</term><note>羜</note>. 

Males are generally called <term>di</term><note>羝</note>. Specifically, a white ram from the Wu region is called a <term>fen</term><note>羒</note>, while a black ram of the Xia variety is called a <term>yu</term><note>羭</note>. Females are called <term>zang</term><note>牂</note>, and a female of the Xia variety is called a <term>gu</term><note>羖</note>.

The horns of the sheep are primarily used to improve eyesight, calm the mind, <term>nourish vital energy</term><note>original: 益氣 (yì qì). A core concept in traditional medicine involving the replenishment of the body's fundamental life force.</note>, and give one a feeling of lightness in the body. They are also believed to ward off malevolent spirits and ghosts.

Among the <term>Six Domestic Animals</term><note>The traditional grouping of animals essential to Chinese civilization: the horse, ox, sheep/goat, pig, dog, and chicken.</note>, the sheep is the most easily raised and the most prolific. Within the span of a single year, a mother may give birth to young, who then produce grandchildren, who in turn produce great-grandchildren. This is known as "three generations produced in one year."

# The Bear

The bear is a creature that resembles both a dog and a pig, yet it possesses feet like those of a human. Its fur is black in color. These animals emerge from their dens in the spring and enter hibernation during the winter. At the center of the chest, there is a white fat that resembles jade; it is exceptionally delicious in flavor. 

It is popularly called **Bear White** <note>original: "Xiong Bai" (熊白); a prized fat found on the back or chest of the bear, historically valued in Chinese cuisine and medicine</note>. Its gallbladder <note>referring to the bile</note> is said to shift locations with the seasons: it resides in the head during the spring, the belly in the summer, the left foot in the autumn, and the right foot in the winter. 

The bear is fond of climbing trees to draw in and circulate its breath, a behavior known as **Bear Hanging** <note>original: "Xiong Jing" (熊經); this refers to an ancient longevity exercise (Daoyin) where one mimics the stretching of a bear to promote health</note>. During its long winter hibernation, the bear does not eat; when it feels hunger, it simply licks its own paws. Because of this habit of "nourishing" itself through its feet, the most succulent part of the animal is found in the paws. 

When traveling through the mountains, they cover dozens of miles, always maintaining specific paths and places to lie in wait. These dens are invariably located within mountain crags or inside hollowed-out dead trees. The people who live in the mountains call these locations **Bear Lodges** <note>original: "Xiong Guan" (熊舘)</note>. ### The Jūn

The nature of the Jūn <note>original: 麕; identified as the Chinese Water Deer (*Hydropotes inermis*)</note> is timid. # The Rat

The rat steals small items, emerging at night and hiding during the day; it is the most cunning of the burrowing creatures. Its varieties are extremely numerous. It enjoys preening and lifting its whiskers. By nature, it is suspicious; when leaving its burrow, it often hesitates to proceed, appearing to look in two directions at once. Because of this, those who are indecisive or wavering are referred to as <term>shoushu</term> <note>Literally "head-rat"; a Chinese idiom for hesitation (shǒushǔ liǎngduān).</note>. Currently, in the Hedong region, there are large rats that can stand upright like humans and cross their two front paws behind their necks; these are called <term>Ritual Rats</term>, also known as <term>Arched Rats</term>.

One variety is the <term>shrew</term> <note>original: 鼩鼠; qúshǔ</note>, the smallest of its kind. It is also called the <term>sweet-mouthed rat</term> because when it bites food—or even humans—one does not feel the pain at the time <note>This likely refers to the belief that certain shrews possessed a numbing saliva.</note>.

Another variety is the <term>chestnut rat</term> <note>original: 栗鼠; lìshǔ</note>, which is dark grey and small. The hairs from its tail can be harvested to make calligraphy brushes. One variety is the <term>mole rat</term> <note>original: 鼢鼠; fénshǔ</note>, which enjoys eating bamboo, and is thus called the <term>bamboo-fen</term>.

Another variety is the <term>mole</term> <note>original: 鼴鼠; yǎnshǔ</note>. It is shaped like a rat but is large and lacks a tail. It is black in color with a very strong, long nose. It constantly travels by tunneling through the earth; if it sees the light of the sun or the moon, it will die. They are found beneath the trees in the forests of deep mountains. The *Shuowen* <note>The *Shuowen Jiezi*, an influential 2nd-century Chinese dictionary of glyphs and meanings.</note> states that it is a creature transformed from a shrike <note>A predatory bird; traditional Chinese natural history often included "metamorphosis" theories where one species turned into another.</note>. # Otter

The <term>otter</term> <note>original: 獺; tà</note> resembles a small dog. It lives in the water and feeds on fish. Generally, during the first month of the lunar year, it kills fish and arranges them on all four sides along the water’s edge; this behavior is known as the <term>fish sacrifice</term> <note>original: 祭魚; jìyú — an ancient phenological observation signifying the beginning of spring</note>. 

By nature, it understands the rising and falling of water levels. When digging its burrow, it invariably estimates the height beforehand to ensure the water will not reach its den. Local villagers use this behavior as a seasonal omen for <term>impending floods</term> <note>original: 潦水; liǎoshuǐ</note>. 

According to ancient accounts, while the number of <term>liver lobes</term> <note>original: 肝葉; gānyè</note> in most animals is fixed, the otter’s liver is unique: it grows one lobe for every month, such that by the twelfth month, it possesses twelve lobes <note>This folk belief likely stems from the otter's association with the lunar cycle and water</note>. 

There is also another variety known as the <term>biantà</term> <note>original: 獱獺; biāntà — possibly referring to a larger species like the sea otter or a legendary aquatic mammal</note>. It is large in form with a head like a horse and a body resembling a bat; it dwells within hollow trees and feeds on fish.

**Vocabulary:** Otter, Fish sacrifice, Flooding, Biantà (Giant Otter), Liver lobes

# The Flying Squirrel <gloss>namely the Ear-Rat</gloss>

The **flying squirrel** <note>original: 鼯; wú</note> is shaped like a small fox. Its wings are generally like those of a bat <note>original: 服翼; fúyì — an archaic term for bats</note>. The fur on its wings, tail, neck, and flanks is a reddish-purple, while its back is the color of pale mugwort <note>a greyish-green</note> and its underbelly is yellow. Its snout and chin are mottled with white; it has short legs, long claws, and a tail roughly three feet in length. 

It prefers to travel in the darkness of night. It flies and also nurses its young <note>referring to it being a mammal despite its flight</note>, which is why it is also called the **"Flying-Birth"** <note>original: 飛生; fēishēng</note>. Its cry sounds like a human calling out. It is said to eat fire and smoke. It has the ability to glide from high places down to the ground, but it cannot fly upward from below.

The philosopher **Xunzi** <note>a third-century BCE Confucian scholar</note> said: "The flying squirrel has <term>five skills but is at its wit's end</term> <note>original: 五技而窮; wǔ jì ér qióng — a famous idiom regarding someone with many mediocre talents but no mastery</note>." This means it can fly but cannot reach the roof of a house; it can climb but cannot reach the very top of a tree; it can swim but cannot cross a valley; it can burrow but cannot hide its body; and it can run but cannot outrun a man. Although it possesses many talents, every one of them has its limits.

It is also known by the names *Yiyou*, *Xiu*, the "Flying Bat," and simply the "Flying Squirrel."

The *Classic of Mountains and Seas* <note>original: 山海經; Shanhaijing — a foundational Chinese text describing mythic geography and beasts</note> states: "On **Mount Danxun**, there is a beast. Its shape is like a rat, but it has the head of a rabbit and the ears of an elk. Its voice is like a howling dog, and it flies by means of its tail. It is called the **Ear-Rat** <note>original: 耳鼠; ěr shǔ</note>."

# The Mule <gloss>original: 驘; luó</gloss>

The **mule** is the offspring of a female horse <gloss>mare</gloss> and a male donkey. It resembles a donkey in appearance but is more robust than a horse. In former times, these animals were not produced within the Central Kingdoms; for this reason, the *Book of Han* <note>The classic history of the Han Dynasty, compiled in the 1st century CE.</note> recorded them as "extraordinary livestock" belonging to the **Xiongnu** <note>A powerful confederation of nomadic tribes on the northern borders of ancient China.</note>.

A horse’s strength is located in its shoulders, whereas a donkey’s strength is in its hindquarters <gloss>original: 髁; kē; hocks</gloss>. A **mule’s** strength, however, is concentrated in its waist. Because the mule possesses a **locked bone** <term>suǒ gǔ; traditionally believed to be a fused bone in the pelvic or thigh region</term> in its thighs, it is unable to reproduce. 

In its physical form, it has large bones like the **ju** <note>An ancient term for a similar equine hybrid, often mentioned alongside the mule.</note> but a small mouth. Its smaller bones are shaped like pestles, yet its head is large. Because a small mouth cannot successfully deliver a large-headed offspring, the mule cannot give birth. This is the reason why the mule does not multiply or breed.

There are also instances where a horse and a cow mate to produce offspring; in such cases, the cow is always the mother <gloss>female</gloss> and the horse is the father <gloss>male</gloss>. 

Today, both the **Sichuan** and **Guang** <note>Refers to the southern regions of Guangdong and Guangxi.</note> regions produce mules. Those born in the **Guang** region have light hooves, while those born in **Sichuan** have heavy hooves. This is the physical legacy of the **Dawu** <note>literally "Great Martial"; likely referring to a specific lineage of heavy-set war horses or a particular breed known for its size.</note>.

# The Mule <gloss>original: 驘; luó</gloss>

The **mule** is the offspring of a female horse <gloss>mare</gloss> and a male donkey. It resembles a donkey in appearance but is more robust than a horse. A horse’s strength is located in its shoulders, whereas a donkey’s strength is in its hindquarters <gloss>original: 髁; kē; hocks or thighs</gloss>. A **mule’s** strength, however, is concentrated in its waist. Because the mule possesses a **locked bone** <term>suǒ gǔ; traditionally believed to be a fused or rigid bone structure in the pelvic or thigh region</term> in its thighs, it is unable to reproduce. 

In its physical form, it has large bones like the **ju** <note>An ancient term for a similar equine hybrid, often mentioned in classical literature alongside the mule.</note> but a small mouth. Its smaller bones are shaped like pestles, yet its head is large. Because a small mouth cannot successfully deliver a large-headed offspring, the mule cannot give birth. This is the reason why the mule does not multiply or breed.

## The Fox <note>original: 狐; hú</note>

The **fox** is a **demonic beast** <note>original: 妖獸; yāoshòu; creatures capable of transformation or supernatural feats</note> that ghosts ride upon. Its physical form is characterized by a **pointed snout** and a **large, bushy tail**. 

Commentators say that foxes are the transformations of **wanton women** <gloss>original: 淫婦; yínfù</gloss> from ancient times. Its name is **Zi** <gloss>Purple</gloss>, and it often refers to itself as **"A-Zi"** <note>Lady Purple; a name frequently appearing in early medieval "strange tales" to denote a fox spirit in human form</note>. It is exceptionally skilled at **enchanting and seducing** people; for this reason, the term **"fox-enchantment"** <note>original: 狐媚; húmèi; a term used to describe a bewitching or deceptive charm</note> was coined. 

By nature, the fox is **highly suspicious**. When the ice on a river begins to set, the fox will listen to the water flowing beneath the surface. Only if it hears **no sound of water** will it venture to cross. People often follow the fox’s lead <gloss>taking its tracks as a guide for safe passage</gloss>. 

The *Explaining Graphs and Analyzing Characters* <note>original: *Shuowen Jiezi*; the foundational Chinese dictionary compiled by Xu Shen around 100 CE</note> states that the fox possesses **three virtues** <note>original: 三德; sāndé</note>: its color is **centered and harmonious** <note>The yellowish-brown color of the fox was associated with the "Center" and the Earth element, considered auspicious</note>; it is **small in the front and large in the back**; and when it dies, it always **points its head toward its home hill** <term>qiūshǒu; a famous literary metaphor for someone who remembers their origins or longs for home at the end of life</term>.

# The Musk Deer <note>original: 麝; shè</note>

The **Musk Deer** is like a small **river deer** <note>original: 獐; zhāng</note> but possesses a powerful **fragrance**. For this reason, its written character is composed of the radical for "deer" (鹿) and the component "to shoot" (射), suggesting how its scent "shoots" out and reaches people even from a great distance. It possesses markings on its coat similar to those of a **tiger or leopard**. Today, they are found in great numbers within the **mountains of Shang and Ru** <note>Refers to the Shangzhou and Ruzhou regions in modern-day Shaanxi and Henan provinces.</note>.

When herds of musk deer leave their **droppings**, they always return to one specific location. Even if they wander far away in search of food, they will inevitably return to that spot. They do not dare leave traces in other places, fearing they might be captured by humans. However, hunters conversely use this habit to track them; by following the scent to these locations, they can surround the entire herd and capture them.

Generally, the musk deer is extremely protective of its **musk gland** <note>The aromatic secretion found in a sac near the male's abdomen.</note>. Whenever it is pursued by hunters and the situation becomes desperate, it will throw itself from a **high cliff** and use its own claws to **gouge out its musk**. After doing so, it allows itself to be bound and faces death.

# The Cat

The **cat** is a fierce one among the small domestic animals. Its nature is **Yin** <note>original: 性陰; xìng yīn. In traditional Chinese philosophy, Yin represents the cool, dark, and receptive principle. Here, the text suggests that because the cat is inherently Yin, it constantly seeks external warmth to balance itself.</note>, and thus it **dreads the cold**; even in the height of summer, it does not hesitate to lie in the sun. 

The tip of its nose is cold and moist throughout the four seasons, becoming warm only during the **Summer Solstice** <note>The longest day of the year, representing the peak of "Yang" or heat energy.</note>. The pupils of its eyes are round in the morning and evening, but at midday, they narrow into a vertical line. After a cat has gained experience in **catching mice**, its ears develop notches resembling the teeth of a saw. This is much like the belief that a **tiger** develops saw-toothed ears after it has devoured a human.

<term>Yin (陰): One of the two fundamental forces in Chinese cosmology. Cats were often associated with the night and the domestic sphere, both Yin domains.</term>
<term>Catching mice (捕鼠): Beyond being pets, cats were primarily valued in historical China as essential protectors of grain stores and libraries against rodents.</term>
<term>Tiger (虎): In Chinese lore, the cat was often seen as the "teacher" or "uncle" of the tiger, explaining the many shared traits described in traditional natural histories.</term>
