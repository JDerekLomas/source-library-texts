## NOTES AND SKETCHES ON HORSES

The equine <note>original: "hippiques"</note> works of Leonardo da Vinci confirm, to the highest degree, his incessant desire to learn and his need to classify the teachings of nature in his memory. He achieved this by fixing the most important divisions of the horse's structure through a series of lines marking proportional limits that were easy to remember. The Master arrived at this by means of a fixed base, which was the length of the animal's head.

It was in this way that Leonardo invented the equine canon <term>canon</term> <note>A rule or standard of mathematical proportions used in art to create a "perfect" or harmonious representation of a figure.</note>; one can therefore be certain that anyone with a special interest in the horse will derive serious instruction from his studies.

The classification of the sketches and drawings, published by Mr. Édouard Rouveyre, begins with those depicting the heads of horses in all positions, on folios 1 to 6.

Next come the forelimbs: folio 7 represents an animal seen from the front and standing squarely; it is exactly similar, regarding the distance between its feet, to the one published by Bourgelat <note>Claude Bourgelat (1712–1779), the founder of scientific veterinary medicine in France.</note> in 1770, which contained the explanation of the geometrical proportions of the horse written by Vincent, a professor at Alfort <note>The National Veterinary School of Alfort, near Paris, one of the oldest veterinary institutions in the world.</note>.

Afterward, from 8 to 20, the forelimbs are represented, sometimes in a weight-bearing position <note>original: "à l'appui"</note>, sometimes raised <note>original: "au soutien"</note>, with all the variations of posture produced by their musculature.

Then, folios 21 to 25 contain the hindquarters, especially the poses of a gallop transitioning into a rear, having the angle made by the legs with the ground very open toward the front—a general form—

The scrupulous analysis of its photograph, used to explain the equine canon <note>The "equine canon" refers to the standard set of mathematical proportions used to depict an ideal horse in art.</note>, agrees with the principal dimensions of Leonardo da Vinci’s horse, although the appearance of his animal is heavy and lacks grace in its relative stiffness. One must look back to the limited knowledge of equine drawing available at the end of the fifteenth and the beginning of the sixteenth century.

Leaf 30 recto represents a horse whose limbs are very lightly indicated; and, although its body is a bit short, it is already more finely drawn than the one on leaf 28. The croup <note>The top of the horse's hindquarters.</note> is less rounded there than in all the drawings dedicated to the studies of the Sforza monument <note>A massive bronze equestrian statue commissioned by Ludovico Sforza, Duke of Milan, which Leonardo worked on for years but never finished.</note>. Here, it seems that Leonardo intended to emphasize the profile of the anterior part of the chest, where the muscular indications, as well as those of the shoulder, are of great accuracy; but, what one will notice—and what will always dominate in the artist's equines—is the thickness of the neck, the boundaries of which contribute to giving the *splenius* <note>A broad, flat muscle at the back of the neck; Leonardo often exaggerated this to show power.</note> an intentional development. This repetition persists constantly throughout the work and makes the animals' heads appear small.

Leaves 32 to 42 represent studies intended for the Sforza statue.

Then come leaves 43, 44 recto, 45, and 46, which feature the great gallop.

Finally, leaves 47 to 56 contain designs for horses agitated in every direction; and a few sketches relating to the casting <note>The technical process of pouring molten metal into a mold to create a bronze statue.</note> conclude these two volumes, the publication of which is of the highest interest and does great honor to Mr. Édouard Rouveyre <note>A prominent 19th-century French publisher known for reproducing Leonardo's manuscripts.</note>.

### NOTES ON THE PLATES

The drawings reproduced in this first part of the work belong to the period of Leonardo's greatest maturity. <note>Likely referring to the period around 1490–1510 when Leonardo was most active in his anatomical investigations.</note>

In these sheets, the Master <note>A traditional title for Leonardo da Vinci</note> does not limit himself to the simple external representation of the animal, but investigates the *inner mechanics* of the body. One can observe:

*   The precise study of the **myology** <term>the study of the muscular system</term> of the limbs.
*   The proportions of the horse, measured with mathematical rigor.
*   The tension of the tendons during movement.

<margin>See the notes on Sheet 13 regarding the "proportions of the Sicilian horse."</margin>

Most of these drawings were executed with **red chalk** <note>A natural clay pigment often used by Renaissance artists for its warmth and ability to create soft gradients</note> or **pen and ink**. Some sheets contain <insert>marginal annotations</insert> written in Leonardo's characteristic *mirror writing* <note>Leonardo wrote from right to left, which can be read normally when held up to a mirror</note>.

### Description of Drawings

This manuscript page is a collection of dynamic sketches focusing on the anatomy and expression of horses.
