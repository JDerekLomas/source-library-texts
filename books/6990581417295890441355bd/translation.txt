# INTRODUCTION.

The present book seeks to unite the borderlands of sciences which, although directed toward one another by many natural relationships, have hitherto stood quite separately alongside each other; namely, the borderlands of *physical* and *physiological acoustics* on the one hand, and *musicology* and *aesthetics* on the other. It addresses itself, therefore, to a circle of readers who have undergone very diverse educational paths and pursue very different interests; it will therefore not be unnecessary if the author declares from the very outset in what sense he has undertaken this work, and what goal he has sought to achieve thereby. The *scientific*, the *philosophical*, and the *artistic* horizons have in recent times moved further apart than is reasonable, and there exists therefore in each of these circles a certain difficulty in understanding the language, the methods, and the purposes of the others. This difficulty may have been the primary reason why the task to be pursued here has not long ago been more thoroughly elaborated and brought toward its solution.

# Introduction.

Musicology <note>original: "Musikwissenschaft"</note> has remained a purely external pursuit, more a sign that the need for a connection between the aforementioned sciences was felt and acknowledged than that anyone actually knew how to establish such a connection. For while physical knowledge could certainly be of use to the builder of musical instruments, the physical introduction has remained quite fruitless for the further development and foundation of the theory of harmony <note>original: "Harmonielehre"</note>. And yet, the most essential facts of this field, the explanation and exploitation of which were the primary concern, have been known since ancient times. Even Pythagoras knew that if strings of the same quality and tension, but of unequal length, were to produce the perfect consonances of the octave, fifth, or fourth, their lengths must stand respectively in the ratios of 1 to 2, 2 to 3, or 3 to 4; and if, as is to be suspected, he received his knowledge in part from the Egyptian priests, it is impossible to see how far back into immemorial times the knowledge of this law reaches. Modern physics has expanded Pythagoras's law by moving from string lengths to vibration numbers <note>term: "Schwingungszahlen" - what we now call frequency (Hertz)</note>, whereby it became applicable to the tones of all musical instruments; furthermore, for the less perfect consonances of the thirds, the numerical ratios of 4 to 5 and 5 to 6 were added to those mentioned above. However, I am not aware that any real progress has been made in answering the question: what do musical consonances have to do with the ratios of the first six whole numbers? Musicians, as well as philosophers and physicists, have mostly rested content with the answer that the human soul can, in some way unknown to us, determine the numerical ratios of the sound vibrations, and that it takes a special pleasure in having simple and easily comprehensible ratios before it.

In the meantime, the aesthetics of music has made unmistakable progress in those questions whose decision rests more on psychological than on sensory factors, notably by emphasizing the concept of motion in the investigation of musical works of art. # Plan of the Investigation.

This theory of **sensations of hearing** <note>original: "Gehörempfindungen"</note> falls within the domain of the natural sciences, specifically within **physiological acoustics**. Until now, the **theory of sound** has been treated almost exclusively from its **physical side**; that is, researchers have investigated the movements performed by sounding solid, liquid, or gaseous bodies when they produce a sound perceptible to the ear. This **physical acoustics** is, in its essence, nothing more than a branch of the **theory of the motions of elastic bodies**. Whether one observes the vibrations performed by stretched strings on a spiral of brass wire—whose movements occur so slowly that the eye can easily follow them, but which for that very reason excite no **sensation of sound**—or whether one allows a violin string to vibrate—whose oscillations the eye can scarcely perceive while the ear hears them—is, from a physical perspective, entirely indifferent. The laws of vibrating motions are exactly the same in both cases; whether they occur quickly or slowly is a difference that changes nothing in these laws. It merely forces the observing physicist to employ different methods of observation, using sometimes the eye and sometimes the ear. In physical acoustics, therefore, the phenomena of hearing are only taken into account because the ear is the most convenient and immediate tool for observing rapid elastic vibrations, and the physicist must understand the peculiarities of this natural instrument used for observation in order to draw correct conclusions from its reports. Consequently, while traditional physical acoustics has indeed gathered various insights and observations belonging to the **theory of the ear's activities**—that is, **physiological acoustics**—these have not been established as the primary goal of its investigations, but only incidentally and piecemeal. The fact that a special chapter on **acoustics** is usually separated in **physics** from the **theory of the motions of elastic bodies**, to which it should essentially belong, is justified only by the fact that the use of the ear has led to unique types of experiments and methods of observation.

## On the Sensation of Sound in General.

Sensory sensations are brought about when external stimuli act upon the sensitive nerve apparatuses of our body and place them in a state of excitation. The type of sensation differs, partly according to the sensory organ that has been called upon, and partly according to the nature of the acting stimulus. Each sensory organ conveys peculiar sensations which cannot be excited by any other: the eye conveys sensations of light, the ear sensations of sound, and the skin sensations of touch. Even if the same solar rays which excite the sensation of light in the eye strike the skin and excite its nerves, they are felt here only as heat, not as light; and likewise, the vibrations of elastic bodies which the ear hears can be felt by the skin, but not as sound, rather as a whirring <note>The author is distinguishing between the auditory perception of frequency and the tactile perception of vibration</note>. Sensation of sound is therefore the mode of reaction peculiar to the ear against external stimuli; it can be produced in no other organ of the body and is entirely distinct from all sensations of all other senses.

Since we have here set ourselves the task of studying the laws of the sensations of hearing <note>original: "Gehörempfindungen"</note>, our first task will be to investigate how many different types of sensations our ear can produce, and which differences in the external means of excitation—namely, the sound—correspond to these differences in sensation.

# First Division. First Section.

The first and principal difference between various types of sound that our ear detects is the distinction between noises and musical tones. The whistling, howling, and hissing of the wind, the splashing of water, and the rolling and rattling of a carriage are examples of the first kind; the tones of all musical instruments are examples of the second kind of sound. To be sure, noises and tones can mix in manifold and changing proportions and pass into one another through intermediate stages, but their extremes are widely separated from one another.

In order to determine the nature of the difference between tones and noises, an attentive observation by the ear alone is sufficient in most cases, without needing to be supported by artificial aids. It generally appears that during the course of a noise, a rapid change of diverse sound sensations occurs. Consider the rattling of a carriage on stone pavement, the splashing and roaring of a waterfall or sea waves, or the rustling of leaves in the forest. # Noise and Musical Tone

...we must first study the laws and peculiarities of these sensations.

We now arrive at the further question: what difference in the external means of exciting the auditory sensations causes the distinction between noise and musical tone? The normal and common means of excitation for the human ear are vibrations of the surrounding mass of air. The irregularly changing sensation of the ear during noises leads us to conclude that, in these cases, the vibration of the air must also be an irregularly changing type of motion; whereas musical tones must be based on a regular, uniformly sustained motion of the air. This, in turn, must be excited by an equally regular motion of the original sounding body, whose impulses are transmitted to the ear by the air.

Physical investigations have now taught us to recognize precisely the nature of such regular motions which produce a musical tone. These are <term>vibrations</term> <note>German: *Schwingungen*; back-and-forth movements</note>, that is, reciprocating motions of the sounding bodies, and these vibrations must be regular and periodic. By a <term>periodic motion</term>, we mean one which returns in exactly the same way after exactly equal intervals of time. The length of these equal intervals of time, which elapse between one repetition and the next of the same motion, we call the *vibration duration* or the *period* of the motion. The specific nature of the motion of the body during the duration of a single period is quite irrelevant here. To illustrate the concept of periodic motion with familiar examples, I cite the motion of a clock pendulum, the motion of a stone attached to a thread and swung in a circle at a constant speed, or the motion of a hammer that is lifted and dropped at regular intervals by the machinery of a water mill. All these motions, however differently they may be shaped in other respects, are periodic in the sense mentioned. The duration of their period, which in these cases usually amounts to one or several seconds, is however relatively long compared to the much shorter periods of sounding vibrations, of which—for the lowest tones—at least 30 occur in a single second.

# The Propagation of Sound

Imagine a stone has been thrown into a calm surface of water. Around the point where the stone struck the surface, a small ring of waves immediately forms, which expands into an ever-larger circle, progressing uniformly in all directions. Corresponding to these rings of waves, sound emanates from a point of vibration in the air and progresses in all directions as far as the boundaries of the air mass allow. The process in the air is essentially the same as on the surface of the water; the main difference is simply that sound spreads out in the spatially expansive "ocean of air" <note>original: "Luftmeere"; a poetic but common 19th-century scientific metaphor for the atmosphere</note> by progressing spherically on all sides, whereas waves on the surface of water can only progress in rings. The "crests" of the water waves correspond to layers in the sound waves where the air is compressed <term>compression: the part of a longitudinal wave where particles are closest together</term>, while the "troughs" correspond to thinned layers <term>rarefaction: the part of a wave where the density of the medium is reduced</term>. On the free surface of the water, the mass can escape upwards where it crowds together, thus forming the crests. Inside the ocean of air, the mass must compress because it has no room to escape.

The water waves, therefore, progress constantly forward without turning back; but one must not believe that the water particles of which the waves are composed have a similar forward motion to the waves themselves. We can easily make the movements of the water particles along the surface visible by letting a small piece of wood float on the water. Such a piece of wood fully mimics the movements of the neighboring water particles. Now, such a piece of wood is not carried along by the rings of waves, but is only rocked up and down, and finally remains at rest in the spot where it was first located. As with the piece of wood, so it is with the neighboring water particles. When the wave ring reaches them, they are set into oscillation; when it has passed by, they are back in their old place and remain at rest, while the wave ring progresses to ever-new parts of the water surface and sets them in motion. Thus, the waves that move across the water surface are continually built out of new water particles, so that what moves forward as a "wave" is only the vibration—the changed form of the surface—while the individual water particles move back and forth in temporary oscillations, but never move far from their original place.

In place of the stone, imagine a sounding body that vibrates the air; instead of the small piece of wood, imagine the human ear, which the vibration waves of the air strike and whose movable parts they set into motion. The air waves emanating from a sounding body transfer the vibration to the human ear in exactly the same way that water transfers it from the stone to the floating object.

From this, it will be easily seen how a body engaged in periodic vibration must also set the particles of air into a periodic motion. A falling stone gives the water surface only a single impact. Now, however, instead of a single stone, imagine a regular series of drops falling into the water from a vessel with a narrow opening. Each drop will excite a ring-shaped wave; each ring-shaped wave will run across the water surface just like its predecessor, and just as it followed the former, its successors will follow it. Thus, a regular series of concentric rings will arise and spread across the water surface. As many drops as fall into the water per second, just as many waves will strike our floating piece of wood per second, and it will be rocked up and down that many times—thus performing a periodic motion whose period is equal to the intervals of time in which the drops fall. In the same way, a periodically moving sounding body in the air produces a similar periodic motion, first of the air mass and then of the <term>eardrum</term><note>original: "Trommelfell"</note> in our ear, the duration of whose vibration must be equal to that of the sounding body.

By <term>timbre</term> <note>original: "Klangfarbe," literally "tone-color"</note> we understand that peculiarity by which the sound of a violin is distinguished from that of a flute, or a clarinet, or a human voice, even when all produce the same note at the same pitch. We must now explain, for these three main differences in sound, which specific peculiarities of sound motion correspond to them.

Regarding first the intensity of sounds, it is easy to recognize that this increases and decreases with the breadth (<term>amplitude</term>) of the vibrations of the sounding body. When we strike a string, its vibrations are initially extensive enough that we can see them; accordingly, its tone is initially at its strongest. Then the visible vibrations become smaller and smaller; in the same measure, the intensity of the tone decreases. We can make the same observation on bowed strings, the reeds of reed pipes, and many other sounding bodies. We must draw the same conclusion from the fact that the intensity of the sound decreases when we move away from the sounding body in the open air, while neither the pitch nor the timbre changes. At a distance, however, only the vibration amplitude of the individual air particles changes within the airwaves. The intensity of the sound must therefore depend on this, but none of its other properties \*).

The second essential difference between various sounds rests in their pitch. We already know from daily experience that tones of the same pitch can be produced by the most diverse instruments by means of the most diverse mechanical processes and in the most diverse intensities. # Pitch and Vibration Frequency

If the duration of the period of two sounds is equal, they possess the same pitch. Therefore: pitch depends solely on the duration of vibration or, what is synonymous, on the vibration frequency <note>The author uses "Schwingungszahl" (vibration number), which we now commonly call frequency.</note>. We are accustomed to using the second as our unit of time, and therefore understand vibration frequency to mean the number of vibrations which the sounding body performs in one second of time. It is self-evident that we find the duration of a vibration if we divide the second by the vibration frequency.

Sounds are higher in pitch the greater their vibration frequency is, or the smaller their vibration duration is.

To precisely determine the number of vibrations of such elastic bodies that produce audible tones is quite difficult, and physicists therefore had to adopt many relatively complicated procedures to be able to solve this task in each individual case. Mathematical theory and diverse experiments had to come to each other's aid toward that end. For the presentation of the fundamental facts in this field, it is therefore very convenient to be able to use a special musical instrument, the so-called **siren**, which makes it possible through its construction to directly determine the number of air vibrations that have produced the tone. The simplest form of the siren is represented in its main features in Fig. 1, according to **Seebeck** <note>August Seebeck (1805–1849), a German physicist who contributed significantly to the field of acoustics.</note>.

**A** is a thin disc made of cardboard or sheet metal, which can be rotated rapidly around its central axis **b** by means of the cord **ff** running around a larger wheel.

# Pitch and Vibration Frequency

The remarkable relationship between the vibration frequencies <note>original: "Schwingungszahlen"</note> of two tones that form a consonant interval with one another is noteworthy. If one creates a row of 8 holes and a row of 16 holes on a disc and blows air against them alternately while the rotation speed of the disc is kept constant, one will hear two tones that stand exactly in the ratio of an octave to each other. If the rotation speed of the disc is increased, both tones will become higher, but in the new pitch range, they will still form an octave with each other. From this, we conclude that a tone forming the higher octave of another makes exactly twice as many vibrations in the same amount of time as the latter.

The disc illustrated above in Fig. 1 has two rows of 8 and 12 holes. When both are blown alternately, they produce two tones that form an exact and pure fifth <note>term: Quinte (Fifth) - an interval spanning five notes of a western scale, such as C to G</note> with each other, regardless of the rotation speed of the disc. It follows that two tones stand in the ratio of a fifth when the higher one makes three vibrations in exactly the same time that the lower one makes two.

When a tone is blown on the row of 8 holes, we need 16 holes to obtain its octave and 12 holes to obtain its fifth. The vibration ratio of the fifth to the octave is therefore 12 : 16 or 3 : 4; however, the interval between the fifth and the octave is a fourth <note>term: Quarte (Fourth) - an interval spanning four notes, such as G to the C above it</note>. From this, we see that two tones form a fourth with each other when the higher one performs four vibrations in the same time that the lower one performs three.

The polyphonic siren designed by Dove <note>Heinrich Wilhelm Dove (1803–1879), a Prussian physicist</note> usually has four rows of 8, 10, 12, and 16 holes. The row of 16 holes gives the octave of the 8-hole row and the fourth of the 12-hole row; the row of 12 holes gives the fifth of the 8-hole row and the minor third of the 10-hole row; the latter gives the major third of the 8-hole row. Thus, the four rows produce the tones of a major chord <note>original: "Dur-Accord"</note>.

Through these and similar experiments, the following ratios of vibration frequencies are established:

When the fundamental tone of a given interval is moved an octave higher, this is called inverting the interval. Thus, the fourth is the inverted fifth, the minor sixth is the inverted major third, and the major sixth is the inverted minor third. The corresponding vibration ratio is determined by doubling the smaller number of the original interval.

From 2 : 3 of the fifth . . . . 3 : 4 the fourth,
from 4 : 5 of the major third . . 5 : 8 the minor sixth,
from 5 : 6 of the minor third . . 6 : 10 = 3 : 5 the major sixth.

These are all the consonant <note>Consonant refers to intervals that sound "stable" or "pleasant" together, as opposed to dissonant ones.</note> intervals that lie within an octave. Their vibration ratios, with the exception of the minor sixth—which indeed forms the most imperfect consonance among those mentioned—are all expressed by the whole numbers 1 to 6.

Thus, through relatively simple and easy experiments with the *siren* <note>A mechanical device used to measure the frequency of a tone by passing air through perforated rotating discs.</note>, one finds the remarkable law confirmed which we mentioned in the introduction: that the vibration numbers of consonant tones stand in the ratio of small whole numbers. In the course of our investigation, we will use the same instrument again to examine the strictness and accuracy of this law in even greater detail.

Long before anything was known about vibration numbers and their measurement, Pythagoras <note>Ancient Greek philosopher (c. 570–495 BC) credited with discovering the mathematical basis of musical intervals.</note> had discovered that if one wishes to divide a string with a bridge so that its two sections produce consonant tones, it must be divided in the ratio of the aforementioned whole numbers. If the bridge is placed so that 2/3 of the string remains on the right and 1/3 on the left, the two lengths stand in a ratio of 2 : 1 and produce the interval of an octave, with the longer piece of string producing the lower tone. If the bridge is placed so that 3/5 of the length lies on the right and 2/5 on the left, the ratio of the pieces is 3 : 2 and the tones form a fifth.

These measurements were already carried out with great accuracy by Greek musicians, and they had founded a rather elaborate system of tones upon them. For these measurements, a special instrument was used, the *monochord* <term>monochord: a scientific and musical instrument consisting of a single string stretched over a soundbox with a movable bridge</term>, on which a single string was stretched over a resonance box, beneath which was a scale <note>original: "Maasstab"</note> to enable the correct placement of the bridge.

It was only much later, through the work of **Galilei** (1638), **Newton**, **Euler** (1729), and **Daniel Bernoulli** (1771), that the laws of motion for strings were understood. Imagine what occurs in a space such as a ballroom. There, we have a number of musical instruments, people speaking, the rustling of dresses, gliding feet, clinking glasses, and so forth. All of these excite wave trains which shoot through the air of the hall, are thrown back from its walls, turn around, then strike another wall, are reflected once more, and so on until they die out. One must imagine that elongated waves, 8 to 12 feet long, emanate from the mouths of the men and from the deeper musical instruments; shorter ones, 2 to 4 feet long, from the lips of the women; and that the rustling of dresses produces a fine, small ripple of waves—in short, a jumble of the most diverse movements, which one can hardly imagine as being complex enough.

And yet the ear is capable of separating all the individual components of such a confused whole from one another. From this, we must conclude that all these different wave trains coexist in the air mass and do not disturb each other. How is it now possible that they coexist, since every individual wave train strives to produce its own particular value of compression or rarefaction <note>The "squeezing" and "stretching" of air molecules that creates sound</note>, and its own velocity of air particles in this or that direction at every point in the space? It is clear that at every single point in the air, at every moment in time, only a single degree of density can exist; the air particles can only execute one specific movement of a specific degree of speed and in a specific direction at a single instant.

What happens in such a case becomes directly visible to the eye in the waves of water. # Analysis of Composite Vibrations

One can survey a highly agitated water surface and, with considerable ease, separate the individual wave trains from one another and follow them individually. In this regard, the eye has a great advantage over the ear because it can survey a large expanse of the water surface simultaneously. It easily distinguishes whether the individual wave trains are straight or curved, whether they share the same center of curvature or not, and in which direction they are propagating. In all these observations, the eye gains just as many aids to distinguish whether two wave crests belong together or not, or rather to find the corresponding parts of the individual waves. Added to this is the fact that on the water's surface, waves of unequal wavelength travel at unequal speeds; thus, even if they coincide at any given moment so that they are difficult to separate, one train immediately rushes ahead while the other lags behind, and they soon become individually visible to the eye again. In this way, it is generally made very easy for the observer to relate each individual system to its specific place of origin and to keep it in view during its further progress. For the sense of sight, therefore, two wave systems can never merge if they have two different places of origin—for example, two wave rings resulting from two stones thrown into the water at different points. Even if the wave rings should coincide at one spot such that they are not easily separated, they will always remain separate throughout the largest part of their circumference. The eye, therefore, will not easily find itself in a position to mistake a composite wave motion for a simple one. # The Varying Range of Sympathetic Vibration

<margin>The duration of a tone affects its ability to resonate.</margin> are firmly attached, so that once struck, they produce a tone of noticeable duration.

If the pitch of the original sounding body is not exactly equal to that of the resonating body, the latter often still vibrates perceptibly—though the effect diminishes as the difference in pitch increases. In this regard, however, different sounding bodies show very large differences, depending on whether they continue to sound for a longer or shorter time after being struck and set into vibration before they have transferred their motion to the air.

Bodies of low mass, which easily transfer their motion to the air and fade out quickly—such as stretched membranes or the strings of a violin—are easily set into <term>sympathetic vibration</term> <note>original: "Mitschwingung"; the phenomenon where one object vibrates because it is exposed to the sound waves of another object at a similar frequency</note>. This is because the movement of the air is easily transferred back onto them, and they are perceptibly moved even by sufficiently strong air vibrations that do not have exactly the same <term>vibration period</term> <note>the time taken for one complete cycle of a sound wave</note> as the body's own natural tone. Therefore, the limits of the pitch range that can induce sympathetic vibration are a bit wider for these objects. Due to the relatively greater influence of air movement on such light and less resistant elastic bodies, their own vibration period can be slightly altered so that it adapts to that of the exciting tone.

On the other hand, massive and heavy elastic bodies that transfer their sound motion only slowly to the air—such as bells and metal plates—and which ring for a long time, are also difficult to set into motion via the air. A much longer "addition of effects" <note>the cumulative buildup of energy over many sound waves</note> is required, and it is therefore necessary to maintain the pitch of their own tone much more strictly if one wishes to set them into sympathetic vibration. However, it is well known that bell-shaped glasses can be set into violent motion by singing their own natural tone into them; it is even told that singers with strong and pure voices have brought such glasses to vibrate so strongly that they shattered. The main difficulty in this experiment is simply maintaining the pitch as steadily, accurately, and for as long as is necessary while exerting the voice strongly.

Strings differ somewhat from the previously mentioned vibrating bodies only in that their various <term>modes of vibration</term> produce tones that correspond to the harmonic <term>overtones</term> of the <term>fundamental tone</term>. # Sympathetic Vibration of Strings

The patterns shown in Figure 17 emerge most clearly when one places a struck tuning fork—sounding the pitch corresponding to that specific vibrational mode—onto the soundboard. If one wishes to produce a specific number of **nodes** <note>points along a vibrating string that remain stationary</note> without requiring every point on the string to perform simple vibrations, it is sufficient to lightly touch one of the desired nodal points with a finger while plucking or bowing the string. By touching the string with the finger, one damps all those simple vibrations that do not have a node at the touched location; only those vibrations remain which allow the string to rest at that spot.

The number of nodes can become quite large in long, thin strings, until finally the segments of the string between each node become too short and stiff to produce a sound. Very fine strings, therefore, produce more high tones than thicker ones. On the violin, or on the lower piano strings, one can still produce tones with ten divisions of the string; on very fine wire strings, however, one can even make tones speak with 16 or 20 divisions of the string.

The vibrational forms of strings described thus far are those in which every point of the string moves back and forth in a **pendulum-like** <note>sinusoidal or simple harmonic motion</note> vibration. These movements, therefore, always excite the sensation of only a single tone in the ear. In all other forms of string movement, the vibrations are not simply pendulum-like, but occur according to a different, more complicated law. This is always the case when one plucks the string in the usual way with the fingers (guitar, harp, zither), strikes it (piano), or draws a violin bow across it. The resulting movements can be viewed as if they were composed of many simple vibrations, which individually correspond to those depicted in Figure 17. The variety of such composite vibrational forms is infinitely large; indeed, the string can take on any arbitrary shape during its movement (provided that one always limits oneself to very small deviations from the position of equilibrium), because from a number of such simple waves, as shown in Figure 17 *a*, *b*, *c*, *d*, any arbitrary wave form can be composed according to what was stated in the second section. # First Division. Third Section.

Accordingly, a plucked string allows a large number of harmonic overtones to be heard alongside its fundamental tone <note>The "fundamental tone" is the primary pitch we perceive, while "overtones" are higher, softer frequencies that give an instrument its unique character.</note>; as a rule, the finer the string, the more overtones are present. The peculiar tinkling sound of very fine metal strings clearly owes its origin to these high secondary tones. With the help of resonators, one can easily distinguish tones up to the sixteenth overtone. The higher ones move too close to one another to be clearly separated.

Therefore, if a string is set into sympathetic vibration <term>sympathetic vibration: when an object vibrates because it is exposed to sound waves that match its natural frequency</term> by a musical sound excited in the surrounding air space—provided that sound corresponds in pitch to the string's fundamental tone—then, as a rule, a whole series of different simple vibration forms of the string will be excited simultaneously. This occurs because when the fundamental tone of the sound corresponds to the fundamental tone of the string, all the harmonic overtones of the sound also correspond to the overtones of the string, and can therefore excite the corresponding vibration form of the string. In general, the string will be brought into sympathetic vibration by air vibrations as often as there are components within those air vibrations (when decomposed into simple vibrations) whose vibration period corresponds to one of the string's tones. As a rule, however, if one such component is present, several others will also be found, and in many cases, it will be difficult to determine which specific tones among those the string can produce have set it in motion. For this reason, ordinary unloaded strings are not as useful as membranes or the air masses of resonators for identifying the tones present in a mass of sound through sympathetic vibration.

To conduct experiments on the piano regarding the sympathetic vibration of strings, one should lift the lid of the instrument so that the strings lie exposed, then slowly press down the key of the string intended to vibrate—for example, *c'*—without letting the hammer strike, and place a small splinter of wood across the strings of *c'*. One will find that the splinter is set in motion, or even thrown off, when certain other strings of the piano are struck; the movement of the splinter is strongest when one of the undertones <note>original: "Untertöne"</note> of *c'* is struck, namely *c, F, C, Ab₁, F₁, D₁* or *C₁*. A more moderate movement also occurs when one of the overtones of *c'* is struck, such as *c'', g''* or *c'''*, though in the latter case the little piece of wood remains lying still if it is placed on the relevant nodal points <term>nodal points: specific spots on a vibrating string that remain stationary while the rest of the string moves</term>
