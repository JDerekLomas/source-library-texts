## An Analytical Exposition on the Wheelwrights' Crafting of the Hub

The Wheelwrights construct wheels. To do so, they require three primary components: the **hub**, the **spokes**, and the **felloe** <note>The "felloe" (yá) is the outer wooden rim of the wheel.</note>. Let us now examine their specific methods for crafting the hub.

In the *Record* <note>The "Artificers' Record" (Kao Gong Ji), the oldest known technical encyclopedia of Chinese craft.</note>, the sequence of operations is laid out with such meticulous order that the logic cannot be disturbed. It begins with the **felling of the timber**. Next, it describes **seasoning the wood with fire**. Following this, it explains how the **intended length of the hub** dictates its **circumference**. Then, it details the method for **clamping the hub** <note>original: 拑 (qián). This refers to the structural reinforcement or the sockets where spokes are joined.</note>, which is determined by the "protective girth." This is followed by the method for **wrapping the hub**. Finally, it describes the **placement of the spokes**. Only after the hub is fully addressed does the text move on to the spokes and the felloe, thereby completing the Wheelwright's duties.

Master Zheng’s commentary <note>Zheng Xuan (127–200 CE), the foundational commentator on the Rites of Zhou.</note> states: The "protective girth" (*fáng*) should be one-third of the measurement. Jiang Ruitian <note>Jiang Yong (1681–1762), a renowned Qing dynasty philologist and mathematician who specialized in ancient technology.</note> argues: *Fáng* means "remainder"; it also refers to "division" or "the grain of the wood." Just as in geography where embankments and boundaries (*fáng*) create divisions in the land, in the grain of wood, the <term>junctions</term> <note>original: 拑 (qián). In woodworking, this refers to the structural divisions or mortise points.</note> are the points of division. By concentrating the structural integrity at these junctions, the hub is formed. Consider how the ribs of a body are not a single piece. Legend says a "Celestial Horse" has more than thirteen ribs, extraordinary steeds have sixteen, and the tortoise shells of the Yuan River possess nine additional segments. Thus, a whole is formed through the careful assembly of these divided parts.

Theorists all agree that the **protective girth** follows a logical proportion, yet their definitions of this term are not uniform. From among these varying views, one must be singled out.

The term **protective girth** (*fáng*) is also understood to mean "remainder" or "excess." The *Royal Regulations* <note>The "Wang Zhi," a chapter in the *Book of Rites* (Li Ji) detailing administrative and ritual standards.</note> states that for sacrifices, one uses a *lè* <note>original: 仂. A term for a fractional remainder or a tithe.</note> of the total quantity, and for mourning rituals, a *lè* of three years' resources. The commentary defines *lè* as one-tenth. 

If one-tenth can be called *lè*, then one-third might likewise be called *fáng*. However, the theory of one-tenth rests upon the authority of the *Royal Regulations*; this evidence is sufficient to challenge the theory of one-third. Regarding the **measurement of the sockets** <note>original: 揲藪 (shé sǒu). *Shé* means to calculate or count out; *sǒu* refers to the hollow center of the hub or the mortises where the spokes are inserted.</note> in the context of the one-third measurement, a close reading of the surrounding text suggests that one cannot definitively rule out the one-tenth proportion. 

If the center of the hub is taken as the socket being measured, then the "excess" of the hub's circumference is one-third. <gloss>This is the view held by Master Zheng Xuan and Jiang Yong.</gloss> But if the depth of the spoke mortise at the hub's edge is taken as the socket being measured, then the "excess" is one-tenth. <gloss>This follows the commentary on the *Royal Regulations*.</gloss>

I contend that the theory regarding the mortise depth is superior. In this view, the **protective girth** is one-tenth of the hub's circumference. The precise figure is three inches, two *fēn*, five *lí*, four *háo*, one *sī*, and six-and-two-thirds *hū* <note>These are traditional Chinese decimal units of length: 1 inch = 10 fēn = 100 lí = 1,000 háo = 10,000 sī = 100,000 hū.</note>. <gloss>The total circumference of the hub is detailed in my "Exposition on the Length and Circularity of the Hub."</gloss> Using this figure, one measures the sockets to determine the depth of the mortise.

# The Wheelwright’s Construction of the Hub, Part II

The **mortise-side** <note>original: 濞 (bì). This refers to the portion of the hub's side or the protective protrusion where the spoke meets the hub.</note> measures three inches; this is the established number used for the **protective girth** <note>original: 防 (fáng).</note>. How do we know it is limited to exactly three inches? We know this by measuring the mortise-side.

This measurement determines the **spoke width** <note>original: 輻廣 (fú guǎng). The breadth of the individual wooden spokes.</note>. The spoke width is three inches. How do we know the spoke width is limited to three inches? In the section on the **Chariot-maker** <note>The *Chē Rén*, an official in the *Rites of Zhou* responsible for constructing heavy-duty vehicles like wagons, as opposed to the *Lún Rén* (Wheelwright) who specialized in wheels for lighter carriages.</note>, the **Big Chariot's** spoke width is three inches. For the **Cypress Chariot** and the **Sheep Chariot** <note>Classifications of ancient vehicles: the Big Chariot was for transport/agriculture; the Cypress and Sheep Chariots were lighter or specialized vehicles.</note>, the spoke width is not explicitly mentioned, but we can infer it is also three inches. Therefore, regardless of whether the wheel is large or small, the width of a spoke must not exceed three inches, nor can it be less than three inches.

<gloss>Note: When the *Chariot-maker* constructs the three types of vehicles—the Big Chariot, the Cypress Chariot, and the Sheep Chariot—the text specifies for the Big Chariot the measurements for hub length, hub circumference, spoke length, spoke breadth and thickness, the number of rim segments (*qū*), and the rim circumference. For the Cypress Chariot, it only lists the hub length, hub circumference, spoke length, and rim measurements. It omits the spoke's breadth and thickness because they are identical to those of the Big Chariot. For the Sheep Chariot, these are also not mentioned, showing that all three vehicles are the same in this regard. If the Sheep Chariot entry lacks the values for hub length, hub circumference, spoke length, and rim measurements, it is because these five values are identical to the Cypress Chariot. For the Big Chariot, the "height" (*chóng*)—the total diameter obtained by combining the socket diameter, spoke length, and hub—is mentioned; for the Cypress Chariot, this is not stated but can be deduced by working backward. For the Sheep Chariot, it is omitted as it matches the Cypress. Finally, the Big Chariot mentions the number of binding cords (*táo*) and the "female-fitting" joints (*pìn fú*). For the other two, only the joints are mentioned because even on the Big Chariot, the cord count does not exceed an inch, and on smaller vehicles, it is reduced to two-thirds of an inch.</gloss>

The wheels produced by the **Wheelwright** have a hub circumference smaller than the three vehicles made by the **Chariot-maker**. Would their spoke width actually be increased instead? Master Zheng <note>Zheng Xuan (127–200 CE), the prominent Han dynasty scholar whose commentaries on the *Rites of Zhou* became the standard interpretation.</note> claims that the spoke width today is three and a half inches, but this is not necessarily the case.

Furthermore, the diameter of the hub circumference is calculated as one foot, zero inches, eight *fen*, four *li*, seven *hao*, two *si*, and a remainder of two <term>non-terminating units</term> <note>original: 不盡 (bù jìn). This indicates a repeating decimal or an irrational number where the division does not result in a whole number.</note>. <gloss>This figure is derived from the circumference of the rim.</gloss>

# The True Measurement of Hub Length Based on the Rim Diagram

The measurement of the hub length is derived from the **Rim Diagram** <term>Ya Tu: A technical schematic or proportional guide used to calculate the dimensions of the wheel's outer felloe</term>. This Rim Diagram serves as the fundamental source for all the measurements described in the following text. Therefore, the diameter of the rim's circumference is not something that should be determined by **subjective guesswork** <note>original: 臆度 (yì dù). The author is critiquing scholars who provide measurements without a geometric basis</note>.

Current commentaries claim that if the rim's thickness is one and two-thirds inches, then the rim's width should be three and five-sixths inches. This is merely guesswork. By such logic, one could just as easily make the rim two inches thick and three inches wide, or one and a half inches thick and four inches wide. A single wheel is composed of **three essential parts** <note>original: 一輪三材 (yī lún sān cái). ### On Steam-Bending the Wheel Rim

The **Wheelwrights** <term>lun ren: artisans specializing in wheel construction</term> say: In all **steam-bending of the rim** <term>rou ya: the process of using heat and moisture to curve straight timber into a circular shape</term>, if the inner side is not jagged, the outer side is not fractured, and the sides are not swollen, this is considered the excellence of fire-treatment. That the rim is formed by bending a single piece of timber, as the scholar **Mr. Jia** <note>Jia Gongyan, a Tang dynasty commentator on the Rites of Zhou</note> suggests, I do not venture to say for certain. However, as for the established rules of the craft, it is clear that a rim is certainly not made by simply joining various pieces of wood together without employing the process of **steaming and curing** <term>yu zhi: treating wood with heat and moisture to make it pliable</term>.

For wheels traveling through marshes, one desires them to be "flexible"; for those traveling through mountains, one desires them to be "resilient." This refers to the different ways the rim is treated after the bending process—either made more curved or more balanced. As the **Cartwrights** <term>che ren: artisans who built the cart bodies and axles</term> say: for marsh-travel, use "reverse-bending"; for mountain-travel, use "side-wise bending." This is the method for bending the rim, which is clearly demonstrated to craftsmen.

I have occasionally discussed this: the bending of the rim has "reverse" and "side-wise" styles. According to the commentary, the material is generally a single piece of timber split into two. When wood is split, it has a **heart** <term>xin: the inner pith or denser heartwood</term> and an **edge** <term>bian: the outer sapwood</term>. When the heartwood is on the outside of the curve, it is called **reverse-bending** <term>fan rou</term>. In the steaming process, if the curve does not follow the natural grain of the wood, it is called "side-wise." Because the heartwood is hard, it is slippery and moves easily. <gloss>The commentary says: "To achieve the 'clinging of the female' [a tight friction fit], one desires the heartwood to be on the outside for its slipperiness." This is correct.</gloss> When one steams the wood against half of the grain, it is called "side-wise." Because the heartwood is hard, it is slippery and easy to shape.

## Discussion on Hubs and Axle-Plates, Part 2

Iron is iron. The **hub-cap** <term>guan: a metal ring or cap fitted onto the end of the wheel hub</term> is a fitting for the end of the hub. These are clearly two distinct things and should not be confused. The *Guangya* <note>original: 廣雅; "The Expanded Erya," a 3rd-century dictionary of synonyms</note> states that **wan** <term>wan: an inlaid metal ornament or iron band</term> and the **axle-plate** <term>jian: a reinforcing metal strip or plate embedded into the wooden axle to reduce friction</term> are both **hub-caps**. Because it uses **refined metal** <term>lian: iron or steel that has been smelted and refined</term>, the *wan*-ornament, and the *jian*-axle-plate to explain the **hub-cap**, it does not mean that "axle-plate" and "refined metal" are simply two different ways of writing the same word.

# The Wheel-maker

Every piece of wood inevitably has a sunny side <term>Yang: the side of the tree facing the sun</term> and a shady side <term>Yin: the side of the tree facing away from the sun</term>, a front and a back. One must **mark** <note>original: 矩 (ju)</note> it carefully so as not to misapply the wood during construction. Therefore, regardless of whether the wood is felled in winter or summer, it must be carved with a marking at the time of cutting.
<gloss>The commentary states: To "mark" (ju) means to carve an identifying sign into the wood.</gloss>

The **sunny side** is characterized by a dense grain and is hard; the **shady side** has a loose grain and is soft. For this reason, one uses fire to "nourish" the shady side <note>a process of heat-treating or tempering the wood</note> to make it as firm as the sunny side. **Note:** The wearing down of a hub occurs because the shady side of the wood is naturally loose and soft. If the effort put into treating it with fire is insufficient, it will fail.

### Divide the length of the hub into five parts: remove one to define the hub-extension, and remove three to define the hub-end.

The commentary is based on the interpretation of **Sinong** <term>Zheng Zhong: a prominent scholar of the Eastern Han dynasty</term>.
