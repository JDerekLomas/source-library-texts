# PREFACE.

THE Poem which forms the subject of this monograph was first published and translated by the late William Wright, Professor of Arabic in the University of Cambridge, in his *Apocryphal Acts of the Apostles* (2 vols. London, 1871). Since then the <term>Syriac</term><note>A dialect of Middle Aramaic that served as a major literary and liturgical language for Middle Eastern Christians.</note> text has been re-edited in the third volume of the *Acts of Martyrs and Saints* <note>original: "Acta Martyrum et Sanctorum"</note> (Paris, 1892), and two German translations, with copious explanatory remarks, have appeared—that of Karl Macke in the *Theological Quarterly Journal* <note>original: "Theologische Quartalschrift"</note> (Tübingen) for 1874, pp. 3—70, and that of Lipsius in his work *The Apocryphal Stories and Legends of the Apostles* <note>original: "Die apokryphen Apostelgeschichten und Apostellegenden"</note> vol. i. (Brunswick, 1883) pp. 292—300, vol. ii. pt. ii. (1884) p. 422.

As Prof. Wright's book has for several years been out of print, it seemed all the more desirable to re-publish the poem in a convenient form. If the piece were an integral part of the Apocryphal Acts, there might be some objection to thus detaching it from the context in which it stands ; but, as a matter of fact, it is an independent composition and may therefore be treated separately. When we consider its antiquity and its highly original character, it must appear extraordinary that it should hitherto have attracted so little attention among theologians ; if I succeed in exciting any further interest in this master-piece of religious poetry, the main object of my work will have been attained. At the same time I venture to hope that I have been able to contribute something fresh towards the elucidation of the text, in particular towards the comprehension of the <term>metre</term><note>The rhythmic structure or pattern of beats in a line of poetry.</note>, which is necessarily of great importance in textual criticism. Since the first editor, Prof. Wright, is universally acknowledged to have been one of the highest authorities in the department of <term>Syriac</term> literature, it may seem presumptuous, in a pupil of his, to think of supplementing, or modifying, the conclusions at which he arrived. # INTRODUCTION

The text here edited is based upon a single manuscript, British Museum Additional 14645, bearing the date <term>1247 of the Greek Era<note>This refers to the Seleucid Era; the date corresponds to 936 AD.</note></term> and containing a collection of Lives of Saints. For a full description, see Wright’s *Catalogue of the Syriac Manuscripts in the British Museum*, No. 952 <note>original: "DCCCCLII"</note> (pp. 1111—1116). Foremost in the collection are placed the Acts of Saint Thomas, or, as the Syriac heading calls them, “The Acts of Judas Thomas the Apostle,” which occupy 49 leaves. The Poem begins on <term>folio 30 verso<note>The "b" side or back of the 30th leaf of the manuscript.</note></term>, and is introduced in the following manner. The Apostle, we are told, in the course of his journeys through India, was arrested and cast into prison by order of a king named Mazdai. In the prison he offers up a prayer, at the conclusion of which we read—*“And whilst he was praying, all those who were in the prison saw that he was praying and begged of him to pray for them too. And when he had prayed and sat down, Judas <note>In Syriac tradition, the Apostle Thomas is often called Judas the Twin.</note> began to chant this hymn. The Hymn of Judas Thomas the Apostle in the country of the Indians.”* Here follows the Poem, with the subscription—*“The Hymn of Judas Thomas the Apostle, which he spake in the prison, is ended.”* But the Poem itself contains not the remotest allusion to the circumstances described in the preceding narrative, nor is there anything in the remainder of the narrative to indicate that the narrator was acquainted with the Poem. The question therefore arises, Was the Poem composed by the author of the Acts or was it derived from some other source?

This is not the place to discuss the origin and history of the Acts of St Thomas, for which the reader may refer to Lipsius, *The Apocryphal Acts and Legends of the Apostles* <note>original: "Die apokryphen Apostelgeschichten und Apostellegenden"</note> volume 1, pages 225—347, volume 2, part 2, pages 423—425, and to Harnack, *The...* <note>The text breaks off here; it likely refers to Adolf von Harnack's "Die Chronologie der altchristlichen Litteratur" (The Chronology of Early Christian Literature).</note>

Here it is enough to say that these Acts are extant both in Syriac and in Greek¹, but it is still disputed in which language they were originally composed. In the Greek *Acts of Saint Thomas* the Poem with which we are concerned is absent, nor is any trace of it to be found in the Berlin manuscript of the Syriac text (Sachau Collection, No. 222)—see the *Acts of the Martyrs and Saints* <note>original title: "Acta Martyrum et Sanctorum"</note> vol. iii. (Paris, 1892) p. 110 note². Hence the controversy as to the original language of the Acts does not in any way affect the Poem, for even those who believe the Acts to have been first composed in Greek admit that the Poem is not a translation but a purely Syriac work. This, as <term>Nöldeke<note>Theodor Nöldeke (1836–1930) was a pioneering German orientalist and a leading authority on Semitic languages.</note></term> pointed out in 1871, is evident from the style and, in particular, from the metrical character of the piece. In these respects it differs greatly from the other hymns and prayers which the Acts contain. Both external and internal evidence therefore lead us to the conclusion that the Poem was borrowed from some extraneous source and inserted—at what period we cannot say—into the Acts. Happily it is not of any great importance to decide how it found its way into this context; the question which interests us is how it originated. We are here entirely dependent on internal evidence; for, as every Syriac scholar must see at once that the piece is much older than the 10th century, the date affixed to the manuscript tells us nothing which we might not have safely assumed.

The considerations of which we have to take account, in conducting this inquiry, may be briefly summed up as follows. aroused by a revelation from on high; thereupon it performs the task assigned to it and returns to the upper regions, where it is reunited to the heavenly robe, its ideal counterpart, and enters the presence of the highest celestial Powers.” But if the general Gnostic <note>Gnosticism was a diverse collection of early religious movements that emphasized "gnosis" (secret knowledge) as the key to the soul's salvation from the physical world.</note> character of the Poem seems evident, the precise nature of the Gnosticism, the date and the authorship are by no means so easy to determine. The difficulty of answering these questions is due mainly to the extreme meagreness of our information respecting the history of Syriac literature at the period when Gnosticism flourished, namely from the 2nd century to the beginning of the 4th. Though there is clear proof that Gnosticism exercised a powerful influence in Syria at that time, not only have the writings of the Syrian Gnostics almost entirely perished—which was merely what we might have expected—but the writings of their orthodox opponents have, with few and small exceptions, perished likewise. The ages of Justin Martyr, of Irenaeus, and of Origen <note>Major early Christian theologians and writers from the 2nd and 3rd centuries.</note> are practically a blank in Syriac literature; the oldest Syriac writer of whom we possess any considerable remains is Aphraates <note>A 4th-century writer known as the "Persian Sage," whose homilies provide a window into early Syriac Christianity.</note>, in the first half of the 4th century¹. Thus the problem before us is one which does not admit of anything like a final solution. Yet there are not wanting indications which, though uncertain if considered separately, may enable us at least to form a plausible hypothesis.

# NOTES.

*82 b* It is difficult to believe that **shining** <note>original Syriac: **ܡܕܩܚܐ**, *madqḥā*</note> is correct here, as the use of the same verb in 82 a and 82 b would be very awkward. Possibly **shining** <note>original Syriac: **ܡܕܩܚܐ**</note> is a mistake for **shining** <note>original Syriac: **ܡܕܢܚܐ**, *madnḥā*</note> in the <term>Aphel: the causative verb form in Syriac</term>, meaning "radiating" or "causing to shine."

*83 a* It seems probable that here a fresh clause begins.

*84 a* Whether **his city** <note>original Syriac: **ܡܕܝܢܬܗ**, *mdīnteh*</note> is a mistake for **his cities** <note>original Syriac: **ܡܕܝܢ̈ܬܗ**, *mdīnāteh*</note>, or merely another pronunciation of the same word, cannot be determined.

*84 b* The word **also** <note>original Syriac: **ܕܐܦ**, *d-ap*</note> presumably refers back to verses 6 a—8 b, where gold, rubies, agates, and adamant <note>A legendary stone of extreme hardness, often associated with diamonds in ancient texts.</note> are mentioned as part of the equipment given to the prince.

*87 a* Read **prepared** <note>original Syriac: **ܡܛܝܒܐ**, *mṭaybā*</note> instead of **and also** <note>original Syriac: **ܘܐܦܐ**, *w-apā*</note>, following the suggestion of the scholar William Wright.

*90 b* Instead of the meaningless **his united one** <note>original Syriac: **ܡܚܝܕܬܗ**, *mḥaydteh*</note>, Wright suggests **his united one**, which I suppose to be a misprint for **his renewal** or **his joy** <note>original Syriac: **ܡܚܕܬܗ**, *mḥadteh*</note>.

*91 a* This verse and the three following are extremely obscure. In the first place, it is not clear whether the speech uttered by the "robe" <note>In this Gnostic poem, the robe is a personified celestial garment representing the prince's divine nature or soul.</note> ends at 91 b or at 92 b; whichever view we adopt, some difficulties arise. The phrase **of the mystery [of deeds?]** <note>original Syriac: **ܕܐܪܙܐ [ܕܥ̈ܒܕܐ ?]**, *d-rāzā d-‘badē*</note>, translated by Wright as "the active in deeds," is very suspicious. Since **soul** <note>original Syriac: **ܢܦܫܐ**, *napshā*</note> is elsewhere treated as feminine in Syriac, we should expect the feminine form **straight** <note>original Syriac: **ܬܪܨܐ**, *trēṣā*</note>, and for the same reason we should expect **arm** <note>original Syriac: **ܕܪܐܐ**, *drā’ā*</note> in 92 b, if the robe is still speaking.

*91 b* Instead of **they were great** <note>original Syriac: **ܕܪܒܘ**, *d-rabbū*</note>, the scholar Theodor Nöldeke proposes **that they reared** <note>original Syriac: **ܕܬܪܒܘ**, *d-tarbū*</note>, meaning "whom they reared in the presence of my father," or perhaps "for they reared me, etc." But as the preceding words are so doubtful, I have not ventured to change the text.

*92 b* To whom does the suffix in **his labors** <note>original Syriac: **ܥܡܠܘ̈ܗܝ**, *‘mālūhy*</note> refer? Possibly the idea may be that the "labours" performed by the prince during his exile in Egypt produced a corresponding effect upon the celestial robe which he had left behind him in his heavenly home.

## NOTES.

*93 b* "Spreading itself out," literally "pouring itself." Wright translates **upon me** <note>original Syriac: **ܥܠܝ**, *ʿlay*</note> as "over me," but this is hardly supported by the context.

*99 b* Read **I** <note>original Syriac: **ܐܢܐ**, *’nā*</note>, following the scholar Wright. In the final verses of the poem, the "Father" initially appears to be distinguished from the "King of kings," whereas in verse 41 a they are treated as the same person. Assuming the text is correct, the only way to resolve this difficulty is to suggest, as Nöldeke does, that in verse 99 b, the **"Majesty"** (literally "brightness" <note>original Syriac: **ܙܝܘܐ**, *zīwā*</note>) of the Father refers to a divine figure distinct from the Father himself. This "Majesty" would then be the subject of the actions in verses 102 a and 104 a. In <term>Mandaitic: A Gnostic tradition from southern Mesopotamia</term> writings, this same word, **brightness** <note>original: *zīwā*</note>, is used to describe a specific rank of heavenly beings. Whether the "Majesty" of the Father is the same as the "next in rank" mentioned earlier, as Nöldeke suggests, is less certain.

*101 a* **"His princes"** <note>original Syriac: **ܘܗܘܦܪ̈ܟܘܗܝ**, *w-hupparkawhy*</note> is derived—as Nöldeke demonstrated in his study of the historian **Tabari** (page 501)—from the Persian word **waspur**, which literally means "son of a house." This term refers to a member of one of the seven great noble families, known by Arabic historians as the **"people of the households"** <note>original Arabic: **أهل البيوتات**, *ahlu-l-buyūtāt*</note>.

*102 a* **"Me"** <note>original Syriac: **ܝܬܝ**, *yātī*</note>—the use of this particle to indicate a direct object <note>original Syriac: **ܝܬ**, *yāt*</note>, while common in the <term>Jewish Targums: Aramaic translations of the Hebrew Bible</term>, is extremely rare in Syriac. However, it should not be viewed as a simple borrowing from Hebrew; it appears in a <term>Palmyrene: An Aramaic dialect from the city of Palmyra</term> inscription (published by De Vogüé in *Central Syria*, No. 15, p. 17) which reads: **"he brought the legions here"** <note>original Palmyrene: **ܐܬܝ ܠܟܐ ܝܬ ܠܓܢܝܐ**, *aty l-kā yāt legnayyā*</note>.

*103 a* Regarding the phrase **"in the voice of my spirit"** <note>original Syriac: **ܒܩܠܝ ܕܪܘܚܐ**, *b-qāly d-rūḥā*</note>, which Wright found impossible to translate, Lipsius suggests reading **"with the voice of the Spirit"** <note>original Syriac: **ܒܩܠܐ ܕܪܘܚܐ**, *b-qālā d-rūḥā*</note>. However, it would be a less drastic change to the text to read **"with the voice of praise"** <note>original Syriac: **ܒܩܠܐ ܕܬܘܕܝܬܐ**, *b-qālā d-tawdītā*</note>, which would match the Greek word for **glory or praise** <note>original Greek: **δόξα**, *doxa*</note>.
