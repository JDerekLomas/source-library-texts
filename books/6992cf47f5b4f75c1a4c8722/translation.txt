In the 39th year of the Kangxi reign <note>1700 CE</note>, Pegu <note>original: "秘古" (Bìgǔ), a major kingdom in Lower Burma</note> once again allied with the military forces of the Dutch and the Westerners <note>Referring to European powers, likely the Portuguese or Dutch East India Company</note> to launch a joint attack on Burma. They won victory after victory, reaching all the way to the capital at Ava <note>original: "阿瓦" (Āwǎ)</note> and capturing the Burmese King Dibuli <note>A phonetic rendering of a Burmese monarch's title or name from this period</note>. At that time, Burma in turn became a dependency of Pegu.

However, the Burmese people are fierce and resilient. Suddenly, there arose a leader from the Alaungpaya tribe <note>original: "阿羅般" (Āluóbān), referring to the founder of the Konbaung Dynasty, King Alaungpaya</note>, who gathered local soldiers and recovered the national capital of Ava. He established himself as king and reclaimed all the former territories of Burma, subsequently dispatching his army to attack and eliminate the Kingdom of Pegu. 

After his son Hsinbyushin <note>original: "山巴領" (Shānbālǐng)</note> succeeded to the throne, Pegu rebelled once more. Hsinbyushin defeated them again and, taking advantage of his victory, also seized Siam <note>modern-day Thailand</note>. After some time, however, his forces were defeated by the Siamese, leaving Burma with only the coastal cities west of Malacca, such as Mergui <note>original: "麻爾古" (Má'ěrgǔ)</note>, Tenasserim <note>original: "底呢" (Dǐní)</note>, and Syriam <note>original: "色領" (Sèlǐng)</note>. 

When Hsinbyushin died, his younger brother Bodawpaya <note>original: "皿底臘疑勃老" (Mǐndǐlà yíbólǎo)</note> ascended the throne and unified Arakan <note>the coastal region of western Myanmar</note> into the single kingdom. Furthermore, his forces captured Cachar <note>original: "加渣爾" (Jiāzhā'ěr)</note> and Cassay <note>original: "加色" (Jiāsè), referring to the Kingdom of Manipur</note>. Burma shares a border with Bengal <note>original: "孟阿臘" (Mèng'ālà)</note>. As Bengal belongs to Britain, the two nations had shared a border for a long time. Conflict eventually erupted between them in 1826 <gloss>the 6th year of the Daoguang reign</gloss>. Britain subsequently mobilized troops from India to attack Burma. 

The Burmese, relying on their long experience in warfare, viewed the British invaders with total contempt. However, the Burmese army lacked strict discipline; while they were sharp and aggressive in their advance, they retreated just as quickly. Because of this, they were thwarted by the British forces. 

The British army was also unfamiliar with the local geography; the waterways and terrain were treacherous, transporting supplies was difficult, and the "evil miasma" <note>A reference to tropical diseases like malaria and cholera which devastated British ranks during the campaign</note> caused much illness. This made it difficult for them to penetrate deep into the interior. The British originally intended to withdraw their forces, but instead, they publicly proclaimed they would strike directly at the capital of Ava <note>original: "阿瓦" (Āwǎ)</note>, advancing rapidly to maintain the pressure.

The Burmese military, assessing their losses and finding their spirit broken, used humble language to sue for peace. They then ceded the coastal territories of Arakan <note>original: "阿臘干" (Ālàgān)</note>, Martaban <note>original: "麻爾" (Má'ěr)</note>, Tavoy <note>original: "古達" (Gǔdá)</note>, Ye <note>original: "阿依底" (Āyīdǐ)</note>, and Mergui <note>original: "尼色領" (Nísèlǐng)</note>. Furthermore, they paid an indemnity for the British military expenses before the fighting finally ceased. 

Despite these losses, Burmese land warfare relies entirely on sturdy, sharpened wooden stockades <note>original: "木柵" (mùzhà); these were a hallmark of Burmese defensive strategy, often proving very effective against infantry charges</note> surrounded by deep, layered moats. At times, even the British soldiers and horses were repelled by these defenses. Land taxes and grain constitute the primary revenue. Beyond these, duties are collected from foreign trade, though these are not treated as standard line items but are instead stored separately in the inner treasury. This is the practice in Burma, Siam, and Annam alike. It is reported that when the King of Burma engaged in hostilities and then negotiated peace with the Westerners, he was able to provide the indemnity for the British military expenses almost instantaneously. This demonstrates the great wealth of that nation. 

The political affairs of Burma, Siam, and Annam are generally similar to those of other Eastern nations. Power is held autocratically by the King, and the various officials are not permitted to act on their own authority. The laws and regulations they employ are a synthesis of the legal codes of both China and India, adjusted through additions or subtractions to suit their needs. 

For example, the official ranks of Burma include the **Grand Minister** <term>original: *Wungyi* (翁疑士); the senior members of the Council of State</term>, the **Palace Minister** <term>original: *Atwinwun* (敬翁多士); ministers who manage internal palace affairs and communicate with the King</term>, and the **Superintendent of Labor** <term>original: *Athiwun* (阿達翁士); officials overseeing the non-military service population</term>. These officials all reside in the national capital to assist the King in governing the realm. 

There are also legal officers who are dispatched to oversee various departments, collect taxes, and deliver provisions to the capital. When the common people have matters to report, they must first bring their petition to the Office of the **Town Governor** <term>original: *Myowun* (麦翁)</term>; only then can they appeal to the Office of the **Council of State** <term>original: *Hluttaw* (羅都)</term>. The administration of punishments includes the use of incantations and rituals of oath-taking <note>Refers to judicial ordeals or spiritual oaths used to determine guilt</note>, which are applied with discretion and are broadly similar to the customs of India. Beyond the borders of India, it is generally known that these peoples possess written scripts, yet the Burmese language is uniquely complex and mixed. It incorporates the language of the Buddha <note>original: "Buddhist language," referring to Pali and Sanskrit terms</note>, the phonetic sounds of <gloss>Tartary</gloss> <note>original: *Dadanli*; referring to Central Asian or Northern ethnic influences</note>, and elements of Chinese phonetics, all of which are blended together. <gloss>European</gloss> people are unable to distinguish the pauses or punctuation in their writing; to the observer, it appears as one long, continuous sentence. It is difficult to both speak and write. 

Their books are all compiled from palm leaves <term>original: *beiye*; dried and polished leaves of the fan palm used for manuscripts</term>. The King, however, uses ivory for his pages, decorating the edges with gold and storing them in boxes traced with gold. There are also volumes where the characters are carved into the material and then gilded with gold. Although their libraries are rich in content, they focus primarily on the discussion of the divine and the spiritual. Works on history, music, medicine, and manuals on sericulture <note>the raising of silkworms for silk production</note> are dismissed as "miscellaneous talk" or "minor books." 

Among these nations, only the literature of <gloss>Annam</gloss> <note>Modern-day Vietnam</note> exclusively follows the Chinese tradition, possessing a depth of scholarship that exceeds that of Burma or Siam <note>Modern-day Thailand</note>. In contrast, Burmese dwellings are of the most humble sort: they drive bamboo into the ground, use rattan to bind the frame, and employ woven mats to create the walls.
