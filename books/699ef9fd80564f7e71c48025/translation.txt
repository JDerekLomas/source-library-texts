The North Star <note>original: "Transmontana stella" - literally the star "across the mountains," the standard medieval term for the Pole Star.</note>, so called, remains in the navel of the sky. For it does not set nor circle the sky like the others, but sitting in the middle, it rules and revolves all the stars. All the other stars gathered to this star, saying with one voice: "Should you always sit and rest while we circle the sky daily and serve you unceasingly? Give way to a greater one and move back a little." To them it said: "You do not know what you ask. For I tire myself out greatly by ruling you, and if I did not keep you in your guards <note>meaning their fixed orbits or boundaries.</note>, you would stray much in your movements <note>original: "montibus" (mountains), likely a scribal error for "motibus" (movements).</note>. The North Star, being disturbed, withdrew and renounced its lordship.

The stars then gloried and exulted greatly, and they held an election for a ruler. But because they were not found in unity, they began to argue and fight with one another; for which reason, like sheep without a shepherd, they wandered, not remaining in their established paths. Thus we also ought to act. What do you wish for? You waste your labor; the world claims all things. This is nothing new to you; you were born under this law. This happened to your father, to your mother, and to your ancestors; this happened to all before you and will happen to all after you." You should understand this while conducting your life. He <note>Seneca</note> also says: "We must bear what we cannot avoid." It is read in the chronicles of the emperors that a certain wife buried Octavia <note>Likely referring to Livia, the wife of Emperor Augustus, who buried her sister-in-law Octavia</note>, and another buried her son named Drusus. And although she was a pagan, yet through the great natural wisdom existing in her, she laid aside all signs of grief, saying: "What profit is there in weeping for what cannot be avoided, and once it comes, cannot be recalled?" Hence Seneca <note>Lucius Annaeus Seneca (c. 4 BC – 65 AD), Stoic philosopher</note> says: "The wise man is not afflicted by the loss of children nor friends." For their death should be borne in the same way he expects his own—certainly not as something better. 

Death is like a certain <term>bridle</term> <note>a metaphor for restraint</note> restraining men so that they might overflow and run about less through the broad paths of desires and lusts. The meditation on death is philosophy, as Plato says. Hence it is read in the life of John the Almsgiver <note>St. John the Merciful (d. 619 AD), Patriarch of Alexandria</note>: in ancient times, after an emperor was crowned, the builders of monuments would immediately enter to him, saying: "From what or from what kind of metal does your majesty command your monument to be made?" They were suggesting to him that he was a corruptible and transitory man, so that he might take care of himself and dispose and govern his kingdom piously. According to that saying in Ecclesiastes 7: "Remember your end, and you shall never sin." 

For Alphonsus <note>Petrus Alfonsi (c. 1062–1140), author of the 'Disciplina Clericalis'</note> recounts in his treatise on prudence concerning the death of Alexander the Great: when a golden tomb was being made, several philosophers gathered at it, of whom one said: "Alexander made a treasure out of gold; now, on the contrary, gold makes a treasure out of him." Another also said: "Alexander used to command the peoples; today the peoples command him." And another said: "Yesterday Alexander could have freed many from death; today he could not avoid the darts of death itself." And another said: "Yesterday Alexander led an army; today he is led by them to burial." And another said: "Yesterday Alexander trod upon the earth, and today he himself is pressed down by it." And another said: "Yesterday the nations feared Alexander; today they consider him worthless." And another said: "Yesterday Alexander had friends; today he has all as equals." And another said: "Yesterday the whole world was not enough for him; today he is content with a five-foot grave." 

If anyone would consider these things well, he would restrain himself in words and ways. It is said of the wicked <note>original: "begetio", likely a corruption of "peccatore" (sinner) or a reference to the "hypocrite" in Job 20</note> that he shall perish like dung in the end. Job 20. Therefore Ecclesiastes commands: "Remember the end. It is better to go to the house of mourning than to the house of feasting; for there the end of all men warns a person, and the youth considers what shall be for him, who must be closed by a similar end." Ecclesiastes 7. 

Therefore pay attention and consider that in the death of anyone, the nose grows cold, the teeth turn black, the face pales, and the nerves of the body are broken; the heart, as it is said, splits from excessive pain. All the limbs wither like wood and stones. Nothing in the world is so abominable and tedious as the corpse of a dead man. It is not thrown into the waters lest the waters be infected. It is not hung in the air lest the air be corrupted. But like the worst poison, it is thrown into a pit, and lest it be seen any longer, earth is heaped upon it more swiftly. Behold how the glory of the world is ended! For it is shut in a most stinking pit, where his heart quickly decays. The eyes waste away in their strength; the ears fall from the head; the nose is torn from the face; the tongue rots in the mouth; his heart bursts in the body. But alas, alas for me, Lord! How will the eyes delight to see beautiful things? [How will] the ears [delight to hear]...

...dreadful vanities. The nose to smell sweet things. The tongue to speak shameful and useless things. The mouth to taste sweets. The heart to think of vain and vile things. Thus Bernard <note>Saint Bernard of Clairvaux (1090–1153), a Cistercian abbot and influential mystic</note> says: "Why are you proud, dust and ashes? Whose conception is in sin, who is born in misery, whose life is a punishment, and whose death is an agony?" Especially when a wretched man declines toward death or old age, his heart is shaken, his head is afflicted, hope languishes, his breath stinks, his face is wrinkled, his stature is bent, his eyes grow dim, his joints wobble, his nostrils run, his hair fails, his teeth rot, and he loses his strength; one moment he is joyful, the next sad, then he becomes infirm. O wretched condition! Why do you not notice how miserable this life is? Consider, therefore, your parents, children, and ancestors, and you will find they are no more. And as Bernard says: "Tell me, where are the lovers of the world who a short time ago were with us in the world? Absolutely nothing remains of them but ashes." And so I ask you, please: where now are the barons, where are the princes, where are the leaders? Surely they have passed like a shadow and have been reduced to nothing. Augustine <note>St. Augustine of Hippo (354–430 AD), a foundational theologian of Western Christianity</note> says the same: "Go to the tomb, take up the bones and discern, if you can, who was the master and who the servant; who was beautiful and who deformed; who was noble and who common; who was wise and who a fool. You will not be able to tell them apart." Therefore, think on where you came from, and blush for where you are; groan for where you are going, and tremble so that you may manage to return to the place from which you were expelled <note>Referring to the fall of man and the desire to return to Paradise</note>. May He who lives and reigns as God without end deign to grant this to us. Throughout all ages of ages. Amen.
