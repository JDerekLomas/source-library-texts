# On the Nature of the Elements

It is first necessary to consider the primary qualities of the four bodies, which the ancients called the <term>Elements</term> <note>From the Greek 'stoicheia', referring to the fundamental building blocks of the physical world: Earth, Water, Air, and Fire.</note>. These are not merely substances we touch, but the very principles of all material existence.

->**The Fourfold Division**<-

| Element | Primary Quality | Secondary Quality |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Fire** | Hot | Dry |
| **Air** | Wet | Hot |
| **Water** | Cold | Wet |
| **Earth** | Dry | Cold |

As the great Philosopher <note>Aristotle</note> says, <insert>in the second book of *On Generation and Corruption*</insert>, "the elements are transformed into one another" <note>original: "elementa invicem transmutantur"</note>. This occurs through the sharing of a common quality. For example, Fire becomes Air when its dryness is overcome by moisture, yet it retains its heat.

<margin>Note the cycle of transformation</margin>

<column-break/>

### Of the Celestial Influence

We must also observe how the <term>Macrocosm</term> <note>The "Great World" or the universe as a whole, which was believed to be mirrored in the "Microcosm" of the human body.</note> governs these earthly mixtures. The stars do not compel the elements, but they incline them toward certain movements. 

*Fire* is drawn upward toward the concave surface of the Moon's sphere, while *Earth*, being the most heavy and <gloss>dense</gloss> element, seeks the very center of the world. <unclear>The movement of the tides</unclear> demonstrates how the Moon specifically governs the element of Water, pulling it in accordance with her phases.

<note>The author here follows the Ptolemaic view of the universe, where Earth sits at the center surrounded by concentric spheres of the elements and planets.</note>
