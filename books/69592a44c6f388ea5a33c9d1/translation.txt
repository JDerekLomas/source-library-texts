# The life of the most famous fabulist Aesop, translated from Greek into Latin by Rimicius <note>Rimicius, also known as Rinuccio da Castiglionchio, was a 15th-century Italian humanist</note> for the most reverend father and lord, Anthony, Cardinal-Priest of the title of Saint Chrysogonus.

The life of the highly renowned fabulist Aesop, translated from the Greek tongue into Latin by Rimicius, dedicated to the most reverend father, Lord Anthony, Cardinal-Priest of the title of Saint Chrysogonus. Furthermore, this same life of Aesop is presented with his fables—which Romulus of Athens once brought from the Greek tongue into Latin for his son Tiberinus—and additionally several fables of Avianus, also of Poligamus <note>likely a reference to Perotti's "Cornu Copiae" or similar humanist collections</note>, of Alfonsi <note>Petrus Alfonsi, a Jewish-Spanish physician and writer</note>, and the jests of Poggio <note>Poggio Bracciolini, a famous Renaissance humanist known for his "Facetiae" or humorous tales</note> and others, each recorded above with its own title. These have been translated from Latin by Doctor Heinrich Steinhöwel into plain and understandable German, not word-for-word, but sense-for-sense <note>original: "sin vß sin"; a common medieval translation philosophy prioritizing the meaning over literal syntax</note>. For the sake of greater clarity of the text, they are often drawn out with a few added or omitted words. This is done to the praise and honor of the most illustrious prince and lord, Lord Sigmund, Duke of Austria, so that some amusement may be received from it, which is also useful if they are read with understanding. According to the teaching of Saint Basil <note>Saint Basil the Great, who wrote on the usefulness of pagan literature for Christians</note>, the reader of this little book should have the understanding of bees toward flowers: they pay no heed to the outward colors, but rather seek the sweetness of the honey and the utility of the wax for their hive, leaving the rest of the flower untouched. Likewise, whoever wishes to read this little book should not highly regard the color of the flowers—that is, the stories or fables—but rather seek to learn the good teachings contained within them for the sake of good morals and virtue.

### What a Fable Is

But if I am to speak of the fables of Aesop, it is first to be noted what a fable is called. Therefore, know that the poets have taken the name "fable" from the Latin word *fando* <note>original: "fando," from the Latin verb *fari*, meaning "to speak"</note>. That is, in English, "speaking"; for fables are not things that have happened, but rather things invented only with words. And they were invented so that through the imagined words of irrational animals, an image of the nature and customs of mankind might be recognized. 

And the first inventor of the fable or parable was the master Alemo of Croton <note>original: "alemo croconiesis," likely referring to Alcman or a similar legendary figure cited in medieval sources</note>. And there are many kinds of fables. Some are called "Aesopic," for that same master Aesop was held in the highest regard in Phrygia; and these are the ones where the irrational animals, who cannot speak, talk with one another—or other things that have no feeling, such as trees, mountains, stones, water, cities, villages, and the like. The other fables are called "Libyan" <note>original: "libistice"</note>; these are the invented fables in which humans speak with animals, or vice versa. Thus also did **Demosthenes**, the highly eloquent master at Athens, act against **King Philip** <note>Philip II of Macedon, father of Alexander the Great</note> when he demanded from the citizens of Athens that they send him ten of the wisest men; if they did so, he would withdraw from the city which he had besieged. Then he composed the fable of how the wolf demanded the dogs from the shepherd, promising that he would then keep peace with the sheep. By this, he intended to advise against what the king had requested, as the fable in the readings of **Aesop** demonstrates. For in the same way (said the master), as the wolf demands the dogs from the shepherd so that he might thereafter strangle the sheep without worry, so does King Philip demand that you send him your superior leaders, so that he might thereafter more easily oppress and destroy you once your guardians have left you.

Furthermore, note the difference between **fable**, **history**, and **argument**. **Histories** are true things that have happened. **Arguments** are those things which, even if they have not happened, it is nevertheless possible that they could happen, such as the comedies of **Terence** and some of **Plautus** and their like. **Fables** are those which have neither happened nor are possible to happen, for they are against nature. He was slow of speech and a babbler, but hiding it with cunning; and being gifted beyond measure with mock-arguments <note>original: "cavillationibus," referring to sophisticated or deceptive reasoning</note>, his master—judging him useless for city service—sent him to the countryside to serve as a ditch-digger. Therefore, one day when the master set out for the countryside, the farm overseer gathered some figs and presented them to the master of the estate, saying: "Take these, the first-fruits of your fields." 

The master, being joyful, said: "By my health, these are most beautiful figs!" and he ordered his servant, named Agathopus, to take them and guard them until he returned from the bath. At that time, it happened that Aesop, coming from his work, asked for his daily bread to be given to him. Agathopus, to whose custody the figs had been committed, having tasted two of them, said to another of his fellow-slaves: "If I did not fear the master, I would eat every one of those figs." 

The other replied: "If you wish me to eat along with you, I will provide a way by which we shall suffer no harm." Then Agathopus said: "By what means can that be done?" And the other said: "When the master comes, tell him that Aesop, coming in from his work, ate them. Since Aesop cannot defend himself because of his slowness of tongue <note>original: "linguae tarditate," referring to Aesop's speech impediment or muteness in early traditions</note>, he will indeed be beaten with lashes, while we shall have fulfilled our desire." 

Having thus plotted between themselves, while they ate the figs one by one, they said to each other: "Woe to you and to your shoulder-blades, wretched Aesop!" And so, talking amongst themselves, they ate all the figs. When the master returned from the bath, he asked for the figs to be served to him. Agathopus said to him: "My master, Aesop, coming from his work, found the storehouse <note>original: "penu"</note> unlocked; entering it, and having no regard for anything, he ate all the figs."
