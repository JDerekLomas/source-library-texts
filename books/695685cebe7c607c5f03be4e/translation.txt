## TO THE READER
# PREFACE.

IF the Reader should demand the Reason for this work; let him have this one.
Near the end of my *Universal Mathematics* <note>original: "Matheseos Universalis"</note>, or *Arithmetical Work*, published at the beginning of the year 1657, I had suggested that it was then my intention to publish shortly another treatise on *Algebra* or *Analysis* <note>original: "Analytica"</note>: but other business intervened, which resulted in it not being done immediately. Having been asked several times to fulfill this quasi-promise, I had written a treatise of this kind and sent it to *London* in the Year 1676 to be put to the press. But since it was delayed there for some time (being somewhat enlarged in the meantime), it was finally put to the press in the year 1683; and in the following year, 1684, the typesetters had finished the entire work, with various Appendices added, as it appears in the English edition; to which the Booksellers (according to their custom) saw fit to assign the (then approaching) Year 1685.

And now, having translated the same into Latin (for the benefit of *Foreigners*), I committed it again to the Press in the Year 1692, which was finished in the present Year 1693, and not a little enlarged.

Indeed, many things had to be inserted in their proper places, partly so that the Objections of certain people might be answered, partly so that some things might be handed down more at length to make them clearer, and other things are interspersed here which had previously been delivered elsewhere in a scattered fashion, and several chapters have been added at the end.

Hence it has happened that some Chapters, on account of these Additions, seem rather long compared to the rest; especially where the Additions were so great that they seemed to deserve new Chapters: which would indeed have been done, if the matter were starting fresh. But I thought it better to endure that, rather than disturb the sequence of chapters as they were in the English Edition by assigning new numbers; for that would be an inconvenience to those who, in their excerpts or citations, followed the earlier order, to which a new order would not correspond. But those things which are added at the end have their own new numbers assigned.

In this work, my purpose was to deliver the Origin, Progress, and various Increments from time to time (as far as it was granted to achieve this) of the *Analytical Art* <note>original: "Artis Analyticæ"</note>, which (taking the name from the Arabs) we now call *Algebra*; and by what steps it arrived at the point to which it has now advanced.

That the Greeks once possessed this, there is no reason for us to doubt, but it was hidden by design, and not to be published rashly.

Certain Examples of this appear in *Euclid*, or at least in *Theon* in his Edition of *Euclid*. Others appear in *Pappus*. And the effects of it (however secretly hidden) are sufficiently conspicuous in *Archimedes*, *Apollonius*, and others.

However, the first of the Greeks (whom I know) to treat it professedly is *Diophantus*. *Xylander* first published him in Latin; and later *Bachet* in Greek and Latin, with various Additions of his own; and now recently *Mr. Fermat* <note>original: "D. Fermatius"</note> has seen to its reprinting, with other items of his own added.

## TO THE READER

That it was formerly in use among the *Arabs* seems no less certain (and perhaps handed down earlier than by *Diophantus* <note>original: "Diophanto"; a 3rd-century Greek mathematician often called the father of algebra</note> among the *Greeks*); it is likewise credible that they received it (not so much from the *Greeks* as) from the *Persians*, and these from the *Indians*.

From the *Arabs* (by way of the *Saracens* or *Moors*) it was brought into *Spain*, and from there into *England* (together with *Algorism* <note>The system of Arabic numerals and the rules for using them</note> or the use of *Numerical Figures*, and other parts of *Mathematics*, and specifically *Astronomy*,) before *Diophantus* seems to have been known to us. From this source we received the name ALGEBRA; and likewise ALGORISM, for the Practice of Arithmetic by Numerical Figures.

From these same people we also received the greatest part of Greek Literature; certainly the *Latin* versions of *Euclid*, *Ptolemy*, and other *Greeks*, which we received from *Arabic* versions before we had them from the *Greek* originals.

And their *Algorism*, or *Arithmetic Practice* by *Numerical Figures* (which the *Greeks* did not previously possess), is a great aid to Algebra.

These Figures seem to have arrived on our shores at least in the Eleventh century, or rather in the middle of the Tenth Century; at least in matters of Astronomy. Although some have felt this did not happen before the Thirteenth Century, perhaps they were not admitted into common use much earlier.

*Archimedes* (in *The Sand Reckoner*) seems to have laid the foundations of this kind of Computation, although he did not apply a Notation suited to it.

*Sexagesimal Fractions* <note>A system based on the number 60, still used today for measuring time and angles</note> (introduced by *Ptolemy*) seemed to supply this defect to some extent, but quite imperfectly.

But those Numerical Figures, after they were accepted by Europeans, gained two quite notable Additions: one of which is *Decimal Parts*; the other, *Logarithms*.

*Regiomontanus* <note>Johannes Müller von Königsberg (1436–1476), a German mathematician and astronomer</note> seems to have introduced *Decimal Parts* (I know not if he was the first of all) silently indeed and unobserved, in his *Trigonometric Canon* around the Year 1450: which *Simon Stevin*, *Henry Briggs*, and others promoted.

Indeed, since this method is far superior to that by sexagesimal parts, *Briggs*, *Gellibrand*, and others of our countrymen have busied themselves introducing it even in those cases where the Sexagesimal method still holds sway. This has been done in great part; and it would be absolutely accomplished, were it not that the calculation of Ancient Tables according to the former method makes it necessary that it be retained to some extent.

The use of *Logarithms* (of the greatest utility in Astronomical and other Trigonometric calculations), first introduced and happily begun by *Lord Napier* (*Lord of Merchiston* among the *Scots*), our own *Briggs* and *Gellibrand* promoted with joined labors.

These things, although they perhaps should not be called parts of Algebra, nevertheless contribute much to its more expeditious practice, and for that reason are explained by us.

The first, however (whom I know), who presented a Treatise on Algebra published in Print, is *Lucas Pacioli*, or called *Luca de Borgo*, a Friar Minor, who published a Treatise in the Italian language at *Venice* in at least the Year 1494 <note>The original text says 1499, but the famous 'Summa' was printed in 1494</note> (shortly after the Invention of the Art of Printing), which also appeared again in the same place a few years later.

He mentions indeed *Leonardo of Pisa* <note>Also known as Fibonacci</note> and others older than himself from whom he had learned, but whose works (as far as I know) are not yet available in Print.
