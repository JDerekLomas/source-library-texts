Language fails me when I think of asking the indulgence of the expectant readers, and the learned translator and commentator of this work, to pardon the unpardonable delay in bringing out this book.

Unusual delays are always ascribable to unusual causes. I am not a publisher either by choice, or by profession. The responsibility has been forced upon me by circumstances; and nobody realises more severely and sorely than I do, how very unfitted I am for the discharge of the duties expected of a publisher.

My explanation, good, bad, or indifferent, is that in March 1929 I accepted a Judgeship in the High Court at Bikaner, Rajputana, and it was only during the intervals of leisure that I could attend to the revision and proof-passing of <term>Niyamsāra</term> <note>The *Niyamsāra* (The Essence of the Rule) is a foundational scripture of Jainism written by Acharya Kundakunda, focusing on the soul's path to liberation.</note>. # INTRODUCTION.

*The Essence of the Rule* <note>original: Niyamsāra</note> is one of the most renowned spiritual <note>original: Adhyatmic</note> works of the venerable teacher Shri Kund Kunda Ācharya. He was the preceptor of Shri Uma Swami, the renowned author of the *Manual for Understanding the Reality of Truths* <note>original: Shri Tatwartha Dhigama Sūtra</note>.

The Sanskrit commentary of *The Essence of the Rule* was written by Shri Padma Prabha Maldhari Deva, who appears to have lived about 1000 A.D. He was preceded by the teacher Shri Amrit Chandra Acharya, who wrote Sanskrit commentaries on the *Five Extensive Realities* <note>original: Panchastikaya</note>, the *Essence of the Doctrine* <note>original: Pravachansara</note>, and the *Essence of the Self* <note>original: Samayasara</note>—the great monumental works on Jain metaphysics by Shri Kund Kunda Acharya. Padma Prabha has frequently quoted and referred to some of the verses of Amrit Chandra in his commentary on *The Essence of the Rule*.

In 1912, a lucid commentary with learned explanatory notes was written in Hindi by the "Ornament of the Jain Religion" <note>original: Jaina Dharma Bhushan</note>, the celibate scholar Brahmachari Sital Prasadaji. No other commentary in Hindi is available.

In 1928, the said Brahmachariji, at the request of the local Jains, passed the rainy season <note>This refers to the "Chaturmas," a four-month period where Jain monks and scholars stay in one place to avoid harming life forms that flourish in the rains</note> at Rohtak and stayed there from the 30th of June to the 12th of November. It was through his kind persuasion and encouragement that the English translation of this work was undertaken and finished by me. The translation and commentaries were written under the direct and immediate supervision of Brahmachariji.

The treatise is named *The Essence of the Rule* <note>original: Niyamsāra</note> because it deals with the path of liberation, which is **Right Belief**, **Right Knowledge**, and **Right Conduct**—the "three jewels" of faith combined. The word *Niyama* literally means "rule" or "law," and *Sāra* means "the essential" or "the right." *The Essence of the Rule* thus signifies the **Right Rule**; that is, the true and indispensable law for the attainment of liberation.

The sole object and the whole gist of this treatise is to show that the all-pure, all-conscious, all-blissful, and self-absorbed soul alone is the <term>Siddha</term> <note>a perfected, liberated soul that has achieved its ultimate state</note>, a perfect soul. If a soul is in bondage with <term>karmic matter</term> <note>in Jainism, karma is considered a subtle form of physical matter that attaches to the soul through actions and passions</note>—that is, if it has any connection, whatsoever, with the **Non-Soul**—it is imperfect, and under delusion. In order to achieve liberation, perfection, and eternal bliss, a soul must sever all connections with the "non-self." When this connection with the non-self is completely broken, the <term>State of Perfection</term> <note>original: Siddha-pada</note> is attained.

Right Belief, Right Knowledge, and Right Conduct have been addressed from two points of view: the absolute <note>original: real</note> and the practical.

The absolute path is the only certain and direct route, while the practical path serves as a helpful means to reach the absolute. The true path of liberation is found in being fully absorbed within the self.

Attachment and hatred—which include all passionate thought activities—are the main causes of <term>karmic bondage</term> <note>In Jainism, karma is viewed as physical matter that sticks to the soul due to passions</note>, while non-attachment or pure thought leads to freedom from this bondage.

1. Practical Right Belief is a true and firm faith in the <term>Trustworthy Authority</term> <note>original: Apta</note> (the all-accomplished, all-knowing source of all knowledge), in the <term>Scripture</term> <note>original: Agama</note> (the written teachings that first came from the omniscient beings), and in the <term>Principles</term> <note>original: Tattvas; the fundamental categories of reality</note>.

The <term>Trustworthy Authority</term> <note>original: Apta</note> must possess three special characteristics:

(a) Freedom from all human defects such as hunger, fear, anger, and delusion; (b) All-knowing wisdom <note>original: Omniscience</note>; and (c) The spontaneous, effortless sharing of truth. Such are the <term>Worthy Ones</term> <note>original: Arhats</note>, the adorable Lords, the most prominent of whom are the twenty-four <term>Ford-makers</term> <note>original: Tirthankaras; the great spiritual teachers who "make a ford" across the river of worldly life</note>.

The <term>Scripture</term> <note>original: Agama</note> is the sacred text composed by highly learned and spiritually advanced saints based on the teachings that came from the <term>Worthy Ones</term> <note>original: Arhats</note>. These scriptures are perfect and free from any contradictions.

The <term>Principles</term> <note>original: Tattvas</note>, categories, or substances are seven in number: (1) <term>the Soul</term> <note>original: Jiva</note>, (2) <term>the Non-soul</term> <note>original: Ajiva; inanimate matter</note>, (3) <term>Inflow</term> <note>original: Asrava; the flow of karmic matter into the soul</note>, (4) <term>Bondage</term> <note>original: Bandha</note>, (5) <term>the Stoppage of Inflow</term> <note>original: Samvara</note>, (6) <term>the Shedding of Karma</term> <note>original: Nirjara; the removal of previously accumulated karmic matter</note>, and (7) <term>Liberation</term> <note>original: Moksha</note> from all karmic contact.

All that exists is included in one of these two principles: soul or non-soul. While a person is alive, it is the soul within the body that perceives and knows all things. A body without a soul is incapable of perceiving or knowing anything. Material objects such as a pen, table, or chair cannot feel or know anything. They are unconscious <note>original: unclear</note> or inanimate substances.

**I. The soul.** It is the only conscious substance. Looked at from the absolute <note>original: real</note> point of view, even a worldly <note>original: mundane</note> soul is pure, peaceful, all-knowing and all-blissful; it possesses this nature potentially. From the practical point of view, such a soul experiences various kinds of pain and pleasure across different conditions of life.

**II. The Non-soul.** It comprises the other five real and independent substances, which, when taken together with the soul, make up the six <term>Substances</term> <note>Sanskrit: Dravyas</note>.

**(1) <term>Matter</term> <note>Sanskrit: Pudgala</note>** is the most prominent of these, and plays a very important part in the theater <note>original: amphitheatre</note> of the universe. The special attributes of the matter-substance (<term>pudgala</term>) are touch, taste, smell, and color. It exists either in the form of atoms or of molecules. Only gross molecules can be perceived by the senses; however, the fine electric and karmic molecules—which compose the electric and karmic bodies of all worldly souls—cannot be perceived by the senses.

**(2) <term>Medium of Motion</term> <note>Sanskrit: Dharma Dravya</note>** is a single, non-physical <note>original: immaterial</note> substance that pervades the entire universe. It acts essentially as a supporting cause for the movement of souls and matter.

**(3) <term>Medium of Rest</term> <note>Sanskrit: Adharma Dravya</note>** is also a single non-physical substance pervading the entire universe. It serves as an essentially supporting cause for souls and matter to remain at rest.

**(4) <term>Space</term> <note>Sanskrit: Akāshā Dravya</note>** is a single, infinite, non-physical substance. Its function is to provide a place for all other substances to exist.

**(5) <term>Time</term> <note>Sanskrit: Kâla Dravya</note>** is a non-physical substance. It is a supporting cause that brings about changes <note>original: modifications</note> in all other substances.

**III & IV. "Inflow" (<term>asrava</term>) and "Bondage" (<term>Bandha</term>).**

Every worldly soul possesses a karmic body made of karmic molecules. The universe is filled with these molecules. The "inflow" of these molecules toward the soul is caused by the soul's own vibratory activities through mind, speech, and body; this process is called <term>àsrava</term>. When these molecules are attracted toward the soul, they are integrated <note>original: assimilated</note> into the existing karmic body. The causes of this integration or "bondage" are the soul's vibratory activities and its passions. This process is known as <term>bandha</term> (bondage). The processes of the Inflow and Bondage of karmic matter occur simultaneously. The main supporting causes for both are:

**V. <term>Stoppage</term> <note>Sanskrit: Samvara</note>.** The "Checking of Inflow" and "Bondage of Karmic" molecules is called Stoppage (Samvara).

**VI. <term>Shedding</term> <note>Sanskrit: Nirjarā</note>.** “The shedding of karmas” already bound with a soul, at maturity, or prematurely, is called Shedding. The premature shedding of karmas is caused by pure thought-activities, brought about by the practice of the right kind of austerities. The shedding on maturity is a natural and automatic process.

**<term>Liberation</term> <note>Sanskrit: Moksha</note>.** “Liberation” is freedom from all karmic matter as a result of the non-existence of the cause of bondage and the shedding off of all karmas previously bound. It is the state of a <term>Perfected Soul</term> <note>Sanskrit: Siddha</note>, the condition of perfection.

Continuous devotion to the <term>Trustworthy Authority</term> <note>Sanskrit: Apta</note>, study of the scriptures, and meditation on the seven principles, cause the subsidence of <term>wrong belief</term> <note>Sanskrit: mithyātva</note> and of the four <term>error-feeding passions</term> <note>Sanskrit: anantānubandhi kashāya; literally "passions bound to infinite worldly wandering"</note>, and as a consequence, the real right belief, which is an attribute of the soul, shines forth in its true splendour. At this stage, the right believer is fully convinced of the true and pure nature of his own soul, and this is Real Right belief.

### RIGHT KNOWLEDGE.

**I. Practical Right Knowledge** is the acquisition of the detailed knowledge of all the seven principles explained above, with the help of the Jaina scriptures. This Right-knowledge must be free from three main defects:
*(a)* **doubt** <note>Sanskrit: Saṅshaya</note>,
*(b)* **Perversity** <note>Sanskrit: Viparyaya; meaning a twisted or opposite understanding</note>, and
*(c)* **Indefiniteness** <note>Sanskrit: anadhyavasāya; a lack of focused or decisive grasping of the truth</note>.
It reveals the complete and precise nature of things.

**II. Real Right knowledge** is to know the true and real nature of the soul as quite distinct from all other non-soul substances.

Constant contemplation of, and unflinching devotion to, the subject matter of practical right knowledge is an auxiliary cause to the attainment of Real Right Knowledge.

### RIGHT CONDUCT.

A right believer, who has fully realised the true and real nature of his own soul, and is bent upon getting rid of the karmic filth which is in bondage with his soul, tries to follow Right Conduct. His main object in doing so, is to be free from attachment and aversion, and from all impure thought-activities and to attain the condition of equanimity.

Practical right conduct consists in observing the following five vows:—
(a) <term>Non-violence</term> <note>Sanskrit: Ahimsa</note> ... refraining from doing injury.
(b) <term>Truthfulness</term> <note>Sanskrit: Satya</note> ... refraining from falsehood.
(c) <term>Non-stealing</term> <note>Sanskrit: Asteya</note> ... refraining from theft.
(d) <term>Chastity</term> <note>Sanskrit: Brahmacharya</note> ... Purity.
(e) <term>Non-attachment</term> <note>Sanskrit: Aparigraha</note> ... Non-possession or non-attachment.

This practical right conduct can be observed either partially or fully. Laymen observe it partially, while those who observe it fully are saints. Partial observance is merely a stepping stone to the conduct of a saint, without following which it is not possible to advance spiritually and to ultimately liberate the soul from karmic bondage.

A layman is required to follow the seven supplementary vows also, as they are helpful in the proper observance of the first five.

Out of these seven, the following three are called <term>Multiplicative vows</term> <note>Sanskrit: Gunavratas</note> because they raise the value of the five vows manifold.

<term>Vow of Limited Direction</term> <note>Sanskrit: Dig-Vrata</note>, a life-long vow to limit worldly activities to fixed points in all the 10 directions: North, South, East, West, North-east, North-west, South-east, South-west, above and below.

<term>Vow of Limited Area</term> <note>Sanskrit: Desha-Vrata</note>, a vow to limit worldly activity for a fixed period “only.”

<term>Vow against Purposeless Sin</term> <note>Sanskrit: Anartha-Danda Vrata</note>. Taking a vow not to commit purposeless sin. It is of five kinds:—
(a) <term>Evil Meditation</term> <note>Sanskrit: Apa-Dhyana</note>, thinking ill of others.
(b) <term>Evil Instruction</term> <note>Sanskrit: Papodesha</note>, Preaching evil of others.
(c) <term>Careless Conduct</term> <note>Sanskrit: Pramada-charya</note>. Inconsiderate conduct, such as uselessly breaking the boughs of trees.
(d) <term>Supplying Weapons</term> <note>Sanskrit: Himsa-dan</note>, preparing or supplying instruments of attack.
(e) <term>Harmful Listening</term> <note>Sanskrit: Dushruti</note>, Reading or listening to improper literature.

The following eleven stages of spiritual progress have been laid down for a layman.

2. **<term>Stage of Vows</term> <note>Sanskrit: Vrata-Pratimâ</note>.** In this stage he observes the five main vows to a limited extent (<term>minor vows</term> <note>Sanskrit: anuvratas</note>), without transgression and follows the seven supplementary vows.

4. **<term>Stage of Fasting</term> <note>Sanskrit: Proshadhopavâsa Pratimâ</note>.** In this stage, he observes a fast faultlessly, on the 8th and 14th days of the fortnight.
5. **<term>Stage of Renouncing Animate Food</term> <note>Sanskrit: Sachitta Tyâga Pratimâ</note>.** In this stage he does not take animate water and vegetable, etc. <note>"Animate" refers to living matter, such as raw water or uncooked plants that still possess the potential for life</note>.
6. **<term>Stage of Renouncing Night-Eating</term> <note>Sanskrit: Ratri-Bhukta Tyaga Pratimâ</note>.** He does not take or give food or drink at night.
7. **<term>Stage of Celibacy</term> <note>Sanskrit: Brahmacharya Pratimâ</note>.** He gives up sexual intercourse even with his wife.
8. **<term>Stage of Renouncing Occupations</term> <note>Sanskrit: Arambha Tyâga Pratimâ</note>.** He gives up all profession and all means of earning money and all worldly occupations.
9. **<term>Stage of Renouncing Possessions</term> <note>Sanskrit: Parigraha-Tyâga Pratimâ</note>.** He gives up all desire for objects of the world and abandons all property except a very few limited number of clothes and utensils.

In Webster’s New International Dictionary it is said that **conation** is present wherever consciousness tends of itself to pass from one condition to another.

**Perception** is that act or process of the mind which makes known an external object; or the faculty by which one has knowledge through the medium or instrumentality of bodily organs.

**Conception** is defined in Beeton’s Dictionary, as the simple apprehension or perception that we have of any object without proceeding to affirm or deny anything regarding it. In this sense the word conception would express very nearly the idea conveyed by the word <term>Darsana</term> <note>The initial, undifferentiated awareness of an object.</note> as used in Jain Philosophy. In general language however the word conception as defined in Webster’s Dictionary signifies that mental act or combination of acts by which an idea or notion is formed of an absent object of perception, or of a sensation formerly felt. When we see an object with eyes open, we have a perception of it, when the same object is presented to the mind, with the eyes shut, in idea only, or in memory, we have a conception of it.

**Cognition** is defined as knowledge or certain knowledge as from personal view or experience.

Of the words cognition, conation, perception, and conception, **conception** would best convey the sense of the word <term>Darsana</term>, but it would be liable to mis-interpretation. <note>In the early 20th century, "conation" was sometimes used by translators of Indian philosophy to describe the "striving" or "initial impulse" of consciousness, though today "perception" or "awareness" is more common for Darsana.</note> Hence conation is the best word we can choose to express the idea. <term>Darsana</term> is that undifferentiated, detail-less, indefinite, hazy first stage of knowledge, which always precedes <term>Jñána</term> <note>Detailed or cognitive knowledge.</note>, which is detailed, definite certain knowledge of an object.

18. ->Commentary.<-

When we think of soul as a substance quite free and distinct from the material **Karmas**, we find that from the pure real point of view, it causes and experiences its own pure thought-activities, blissfulness, peace, etc. When we think of a mundane soul which is in bondage with **Karmas**, we find that from the impure real point of view, it causes and experiences its own impure thought-activities, anger, pride, greed, pain, pleasure, etc. These impure thought-activities arise in soul, through the operation of deluding **Karmas**; they are not the soul's own pure and real thought-activities. Whenever any impure thought-activity appears in a mundane soul, the **Karmic** matter is itself attracted towards the soul and bound with it. Thus the soul does not really cause the bondage of the **Karmas** and is from the practical point of view, said to be the causer of **Karmas**. When any material **Karma** which was bound some time before comes into operation and disturbs the thought activity of the soul, it is then that the soul experiences pain or pleasure. Thus the soul, in reality, experiences its own impure thought-activity; but from the practical point of view only it is said to experience the results of material **Karmas**.

**26. That substance which is its own beginning, middle, and end, which cannot be perceived by the senses, and which is indivisible, should be known as an atom.**

### Commentary.

In <term>Gáthás</term> <note>verses</note> Nos. 20—26, the substance “Matter” is described. It is called <term>Pudgala</term> <note>from 'pud' (to combine) and 'gala' (to dissolve)</note>, because it undergoes "Pooryati" <note>original: "pūryati," meaning to fill or combine</note> and forms into molecules, and "galati" <note>dissolves</note> into atoms; it is also so named because it changes in its attributes of color, smell, taste, and touch.

An atom is the smallest possible part of matter. It cannot be divided further.

Those atoms which cause the formation of the four root elements—earth, water, fire, and air—are called **cause-atoms**. When any molecule is broken down into the smallest possible units, the atoms so obtained are called **effect-atoms**. By the combination of atoms in different proportions, various kinds of molecules are formed (see the *Gommat-sara Jiva Kanda*, verses 594). In verses 21–24, the six prominent divisions of material molecules were mentioned:

**Gross-gross** are solid objects which, when separated or broken apart, cannot reunite themselves without the help of some third object, such as paper, wood, or cloth.

**Gross** are liquid objects which, once separated, have the capacity to combine together again without the intervention of any other object, such as water or milk.

**Gross-fine** are those objects which are visible to the eye but cannot be grasped by the hand, such as shade, light, or sunshine.

**Fine-gross** are those objects which are not visible to the eye but are detectable by the other four senses, such as air, sound, smell, and taste.

**Fine** are those molecules which are not recognizable or perceivable by any of the five senses, such as <term>karmic molecules</term> <note>subtle matter that binds to the soul based on actions</note>.

**Fine-fine** are those molecules which are composed of at least two ultimate atoms (<term>Parmánu</term>) and do not fall into the category of the first five kinds.

### Glossary of Terms
**Natural modification:** The inherent state of a single, isolated atom.
**Matter (Pudgala):** In Jainism, this refers to anything that can be sensed; atoms are "real" matter, while molecules are "practical" matter.
**Dharma & Adharma:** Not "religion" or "sin" in this context, but the ontological "mediums" that allow for movement and stillness.
**Akasha:** The substance of Space which provides the "room" for everything else to exist.
**Ávali:** A traditional measurement of time, roughly translated as a "wink" or a very short duration.

### Commentary.

In <term>Gatha</term> <note>A "Gatha" is a specific style of verse or stanza found in ancient Indian texts.</note> No. 9, six principles were mentioned. Out of those, the two—Soul and Matter—were dealt with in verses 10 to 29. In verses 30-36, the remaining four principles have been explained. They act as auxiliary causes to the main functions of Soul and Matter.

**Medium of motion** <note>original: *Dharma*</note> is an essentially auxiliary cause of the movement of Soul and Matter. <note>Just as water helps a fish to swim without forcing it to move, this medium allows for movement.</note>

**Medium of Rest** <note>original: *Adharma*</note> is an essentially auxiliary cause of the static or stationary condition of Soul and Matter. <note>Like the shade of a tree provides a place for a traveler to rest, this medium allows for stability.</note>

**Space** <note>original: *Akasha*</note> is that which provides room or "place" to all substances, including Soul and Matter.

**Time** <note>original: *Kala*</note> is the auxiliary cause that brings about modifications—meaning changes or aging—in all substances, including Soul and Matter.

Out of these six principles, space is the largest, having infinite dimensions in all directions. Within space, there is the **universe** <note>original: *Loka*</note> which contains all the other five substances; these five cannot exist beyond the boundaries of the universe.

Of the medium of motion and rest, each is a single, immaterial substance pervading the entirety of the universe.

The units of time (each of which is an immaterial and individual substance) are very closely packed together in the universe. They are innumerable in number.

Soul and Matter, being infinite, are found everywhere in the universe, in their various gross or fine forms. In connection with this subject, it is necessary to define and explain what a spatial unit (<term>Pradesha</term> <note>A *Pradesha* is the smallest indivisible point of space.</note>) is.

A **spatial unit** is that part of space which is occupied by the smallest indivisible particle of matter. It is a unit generally employed in measuring the dimensions of substances in the universe. If we measure the universe taking a spatial unit as our measure—

...then we find that there are countless spatial units in it. The medium of Motion <note>original: *Dharma*</note> and the medium of Rest <note>original: *Adharma*</note> are as vast as the universe and thus have a similar number of spatial units. Souls are found in various bodies of different sizes in their worldly state, but each soul still possesses the capacity to expand throughout the entire universe. Thus, the spatial units of each individual soul are equal in number to those of the universe—that is, they are countless. Matter exists in the universe either in the form of atoms or in the form of molecules. Molecules are composed of either a specific number, a countless number, or an infinite number of atoms. The spatial units occupied by matter are thus numerable, countless, or infinite.

Space as a substance is infinite in its dimensions; it therefore possesses an infinite number of spatial units.

All the five substances, with the exception of Time, occupy more than one spatial unit. They are therefore called the five extensive substances, or <term>Panch-Astikayas</term> <note>From Sanskrit: *pancha* (five) + *asti* (exists) + *kaya* (body/extension). These are the fundamental realities that occupy space.</note>.

The points of time are also countless. Each one of these points has its own separate and independent existence. Though each one of them is connected with another, they never merge or combine. There are countless spatial units in the universe, so the number of points of time is likewise countless. Because each point of time occupies only one spatial unit, and never more than one in any case, it is called a "non-extensive substance," or <term>Akáya-Dravya</term> <note>Literally "a substance without a body or volume."</note>.

In the universe there are only a countless number of spatial units; how then, it may be asked, can it provide space for an infinite number of atoms and molecules? The explanation is simple. Material atoms possess the capacity to compress an infinite number of themselves into a single molecule, which may occupy no more than one spatial unit. Consider the case of light: the space in a single room may be occupied by the light of one lamp, and also by the light of as many additional lamps as may be placed there.

In <term>Verse 31</term> <note>original: Gatha 31</note>, "Past Time" is defined as being equal to a specific number of winks <note>A "wink" is a traditional unit of time, roughly the duration of a blink.</note> multiplied by the total number of souls that have attained liberation.

As a matter of fact, 608 worldly souls are liberated within every period of 6 months and 8 instants. This process has been going on continuously and uniformly since eternity. If we convert 6 months and 8 instants into winks and divide by 608, we obtain the average time for one soul to obtain liberation—which is a specific number of winks. The number of winks thus obtained is constant and does not vary. The number of souls liberated in the past is infinite, and as shown above, it increases by 608 within every 6-month period.

## Commentary.

The universe is full of souls. They are of two kinds:—
->Liberated and Mundane.<-

Liberated souls are those which are quite free from <term>karmic|In Jainism, karma is considered a physical substance—a subtle form of matter or 'dirt'—that attaches to the soul through our actions and thoughts</term> dirt and have no possibility of ever appearing again in the conditions which arise from the bondage of the <term>karmic</term> matter with the soul. They are all-powerful, all-knowing, all-perceiving, all-blissful, and absorbed in their own real and pure nature. These liberated souls always maintain their individual existence and abide at the topmost point of the universe <note>This refers to the *Siddhashila*, the crescent-shaped region at the peak of the Jain cosmos where perfected souls dwell in eternal bliss.</note>.

Mundane souls are those souls which are in bondage of <term>karmic</term> matter. It is on account of this matter that they exist in the universe, in different conditions of life, and experience innumerable kinds of pain and pleasure. These souls are so avaricious that their worldly wants are never satisfied. Whatever pleasures they enjoy are sensual, so they cannot satisfy their desires. It is a matter of daily experience that the more the desires of a mundane soul are satisfied, the more they are multiplied in every direction. On one side these souls experience the fruits of their past <term>karmas</term>, which shed off after fruition; on the other side, at the same time, they bind fresh good or evil <term>karmas</term>, on account of their multifarious impure thought-activities. This process has been going on since eternity and will go on as long as the soul does not identify and realize itself and does not adopt proper measures to walk on the path of liberation. It is due to these simultaneous operations of fruition <note>the 'ripening' of past actions</note> and bondage <note>the accumulation of new matter</note> that souls transmigrate from one condition of life to the other.

It should be noted that there are 8 main kinds of <term>Karmas</term>:—

(a) Knowledge-obscuring Karma <note>original: *Jnánávarṇíya Karma*</note> is that matter which obscures the knowledge-attribute of the soul.

(b) Perception-obscuring Karma <note>original: *Darshanávarṇíya Karma*</note> <note>The source uses the term 'Conation,' an older word for the faculty of perception or undifferentiated insight that precedes specific knowledge.</note> is that matter which obscures the perception attribute of the soul.

(c) Feeling-producing Karma <note>original: *Vedaníya Karma*</note> is that Karma by the operation of which mundane souls come in contact with agreeable or disagreeable objects, which in their turn cause the feelings of pleasure or pain in the Soul, according to their various degrees of attachment and aversion therein.

---

(d) **Deluding Karma** (*Mohaniya Karma*) is that karma which obscures the soul’s attributes of right belief and right conduct. <note>original Sanskrit: *Mohaniya Karma*. This is considered the most dangerous karma as it prevents the soul from seeing the truth.</note>

(e) **Life-span Karma** (*Áyu Karma*) is that karma which causes and determines the stay of a soul within a particular body. <note>original Sanskrit: *Áyu Karma*</note>

(f) **Body-making Karma** (*Náma Karma*) is that karma, the operation of which causes the condition of existence, the formation of different kinds of bodies, and the change from one condition of existence to another. <note>original Sanskrit: *Náma Karma*. This functions like a biological architect, determining physical traits.</note>

(g) **Status-determining Karma** (*Gotra Karma*) is that karma by the operation of which a soul is to take birth in a high or a low family. <note>original Sanskrit: *Gotra Karma*</note>

(h) **Obstructive Karma** (*Antaráya Karma*) is that karma which obscures the infinite power of the soul. <note>original Sanskrit: *Antaráya Karma*</note>

Out of these eight karmas, types (a), (b), (d), and (h) are known as the **destructive karmas** (*ghátíya*); they are so named because they destroy the manifestation of the real and essential attributes of the soul. The expression of these attributes in <term>mundane souls|Samsari Jivas, or souls still caught in the cycle of birth and death</term> varies inversely to the proportionate decrease of the forces of these four destructive karmas.

The remaining four—namely (c), (e), (f), and (g)—are called **non-destructive karmas** (*Aghátíya Karmas*). They are so called because they do not obscure the real attributes of the soul, but instead affect only the external environments of the soul. Because mundane souls are in the bondage of these eight karmas, they forget their real nature, never realize their true selves, and wander in different conditions of existence—distressed, discontented, and yearning for sensual pleasures.

In order to avoid all this worry and to obtain real and eternal peace and happiness, it is necessary to get rid of the "dirt" of these karmas.

Thus, it is necessary for a mundane soul longing for eternal happiness and salvation that it should identify and realize itself. Identification means knowing the true nature of the self and the <term>karmic matter|In Jainism, karma is a physical substance—fine particles of matter—that coats the soul</term>, which are two substances entirely different from each other.

**The absolute point of view** <note>original Sanskrit: *Nishchaya Naya*</note> is that which deals only with the true and pure aspect of a substance, regardless of its relation with other substances.

**The practical point of view** <note>original Sanskrit: *Vyavahara Naya*</note> is that which deals with a substance by having regard to its relations with other substances.

### Commentary.

<term>Right Belief</term> and <term>Right Knowledge</term>, together with <term>Right Conduct</term>, were described as the path to <term>liberation</term> in <term>Verse|Gatha</term> 3. 

For belief to be truly called <term>Right Belief</term>, it must be free from perversity and false motives. The true motive of every soul seeking spiritual progress is the realization of the self and the attainment of perfect peace, freedom, and happiness. Any other motives that do not lead toward this goal—but instead mislead the soul toward worldly pleasures, which are actually the causes of misery—are false and perverse. 

It is also necessary that <term>Right Belief</term> be free from the defects of wavering, impurity, and a lack of steadfastness. In a "wavering" state of belief, the core of the faith remains sound, but the form is slightly disturbed, much like the surface of an ocean is disturbed by waves. A common example of this is a person who worships <term>Shanti Natha|The 16th Tirthankara, often invoked for peace</term> in preference to other <term>Tirthankaras|Enlightened Teachers</term> specifically for the sake of acquiring worldly peace, wealth, or comfort, rather than focusing on spiritual liberation.

Impurity here implies the following five defects:
(a) <term>Shanká|Doubt</term>,
(b) <term>Kamkshá|Desire for sense-pleasures</term>,
(c) <term>Vichikitsá|Disgust or uncertainty about the path</term>,
(d) <term>Anyadrashti samstava|Admiration for those with wrong beliefs</term>.

Non-steadfastness.—This condition arises when right belief is not quite fixed in the true nature of things. For example, worshipping in one's own temple in preference to the temple built by others. A person who has a steadfast right belief knows that it does not make the slightest difference whether you worship in your temple or in one built by your neighbor. <note>The author is emphasizing that spiritual truth is universal and not tied to pride of ownership or sectarian locations.</note>

The external cause of right belief is to study the Jaina Scriptures and to attend upon those who know them fully well, with the object of understanding the principles from them. The internal cause of right belief is the subsidence, or destructive-subsidence, or destruction of the right belief-deluding <term>Karma|The spiritual "dust" or matter that clouds the soul's natural qualities due to previous actions</term> and of the four error-feeding (<term>anantunubandhi|Passions that bind the soul to the infinite cycle of rebirth</term>) passions. (For details see Gatha 26 Gommatsara Jiva Kanda by Mr. J. L. Jaini S. B. J. Vol V.)

Right knowledge should always be free from doubt, perversity, and indifference. A soul possessing right knowledge has clear and correct knowledge of the essential principles and has the capacity of discrimination between what is worth renouncing and what is worth realizing.

Along with these two, *i.e.*, right belief and right knowledge, right conduct is an essential factor for the attainment of liberation; because neither any one of the three, nor any two of the three can constitute the path of liberation. Right conduct has been described here as being of two kinds. When austerities, etc., are practiced from the practical point of view, with the aim of attaining real right conduct, *i.e.*, self-absorption, they are said to be practical right conduct. Being absorbed in one's own true nature is the real austerity and the same is the real right conduct.

### Commentary.

According to Jaina Philosophy there are 14 spiritual stages of thought-activities <note>These are known as Gunasthanas, representing the gradual evolution of the soul.</note>, which a soul, desirous of being liberated and of attaining perfection has to pass through. The first stage is of wrong belief; the second and third are the intervening progressive stages of thought-activities between wrong belief, and right belief of the 4th stage.

A soul which enters the 4th stage is said to have reached the threshold of liberation. At this stage, the soul has got only right belief. As soon as that soul begins to follow the practical vows of a layman, it is said to enter the 5th stage. Again when this very soul, rising higher up, renounces the world and observes the vows fully and wholly, it is said to have entered the 6th and 7th stages, where it is known as a saint. While a saint remains in these two stages and improves his qualifications, so that he has the capacity of controlling the whole order of saints and supervises their general conduct, he is said to be the Head of the order of saints or an <term>Áchárya<gloss>The spiritual leader and administrative head of the monastic community.</gloss></term>, and as such he follows and practises the five kinds of conduct as given in <term>Gatha<gloss>A specific meter or verse form in Prakrit literature.</gloss></term> 73.

They are as follows :—
(a) Conduct of right belief (<term>Darshanáchára</term>) performance of all those duties which keep right-belief firm.
(b) Conduct of knowledge (<term>Jnáná Áchára</term>) minute and detailed study of the scriptures, with the view of increasing right knowledge.
(c) Conduct of observing saintly character, (<term>Cháritra-áchara</term>). Thirteen kinds of rules of conduct have been described in <term>Gáthás</term> 56-70.

## Commentary.

In Jaina Scriptures the following six essential duties <term>Essential Duties<gloss>Avashyaka; the six daily primary religious obligations that a Jain monk or layperson must perform to purify the soul.</gloss></term> have been prescribed for a saint:—

1. Repentance <term>Repentance<gloss>Pratikramana; literally "returning" from sins committed in thought, word, or deed.</gloss></term>.
2. Renunciation <term>Renunciation<gloss>Pratyâkhyâna; the formal rejection of future possibilities for sin or the renouncing of specific foods/activities.</gloss></term>.
3. Praising the Worshipful Lords <term>Hymns of Praise<gloss>Stuti; praising the attributes of the twenty-four Tirthankaras (enlightened teachers).</gloss></term>.
4. Obeisance to the Worshipful Lords, <term>Respectful Bowing<gloss>Vandanâ; specific acts of veneration directed toward the masters or the image of the Jina.</gloss></term>.
5. Practising of equanimity, <term>Equanimity<gloss>Sâmâyika; a state of meditative calm where one views all living beings and circumstances with impartiality.</gloss></term>.
6. Relinquishment of bodily attachment <term>Bodily Abandonment<gloss>Kâyotsarga; a meditative posture—standing or sitting—where the ascetic ignores physical discomfort to focus entirely on the soul.</gloss></term>.

From the practical point of view <note>Practical point of view (Vyavahara Naya) refers to the conventional or ritualistic path, whereas the "real point of view" refers to the ultimate internal experience.</note>, a saint is said to make repentance, when he recounts his sins and transgressions committed during the performance of his daily routine, and makes penance for them in accordance with rules laid down in the scripture called **"Pratikramaṇa Sutra."** This kind of repentance is of a lower degree compared to the repentance described in Gathas 83 to 93 from the real point of view. Practical repentance is only a step to reach the real repentance which consists in directing the pure thought-activity of soul towards the concentration and realisation of the pure self. This kind of repentance causes the shedding of <note>The text here refers to *Nirjara*, the process of purging or "shaking off" accumulated karmic particles from the soul.</note>

### Commentary.

This chapter on confession (**Álochaná** <note>the ritual of acknowledging and repenting for spiritual failings</note>), has been dealt with from the real point of view only.

Confession (**Álochaná**) from the practical point of view means full and voluntary acknowledgment of one's own faults and transgressions to the Head of the order of saints. It is not pure, but good thought-activity. It is only an auxiliary cause to the real confession. It cannot destroy <term>karmas</term> <note>the subtle particles of matter that attach to the soul through actions and passions</note> in as great a number and quantity as the real and pure confession does. Realization of one's own soul, as quite free from all foreign and impure thought-activities, is the real confession. In this condition, all the four kinds of confession as given in <term>gáthá</term> <note>a verse or stanza of the sacred text</note> 108, are observed simultaneously. Withdrawal of thought-activity from a fault or defect, which a saint has to acknowledge before the Head of the order, is the real confession, (**Álochaná**); and it exists when he is absorbed in his own pure self. At the same time, his pure thought-activity causes the shedding off of <term>karmas</term>, which were bound to his soul on account of the defects of transgression, which he has to acknowledge before the Head of the order, and of other <term>karmas</term> previously accumulated. This process is known as the eradication of the <term>karmas</term> (**Álunchhana** <note>literally "plucking out," representing the internal removal of the roots of sin</note>). In the condition of self-absorption, it is self-evident that there can be no attachment or aversion of any kind in the mind of a saint which may mar the natural beauty of his soul. That is why it is said that real confession includes non-deformity (**Avikriti Karana** <note>the state of the soul remaining in its natural, undistorted form</note>).

Again it is quite apparent that in self-realization, the soul is practically free from passions and desires, such as lust, pride, anger, etc. So purity of thought (**Bháva Shuddhi**) also exists simultaneously with the other three in real confession. The main object of the author is clearly to emphasize that a saint should not rest content only with practical confession, but if he wants to make any real spiritual progress, he should practice real confession; which only is the cause of liberation from karmic bondage.

The state of mind that results from observing the five vows, practicing the five kinds of carefulness, maintaining moral character, and exercising self-control—as well as the focused restraint of the senses—is defined as expiation <note>original: **Práyashchitta**, a process of spiritual purification or "making right" one's internal state</note>. This practice should be performed continually.

<note>original Sanskrit: "क्रोधादिस्वकीयभावक्षयप्रभृतिभावनायां निग्रहणं । प्रायश्चित्तं भणितं निजगुणचिन्ता च निश्चयतः ॥ ११४ ॥"</note>

114. Engaging in the discipline of destroying or subduing one’s own impure thought-activities, such as anger and other passions, while simultaneously meditating upon the inherent attributes of one’s own soul, is considered expiation from the absolute <note>real or ultimate</note> point of view.

<note>original Sanskrit: "क्रोधं क्षमया मानं स्वमार्दवेन आर्जवेन मायां च । सन्तोषेण च लोभं जयति खलु चतुर्विधकषायाणाम् ॥ ११५ ॥"</note>

115. A saint truly conquers the four kinds of passions <note>original: **kashayas**, the internal "sticky" emotions that bind karma to the soul</note> in this manner: he overcomes anger with forgiveness, pride with self-humility, deceit with straightforwardness, and greed with contentment.

<note>original Prakrit: "कायाईपरदव्वे थिरभावं परिहरत्तु अप्पाणं । तस्स हवे तणुसग्गं जो झायइ णिव्वियप्पेण ॥ १२१ ॥"</note>

121. He, who discarding the idea of the durability of other objects, such as the body, etc., and meditates upon his own soul with a concentrated mind, is said to have a “withdrawal of attachment from the body” (<term>Káyostsarga</term>) <note>a meditative practice in Jainism where the ascetic stands or sits motionless, ignoring the body to focus entirely on the soul</note>. This is also considered a form of expiation.

### Commentary.

Though a saint generally tries his best to observe his vows and follow his daily routine regularly and cautiously; still, it sometimes happens that on account of the operation of some conduct-deluding <term>karmas</term> <note>subtle matter that binds to the soul and obscures its true nature</note>, his thoughts and actions fall short of the requisite standard. Thus a slight defect or flaw appears in the observance of his saintly conduct and on account of this, demeritorious karmas are bound to his soul. In order to counter-balance or reduce the effect of these karmas so bound, he is required to resort to the practice of expiation.

The word <term>Práyashchitta</term> <note>literally "atonement" or "expiation"</note> means the purification of one’s soul from sins committed. A saint, whenever any transgression is committed by him, goes to the head of his order and frankly acknowledges his faults before him. Whatever punishment is proposed by the leader by way of atonement, the saint gladly accepts and complies with the orders with proper care and caution. This is called practical expiation.
