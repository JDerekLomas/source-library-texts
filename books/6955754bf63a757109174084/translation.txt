(Having bowed to the Supreme Lord) as well as to Brahma and the other teachers:
-> This summary of the <term>Gandharva-shastra</term><note>The authoritative science or scripture of sacred music</note> is spoken by me in its essential form. ||1|| <-

In the beginning, the Self-born Creator <note>Brahma</note> gave the <term>Gandharva</term> to Narada and others;
-> It was then brought down to earth by Narada in accordance with the sacred rules. ||2|| <-

A collection of notes <note>Svaras</note> residing in words, well-measured by time-beat <note>Tala</note>,
-> and performed with focused attention, is designated as Gandharva. ||3|| <-

One should learn the words from common usage <gloss>missing? here</gloss> and through the favor of the science of grammar;
-> "Focused attention" is well known as the application of a perfectly concentrated mind. ||4|| <-

Other matters must be spoken of by one desiring brevity here;
-> Among those, the section on notes is taught first, because it constitutes the song. ||5|| <-

The micro-tones, the notes, the two scales, the scales in sequence with their melodic movements,
-> the registers, the styles, the "dry" instrumental playing, and the two types of overlapping notes, ||6|| <-

the melodic modes, and the melodic patterns joined with various ornaments:
-> this is the <gloss>region? two</gloss> scope of the section on notes; now follows the determination in brief. ||7|| <-

In the chest of men, there is a twenty-two-fold sound called the low register;
-> that same sound becomes the medium register in the throat, and is sung as the high register in the head. ||8|| <-

Each becomes higher and higher in pitch, while on the <term>Veena</term><note>A traditional Indian stringed instrument</note> they go from lower to higher strings;
-> because they are perceived through hearing, these specific distinctions of sound are termed <term>Shrutis</term><note>Micro-tones; literally "that which is heard"</note>. ||9|| <insert>vi</insert>

Having selected some from among those, they are sung in all songs;
-> those which are respected among them attain the status of a <term>Svara</term><note>A musical note</note>. ||10||

The notes are the seven beginning with <term>Shadja</term><note>The tonic or first note</note>; the scales are two: the Shadja-scale and the Madhyama-scale.
-> Some also speak of a Gandhara-scale, but <gloss>indeed</gloss> it is not found here <note>meaning it is not used in the earthly musical system</note>. ||11||

In the Shadja-scale, whatever sound is taken as the Shadja;
-> the third sound above that would be <term>Rishabha</term>, of this there is no doubt. ||12||

The second from that is <term>Gandhara</term>; the fourth from that is <term>Madhyama</term>;
-> from Madhyama, <term>Panchama</term> is likewise the fourth; the third from that is <term>Dhaivata</term>. ||13||

From that, <term>Nishada</term> <gloss>second</gloss> <gloss>is? would be</gloss> the second; then the Shadja is the fourth.
-> In the Madhyama-scale, the Panchama is the third from the Madhyama. ||14||

The note *Nishada* becomes known as *Kakali* by raising it by two *shrutis* <gloss>(micro-tones)</gloss>. In the same way, *Gandhara* becomes known as the *Antara* note <insert>by a similar raising of pitch</insert>. ||16||
<note>Kakali Nishada and Antara Gandhara are "altered" notes used to bridge intervals, similar to sharps in Western music.</note>

Because these two <insert>Kakali and Antara</insert> cannot serve as the primary tonic (*amsha*), they are not classified as independent notes in the division. Therefore, the authorities have declared them to be variations of *Nishada* and *Gandhara* <gloss>(or: as belonging to them)</gloss>. ||17||

The note which is most frequent in a composition is the *Vadi* <term>sonant/melodic center</term> or the *Amsha* <term>predominant note</term>. Those notes which harmonize with each other are known as *Samvadi* <term>consonant</term>, having an interval of thirteen or nine *shrutis* between them. ||18||

The remaining notes are called *Anuvadi* <term>assonant</term>, while those with an interval of two *shrutis* are *Vivadi* <term>dissonant</term>. One who knows the application of notes should recognize these four types of swaras. ||19||

In the *Madhyama Grama* <term>the scale starting on the fourth note</term>, the note *Panchama* cannot be the tonic (*amsha*). In the *Shadja Grama* <term>the scale starting on the first note</term>, *Dhaivata* cannot be the tonic. However, *Madhyama* can be the tonic everywhere. ||20||

Whichever numerical position the notes *Shadja* and *Madhyama* hold in their respective scales (*gramas*), the *Murchana* <term>mode/melodic sequence</term> holds that same numerical position. ||21||

1. *Uttaramandra*, 2. *Rajani*, 3. the third is *Uttarayata*, 4. the fourth is *Shuddhashadja*, 5. the fifth is *Matsarikrita*, ||22||

6. the sixth is *Ashvakranta*, and 7. the seventh is *Abhirudgata*. These are the seven *Murchanas* of the *Shadja Grama*, known through the sequence of notes. ||23||

In the *Madhyama Grama*, the modes are: 1. *Sauviri*, 2. *Harinashva* <gloss>(named thus)</gloss>, 3. *Kapolanata*, 4. the fourth is *Shuddhamadhyama*, ||24||

5. *Mandi*, 6. *Pauravi*, and 7. *Hrishyaka* <gloss>(the suffix 'ka' added)</gloss>—these are the seven *Murchanas*. They are remembered as being complete (seven notes), or modified into hexatonic (six) or pentatonic (five) forms. ||25||

If one makes *Gandhara* into *Dhaivata* by raising it by <gloss>(two)</gloss> *shrutis*, and accordingly adjusts *Madhyama* and the others, and *Nishada* and the others in their fixed positions, ||26||

Then, whatever numerical position that *Murchana* held in the *Shadja Grama*, the corresponding *Murchana* of the *Madhyama Grama* is produced. ||27||

By lowering the pitch by two *shrutis* and making *Dhaivata* into *Gandhara*, one should realize the *Shadja Murchanas* from the *Madhyama* ones, as described before. ||28||

These *Murchanas* and their progressions (*sarana*) have been described by the experts in the *Vaishika* arts. Having established the *Murchana* in this way, the other sequences follow the instrumental <gloss>(?)</gloss> order. ||29||

The *Murchanas* that are sung with five notes or six notes are called *Tanas* <term>melodic fragments</term>. The authorities have declared that there are eighty-four such *Tanas*. ||30||

That which is perceived in one *Jati* <note>melodic mode or type</note> as being similar to another *Jati*,
->and because of its frequent occurrence within the same scale, is called a "Shared Mode" (*Jati-sadharana*). [47]<-

The *Jatis* are known to be eighteen in number. Seven of them are named after the notes (*Svaras*).
->These are the "Pure" (*Shuddha*) types; the remaining ones are "Modified" (*Vikrita*), arising from their hybridization. [48]<-

From the union of the *Shadji* and *Madhyami* modes comes <term>Shadjamadhyama</term> <note>literally "the middle of the first note"</note>.
->From *Shadji* and *Gandhari* is born <term>Shadjakashiki</term>. [49]<-

From those same two, when combined with *Dhaivati*, <term>Shadjodichyavati</term> is formed.
->From these combined with *Madhyama*, <term>Gandharodichyava</term> is produced. [50]<-

From the mixture of *Gandhari*, *Madhyama*, and *Panchami*,
->and when combined with *Dhaivati*, <term>Madhyamodichyava</term> is formed. [51]<-

From these, <term>Rakta-gandhari</term> arises if *Naishadi* is the fourth component.
->From the mixture of *Arshabhi* and *Gandhari*, the mode <term>Andhri</term> is born. [52]<-

From these two, when combined with *Panchami*, *Nandayanti* is produced.
->The *Gandhari* modes, when combined with *Nishada*, create <term>Karmaravi</term>. [53]<-

From <term>Gandhari</term> and <term>Panchami</term> comes <term>Gandharapanchami</term>.
->The hybrid mode <term>Kaishiki</term> is formed by excluding *Arshabhi* and *Dhaivati*. [54]<-

The <term>Graha</term> <note>starting note</note>, <term>Amsha</term> <note>dominant note</note>, <term>Tara</term> <note>high register</note>, and <term>Mandra</term> <note>low register</note>, as well as <term>Shadava</term> <note>six-note scale</note> and <term>Auduvita</term> <note>five-note scale</note> in sequence; [55]

The "Paucity" (*Alpatva*) and "Abundance" (*Bahutva*), and the <term>Nyasa</term> <note>final note</note> and <term>Apanyasa</term> <note>intermediate stop</note>—
->these ten are the <term>Jati-lakshanas</term> <note>defining characteristics of a melodic mode</note> as they apply to each *Jati*. [56]<-

The definition of these ten will now be stated in brief:
->There, the <term>Graha</term> is the initial note of a song; the *Amsha* has been described previously. [57]<-

The <term>Tara</term> <note>high register</note> is desired to extend five notes above the *Amsha* <note>dominant note</note>.
->In the *Nandayanti* mode, it is not prescribed to go beyond the note *Shadja*. The <term>Mandra</term> <note>low register</note> is the lower limit, ending at the *Amsha* or the *Nyasa*. [58]<-

In a song, the use of six or five notes constitutes <term>Shadava</term> and <term>Auduvita</term> respectively.
->"Paucity" and "Abundance" result from the infrequent or frequent use of specific notes. [59]<-

The <term>Nyasa</term> is the final note of a song; the <term>Apanyasa</term> occurs within the *Vidari* <note>musical phrase or section</note>.
->The <term>Apanyasa</term> is like the *Nyasa*; I shall describe them according to each *Jati*. [60]<-

That note without which a mode becomes deficient is its "Paucity" (*Alpaka*).
->The *Amsha* and others, and the different *Nyasas*, give names to the various note-modes. [61]<-

When the <term>Graha</term>, <term>Apanyasa</term>, and <term>Amsha</term> are the same,
->and it ends on the *Mandra* and is "Full" <note>using all seven notes</note>, it is then called a "Pure" <term>Jati</term>. [62]<-

# Dattilam

The <term>Madhyama</term> <note>The fourth note of the scale, 'Ma'</note> jati is known to have five <term>Amshas</term> <note>The dominant or 'soul' notes of a scale</note>, excluding those with two <term>Shrutis</term> <note>The smallest audible intervals or microtones</note>. In this jati, the notes <term>Shadja</term> <note>The first note, 'Sa'</note> and <term>Madhyama</term> are used frequently, while others are diminished in order. ||78||

The <term>Madhyamodichyava</term> jati is similar to <term>Gandharodichyava</term>. It must always contain all seven notes, and its dominant note (<term>Amsha</term>) is <term>Panchama</term> <note>The fifth note, 'Pa'</note>. ||79||

In the <term>Panchami</term> jati, the masters say the dominant notes are <term>Rishabha</term> <note>The second note, 'Ri'</note> <insert>and Dhaivata <note>The sixth note, 'Dha'</note></insert> and <term>Panchama</term>. The secondary endings (<term>Apanyasa</term>) are <term>Nishada</term> <note>The seventh note, 'Ni'</note> and <term>Shadja</term>. There is a frequent melodic movement between <term>Madhyama</term> and <term>Rishabha</term>. ||80||

In this same jati, <term>Shadja</term>, <term>Madhyama</term>, and <term>Gandhara</term> <note>The third note, 'Ga'</note> are described as being used sparingly. <term>Nishada</term> and <term>Gandhara</term> are omitted in the same way as in the <term>Madhyama</term> jati. ||81||

In the <term>Gandharapanchami</term> jati, performers should know that <term>Panchama</term> is the dominant note. <term>Rishabha</term> serves as the secondary ending, and <term>Gandhara</term> is desired as the final note (<term>Nyasa</term>). ||82||

Whatever melodic movements (<term>Sanchara</term>) were described for <term>Gandhari</term> and <term>Panchami</term> should also be understood for this jati, but it is always played with all seven notes (<term>Purna</term>). ||83||

In the <term>Andhri</term> jati, the dominant notes are known to be <term>Shadja</term>, <term>Madhyama</term>, and <term>Dhaivata</term>. It becomes hexatonic (<term>Shadava</term>) <note>A six-note scale</note> by omitting <term>Shadja</term>, and <term>Gandhara</term> is the preferred final note. ||84||

In the <term>Nandayanti</term> jati, <term>Madhyama</term> and <term>Panchama</term> are the secondary endings. The starting note (<term>Graha</term>) and the final note are <term>Gandhara</term>, while <term>Panchama</term> is declared the dominant note. ||85||

Its hexatonic and pentatonic (<term>Auduvita</term>) <note>A five-note scale</note> forms are known to be like those of <term>Andhri</term>. There should be melodic movement in the lower <term>Rishabha</term>, and <term>Madhyama</term> should never be skipped. ||86||

In the <term>Karmaravi</term> jati, the dominant notes are <term>Shadja</term>, <term>Gandhara</term>, and <term>Panchama</term>. It is a full seven-note scale; <term>Panchama</term> is the final note, and there is much movement toward <term>Gandhara</term>. ||87||

In the <term>Kaishiki</term> jati, <term>Rishabha</term> is not a dominant note. The two notes with two microtones <note>Gandhara and Nishada</note> are remembered as the final notes. <term>Rishabha</term> and <term>Dhaivata</term> are to be dropped respectively <note>to form six or five-note scales</note>. ||88||

<term>Panchama</term> may also be a final note. When <term>Nishada</term> or <term>Dhaivata</term> are dominant, <term>Rishabha</term> is the secondary ending; some say it can even act as a dominant note. ||89||

In this jati, <term>Panchama</term> is strong, as is <term>Nishada</term>. Thus, there are sixty-three dominant notes in total; by considering each one individually, ||90||

the rules for six-note and five-note scales are applied. The <term>Shadjamadhyama</term> jati does not become hexatonic when <term>Nishada</term> is the dominant note, ||91||

Their first note was <term>Rishabha</term> <note>The second note or 'Re'</note>, from which they would "move" or "flow." Until this <term>Margi Sangeet</term> was created, there was only <term>Samika</term> <note>Chanting of the Sama Veda</note> singing. By "measuring" from those six, the seventh note, 'Sa', was created. Because it was born from the <term>Shat</term> (six), it was called <term>Shad-ja</term> <note>Literally "born of six"</note>. Since the position of 'Sa' was already vacant during the Vedic period, this <term>Margi Sangeet</term> was born in the immediate future of that tradition. Therefore, in certain types of singing, omitting 'Sa' did not feel unpleasant. If someone were to sing a <term>Jati</term> from this <term>Margi Sangeet</term> before modern singers today while omitting 'Sa', you would never be able to perceive that "he is not using Sa." If he uses a <term>Tivra</term> (sharp) <term>Nishada</term> <note>The seventh note or 'Ni'</note>, you would mistake that very note for 'Sa'. In reality, the sharp <term>Nishada</term> of that era has become the 'Sa' of today. If a singer omits the 'Sa' of the fourth register, it remains omitted even today. You have never heard its use with your ears, nor is it in your throat. If you tried to force the use of that pitch, listeners would immediately scream that it is <term>Kansura</term> <note>Out of tune/discordant</note>! Tell me then, how can you understand that 'Sa' has been omitted? Especially when omitting 'Sa' is [inherent] in your nature...

It is said that when the cessation of sound occurs equally within the measure of the performance, it is balanced. And from the syllables of the words that are completed in half the time, the <term>Ardha-Magadhi</term> <note>literally 'Half-Magadhi'; a specific style of ancient song</note> style is spoken. ||238||

The song-style generally produced by short syllables within a single rotation (rhythmic cycle) is called <term>Sambhavita</term>. And that which is produced by long syllables is <term>Prithula</term>, which is always considered to have the grasp of the syllables in the southern style. ||239||

In the various divisions of <term>Margi music</term> <note>the 'classical' or 'sacred' path of music, as opposed to regional folk styles</note>, these four song-styles are prescribed for use in their proper places. They are 'intended' for the Path, the foundations of which are called <term>Dhruvas</term> <note>fixed rhythmic or melodic structures</note>. In the Ratnakara <note>referring to the Sangita Ratnakara, a later monumental musicological text</note>, there are sixteen types of Dhruvas. ||240||

That which is performed with a well-bound, steady tempo is called <term>Matrika</term>. Double the Matrika is <term>Chitra</term>, and double the Chitra are the two 'Uttara' (later) parts. ||241||
