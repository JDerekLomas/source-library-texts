# Imperial Crowns and Clothing

<note>original: "御用冠服" (Yùyòng Guānfú). This term refers specifically to the ceremonial wardrobe and headgear reserved for the Emperor’s personal use during state rituals and court functions.</note>

## Ceremonial Crown (Mian)

<note>The Miǎn (冕) is the most formal ceremonial crown worn by the Emperor, characterized by the flat rectangular board (yàn) with strings of pearls or beads (liú) hanging from the front and back. The number of bead strings—usually twelve for the Emperor—signified the highest possible rank in the cosmic and social order.</note>

## Crown that Reaches Heaven (Tongtianguan)

<note>The Tōngtiānguān (通天冠, "Crown that Reaches Heaven") was a high-status crown worn by emperors during significant court ceremonies that were slightly less formal than the great sacrifices requiring the Miǎn. It is characterized by its tall, upward-reaching shape, symbolizing the Emperor's role as the mediator between Heaven and Earth.</note>

<term>Imperial Crowns and Clothing</term> <note>original: "御用冠服" (Yùyòng Guānfú). The strictly regulated system of dress for the sovereign.</note>

# Black Gauze Hat with Folded-up Wings

<note>original: "烏紗折上巾" (Wūshā zhéshàngjīn). This headpiece, also commonly known as the "Winged Crown of Goodness" (yìshànguān), served as the Emperor’s primary daily hat for conducting government business. Unlike the wings of a standard official’s hat which protrude sideways, the wings on the Emperor's hat are folded upwards, symbolizing his unique and supreme status.</note>

# Leather Cap

<note>original: "皮弁" (Píbiàn). A highly ancient and prestigious form of ritual headgear. By the Ming Dynasty, it was constructed from several vertical segments of white deerskin joined together. Each seam was typically adorned with rows of colorful jade or gemstone beads, reflecting the "twelve-vein" system of rank. It was worn during significant but slightly less solemn occasions than the Great Sacrifices, such as seasonal court audiences or when the Emperor acted in a military capacity.</note>

# Dark Robe

<note>original: "玄衣" (Xuányī). The Dark Robe is the upper garment of the Emperor’s most formal ritual attire, known as the *Mianfu*. In Chinese cosmology, "Xuan" (a deep, dark color often interpreted as black or a very dark blue-black) represents the mystery and depth of the Northern Heavens. This robe acts as a canvas for the first six of the Twelve Ornaments, which symbolize the virtues of a righteous ruler. In this illustration, the dragons (symbolizing adaptability and power) and the "flowery creature" or pheasant (symbolizing literary elegance) are prominently displayed on the sleeves and shoulders.</note>

# Crimson Skirt

<note>original: "纁裳" (Xūncháng). The Crimson Skirt is the lower garment of the ceremonial dress. Its color, "Xun" (a reddish-orange or crimson), represents the Earth, complementing the celestial upper robe. This skirt is traditionally decorated with the remaining six ornaments: the temple cups (filial piety), waterweed (purity), fire (brilliance), grains of rice (the ability to nourish the people), the axe (the power to judge), and the "fu" symbol (the ability to discern right from wrong). These patterns are usually arranged in pairs or groups across the fabric panels.</note>

## The Leather Belt

->Leather Belt<-

<note>original: "革帶" (Gédài). This is a rigid, formal belt used in Ming dynasty court attire. Unlike a functional belt, it was primarily a vehicle for rank-signifying plaques made of jade, gold, or horn. It was worn loosely around the outer robe, often resting on the hips or suspended by loops, creating a distinctive silhouette that marked the wearer as a high-ranking official or member of the imperial household.</note>

->Grand Sash<-

<note>original: "大帶" (Dàdài). A ceremonial silk sash or girdle. Unlike the rigid leather belt, the Grand Sash was made of soft fabric and featured long hanging streamers known as *shen*. It was tied around the waist over the robe, symbolizing the "bound" or disciplined nature of the Confucian gentleman. The colors of the sash and its borders were strictly regulated according to the wearer's social standing.</note>

## The Ceremonial Ribbon-Sash

->Ceremonial Ribbon-Sash<-

<note>original: "綬" (Shòu). A long, wide, intricately woven silk ribbon that hung from the back of the waist. In the Ming dynasty ritual system, the color, density of the weave, and the specific floral or geometric patterns were strictly regulated to indicate the wearer's rank. For the Emperor, these were often made of yellow or multicolored silk with dragon motifs, ending in a heavy fringe.</note>

## The Ritual Jade Pendants

->Ritual Jade Pendants<-

<note>original: "珮" (Pèi). This refers to a complex "set" of carved jade plaques (often called *Yupei*) suspended from the waist by silk cords interspersed with small beads. These served a dual purpose: they were beautiful markers of high status, and they functioned as a "metronome" for the wearer's conduct. The jade pieces were designed to strike one another as the wearer walked; a steady, rhythmic "clinking" sound indicated a calm and dignified gait, whereas a chaotic sound suggested a lack of self-control.</note>

<term>Ceremonial Ribbon-Sash</term> <note>original: "綬" (Shòu). A woven textile ornament that identifies the wearer's position in the imperial hierarchy.</note>

# Stockings

<note>original: "襪" (Wà). In the context of the Ming Dynasty imperial ritual dress (Mianfu), these were not simple socks but formal stockings made of crimson silk. <note>original: "舄" (Xì). These represent the highest tier of formal footwear in ancient China. For the Emperor, these were traditionally red to match the rest of his lower ritual garments.</note>

# Imperial Crowns and Garments
<note>original: "御用冠服" (Yùyòng guānfú). This refers specifically to the attire worn exclusively by the Emperor for the most formal state rituals.</note>

In our current dynasty <note>The Ming Dynasty (1368–1644).</note>, for the sacrifices to Heaven, the "Great Fur" robe is no longer used; instead, the **Ceremonial Dragon Robe and Crown** are worn. <term>Dragon Robe and Crown</term> <note>original: "衮冕" (Gǔnmiǎn). This is the "Gun" robe—decorated with dragons—and the "Mian" crown—a flat-topped mortarboard style with dangling strings of beads.</note> This ensemble is worn for sacrifices to Heaven and Earth, the Imperial Ancestral Temple, the Altars of Soil and Grain, and the First Farmer. It is also worn on New Year’s Day, the Winter Solstice, the Emperor’s Birthday, during formal court audiences, and for the solemn ceremonies of investiture or appointment.

The ensemble consists of a **Dark Upper Robe** and an **Ochre-Red Lower Skirt**. <note>original: "玄衣纁裳" (Xuányī xūncháng). The top is covered with a dark exterior and lined with vermilion. At the front and back of the crown, there are **Twelve Tassels**; each tassel consists of twelve jade beads in five colors. <term>Twelve Tassels</term> <note>original: "十二旒" (Shí'èr liú). These strings of beads hung before the Emperor's eyes, symbolically reminding him to remain focused and not see every minor fault of his subjects, maintaining a sense of impartial majesty.</note> A jade hairpin secures the crown, accompanied by vermilion tassels.

The upper robe features **Six Emblems** painted upon it: the Sun, the Moon, the Stars, the Mountains, the Dragons, and the "Flowery Bird." <note>original: "華蟲" (Huáchóng), usually depicted as a pheasant, representing literary refinement.</note> The lower skirt features **Six Emblems** embroidered upon it: the Ancestral Temple Vessels, aquatic weeds, fire, grains of rice, the axe-head, and the "distinct" pattern. <term>Six Emblems</term> <note>Together, the 6 on the robe and 6 on the skirt make the "Twelve Symbols of Imperial Authority," representing the Emperor's virtues (e.g., the axe represents the power to judge, fire represents brilliance).</note>

<note>original: "翡翠" (fěicuì). While often translated as jade, in the context of Ming crowns, it refers to the "tian-tsui" technique—decorating metal with the iridescent blue feathers of the kingfisher bird.</note>

<note>original: "黼領" (fǔ lǐng). The axe pattern is one of the "Twelve Ornaments" of imperial authority, symbolizing the power to distinguish right from wrong.</note>

# The Square Heart and Curved Collar

<vocab>Square Heart and Curved Collar (方心曲領, Fāngxīn Qūlǐng), Clothing (衣服, Yīfú)</vocab>

-> **The Cosmological Collar** <-

<term>Square Heart and Curved Collar (方心曲領, Fāngxīn Qūlǐng)</term>: A specialized ritual neckpiece worn over the primary ceremonial robe. It consists of a rigid circular band that fits around the neck, with a rectangular (square) pendant that hangs down over the wearer's chest.

<note>This accessory is one of the most philosophically significant parts of Chinese ritual dress. It embodies the ancient concept of *Tiānyuán Dìfāng* (天圓地方), or "Round Heaven and Square Earth." The circular collar represents the sky/heavens, while the square pendant represents the earth. By wearing it, the Crown Prince or Emperor physically represents his role as the mediator between the two realms.</note>

<insert>As depicted in traditional woodcuts, the pendant is kept flat and centered on the chest, often finished with silk ribbons that tie behind the neck to secure the piece in place.</insert>

# Ceremonial Dress of the Crown Prince

In our current dynasty, when the Crown Prince accompanies the Emperor to perform sacrifices to Heaven and Earth, the Imperial Ancestral Temple, or the Altars of Soil and Grain, as well as when he receives his formal investiture, attends the New Year’s Day or Winter Solstice audiences, celebrates the Emperor’s birthday, or takes a consort, he wears the **Gunmian** <note>original: "衮冕"</note>.

### The Nine-Fringed Crown

The system of this crown includes **nine strings of fringes** <note>original: "九旒", jiǔliú</note>. Each string is strung with nine jade beads. It is secured with red silk cords and a gold hairpin that guides the assembly, and it features two jade ear-plugs <note>The "ear-plugs" (瑱, tiàn) were small jade stones hanging by the ears to symbolize that the wearer should not listen to slander or trivial talk.</note>.

### The Sacrificial Robe of Nine Emblems

The **Gun Robe** <note>original: "衮服"</note> is adorned with **nine emblems** of authority:

*   **The Upper Robe**: Made of black silk <note>original: "玄衣", xuányī</note>, it is painted with five emblems: **Mountains**, **Dragons**, **Flowery Fowls** (pheasants), **Fire**, and **Ritual Vases** (depicting a tiger and a monkey).
*   **The Lower Skirt**: Made of vermilion silk <note>original: "纁裳", xūncháng</note>, it is embroidered with four emblems: **Waterweed**, **Grains of Rice**, the **Axe-head**, and the **"Fu" Symbol** <note>The "Fu" (黻) is a distinct black and blue pattern resembling two "bow" shapes facing away from each other, symbolizing the ability to distinguish right from wrong.</note>.

### Accoutrements and Ornaments

*   **Inner Garment**: A white gauze tunic with an "axe-head" pattern embroidered on the collar.
*   **Knee-cover** <note>original: "蔽膝", bìxī</note>: Matches the vermilion color of the skirt and is embroidered with the emblems of **Fire** and **Mountains**.
*   **Belts and Pendants**: A leather belt with gold hooks and a buckle. Jade pendants hang from five-colored ribbons: red, white, black, light blue, and green. These ribbons are woven on a solid red base with a count of **320 threads** <note>original: "三百二十首"</note>.
*   **Small Ribbons**: Three smaller ribbons in colors matching the large ones are interspersed with **three jade rings**.
*   **Large Sash**: Made of white silk on the outside and vermilion on the inside. The top edge is bordered in red, and the bottom edge in green.
*   **Footwear**: Worn with white socks and the **Red Ritual Shoes** <note>As described on the previous page, these are the double-soled "Chìxì".</note>.

<column-break/>

### The Leather Cap Ensemble

On the first and fifteenth of each lunar month, and during court occasions such as the proclamation of imperial edicts, the offering of incense, the presentation of formal petitions, or when receiving tribute and audiences from foreign nations, the Crown Prince wears the **Pibian** <note>original: "皮弁"</note>.

<term>Pibian (皮弁)</term>: A ceremonial cap originally made of deer skin. In the Ming system, it served as a high-level court uniform, slightly less formal than the sacrificial "Gunmian" but more solemn than daily robes.

<insert>Note: The number of emblems (nine) and crown fringes (nine) for the Crown Prince is a specific rank indicator. The Emperor himself wore twelve, symbolizing his unique status at the top of the celestial hierarchy.</insert>

In the current dynasty, when a Prince receives his patent of investiture, assists in sacrifices, visits the ancestral temple, or participates in court greetings for New Year’s Day, the Winter Solstice, or the Emperor’s Birthday—as well as when taking a consort—he shall wear the **Ritual Robes and Crown**. 

<note>original: "衮冕", gǔnmiǎn. This was the most formal attire in the Ming hierarchy, second only to the Emperor's twelve-emblem set.</note>

### The Nine-Emblem Crown
The Crown uses nine tassels <note>original: "九旒", jiǔliú</note> strung with jade beads of five colors. The chin straps are made of red silk cords. The ear-fillers are made of blue silk padding, and the assembly is secured by a gold hairpin which serves as a guide through the crown.

### The Sacrificial Robe
The **Sacrificial Robe** consists of a blue upper garment and a vermilion lower garment <note>original: "纁裳", xūncháng</note>. 

*   **On the upper garment:** Five emblems are painted: the **Mountain**, the **Dragon**, the **Flowery Bird** (pheasant), **Fire**, and the **Ancestral Temple Cups**. 
*   **On the lower garment:** Four emblems are embroidered: **Pondweed**, **Grains of Rice**, the **Axe**, and the **"Fu" Symbol** (a black and white geometric pattern signifying discernment). 

<term>Nine Emblems (Jiǔzhāng): While the Emperor wore twelve sacred symbols, a Prince was permitted nine, reflecting his high status while maintaining the ritual hierarchy that placed the Emperor at the summit.</term>

### Accessories and Undergarments
Beneath the robe, a white gauze inner garment is worn, featuring a collar decorated with the "fu" (axe) pattern and blue borders. A vermilion **Knee-Cover** <note>original: "蔽膝", bìxī</note> is attached to the waist, embroidered with two emblems: fire and mountains. 

The ensemble is secured with a leather belt featuring a gold hook. It also includes hanging jade pendants and ceremonial ribbons. A **Large Sash** <note>original: "大帶", dàdài</note> is worn, made of white silk on both the interior and exterior, finished with vermilion and green borders. The outfit is completed with white stockings and vermilion shoes.

### The Skin Cap Attire
For audiences held on the new moon and full moon, the proclamation of imperial edicts, the offering of incense, the submission of memorials, or when receiving foreign envoys and tribute-bearers, the Prince shall wear the **Skin Cap** attire.

<note>original: "皮弁", pibiàn. A cap traditionally made from strips of deerskin, symbolizing a blend of military readiness and ancient refinement.</note>

### Quail Rank Patch
<note>Designated for the **Eighth Rank**, **Ninth Rank**, and **Miscellaneous Posts**.</note>
The <term>Quail Rank Patch<note>original: "鵪鶉補", ānchún bǔ. The quail is a small, hardy bird chosen for its symbolic phonetic connection to peace.</note></term> was worn by the lowest-ranking officials and those in miscellaneous administrative roles. The quail was chosen primarily as a <term>rebus<note>A visual pun where an image represents a word with the same sound.</note></term>; the word for quail in Chinese (*an*) is a homophone for "peace" or "tranquility" (*an*). Thus, the badge served as a reminder that even the lowliest official's primary duty was to ensure the peace and stability of the realm.
