# A NOTE

THIS book is intentionally *not* the work of Brother Perdurabo <note>Latin: *Frater Perdurabo*, the magical motto of Aleister Crowley, meaning "I will endure to the end."</note>. Experience shows that his writing is too concentrated, too obscure, and too hidden <note>original: "occult"</note> for ordinary minds to understand. It is thought that this record of disjointed fragments of his casual conversation may prove to be both more intelligible and more convincing. At the very least, it should provide a preliminary study which will enable the student to approach his real work from a position of some general knowledge and an understanding of his ideas, as well as the symbols <note>original: "form"</note> in which he represents them.

Part II, “Magick,” is more advanced in style than Part I; the student is expected to know a little of the literature of the subject and to be able to take an intelligent view of it. This part is, however, truly explanatory of Part I, which is only a rough, preliminary sketch.

If both parts are thoroughly studied and understood, the pupil will have obtained a real grasp of all the fundamentals and essentials of both Magick and Mysticism.

I wrote this book down from Brother Perdurabo’s dictation at the Villa Caldarazzo, Posilippo, Naples, where I was studying under him.

## THE TRAINING FOR MEDITATION

### PRELIMINARY REMARKS

Until now, we have spoken only of the mystic path, and we have kept particularly to its practical, outer <note>original: exoteric</note> side. Any difficulties we have mentioned have been purely natural obstacles. For example, the great question of the surrender of the self—which is so prominent in most mystical writings <note>original: treatises</note>—has not been referred to at all. We have only said what a person must do; we have not considered at all what that doing may involve. The rebellion of the will against the terrible discipline of meditation has not been discussed; we may now devote a few words to it.

There is no limit to what theologians call “wickedness.” Only by experience can the student discover the ingenuity of the mind in trying to escape from control. He is perfectly safe so long as he sticks to meditation, doing no more and no less than that.

# CHAPTER I
## THE TEMPLE

The Temple represents the external Universe. ## CHAPTER II

### THE CIRCLE

The Circle announces the nature of the Great Work <term>Great Work: The ultimate spiritual goal in occultism, often described as the union of the individual soul with the divine or the realization of one's true will.</term>.
Although the Magician has been limited in his choice of room, he is more or less able to choose which part of the room he will work in. He will consider convenience and possibility. His circle should not be too small, which would restrict his movements; it should not be so large that he has long distances to travel across it. Once the circle is made and consecrated <note>consecrated: formally dedicated to a sacred purpose through ritual</note>, the Magician must not leave it, or even lean outside of it, for fear that he might be destroyed by the hostile forces that exist outside <note>original: "without"</note>.

He chooses a circle rather than any other geometric figure for many reasons; for example <note>original: "e.g."</note>:

1. He confirms through this his identity with the infinite.
2. He confirms the equal balance of his work, since all points on the circumference <note>circumference: the outer boundary or edge of the circle</note> are the same distance from the center <note>original: "equidistant from the centre"</note>.
3. He confirms the boundaries implied by his devotion to the Great Work. He no longer wanders about aimlessly in the world.

The Scourge, the Dagger, and the Chain represent the three alchemical principles of Sulphur, Mercury, and Salt. These are not the literal substances that we now call by these names; instead, they represent "principles," whose operations modern chemists have found it more convenient to explain in other ways. ## THE CUP

As the Magic Wand is the Will, the Wisdom, and the Word of the Magician, so the Magic Cup is his Understanding.

This is the cup about which it was written: "Father, if it is Your Will, let this cup pass from Me!" And again: "Can you drink from the cup that I drink from?"

This Cup is full of bitterness, and of blood, and of intoxication.

The Understanding of the Magus is his link with the Invisible, on the passive side.

His Will errs actively by opposing itself to the Universal Will.

His Understanding errs passively when it receives influence from that which is not the ultimate truth.
