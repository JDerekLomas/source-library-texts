# A Medley of Problems
## From Books 9 and 10 of the Digest

->I.<-

**1.** If someone’s slaves commit a theft, an action for "noxal" theft <note>original: "noxalis furti actio"; a "noxal" action allowed a master to either pay the damages or surrender the slave to the victim to settle the debt</note> is granted against the master. However, this is done in such a way that the penalty is not pursued against each individual slave separately. *Arumaeus, Disputations on the Laws, 10, thesis 6.*

2. When a free man has been killed, his heirs can effectively sue under the Aquilian Law <note>original: "Lex Aquiliâ"; the Roman law governing compensation for damage to property and persons</note>. *Costalius on the law; Wisembach in his commentary on the Aquilian Law, number 7; see also Cujas, Observations 21, 20.*

3. If one person mortally wounds someone, but another actually hastens the death, the first person is held just as liable for the killing as the second, provided it is certain that the wounds inflicted by both were lethal. *Digest 9.2.11, Section: Celsus; Law 51; Arumaeus, Disputations on the Laws, 10, thesis 3.*

4. They say the reasoning of the Aquilian Law is quite strange because it imposes no penalty if an animal is killed at a time when it is worth the most; instead, it only applies when the value has depreciated. <note>The author is discussing the calculation of damages based on the highest value of the property within the preceding year or month.</note> *Law 21; Law 23; Hotman on the Institutes regarding the Aquilian Law.* Perhaps this was established so that cheaper livestock and slaves—whose low value might otherwise expose them to the malice of others—would be better protected.

5. A neighbor is held liable to another neighbor for restoring damages if a fire broke out from his house. However, the plaintiff must prove that the head of the household was at fault. *See Virgil Pingitzer, Jena Decisions, 24; Graveus, Practical Conclusions, 21; Andreas Gerhardi, thesis 6; Pacius; Hackelman, thesis 24; Raudensis, Decision 6.*

6. Under Civil Law, division of property among brothers is carried out either by the authority of a judge or by drawing lots. *Law 4, section: "but also tablets"; Law 5, 15, 23, 27 on the division of a family estate; Code of Justinian, various citations; Faber, book 3, title 25; Graveus, conclusion 116.* I do not believe this was changed by Canon Law <note>the law of the Church</note>. *See Gail, Observation 116, number 12; Hackelman, thesis 7.*

7. A division of an inheritance arranged by drawing lots by parties who are aware of the facts cannot be rescinded, even if there is an immoderate inequality between the shares—unless the shares were made so unequal through an error. *Fachinaeus, chapter 36; The Presiding Lord's treatise on Appeals, chapter 7, number 11.*

8. The costs of education <note>original: "Sumptus Studiorum"</note> are not included in the "collation" <note>original: "collationem"; the legal process of bringing gifts received during a parent's lifetime back into the estate to ensure a fair split among heirs</note>. *Digest 10.2.50 on the division of a family estate; Thoming, Decision 32; Giphanius on the Civil Law.*

9. I would think that a legal action for "dividing common property" <note>original: "communi dividundo"</note> is available to one joint-debtor <note>original: "correi"</note> against another joint-debtor. This is contrary to the views of Molinaeus and Antonius Faber. *Digest 10.3.14; compare Digest 16.3.17.*

10. **The trio of double actions** <note>original: "Trigam duplicium actionum"; this refers to three specific Roman legal actions—the division of a family estate, the division of common property, and the regulation of boundaries—which were "double" because both parties acted as both plaintiff and defendant</note> we attribute to mixed actions. *Wesenbeck & Cujas in the commentary on the titles for regulating boundaries, dividing a family estate, and dividing common property. The common Doctors <note>learned legal scholars</note> on the section "Certain" in the Institutes on actions, against Donellus on the section "All" and the section "Certain" in the Institutes on actions.*

### From Books 11 and 12 of the Digest <note>original: "ff.", the standard symbol for the Digest or Pandects, a massive compilation of Roman legal opinions</note>.

### I.

1. An **Interrogatory** action is so named not because the opponent is forced to respond to an interrogation, but because the action is granted based on the response of the person questioned, just as if they had been legally bound. *See Duarenus, 1st Annual Disputations, chapter 43.*
2. **Interrogations** which used to benefit the questioner in establishing a case, although they were less frequent in the past and eventually fell out of use entirely, we nevertheless find that those which lead to evidence are still in active practice <note>original: "in viridi observantia"; literally "in green observance," meaning currently in force or freshly observed</note>. These can take place in any part of the trial, but not after the conclusion of the case. *Hackelmann, Illustration 8, Proposition 19 <note>original: "θ. 19"; the Greek letter theta was often used to abbreviate 'thesis' or 'proposition'</note>.* However, the Judge may still question the litigants even after the case has been concluded. *Law 21, on interrogations made in court.*
3. The **funeral privilege** <note>a legal priority given to those who pay for a funeral to be reimbursed from the deceased's estate</note> granted for expenses ought also to be extended to expenses incurred due to illness. *Wesenbeck in the Commentary on religious matters and funeral expenses, at the end. Negusantius on pledges, part 5, section 2, number 28. Regarding the funeral action, see by all means Timaeus Faber, Annotations, book 1, chapter 3 and following.*
4. A **ward** <note>original: "pupillus"; a child under the age of puberty who is under the care of a guardian</note> who enters into a contract without the authority of their guardian <note>original: "tutor"</note> is not even naturally obligated <note>a "natural obligation" is one that is morally binding but usually cannot be enforced by a lawsuit</note>, unless it is proven that they were made wealthier by those obligations. *See Gaedd., on stipulations, 7, conclusion 12. Vultejus, Disputations 16. Arumaeus, Treatise on Default, chapter 6, number 8. Reiner Bachovius, 1st Miscellany, chapter 12.* In that case, they are civilly obligated; however, this civil obligation is still called a "natural obligation" in the context of the old law, in Law 5, in the introduction to the title on the authority and consent of guardians.
5. **In the same way**, a minor past the age of puberty <note>original: "Pubes Minor"; a person between the ages of 14 and 25</note> who has a curator is not civilly obligated without the curator's consent, except to the extent that they have been made wealthier. *Gaedd. on Law 21, on the significance of words. Robert, 1st Observations, chapter 12.* But just as an Oath binds them today, in the past a **Stipulation** <note>a formal verbal contract in Roman law</note> also bound minors. *Law "Puberes" 101, Law "Ult." section 2, on verbal obligations.*
6. **If coins of a specific type**, for example, one hundred Joachimsthalers <note>original: "Joachimici"; silver coins from Joachimsthal, the etymological ancestor of the "dollar"</note>, were given *as* Joachimsthalers—and not just a certain sum measured in Joachimsthalers—then they must be returned in the same type of currency. *Coler on Executive Processes, part 1, chapter 10, number 14. Andreas Gerhard, Disputations on the Institutes 12, question 6. See Antoine Favre in the treatise on various payments of monetary debts, and Jacob Alemannus in the "Palestra" <note>Arena</note> of Consultations; add also Mr. Bocer's treatise on coinage, throughout.*

7. Although in what they call the court of the soul <note>original: "foro quem animæ vocant"; this refers to the court of conscience or ecclesiastical jurisdiction</note>, there is no difference between simple speech and an Oath: nevertheless, in the court of litigation <note>original: "foro contentioso"; a secular civil court</note>, where one must deal even with those who are merely tolerable, this remedy is considered necessary and is lawfully employed. *Ægidius Hunnius on Matthew chapter 5, at the words: "again you have heard it said," etc. Lambert Daneau, 2nd book of Christian Ethics, chapter 9, from the beginning. Covarrubias in the chapter "Although," part 1, re-reading, section 6, number 1, on pacts, in the 6th book. Pierre Grégoire of Toulouse, in the Syntagma of Law, book 50, chapter 3.*

8. He who swears by God in a fit of passion <note>original: "calore"; implies a heat-of-the-moment outburst</note> answers to God alone; but he who does so deliberately also has the Magistrate as an avenger. *Menochius, 2nd book on Arbitrary Remedies, question 319. Duarenus, 2nd book of Disputations, 37. Robertus, 2nd book of Sentences, 20. Fachinæus, 1st book of Controversies, 14. Pierre Perrenon, Animadversions, book 2, chapter 8. Dissenting: Cujas, 2nd book of Observations, 19, and 8th book of Observations, 7. Add Herdesianus in the special treatise.*

9. An oath regarding the value of a suit <note>original: "in litem juratur"; an oath taken by a plaintiff to estimate the value of the disputed matter</note> is not taken in all "Good Faith" actions; since not all such actions concern a thing to be restored or produced. *Antoine Fabre, 6th book of Conjectures, 17. Arumæus, Disputations on the Laws, volume 11, title 12. Bronchorst, 2nd book of Contradictions, 32.*

10. Although a person may receive a debt owed to them; nevertheless, if the one who gives it does not owe it, the right to recover the payment <note>original: "repetitio"; the legal right to claim back money paid by mistake</note> is available. *Law 19, section 1, under the title on the recovery of undue payments. See law 44 under the same title. Cujas, 8th book of Observations, 9. Doneau, 14th book of Commentaries, 13. Charondas, 2nd book of Probabilities, 15.*

### Books 13 and 14 of the Digest <note>original: "ff.", the standard abbreviation for the Digest, a collection of juristic writings</note>

### I.

1. An **Action for stolen property** <note>original: "Condictio furtiva"; a claim used to recover the value of stolen goods</note> is granted for the full amount against an heir. *Antoine Fabre, 4th book of Conjectures, 1, against Cujas, 7th book of Observations, 37, and 13th book of Observations, 34. Philippus Matthæus on law 38, number 22, and following, on the Rules of Law.*

2. The **Action for grain** <note>original: "Condictio Triticaria"; an action to recover a specific quantity of grain or other non-monetary goods</note> (taking its name from its author; *Duarenus on the title of the Digest regarding the grain action, and in the same place Doneau. Dissenting: Cujas in the summaries and Hotman in the same place in his commentaries*) is a matter of strict law <note>original: "stricti Juris"; meaning the judge must follow the letter of the law exactly without considering equity or fairness</note>: *Robertus, 2nd book of Animadversions, 4 and 5. Doneau on law 3, number 13, on the grain action, against Cujas and Borcholt—who never fails to follow Cujas—chapter 7 on loans, number 95.* By this action, we seek things other than minted money. *Law 1, at the beginning, in the Digest on the grain action, and Cujas in the summary there.*

3. What ought to be given at a certain place is not only rightly sought at that place; but also elsewhere, without the danger of "claiming too much" <note>original: "plus petitionis"; a procedural error where a plaintiff loses their case for asking for more than is strictly owed</note>: *the entire title of the Digest on that which is owed at a certain place, and the entire title of the Code on where one who owes at a certain place should be sued, etc.* However, the debtor can be sued not in just any other place; rather, the jurisdiction of the court <note>original: "ratio fori"</note> must be considered. *Duarenus on the title regarding that which is owed at a certain place, chapter 4. Hackelmann, Illustrious Questions, 10, question 16.*

4. A person is seen to be making a gift if they pay while knowing the debt is not owed. *Law 33 on the Rules of Law. Law 9 in the Code on the recovery of undue payments. Costalius and Gothofredus on law 11 regarding promised money. Dissenting: Zasius on the section "established," number 12, and in the same place Jason, number 40, in the Institutes on actions.*

5. One who has received something as a loan for use <note>original: "utendum aliquid accepit"; the legal contract of *commodatum*</note> is not liable for damage caused by injury if it could not have been prevented by any care or diligence on his part. *See Digest 13.6.5.2; 13.6.19; Digest 19.2.41. Hotman, Observations, book 4, chapter 12.*

6. If you have given another person's property as a pledge, and afterward you began to be the owner of that property; an "equitable pledge action" <term>utilis actio pignoratitia: a legal remedy granted by a judge to enforce a right that doesn't strictly fit the letter of the law but fits its spirit</term> is granted to the creditor. The same is not to be said if I should become the heir—without my own will—to Titius, who had pledged my property. *Digest 13.7.41, on the pledge action; Digest 13.7.22, the law "If to Titius." See Vultejus, Disputations, 17; Robertus, Readings, book 2, chapter 5; Charondas, Verisimiles, book 1, chapter 20.*

7. In a pledge action, liability arises for both fraud and negligence, as in a Loan for Use; it also covers the duty of safekeeping; but "superior force" <note>original: "vis major"; acts of God or unavoidable disasters like floods or fire</note> does not incur liability. *Digest 13.7.13.1, on the pledge action. See Cujas, Observations, book 18, chapter 24.*

8. A son under paternal authority <note>original: "Filiusfam."; a son still legally subject to the power of his father, regardless of his age</note> cannot renounce the Macedonian Decree of the Senate <term>Senatusconsultum Macedonianum: a law named after a notorious usurer, Macedo, which prohibited lending money to sons still under their father's power to prevent them from wishing for their father's death to pay debts</term>. *Digest 14.6.13, on the Macedonian Decree. Covarruvias, in the chapter "Although," concerning pacts, in the 6th book, part 2, section 3, under number 6. Pietro Mozzi, on contracts, title on Loans, the section on how a loan fails, number 19. Fachinaeus, Controversies, book 2, chapter 26. Zasius dissents in his 2nd Response, 28.*

9. If a son under paternal authority has paid a debt while still in his father’s power, once the money has been spent by the creditor, the father has no legal action to recover it. *Digest 12.1.14, on things credited. See Digest 14.6.9.1, on the Macedonian Decree. Dr. Harpprecht, Disputation 2, thesis 4, letter b, part 2. Pacius, Antinomies, book 3, 65.*

### From Books 15 and 16 of the Digest

->I.<-

1. If there is nothing in the "allowance" <term>peculio: a fund or property managed by a son or slave that remained the legal property of the head of the household</term>, one who is about to sue regarding that allowance cannot be sent into the possession of the goods of the Father or Master. *Digest 15.1.50, at the beginning, on the allowance. See the law of Fulcinius, the section "if anyone by action," where it says "it can be." Digest 42.4, on the reasons for entering possession. Pacius, Antinomies, book 4, 63. Cujas, book 9 of Questions by Papinian.*

2. If something has been turned to the profit of the Father or Master <note>original: "in rem... versum"; referring to the "actio de in rem verso," an action to recover money used for the benefit of someone's estate</note>, even if it afterward ceases to be a profit, the action for "turning to profit" is still granted. *See Digest 15.3.3.7; 15.3.10.6; 15.3.17; 15.3.19, on the action for turning to profit. Cujas, Treatise 8 on Africanus, in the explanation of said law 17. Pacius, Antinomies, book 4, 68.*

3. I would think that a woman cannot renounce the Vellejan Decree of the Senate <term>Senatusconsultum Vellejanum: a Roman law that prohibited women from taking on the debts of others or acting as a guarantor, intended to protect them from financial exploitation</term>. *Arumaeus, Disputations on the principal laws, 12, thesis 7. Doneau, Commentaries, book 12, final chapter, near the end. Hackelman, Illustrious Disputations, 9, thesis 12. See the final law, second to last section, on the Vellejan Decree.*

4. If the mother of a ward <note>original: "Pupilli"; a child under guardianship</note> has promised indemnity to a Tutor, she can use the help of the Vellejan Decree. *Code 4.29.6.1 and following, on the Vellejan Decree. Pacius, Antinomies, book 4, 72. Cujas, Treatise 4 on Africanus, in the explanation of the law on tutors, Digest 16.1, on the Vellejan Decree.*

5. A woman who takes a loan from another person in order to pay it on behalf of her husband can protect herself by the Decree of the Senate against a creditor who knows the purpose of the loan. *Digest 16.1.28, the section on the farm, on the Vellejan Decree. See Code 4.29.13, on the same. Duaren, chapter 2, on the Vellejan Decree. Hotman, Observations, book 6, 2.*

6. We judge that an obligation is canceled by **set-off** <term>compensation: (compensatio) the process of balancing mutual debts between two parties so they cancel each other out</term> by the operation of the law itself. *Arumæus, Disputations on the Laws, 12, question 8; Cujas, Observations, book 8, chapter 16; Donellus, Commentaries, book 16, chapter 15; Bronchorst, Antinomies, book 2, chapter 41.*

7. It appears that a **loan for use** <note>original: "commodatum"; a contract where an item is lent for free and must be returned</note> does not reject set-off in the same way that a **deposit** <note>original: "depositum"; property left with someone for safekeeping</note> does. *Cujas, Observations, book 9, chapter 37, and on Paul's Sentences, title "On Deposits"; Hotman, Amicabilia, book 1, chapter 21; Bronchorst, Antinomies, book 2, chapter 42; see the final law in the Code on loans for use; Arumæus, Disputations on the Laws, 12, question 2.*

8. A **deposit** does not consist of immovable property <note>such as land or buildings</note>; this is held with the common opinion against *Gaeddæus on Law 110 regarding the "Signification of Words," number 3, and on the title "On Sequestration," number 8, etc.*

9. Possession of a deposited item does not pass to a **Sequestrator** <term>sequestrum: a neutral third party chosen to hold an item that is currently the subject of a legal dispute</term>. *Arumæus, Disputations on the Laws, 12, question 9; see Gaeddæus on Sequestration, number 121; Charondas, Verisimiles, book 1, chapter 6; Coras, Miscellany, book 3, chapter 2; Donellus, Commentaries, book 5, chapter 13.*

10. Regarding the specific case of Law "Lucius," 24, in the Digest on Deposits: we assert that it is a **Deposit**, not a **Loan** <term>mutuum: a loan of items that are consumed by use, like money or wine, where the borrower returns an equal amount of the same kind</term>. *Arumæus, said Disputations, question 10.*

## From Books 17 and 18 of the Digest

1. An **agent** <term>procurator: a person appointed to manage another's affairs or legal case</term> is liable for both fraud and every degree of negligence. *The law "From an Agent," 13; the law "On a Mandated Matter," 21; Code on Mandates; Donellus, Commentaries, book 13, chapter 11; Pacius, Antinomies, book 4, chapter 83.*

2. A **mandate** <note>original: "mandatum"; a contract to perform a service for another without pay</note> is dissolved by death: *Donellus, Commentaries, book 16, chapter 23;* unless something else was arranged in express words or by the nature of the business itself. *Digest on Mandates, law 12, final section, and the following law; add the final law of the Digest on payments; see Pacius, Antinomies, book 4, chapter 85; Bronchorst, Antinomies, book 2, chapter 46; Cujas, Observations, book 1, chapter 38, and book 2, chapter 31.*

3. Even partners in a single matter ought to be condemned only for what they are able to perform <note>this refers to the "benefit of competence," which protects a person from being sued for more than they actually own, preventing total financial ruin</note>. *Law 63 on partnerships; law 16 on matters already judged; Bronchorst, Antinomies, book 2, chapter 51; Pacius, Antinomies, book 4, chapter 100.*

4. If an item is **gilded**, the sale is valid even if I think it is solid gold; however, the sale is not valid if bronze is sold as gold <note>Roman law distinguished between a mistake in quality (gilded vs. solid gold) and a mistake in the very substance of the object (bronze vs. gold)</note>. *Digest on contracting a sale, law 14 and law 41, the section "A Table"; Cujas, Observations, book 2, chapter 4; Gaeddæus, on Stipulations, book 3, chapter 46; Pacius, Antinomies, book 5, chapter 4; Bronchorst, Antinomies, book 2, chapter 53; see Fachin, Controversies, book 2, chapter 1.*

5. **Obscurity in an agreement** ought to harm the seller (if he was the one who spoke it) rather than the buyer. *Digest on contracting a sale, law 21 and law 34 at the beginning; Pacius, Antinomies, book 2, chapter 7; Guillaume Forner, Select Works, book 2, chapter 18; Bronchorst, Antinomies, book 1, chapter 40.*

6. There is such a thing as the purchase of **annual revenues** <note>this refers to the legal validity of buying the rights to future yearly income or annuities</note>. *Chapters 1 and 2 on Buying and Selling in the "Extravagantes"; Gail, Observations, book 2, chapter 7; against Hotman, Observations, book 2, chapter 15; see Friedrich Mind, on Mandates, book 2, chapter 72, number 5; Fachin, Controversies, book 2, chapter 43.*

7. **Earnest money** <term>arrhae: a small deposit or token given to show that a contract is being entered into</term> is usually given both in the name of a sale to be contracted and for a sale already contracted; I believe there is a great difference between these two types of earnest money. *Wesenbeck, in the summary on contracting a sale, number 9; Bronchorst, Antinomies, book 2, chapter 54.* And from there...

5. As a rule, a debtor is not permitted to pay a creditor in small installments against the creditor’s will. *Hotoman, Illustrious Questions 20. Gæddæus on the law 'On the Meaning of Words.'* Except in cases where the total amount being sought is not <term>liquid: a debt that is clear, certain, and determined in value</term>. *See law 21 on credited things; and the final law on how a pledge or mortgage is dissolved.*

16. The variety of customs by which nations differ is wonderful. Even leisure itself is not uniform among them. The Barbarians used to sit during their leisure; the Romans would walk or lie down. *Justus Lipsius, <note>Justus Lipsius (1547–1606) was a famous Flemish humanist and Stoic philosopher.</note> Letters to the Belgians, century 1, letter 3.*

17. Josephus concludes that ancient peoples lacked laws in *Against Apion, book 2*, because the word <term>Nomos</term> <note>original Greek: Νόμος; meaning "Law"</note> is found neither in the works of Orpheus, Musaeus, nor Homer. Plutarch agrees in his *Book on Homer*, as do many more recent writers. All of them—as John Selden <note>John Selden (1584–1654) was an English jurist and scholar of ancient law.</note> recently observed in *The English Janus, book 1, folio 16*—reason with too little accuracy. For we read the word <term>themistas</term> <note>original Greek: θέμιστας; meaning "divine commands" or "established customs"</note> very frequently in Homer and Hesiod; and in both we find the goddess <term>Eunomia</term> <note>original Greek: Ευνομία; meaning "Good Order" or "Governed by Good Laws"</note>, derived from the same root as <term>nomos</term>.

18. Pythagoras is not being absurd when he recognizes a most sweet harmony and concord in the motion of the heavens. But from that Pythagorean doctrine, it is easy to answer: why does Music drive away demons and melancholy? <note>Referring to the biblical account in 1 Samuel 18, where David's harp-playing soothes King Saul.</note> Why does it depress or uplift the soul? Even the oratorical <term>fistula</term> <note>a small pitch-pipe used by the Roman orator Gaius Gracchus to regulate the tone of his voice</note> of Gracchus teaches us this. *Aulus Gellius, book 1, chapter 11.* Indeed, it was once believed that Music could heal certain diseases. There is such a profound affinity between the bodies and minds of men, and therefore also between the vices or remedies of souls and bodies, as Aulus Gellius <note>The transcription uses "Agellius," a common Renaissance spelling for the Roman author of *Attic Nights*.</note> writes. *Book 4, chapter 13.*

19. Once, for a span of many years, "Barbarism" <note>In this context, "Barbarism" refers to the perceived lack of classical learning and refined Latin during the Middle Ages.</note> had haughtily triumphed over good literature; now an exotic, deceptive, and courtly "new prudence" exults. It is not that this new prudence knows or teaches anything better; rather, to excuse its own shameful ignorance, it scorns literature. From this, young men can learn nothing except an ignorance of letters. I would not disapprove of an "Elias" <note>likely a reference to the "Elijah" who was expected to come and restore learning or reform the world, or perhaps a specific scholar of the era</note> who would explode and purge the vanity of literature; but since I see the world is always occupied with extremes, I find my rest in the center.
