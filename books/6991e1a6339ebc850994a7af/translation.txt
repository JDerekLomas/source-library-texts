# TO THE ILLUSTRIOUS MARCUS ANTONIUS AMULIUS
## VENETIAN PATRICIAN, FROM DONATUS
### OF VERONA.

*Since I wished at this time, for most honorable reasons, to publish those trifles—for what else should I call them?—which you so earnestly requested with your characteristic kindness* <note>original: *humanitas*; a Renaissance ideal encompassing both refinement of character and a classical education.</note> *last summer when you had traveled to us in the Vicentine mountains* <note>The hills around Vicenza in Northern Italy, where scholars often retreated to escape the summer heat.</note>, *I decided to imitate those who are about to make a journey through unknown regions. If such people are endowed with even moderate prudence, they do not dare to do this without a reliable guide. And so, since I was about to release into the hands of men these writings of ours which you see—Marcus Antonius, the ornament of the Venetian nobility—whatever their quality may be, I wanted to send them forth without fear of danger, so that they might arrive without any error at their destination. Therefore, I wished to go first to you, who are most skilled in this journey, and I have commanded my writings to always follow in your footsteps.* 

*Indeed, I confess that I could have sent them ahead to many others whom they might follow for the same reason. However, I understood that three things especially are necessary in an excellent guide: a thorough knowledge of all places and routes, favor among those whom the traveler will approach, and affection toward the traveler himself. Among others, I saw that one or another of these qualities would be lacking; in you alone, I perceived them all. Furthermore, you are already so proficient in these studies of ours—especially at your young age—that he whose writings please you can easily disregard the contrary judgments of all others. This is no different than that famous poet who, while reciting his own poem, saw everyone else depart except for Plato; he rightly disregarded all those who had left as long as Plato remained* <note>This refers to an anecdote about the poet Antimachus of Colophon, who valued the intellectual approval of the philosopher Plato over the entire crowd. It illustrates the idea that the opinion of one expert outweighs the opinion of the masses.</note>. *Your reading of these writings of mine before they are sent out <term>rather than</term>* <note>original: <term>q̃;</term>, a common shorthand for *quam*.</note> *allowing them to be published, will be for them what a guide on a journey...*

# TO THE READER.

*Xenophon the Socratic’s dialogue on household management* <note>original: "de re familiari"; referring to the *Oeconomicus*, a treatise on estate management and domestic duties.</note>, *a work than which no other more exquisite exists among the Greeks, nor any more perfect in all its parts, Cicero had once made Latin.* That he performed this labor is shown both by the readings of ancient grammarians, who frequently use passages taken word-for-word from that work, and by Columella <note>Lucius Junius Moderatus Columella (4–70 AD), the most important Roman writer on agriculture.</note>—whom we all know wrote most accurately among us regarding agriculture—and especially by the testimony of Cicero himself concerning his own work. His words in the second book of *On Duties* <note>original: "de officijs"; Cicero’s famous philosophical treatise addressed to his son.</note> to his son Marcus are those which seem most suitable to this place and purpose. "Family property," he says, "should be acquired by those means from which dishonor is absent; it should be preserved by diligence and thrift; and it should also be increased by those same means. Xenophon the Socratic pursued these matters most suitably in that book which is titled *Oeconomicus* <note>From the Greek *oikos* (house) and *nomos* (law/management), the root of the modern word "economics."</note>; which we ourselves translated from Greek into Latin when we were about the age you are now." Therefore, it should no longer be a matter of doubt to anyone that Cicero, as we have said, performed that labor. 

But since this most excellent work of Cicero has perished, along with many other distinguished works by the same man, several friends have requested of me for some years now that I should render it into Latin once again. Indeed, I used to sharply refuse them, because I saw that the Greek work was supported by such a style of speaking—having stood now for so many centuries—that it seemed no Latin oration would ever respond to such excellence. I also had Saint Jerome <note>original: "Hieronymum"; the 4th-century scholar and Bible translator.</note> as a witness to this difficulty, who affirmed that not even Cicero himself had pleased everyone in that work. For in the preface which he uses in the *Chronicle of Eusebius* <note>A universal history translated and expanded by Jerome.</note>, he says: "Even our Tully <note>original: "Tullius"; Marcus Tullius Cicero.</note> translated entire books of Plato word-for-word; and when he had already published Aratus <note>A Greek poet whose work on astronomy, the *Phaenomena*, was translated by Cicero.</note> in Roman hexameter verses, in Xenophon’s *Oeconomicus*...

It would therefore be enough, they said, if for the sake of teaching alone, I were to encompass the matter itself plainly and clearly in pure Latin words. That ornament, that sweetness of Xenophon, those "little flowers" <note>original: "floſculos"; a common rhetorical term for stylistic embellishments or "flowers of rhetoric."</note> of Cicero—they said they would not miss them if I did not provide them, provided that they achieved this one thing: that they might easily understand what was written. 

I yielded, therefore. For what else could I do when such men were commanding? I rendered the work into Latin in whatever way I could, yet certainly with such faithfulness that I could not have shown more. In this, I am so far from thinking I have omitted anything found in the Greek—at least from those copies I was able to obtain—that I instead thought some words ought to be added in certain places where they were not in the originals. This is a practice which both Jerome, in that book he wrote *On the Best Kind of Translating* <note>original: "de optimo genere interpretandi"</note>, and Cicero, in the third book of *On Ends* <note>original: "de finibus"; a philosophical work on the definitions of good and evil.</note>, teach should be done to bring light to a translation without any harm to the author. Finally, I cared for nothing more than that Xenophon should speak in Latin and plainly: that is, in such a way that he could be understood even by a drowsy reader.

# XENOPHON’S MEMOIRS
# OF THE DISCOURSES OF SOCRATES
## BOOK FIVE, WHICH IS ENTITLED THE OECONOMI-
## CVS, THAT IS,
# ON ESTATE MANAGEMENT.

**I** heard the same man <note>referring to Socrates</note> discussing estate management <note>original: "re familiari"</note> in this manner. Come now, he said, tell me, O Critobulus <note>a student of Socrates known for his wealth</note>, what do you think of estate management? Is it the name of a certain branch of knowledge? Just as we see there is a certain art of healing, of casting bronze <note>original: "fingendi æris"</note>, and of building. Indeed, it seems so to me, said Critobulus. Can we then, just as we are able to say of those arts what duty and office each one has, assign some duty of its own to estate management? Certainly, said Critobulus, I think it is the duty of a good head of the household <note term="pater familias">The "pater familias" was the absolute head of a household in Roman law, responsible for its economic and moral health.</note> to manage his own estate well. Then Socrates said, could he not also manage another’s estate just as well as his own, if he wished, provided someone entrusted the care of it to him? For a man skilled in building would perform his duty for another no differently than for himself. What then of the estate manager? Do you think he would do the same? I certainly do, O Socrates. Then he said, could anything then prevent a man who has learned this art—even if he has no money of his own—from hiring out his services for pay <note>original: "mercede"</note> to manage someone else’s property, just as another might do for building, provided he has attained skill in building? Then Critobulus said, Truly, he would be worthy of a great reward who, having undertaken such care, performs the necessary duties as he ought, and increases the estate itself by acquiring wealth. But then, what should we consider this "estate" <note>original: "res familiaris"</note> to be? Is it the same thing as a house? Or is it also whatever lies outside...

...whatever home someone possesses, all things are to be considered part of the estate <note>original: "re familiari"</note>. And Critobulus said, it certainly seems to me that even if things are not in the same city, whatever a person possesses is nevertheless part of his entire estate. Surely some people even possess enemies? They certainly do, and quite many of them, in fact. Shall we then say that their enemies are also among their possessions? At this Critobulus said, indeed, that would be truly ridiculous if a person, while increasing the number of his enemies, were also to receive a reward for such a "service." <note>Socrates is using irony here: if wealth is defined as everything one "possesses," and one "possesses" enemies, then increasing one's enemies would technically be increasing one's wealth—an absurdity.</note> Certainly so, since we have already agreed that a person's estate is the same thing as his possessions. 

Then Critobulus said, I would certainly call a "possession" <note>original: "possessionem"</note> whatever good thing someone may possess; but if there is something bad in it, I would not call it so. You seem to me, Socrates replied, to call "possessions" only those things that are useful to each person. Exactly so, he said; for if there are things that cause harm, I would rather call them by the name of "loss" <note>original: "detrimenti"</note> than "wealth" <note term="pecuniae">The Latin "pecunia" usually means money, but here it translates the Greek "chremata," meaning anything useful or valuable that one owns.</note>. 

Therefore, even if someone buys a horse but does not know how to use it, and instead falls headlong from it and suffers misfortune, is the horse still considered "wealth" for him? Not at all, if indeed wealth is something good. Consequently, would even land not be wealth for a man if he works and tills it in such a way that he suffers a loss from the cultivation? Indeed, not even land should hold the name of "wealth" if, while the farmer must be fed, the cultivation reduces him to poverty and hunger. 

Should the same logic then apply to livestock? For if someone suffers a loss because he does not know how to use livestock, then the livestock would not be considered "wealth" for him either. Not in my judgment, at any rate. You, therefore—as far as I hear—call whatever is beneficial by the name of "wealth," but whatever is harmful, you do not. 

Exactly so. It is possible, then, for one and the same thing to be "wealth" to the man who knows how to use it properly, but to the man who does not know, it is not wealth. I hold that very same opinion: which indeed also happens in the case of flutes <note>original: "tibijs"; these were double-reed instruments like an oboe, central to Greek social life.</note>. For if someone knows how to play the flutes skillfully, then they may hold the name of "wealth"; but if someone does not know how, they are no more wealth than stones, which are of no use—unless, perhaps, he sells them. This very point is again agreeable to us: to those who sell them, flutes provide the use of wealth...

They conclude that an object, such as a horse or a flute, is only "wealth" if it is useful; otherwise, it is a liability or a loss.</summary>
