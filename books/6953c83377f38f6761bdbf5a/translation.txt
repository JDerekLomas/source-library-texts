# Introduction

THE BOOK you are about to read, a manual of Yoga taught by the sage Gheranda to his student Chanda, is the most comprehensive of all the foundational texts of **Hatha Yoga** <note>original: *Haṭha Yoga*; a system of physical techniques designed to prepare the body for spiritual awakening</note>. At the beginning of the book, Chanda asks Gheranda to tell him about the Yoga of the body, which is the cause of knowledge of the **Ultimate Reality** <note>original: *Tattva-jñāna*; the realization of the essential nature of existence</note>. Gheranda agrees, and the book is thus called the *Collection of Gheranda* <note>original: *Gheranda Saṃhitā*</note>.

It sets itself apart from other books on **Hatha Yoga** in two notable ways. Firstly, it calls its Yoga <term>ghata Yoga</term> <note>original: *ghaṭa-yoga*</note> or <term>Yoga based in the vessel</term> <note>original: *ghaṭastha-yoga*</note> and not Hatha Yoga. The usual meaning of <term>ghata</term> is “pot” or “vessel,” but here it refers to the body—or rather, the whole person—since the techniques taught by Gheranda work on both the body and the mind. 

Secondly, it is unique in teaching a sevenfold path to perfection of the person. A few **Hatha Yoga** texts replicate the classical description of Yoga provided by the sage **Patanjali** as <term>eight-limbed</term> <note>original: *aṣṭāṅga*; the traditional eight stages of yoga practice including ethics, posture, and meditation</note>, but there are numerous other classifications. For example, the *Light on Hatha Yoga* <note>original: *Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā*</note> consists of four chapters corresponding to the four stages of its Yoga, while the *Collection of Goraksha* <note>original: *Gorakṣa Saṃhitā*</note>, echoing several earlier **Tantric** <note>referring to a diverse range of esoteric traditions that utilize the body and ritual to achieve spiritual liberation</note> texts, describes its Yoga as six-limbed.

# Introduction

The seven chapters correspond to the seven means of perfecting the person. Each chapter teaches a group of techniques that, when mastered, will lead to one of the seven means listed in verse 1.9. The first chapter describes six types of cleansing techniques by which **purification** <note>original: *śodhana*</note>, the first means to perfecting the person, can be achieved. The second chapter describes thirty-two **postures** <note>original: *āsanas*</note> by which **strength** <note>original: *dṛḍhatā*</note>, the second means, is attained. In the third chapter Gheranda teaches twenty-five **seals** <note>original: *mudrās*; gestures or locks used to direct energy</note>, which lead to **steadiness** <note>original: *sthiratā*</note>, the third means. The fourth chapter describes five techniques for **withdrawal of the senses** <note>original: *pratyāhāra*</note>, which brings about **calmness** <note>original: *dhairyatā*</note>, the fourth means. The fifth chapter starts with instructions on where the yogi should live, what he or she should eat, and at what time of year yogic practice should be started. It then lists ten kinds of **breath control** <note>original: *prāṇāyāma*</note>, the practice of which leads to **lightness** <note>original: *lāghavam*</note>, the fifth means. The sixth chapter describes three types of **meditation** <note>original: *dhyāna*</note>, using which the yogi can achieve **realization of the self** <note>original: *pratyakṣam*; direct perception of the inner self</note>, the sixth means. Finally, in the seventh chapter, Gheranda teaches six types of **deep absorption** <note>original: *samādhi*</note>, which lead to **abstraction** <note>original: *nirliptatā*; a state of being unattached or untainted by the world</note>, the ultimate means of perfecting the person.¹

Like the other root texts of **Hatha Yoga** <note>the yoga of physical discipline</note>, the *Collection of Gheranda* <note>original: *Gheranda Saṃhitā*</note> does not concern itself with **ethical restraints** <note>original: *yama*</note> and **personal observances** <note>original: *niyama*</note>, the restraints and observances that make up the first two limbs of classical Yoga. The chapters on **physical postures** <note>original: *āsanas*</note> and **seals** <note>original: *mudrās*; gestures or locks used to direct internal energy</note> are similarly unparalleled in the number of practices taught. The difference between physical postures and seals is not made clear by Gheranda—several of the seals seem to be no more than physical postures. We are told in the first chapter that physical postures lead to strength and seals to steadiness. In other texts, however, the purpose of seals is said to be the awakening of the **serpent power** <note>original: *Kuṇḍalinī*; the dormant spiritual energy at the base of the spine</note>. In five of the twenty-five seals listed this aim is made explicit, but awakening of the **Goddess** <note>another name for the Kundalini energy</note> is also given as one of the fruits of **breath control** <note>original: *prāṇāyāma*</note> in verse 5.57.

A further unique aspect of this book lies in its positioning of the chapter on **sensory withdrawal** <note>original: *pratyāhāra*</note> before that on breath control. In the classical system, the last six limbs are successively more subtle, moving from the physical realm to the mental. Breath control is, of course, a more physical practice than sensory withdrawal, but here the **bee breath** <note>original: *bhramarī prāṇāyāma*; a practice involving a humming sound</note> is said to lead to **deep absorption** <note>original: *samādhi*</note>; indeed, it is one of the six varieties of the **royal path** <note>original: *Rāja Yoga*</note> or deep absorption given in the final chapter. This may account for the position of the chapter on breath control. Most of the rest of the chapter is similar to other texts, apart from the teaching of the **unuttered sacred chant** <note>original: *Ajapa Gāyatrī*</note>, the prayer constantly but involuntarily repeated by all living beings.
