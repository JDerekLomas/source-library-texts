**These papers** came directed to me, I suppose, for the purpose of being made **public**. Having sent them to the **press**, which is the only means by which the **entirety** <note>original: "University"; here meaning the universal body or whole of mankind.</note> of **mankind** is to be informed, I am released from that **trust**.

The **author** is pleased to conceal his identity. To clarify this further, consider that money is a product manufactured from <term>bullion</term> <note>original: "Bullion"; gold or silver in the form of bars or ingots.</note> at the Mint. If the raw materials are ready, and the workmen are available as well, it is absurd to say that the product is lacking.

For instance: do you have grain and need flour? Take the grain to the mill and grind it. You might say, "Yes, but I lack flour because others will not take their grain to the mill, and I have none of my own." If that is the case, then buy grain from them and take it to the mill yourself. This is exactly the situation with money. A very wealthy man owns much silver plate for the sake of prestige and display. A poorer man might think that if this plate were minted into coins, the public—and himself included—would be better off. But he is completely mistaken, unless you also force the wealthy man to spend his newly minted money.

For if he simply stores it away, the situation has certainly not improved. If he exchanges it for diamonds, pearls, and so on, the outcome is the same; the money has merely moved from one person to another. It is even possible that the money is sent to the *Indies* to pay for those jewels. If he then buys land, the money again just changes hands; for everyone except the brokers involved, the case remains the same. Money will always have an owner and never goes looking for a home like a beggar; instead, it must be purchased with something of actual value <note>original: "in solido"; a Latin legal term meaning "as a whole," used here to mean for a full and solid equivalent value.</note>.

# POSTSCRIPT.

If every man has <term>plate</term> <note>original: "Plate"; household items made of gold or silver, such as cups or dishes, which served as a form of personal savings.</note> in his house, the nation possesses a solid fund consisting of those metals which the whole world desires and would willingly take from us; and this exists in a much greater measure than would be possible if men were not allowed that liberty. For the poor tradesman, out of an ambition to have a piece of plate upon his cupboard, works harder to purchase it than he would do if that inclination were restricted, as I have said elsewhere.

There is required for carrying on the trade of the nation a determinate sum of <term>specific money</term> <note>original: "Specifick Money"; physical minted coins with a set face value.</note>, which varies and is sometimes more, sometimes less, as our circumstances require. Wartime calls for more money than a time of peace because everyone desires to keep some by them to use during emergencies; they do not think it prudent to rely upon money currently circulating in trade, as they do in times of peace when payments are more certain.

This ebbing and flowing of money supplies and accommodates itself without any aid from politicians. For when money grows scarce and begins to be hoarded, then the <term>Mint</term> <note>The government facility where bullion is coined into money.</note> immediately works until the need is filled up again. And on the other side, when peace brings out the hoards and money is plentiful, the Mint not only ceases, but the surplus of money will be presently melted down, either to supply the home trade or for transportation <note>original: "Tranſportation"; here meaning export to other countries.</note>.

Thus the "buckets" work alternately: when money is scarce, <term>bullion</term> <note>original: "Bullion"; uncoined gold or silver valued by weight.</note> is coined; when bullion is scarce, money is melted. I do not allow that both should be scarce at one and the same time; for that is a state of poverty and will not occur until we are exhausted, which is a subject beyond my current focus.

# POSTSCRIPT

Some people have imagined that if a law restricted the value of an ounce of silver to 5 shillings in all transactions, and if the <term>Tower</term> <note>The Tower of London, which housed the Royal Mint where coinage was produced.</note> coined that same ounce into 5 shillings 4 pence or 5 shillings 6 pence, then all the silver <term>plate</term> <note>Household items made of silver, such as dishes or candlesticks.</note> in England would soon be turned into coins. The answer to this, in short, is that the principle they build upon is impossible. How can any law hinder me from giving another man whatever I please for his goods? The law may be evaded a thousand ways. For instance: I must not give, nor he receive, more than 5 shillings per ounce for silver; however, I may pay him 5 shillings and then present him with 4 pence or 6 pence more as a gift. I may give him goods in barter at a higher profit; and so on, through other contrivances, without end <note>original: "ad Infinitum."</note>.

But suppose it actually took effect, and by that means all the silver in England were coined into money; what then? Would anyone spend more on clothes, <term>equipages</term> <note>A carriage with its horses and attending servants; a sign of status.</note>, housekeeping, and so on than is currently done? I believe not. In fact, the contrary would likely happen: for the <term>gentry</term> <note>The social class just below the nobility.</note> and the common people, being denied the pleasure of displaying silver plate in their houses, would in all probability be discouraged in all other expenses. Therefore, if this could be done—though I affirm it cannot—instead of procuring the desired effect, it would bring about all the harms of a <term>sumptuary law</term> <note>A law intended to restrain luxury or extravagance, particularly in food and clothing.</note>.

Whenever money is made lighter, or mixed with a <term>baser alloy</term> <note>original: "baser in allay"; adding more non-precious metal to the coin, effectively devaluing it.</note> (which is the same thing), the result is that the price of <term>bullion</term> <note>Uncoined gold or silver, valued by weight.</note> immediately rises to match it. So in reality, you change the name of the currency, but not the value of the thing itself. Whatever the difference is, the tenant and the debtor benefit from it; for rent and debts will be paid back with less value than was originally owed, because the intrinsic value of the coins is lower than it was before.
