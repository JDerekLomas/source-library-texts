<note>original: 火禽 (Huǒqín). A general term for avian-based incendiary tactics, where birds were used to fly over walls and ignite enemy supplies.</note>

<note>original: 雀杏 (Quèxìng). A specific incendiary technique where small combustible pellets—the size and shape of apricot kernels—were tied to the legs of sparrows. The birds would instinctively return to their nests in thatched roofs or granaries, setting the enemy's camp ablaze.</note>

## Wooden Man and Living Horse
<note>original: 木人活馬 (Mù rén huó mǎ). These terms refer to early mechanical decoys or "automata" used for psychological warfare and deception. A "wooden man" could be a soldier-shaped puppet used to draw fire or simulate a larger force, while a "living horse" refers to complex wooden frames or mechanical transport systems designed to move swiftly across the battlefield, sometimes appearing to move under their own power to confuse the enemy.</note>

## Fire Oxen
<note>original: 火牛 (Huǒ niú). This is a reference to a classic Chinese military tactic famously used by General Tian Dan during the Warring States period. It involved tying reeds or brushwood to the tails of oxen, soaking them in oil, and igniting them while driving the panicked herd toward the enemy lines. By the time of this manuscript, the tactic had been refined to include mounting actual incendiary devices on the animals' backs.</note>

## Thunder-Crash Cannon for Charging Fire Oxen
<note>original: 衝陣火牛轟雷砲 (Chōng zhèn huǒ niú hōng léi pào). A specialized firearm designed to be used in conjunction with the "Fire Ox" charge. These small cannons were likely mounted on the animals or used by accompanying troops to provide a deafening blast—the "thunder-crash"—intended to shatter the morale of enemy formations and panic their horses, while the burning oxen physically broke through the ranks.</note>

# Fierce Fire Oil Cabinet

**Fierce Fire Oil Cabinet**, **Treatise on Armament Technology**, **Flamethrower**
<note>The "Fierce Fire Oil Cabinet" (Měng Huǒ Yóu Guì) is one of the most technologically advanced weapons described in the *Wubei Zhi*. It functioned as a continuous-stream flamethrower. "Fierce Fire Oil" refers to naphtha or refined petroleum, which was prized in medieval warfare because it was difficult to extinguish and could even float and burn on water. The "cabinet" or "tank" served as the reservoir for this fuel.</note>

<note>Based on historical records and the accompanying illustrations, this device utilized a double-action piston pump—a significant mechanical achievement—allowing it to spray a constant stream of fire rather than intermittent bursts. It was primarily used in naval battles to incinerate enemy sails and in siege defense to clear attackers from walls.</note>

### Construction and Operation

The construction of these jars requires them to be cast from molten bronze. Each jar measures five feet in length with a diameter of one foot. Nine of these jars are bound together in a single cluster with iron hoops and mounted securely upon a wooden frame. 

To load the weapon, large stone projectiles weighing twenty *jin* <note>A traditional unit of weight; twenty *jin* is approximately 12 kilograms or 26 pounds.</note> are placed into the front of the barrels. Behind these, one must insert a <term>movable sabot <note>original: 活馬 (huó mǎ). Literally "living horse," this was a wooden wad or disk placed between the gunpowder and the projectile to create a better seal, ensuring the gases from the explosion pushed the shot out with maximum force.</note></term>, followed by the propellant powder.

If the enemy forces are overwhelming and our own soldiers are unable to withstand their advance, employ this weapon to attack them. A single discharge can reach a distance of over ten *li* <note>A traditional unit of distance; ten *li* is roughly 5 kilometers or 3 miles.</note>. # Design of the Linked War Chariot

<note>The term "Linked War Chariot" (聯絡戰車, liánluò zhànchē) refers to a tactical system where multiple chariots were physically or strategically connected to form a "wagon fort." This allowed a mobile army to quickly establish a fortified perimeter against cavalry charges.</note>

->**Tactical Formation and Interconnectivity**<-

The diagram illustrates the **Linked War Chariot** <note>original: "聯絡戰車" (liánluò zhànchē)</note> in a staggered, diagonal deployment. Unlike standalone carts, these units were designed to operate in unison, creating a continuous defensive line that bristled with both melee and long-range weaponry. 

By "linking" the chariots, the military engineers of the Ming Dynasty addressed the primary weakness of mobile artillery: vulnerability during reloading. While the front of the chariot is protected by shields and spears, the gaps between the units are covered by the overlapping fire of the **Hundred Tiger** and **Long Snake** rocket cases described previously. 

<margin>The **Shield and Spear Wall** <note>In the diagram, the front of each chariot is equipped with protective screens and protruding spears to deter enemy infantry and horses from reaching the gunners.</note></margin>

Each chariot in this formation functions as a self-contained combat cell, yet the "linked" nature—often achieved through chains or interlocking beams—ensures that if one unit is pressured, the neighboring units can provide flanking fire. This system represents a peak in pre-modern combined arms warfare, integrating:
1.  **Mobility:** High-clearance wheels for traversing varying terrain.
2.  **Firepower:** Modular racks for massed rocket volleys.
3.  **Defense:** Interlocking formations that mimic the stability of a permanent city wall.

The banners and standards shown atop each chariot served a dual purpose: they were used for signaling commands across the noise of the battlefield and acted as psychological markers to maintain the discipline of the formation during the chaos of an engagement.
