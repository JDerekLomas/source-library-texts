# Sugarcane
<term>甘蔗 *ganzhe*. Sugarcane (*Saccharum officinarum*). A tall perennial grass used for sugar production. While native to tropical regions of Southeast Asia and South Asia, it was established in southern China by the Han dynasty.</term>

The *Shuowen* <note>original: 說文 *Shuowen Jiezi*. The first Chinese dictionary to analyze the structure of characters, compiled by Xu Shen during the Han Dynasty.</note> says: It is called *zhuzhe*. According to various commentaries and records, it is also known as *ganzhe* <note>written as 竿蔗 "pole cane" or 干蔗 "dry cane"</note>, *handan* <note>邯鄲, a name shared with a famous northern city, though here used as a phonetic variation for the plant</note>, *ganzhe* <note>甘蔗 "sweet cane," the modern standard name</note>, or *duzhe* <note>都蔗 "capital cane"</note>. These names vary by location.

In Yudu County <note>Located in modern-day Jiangxi province.</note>, the soil is rich and fertile, making it particularly suitable for sugarcane. Its flavor and color are superior to those found in any other county. A single segment of the stalk is several inches long. The local commandery sends this cane as a tribute to the imperial court.

The *Record of Foreign Curiosities* <note>original: 異物志 *Yiwu Zhi*. A work describing the exotic plants, animals, and customs of the southern regions (modern Guangdong, Vietnam, and beyond) during the Han and Jin periods.</note> states: Sugarcane is found both near and far. However, the sugarcane produced in Jiaozhi <note>A region covering parts of modern-day northern Vietnam and Guangxi.</note> is exceptionally pure and high in quality. The thickness of the stalk is consistent from the root to the tip, and the flavor is perfectly uniform. It measures several inches in circumference and grows to over ten feet in length, looking much like a stalk of bamboo. When cut and eaten, it is sweet. If one squeezes the stalk to extract the juice, it is like malt syrup <note>original: 飴餳 *yitang*. A thick, sweet liquid usually made from fermented grains.</note>; this is called "sugar," and it is considered even more precious.

Furthermore, if the juice is boiled and then dried in the sun, it congeals into a substance like ice. When broken into pieces like gaming dice <note>original: 博 *bo*. Referring to the square pieces used in ancient Chinese board games like Liubo.</note>, it melts instantly upon entering the mouth. People of our time call this "stone honey" <note>original: 石蜜 *shimie*. An early term for rock sugar or granulated sugar, which was a luxury item imported or refined using southern techniques.</note>.

# Pondweed

The **Classic of Poetry** <note>original: *Shi*; referring to the *Shijing*, the oldest existing collection of Chinese poetry</note> says: "Where do we gather the **pondweed**?" The commentary states that this refers to **clustered pondweed** <note>original: *juzao*</note>.

On the eighteenth day of the fourth month in the summer of the <term>Jiazi year of the Shaoxing era<note>original: 紹興甲子; This corresponds to the year 1144 AD during the Southern Song Dynasty.</note></term>, Lord Zhang, the Governor of <term>Longshu<note>original: 龍舒; An ancient name for Shucheng County in modern-day Anhui Province.</note></term>, sent a special envoy to deliver a letter. He stated that during his recent days of leisure, he had completed the carving of printing blocks for the **Essential Techniques for the Peasantry** <note>original: 齊民要術; *Qi Min Yao Shu*</note>. Intending to broaden its circulation, he requested that I write a preface to place at the head of the volume.

I respectfully note that the **Essential Techniques for the Peasantry** was formerly circulated mostly in the <term>Eastern Provinces<note>original: 東州; *Dongzhou*, referring generally to the regions of Shandong and eastern Henan.</note></term>. When I was studying at the <term>Two Academies<note>original: 兩學; Likely referring to the two supreme imperial educational institutions, the Taixue (Imperial University) and the Wuxue (Military Academy), in the capital.</note></term>, the scholars and officials from the Eastern Provinces spoke of the methods of planting and animal husbandry found within the *Essential Techniques* as a celebrated topic of the time. I was delighted to hear of this and wished to seek out a <term>fine edition<note>original: 善本; *Shanben*, a high-quality, accurately transcribed or printed version of a text.</note></term> to examine for myself, but I was unable to obtain one.

Now, the Governor has obtained a copy from the <term>Hermit of the Neighboring Woods<note>original: 鄰林居士; *Linlin Jushi*</note></term>, Xiang Bogong. Bogong has been attentive to learning and inquiry since his youth; consequently, many of the famous scholars and officials of the age associated with him and enjoyed transmitting his books. This particular book is the version collated by the <term>Institute for the Veneration of Literature<note>original: 崇文院; *Chongwen Yuan*, the imperial library and editorial office of the Song Dynasty.</note></term> during the <term>Tiansheng era<note>original: 天聖; The reign period of Emperor Renzong of Song, 1023–1032 AD.</note></term>—it is not merely a common version, but one issued by the imperial court...

Important texts are often difficult to obtain, yet the Governor managed to acquire this one and had it printed at the prefectural headquarters. His desire was for everyone under heaven to understand the way of **attending to agriculture** and **valuing grain**. From this, the Governor’s noble intentions are clearly visible. 

I once read the chapter **"Against Luxurious Ease"** <note>original: 無逸; *Wu Yi*, a famous chapter from the *Book of Documents* where the Duke of Zhou warns the young King Cheng about the dangers of a pampered life.</note> in which the Duke of Zhou cautioned the King. It says: "If they do not understand the hardships of **sowing and reaping** <note>original: 稼穡; *jiase*, a classical term for the grueling cycle of agricultural labor</note>, they will descend into idle ease and coarse language. They will become boastful and arrogant; otherwise, they will even insult their own parents, saying, 'The people of old had no knowledge or understanding.'" 

Indeed, when people do not understand the hardships of farming, the resulting calamity can even lead them to disrespect their own parents without any sense of fear. Is the harm this does to moral education any small matter?

I have often remarked that throughout history, no officials are closer to the people than the <term>Prefects and Magistrates<note>original: 守令; *Shouling*, the local administrators responsible for the direct welfare and taxation of the common people.</note></term>. Thus, these local officials have always taken the **encouragement of agriculture** <note>original: 勸農; *quannong*, an official government policy of promoting farming techniques and land clearance.</note> as their primary duty. 

The upright officials of the Han dynasty, such as **Zhao Xinchen** and **Gong Sui** <note>Zhao Xinchen and Gong Sui were famous "model officials" (Xunli) of the Han era, celebrated for transforming impoverished or rebellious regions into prosperous farming communities through personal leadership.</note>, were of this kind. They even went so far as to convince the people to sell their knives to buy calves and sell their swords to buy oxen. <unclear/> Now, the Governor uses this book to record the methods of farming and sowing...
