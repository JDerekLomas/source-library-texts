## EDITOR'S NOTE

To be useful to the many admirers of Leonardo da Vinci, and to place at their disposal unpublished materials <note>original: "inédits" — meaning works not previously printed or made public</note> indispensable to their studies: such is the goal we propose to achieve by publishing these collections.

We will reproduce the unpublished sheets of Leonardo found at Windsor Castle in separate volumes—each forming a whole as complete as possible—in the order that seems most rational to us, while preserving the connection between pages whenever we judge it to be certain.

Since most of the texts and the admirable drawings accompanying them do not offer proof of a continuous sequence of work or ideas—the subjects of art, science, etc., being, moreover, very diverse and intermingled—we shall choose and group the primary subjects. We leave to specialists the task of determining their value and chronology, as well as identifying others.

It was necessary to adopt this classification: a definitive publication concerning the manuscripts of the great Italian master <note>Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519)</note> belonging to the Library of Windsor Castle is not feasible, as the notebooks have been disjointed and many sheets are lost.

Without, however, sharing the opinion of Mr. Marcel Lallemand <note>A 19th-century writer and critic</note> who, in the journal *Italy* (Rome, May 28, 1894), wrote: "that with a set of mirrors, Leonardo da Vinci's writing could be read easily and that, as for the old Italian <note>Leonardo famously wrote in "mirror script," from right to left, which is legible when held to a mirror</note> which he uses, the most novice of student archivists <term>archivists: professionals responsible for preserving and organizing historical records</term> of the Peninsula <note>Referring to the Italian Peninsula</note>...

# Page 152

<note>This folio contains Leonardo da Vinci's celebrated anatomical studies of the human fetus in the womb. Although Leonardo utilized a bovine uterus as a model for the placental structure (seen in the top-center drawing), his observations of fetal positioning were groundbreaking. The text is written in his characteristic mirror script.</note>

<margin>
In this fetus, the length of the feet is equal to the length from the chin to the nape of the neck, and from the nape to the top of the head.
</margin>

<margin>
Because the man with long feet has short legs, and the man with short feet has long legs.
</margin>

In this fetus, the nape of the neck is closer to the mother's nape than its face is; this occurs because, since the fetus has its head downward and its <term>tailbone<note>original: "osso del chulo"</note></term> upward, and the mother has her tailbone downward, it is necessary for the fetus's nape to be turned toward the mother's nape. This ensures that, upon being born, its face is turned toward the earth, because then the fetus has a better way to turn itself and move its head, and to help itself with its hands and feet to come out—which it could not do if the face were turned toward the mother's nape.

<margin>
If the nape of the fetus were turned toward the mother's <term>opening<note>original: "buso," referring here to the birth canal</note></term>, the fetus would suffocate, because in wanting to turn itself to exit, it would find itself in a position where it could not turn.
</margin>

<note>Studies of fetal posture showing the "fetal position" with knees drawn to the chin and arms crossed.</note>

<margin>
In cold weather, a man has small feet and thick legs.
</margin>

The fetus does not breathe, because the soul cannot be in two bodies; therefore, the fetus has neither a soul nor a life of its own, but is part of the mother's life. Thus, it does not feel or move itself, but the mother feels and moves; likewise, the mother's food nourishes the fetus, and the mother's thoughts and passions are within the fetus.

<note>Leonardo debates the independence of the fetus's soul and physiological functions from the mother, reflecting the medieval and early Renaissance view that the fetus was an extension of the mother's body until birth.</note>

I ask whether the reasoning regarding the <term>menses<note>original: "mēstrua"</note></term> is true—that it serves for the nourishment of the fetus—and whether the fetus takes in food through the mouth or not...

<margin>
Look how the feet of the fetus cross, and how the hands rest upon the knees, and how the head bows down between the knees.
</margin>

### On the Movement of the Fetus

The fetus moves within the womb not by its own will, but as a result of the mother's movements or the shifting of the fluids. It lies doubled upon itself, with its knees drawn up to its chin, occupying the smallest possible space.

# THE ANATOMY OF THE HEART AND VEINS

The heart is a principal muscle in its strength, and it is much more powerful than the other muscles. <note>Leonardo viewed the heart as a unique muscle, driven by heat and vital spirits, rather than just a mechanical pump in the modern sense.</note> The blood which returns when the heart opens again is not the same as that which closes the valves.

### On the Movement of the Blood
The heat is generated by the movement of the heart, and this is shown because the more rapidly the heart moves, the more the heat increases, as the pulse of those in a fever teaches us, moved by the heat of the heart. <note>Leonardo correctly identified the correlation between heart rate and body temperature, though he attributed the heat to the friction of blood movement.</note>

<column-break/>

<margin>The origin of the sea is the opposite of that of the blood, because the sea receives into itself all the rivers, which are caused solely by the aqueous vapors raised into the air; but the sea of the blood is the cause of all the veins.</margin>

### The Reason for the Voice
The voice is produced in the whistle of the trachea, which is composed of cartilaginous rings. These rings compress and dilate by means of the muscles that embrace them; and these, in a single order, pull themselves upward and downward by the muscle that comes from above and by that which comes from below.

<margin>
Because the anatomy is not perfect, it has not seemed right to me to give the final judgment until I see the anatomy of a living person. <note>Leonardo acknowledges the limitation of dissecting cadavers, where the lack of breath makes it impossible to observe the voice in action.</note> This is because commonly the dead <unclear>appear</unclear> <unclear>complete</unclear>, but they lack the wind that sends out the voice.
