<term>Bulrush Raft (蒲筏)</term> <note>original: "Pufa"</note> A simple raft made from bundles of woven reeds or bulrushes. These were common in marshy areas and among civilian populations for basic transport.

<term>Framed Raft (械筏)</term> <note>original: "Xiefa"</note> A raft constructed with a more complex internal frame or "mechanisms." In a military context, these were often used as stable platforms for moving heavy equipment or as part of temporary pontoon bridges.

## Oxtail Dance
<term>Oxtail Dance</term> <note>original: "旄舞" (Maowu)</note>
The Oxtail Dance is one of the "Six Small Dances" performed by youths in the ritual traditions of the Zhou Dynasty. As shown in the illustration, the dancer carries a ritual staff called a *Mao*. This implement consists of a pole topped with a decorative animal head—typically a dragon—from which hangs a plume of yak tail hair or dyed oxtail. This dance was historically performed during sacrifices to the spirits of the Earth and Grain, as well as during ceremonies honoring the spirits of the four directions, where the swaying tails symbolized the movement of clouds and the breath of the living world.

<column-break/>

## Ritual Battle-Axe
<term>Ritual Battle-Axe</term> <note>original: "戚" (Qi)</note>
The *Qi* is a ceremonial battle-axe used in martial ritual dances, most notably the "Great Martial Dance" <note>original: "大武" (Dawu)</note> which commemorated the military victories of King Wu of Zhou. Unlike a functional weapon of war, the ritual axe is highly decorated, often featuring a broad, curved blade and an ornate socket. In Confucian ritual theory, the axe represents the "martial" (wu) power of the sovereign to eliminate disorder and protect the state. It is traditionally paired with a shield (*Gan*); together, the "Shield and Axe" dance serves as a powerful symbol of strength and the defense of civilization.

# Human Dance
<term>Human Dance</term> <note>original: "人舞" (Rén Wǔ)</note>

<vocab>Human, Dance</vocab>

The **Human Dance** is a fundamental ritual choreography that dates back to the Zhou Dynasty. Unlike the martial dances that preceded it—which utilized shields, axes, and oxtails—the Human Dance is characterized by being performed with "empty hands." In this tradition, the dancer does not carry an external implement; instead, the ritual significance is conveyed through the rhythmic movement of the arms, the precise positioning of the fingers, and the manipulation of long, flowing sleeves.

As depicted in the woodcut illustration, the performer wears a traditional scholar’s cap and voluminous robes. This dance was historically taught by the Junior Master to the sons of the nobility, serving as an essential part of their education in ritual poise and self-discipline. It represents the "Civil" (Wen) aspect of ancient Chinese performance, emphasizing that true harmony is achieved through internal cultivation and graceful social conduct rather than through the display of weapons.

# The Ancient Flute (Yuè)
<term>Yuè</term> <note>original: "籥" (Yuè)</note>

The **Ancient Flute** is a vertical, end-blown wind instrument traditionally crafted from bamboo. Unlike modern flutes, the ritual *Yuè* is characterized by its simplicity, typically featuring only three finger holes. In the context of ancient Chinese ritual performance, it represents the "civil" or "refined" (Wen) aspect of music. 

The instrument is not merely a musical device but a symbolic one; when held during the "Yuè Dance," it signifies the harmony between the human spirit and the natural world. As seen in the illustration, the flute is slender and straight, designed to be held vertically. It was historically played during seasonal sacrifices and state ceremonies to invoke a sense of order and grace.

<note>Historical texts note that the *Yuè* was used specifically to perform the music of the Xia Dynasty, symbolizing the antiquity and continuity of Chinese civilization.</note>

# The Pheasant-Feather Staff (Zhái)
<term>Zhái</term> <note>original: "翟" (Zhái)</note>

The **Pheasant-Feather Staff** is a ceremonial dance prop composed of a wooden handle adorned with the long, striking tail feathers of a Reeves's Pheasant. In ancient choreography, particularly the civil dances performed at the Confucian temple or the imperial court, the *Zhái* is often paired with the *Yuè* flute. 

The dancer typically holds the *Yuè* in the left hand and the *Zhái* in the right. The feathers are not merely decorative; they serve to extend the dancer's movements, creating fluid, sweeping lines that emphasize the elegance of the ritual. The head of the staff, as depicted in the woodcut, is often carved into the shape of a bird's head, reinforcing the connection to the "vermilion bird" and the celestial rhythms.

# The Great Standard
<term>Dào</term> <note>original: "纛" (Dào)</note>

The **Great Standard** is a massive ceremonial and military banner that serves as a primary symbol of supreme authority and imperial presence. Historically, the *Dào* was distinguished by the use of "wild ox hair" <note>likely referring to yak tails</note> or dyed hemp, which was fashioned into large, tiered tassels or a thick canopy at the top of a stout pole.

In the context of the Ming Dynasty military, these standards were placed at the entrance of a commander's tent or carried at the vanguard of a grand procession. They were more than mere markers; they were considered sacred objects. Before a campaign, ritual sacrifices were often offered to the *Dào* to ensure divine protection and victory. As shown in the illustration, the standard features a tiered, umbrella-like structure, designed to be visible from a great distance to signify the location of the commanding officer.

# The Feathered Banner
<term>Jīng</term> <note>original: "旌" (Jīng)</note>

The **Feathered Banner** is a specialized signaling flag used to convey imperial orders or to distinguish individuals of exceptional merit. Its most defining characteristic, seen in the accompanying diagram, is the ornamental attachment at the top of the staff—typically consisting of colorful pheasant feathers or yak tails arranged to flow in the wind.

According to ancient records of rites, the *Jīng* was used by the Emperor’s messengers when traveling on official business or to summon high-ranking officials to court. In the hierarchy of ancient flags, the *Jīng* specifically denoted the "summons." The presence of this banner commanded immediate attention and obedience, acting as a physical manifestation of a royal decree in transit.

## The Command Banner
<term>Huī</term> <note>original: "麾" (Huī)</note>

The **Command Banner** serves as the visual voice of a military leader on the battlefield. Under the established **Ceremonial Regulations** <note>original: 儀制 (Yízhì)</note>, this specific type of flag is used to direct the movements of the various divisions of an army.

In ancient tactical manuals, to "point the Huī" was to signal a specific maneuver; the visibility of the banner ensured that even amidst the noise and dust of combat, the commander's will remained clear to his soldiers.

## The Imperial Tally-Staff
<term>Jié</term> <note>original: "節" (Jié)</note>

The **Imperial Tally-Staff** is one of the most ancient and sacred symbols of delegated sovereign power in the Chinese tradition. It is not merely a decorative object but a legal instrument of the state.

# Music and Dance
<note>original: 樂舞 (Yuèwǔ)</note>

The regulated number of dancers for the **Row Dances** <note>original: 佾 (yì), a row of eight dancers</note> is determined by rank: the **Son of Heaven** <note>the Emperor</note> is entitled to eight rows <note>totaling 64 dancers</note>; the Feudal Lords are entitled to six rows <note>36 dancers</note>; High Officials use four rows <note>16 dancers</note>; and Scholars/Minor Officials use two rows <note>4 dancers</note>. 

In the **Eight-Row Dance**, there are sixty-four performers for the **Civil Dance** <note>original: 文舞 (Wénwǔ)</note> and sixty-four for the **Martial Dance** <note>original: 武舞 (Wǔwǔ)</note>. For the Civil Dance, the performers hold **feather-topped staves** <term>Dào</term> <note>original: 翿</note>, **bamboo flutes** <term>Yuè</term> <note>original: 籥</note>, and **pheasant feathers** <term>Dí</term> <note>original: 翟</note>.

The instruments used to regulate the rhythm of the music include the **Xiang** and **Ying** drums, the **Zhu** <note>a wooden box struck from the inside to start the music</note>, and the **Yu** <note>a wooden tiger-shaped instrument scraped to signal the end of the music</note>. There are also metal instruments such as the **Bronze Hand-Bell** <note>original: 金饒 (jīn náo)</note>, the **Bronze Gong-Bell** <note>original: 金鉦 (jīn zhēng)</note>, and various **Clapper-Bells** <note>original: 金鐸 (jīn duó)</note>, including single-headed and double-headed varieties.

In the **Present Dynasty** <note>referring to the Ming Dynasty, 1368–1644</note>, since the pacification of the world, these dances are performed during the **Great Sacrifices** at the Altars of Heaven and Earth, the Altars of Soil and Grain, the Altar of the First Agriculturist, and the **Imperial Ancestral Temple**. In these rites, the **Dance of Military Achievement** and the **Dance of Civil Virtue** are both performed. They serve to symbolize the completion of great deeds and the manifestation of virtue, representing the perfect balance of civil and martial governance.

# Watercraft

The *Huainanzi* <note>original: 淮南子; a 2nd-century BCE philosophical compendium compiled at the court of Liu An.</note> states that upon seeing hollowed-out wood floating, humanity understood how to construct a **boat**. The *Spring and Autumn Annals of Master Lü* <note>original: 呂氏春秋 (Lüshi Chunqiu); an encyclopedic text from the late Warring States period.</note> claims that **Yu Xu** <gloss>pronounced "Xun"</gloss> was the creator.

The *Treatise on the Principles of Things* <note>original: 物理論 (Wulilun); a Jin dynasty work exploring the origins of technology.</note> says **Hua Hu** was the inventor, while the *Mozi* <note>original: 墨子; the foundational text of Mohism.</note> attributes the creation to **Gong Chui**. The *Classic of Mountains and Seas* <note>original: 山海經 (Shanhaijing); a collection of mythic geography.</note> credits **Panyu**.

The *Record of Enlightening the Ignorant* <note>original: 發蒙記 (Famengji) by Dong Zhe.</note> says **Bo Yi** created them, and the *Book of Lineages* <note>original: 世本 (Shiben); a genealogical record of the pre-Qin era.</note> attributes the invention to **Gu** and **Huo Di**, noting that these men were all **ministers of the Yellow Emperor**.

The *Explanation of Names* <note>original: 釋名 (Shiming); a Han dynasty dictionary.</note> states that the **Yellow Emperor** fashioned them himself. The *Record of Heretofore Lost Works* <note>original: 拾遺記 (Shiyiji).</note> says that **Emperor Xuan** <term>Xuanhuang (軒皇): Another name for the Yellow Emperor.</term> transformed the practice of riding on rafts into the creation of boats and oars.

According to the *Inner Biography of the Yellow Emperor* <note>original: 黃帝內傳 (Huangdi Neichuan).</note>, after the Emperor had slain the rebel **Chiyou**, he personally fashioned boats and oars. The *Appended Remarks of the Book of Changes* <note>original: 易繫辭 (Yi Xici).</note> records: "The Yellow Emperor’s clan arose; they hollowed out wood to make **boats** and sharpened wood to make **oars**."

It is most likely that attributing these inventions to the era of the **Yellow Emperor** is the correct historical view.

# The Fujian Ship

The **Fujian ship** <term>Fuchuan</term> <note>original: 福船</note> is as tall and grand as a multi-story building, capable of carrying one hundred men. Its hull is designed with a pointed bottom and a wide top; the bow is high and "open-mouthed," while the stern towers upward. A triple-layered **sterncastle** <term>duolou</term> <note>literally "rudder tower"</note> is constructed upon the aft. The sides of the vessel are protected by bulwarks reinforced with **hardy bamboo** <note>original: 葽竹 (*yaozhu*), likely a species of tough, thick-walled bamboo used for structural reinforcement</note>, which stands as firm as a wall.

The ship is rigged with two masts and divided internally into four distinct levels:

*   **The lowest level** is not fit for habitation. It is filled entirely with soil and stones to serve as ballast, preventing the ship from becoming too light and drifting or capsizing in the wind.
*   **The second level** serves as the sleeping quarters for the soldiers. The flooring here is recessed; one must descend via a ladder from above to reach it.
*   **The third level** is protected by six ports on both the left and right sides. In the center are the water tanks. This level serves as the primary station for adjusting the sails and for cooking meals. Wooden winches are positioned at the front and back, rigged with palm-fiber cables; it is from here that the **anchors** <term>ding</term> <note>original: 掟. These were often large wooden anchors weighted with stones.</note> are raised and lowered.
*   **The uppermost level** is like an open terrace. It must be accessed through a hatchway ladder from the third level. On both sides are wing-like boards that serve as railings, which soldiers lean against to attack the enemy.

From this height, arrows, stones, and firearms can all be launched while looking down upon the opponent. If a smaller enemy vessel is encountered, the Fujian ship can simply ram and plow over it, sinking it instantly. Because the enemy finds it difficult to attack upward from a lower position, this vessel is truly a superior implement of naval warfare.

# The Cangshan Boat
<note>original: 蒼山船; Cangshanchuan. A versatile, medium-sized warship favored for coastal defense during the Ming dynasty due to its agility.</note>

Each oar is mounted on two **lever-pivots** <term>tiao</term> <note>original: 跳; these are mounting beams or fulcrums that allow large sculling oars to be operated with high leverage</note>, and each lever-pivot is manned by two people <note>This implies a total of twenty rowers, with four men powerering each large oar assembly</note>. If the crew encounters an **east wind**, the ship can immediately travel **west**; if they encounter a **south wind**, it can travel **north** <note>This means the ship does not need to physically turn its entire hull around—a dangerous and slow maneuver for large ships in rough seas—but can simply engage the opposite rudder to change direction</note>.

Among all the various vessels found on the **sea routes**, none surpasses this design in terms of **utility and advantage**.

### The Centipede Ship

The **vessel** is called the **"Centipede"** <note>original: 蜈蚣 (Wugong)</note> because its shape resembles the creature. Its design originated with the **foreigners of the Southeast** <note>original: 東南夷 (Dongnan Yi); this refers to Southeast Asian or European sailors, likely the Portuguese, who were encountered in the Southern seas.</note> and was specifically constructed to carry the heaviest of the **Frankish Cannons** <term>Folangji (佛郎機) — a type of breech-loading swivel gun introduced to China by the Portuguese in the early 16th century.</term>. 

The largest of these cannons weigh one thousand catties <note>approximately 1,300 lbs / 600 kg</note>, while even the smallest weigh one hundred and fifty catties. Their power is extraordinarily destructive; whether they strike wood, stone, copper, or tin, there is nothing they do not shatter, and anything they touch is scorched. Their speed is so swift that even the roar of thunder or a flash of lightning cannot surpass their momentum; even spirits could not chase them down. When this technology reached **China**, the Middle Kingdom adopted it to control and defend against these very invaders. Among all the various implements of fire-warfare—including **cannons**, **fire-arrows**, **fire-spears**, and **explosive canisters** <note>original: 毬 (qiu), literally "balls," referring to incendiary or explosive projectiles.</note>—none can surpass the effectiveness of this ship.

# The Sand Ship

<term>Shachuan (沙船) — Literally "Sand Ship," this is a classic flat-bottomed Chinese vessel named for its ability to navigate safely over underwater sandbanks without running aground.</term>

The **Sand Ship** <note>original: 沙船</note> is fully capable of navigating the vast reaches of the open ocean. Its hull is characterized by a **flat bottom**, which allows it to enter **sandbanks** <note>original: 沙洲 (shazhou); these refer to the treacherous silt deposits and shoals common in the Yellow Sea and the Yangtze River estuary</note> safely; it is for this reason that the vessel received its name.

The largest of these vessels are quite grand, featuring **masts** that exceed ten *zhang* <note>A zhang (丈) is a traditional unit of length; ten zhang is approximately 33 meters or 110 feet</note> in height, with a hull width that is proportionally vast to maintain balance.

The standard method for constructing such a vessel is as follows:
*   First, the **bottom** is laid.
*   Next, the **two sides** are raised.
*   Then, the **cross-beams** <note>original: 梁 (liang); these provide the lateral structural integrity for the wide hull</note> are installed.
*   Finally, the **masts** are stepped and secured.

Because the bottom is flat, the sailors do not need to fear the hazards of shallow waters.

<note>Historical context: In the Ming Dynasty, the Sand Ship was one of the "four great ship types." While the deep-keeled ships of the south (like the Fuchuan) were better for the rocky, deep waters of the Fujian coast, the Sand Ship was the "workhorse" of the north. Its stability was so renowned that contemporary records claim it could "sail through wind and waves as if walking on dry land."</note>

# The Ship’s Anchor

When running before the wind and managing the sails during an urgent pursuit of pirates, using the **capstan** <term>Cheguan (車關) — A manual windlass or winch used on traditional Chinese vessels to haul in heavy anchor cables.</term> to raise the anchor <note>original: 起綻 (qizhan); in this context, the character 'zhan' is a variant or error for 'ding' (anchor), referring to the act of weighing anchor</note> by manual labor is too slow and may result in a costly delay. 

To prepare for such an emergency, one should untie the mooring line and attach a float to the end <note>original: 綴尾 (zhuiwei); literally 'to attach a tail,' referring here to a buoy or marker tied to the cable</note>, letting it drift in the water. This allows the ship to depart immediately to catch the wind, while ensuring the anchor can be easily located and retrieved upon the ship's return.
