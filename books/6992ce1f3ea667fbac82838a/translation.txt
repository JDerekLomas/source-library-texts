### Helmets

Master Mao <note>The author, Mao Yuanyi (1594–1640), often refers to himself as "Master Mao" or "Maozi" in the style of classical philosophers.</note> says: The <term>helmet<note>original: 盔 (kui)</note></term> is the same as what the ancients called the <term>moumou<note>original: 鍪牟 (moumou). These were ancient bronze or iron head-coverings, often shaped like a cauldron or cap, used as far back as the Warring States period.</note></term>. There are many different styles of these. Approximately five types are recorded in the <term>Military Classics<note>original: 武經 (Wujing), specifically referring to the *Wujing Zongyao* (Essentials of the Military Classics), a Song Dynasty compendium that served as the primary reference for military technology before Mao's time.</note></term>, whereas there are six types in common use today; I have provided further illustrations of these modern versions. The descriptions provided here follow contemporary standards, as the specific designs of the ancient ones can no longer be definitively verified.

# Helmet and Neck Guard

<term>Helmet<note>original: 頭鍪 (Tóumóu). This term specifically refers to the rigid, protective bowl of the helmet.</note></term> and <term>Neck Guard<note>original: 頓項 (Dùnxiàng). Literally "bowed neck," this refers to the protective aventail—a skirt of mail, fabric, or laminated plates—that hangs from the helmet to shield the neck and shoulders.</note></term>

<note>The illustration accompanying this text (likely from the military compendium *Wubei Zhi* or *Wujing Zongyao*) shows a woodcut of a traditional helmet. It features a vertical plume at the crown and a tiered, laminated neck protector attached to the lower rim, with chin straps for stability.</note>

The **Helmet** and its attached **Neck Guard** constitute the primary head protection for the Ming soldier. The rigid crown is often topped with a decorative plume, while the neck guard is designed with multiple layers. This tiered construction is essential for defense; it allows the soldier to rotate their head with ease while the overlapping plates or reinforced fabric deflect strikes from blades or arrows that might otherwise find a gap at the throat.

In this era of warfare, headgear served both a functional and a symbolic role. While the sturdy construction protected the wearer from the impacts of hand-to-hand combat, the height of the plume and the ornamentation on the bowl often signaled the rank and status of the officer within the military hierarchy.

# Helmet and Neck Guard

<term>Helmet<note>original: 頭鍪 (Tóumóu). In a military context, this refers to the rigid bowl of the helmet, designed to protect the skull from concussive blows and sharp weapons.</note></term>
<term>Neck Guard<note>original: 頓項 (Dùnxiàng). Also known as an aventail, this is the protective "skirt" attached to the rim of the helmet. It shields the throat, the nape of the neck, and the shoulders.</note></term>

<note>The original woodcut illustration (noted in the technical description) shows a helmet typical of the Ming Dynasty. # Helmet and Neck Guard

<term>Helmet<note>original: 頭鍪 (Tóumóu). This term refers to the rigid shell or bowl of the headgear. In the Ming Dynasty, these were typically constructed from forged iron plates or hardened leather to withstand the impact of maces, arrows, and blades.</note></term>
<term>Neck Guard<note>original: 頓項 (Dùnxiàng). Literally meaning "arranged around the neck," this is a flexible curtain of protective material—often brigandine or lamellar—that hangs from the helmet to shield the wearer’s throat, nape, and shoulders.</note></term>

<note>The woodcut illustration on this page shows a standard military helmet of the late Ming period. It features a central spire designed to hold a decorative plume and a segmented neck guard. This design was highly practical; the "flaps" of the neck guard could be tied under the chin for full protection or left loose for better ventilation and movement during travel.</note>

The <term>Treatise on Armament Technology<note>original: 武備志 (Wǔbèi Zhì). This massive 240-volume encyclopedia, compiled by Mao Yuanyi in 1621, serves as a definitive record of Ming military science.</note></term> emphasizes that the <term>Helmet<note>Tóumóu</note></term> and <term>Neck Guard<note>Dùnxiàng</note></term> are inseparable components of a soldier's <term>Armor<note>original: 盔甲 (Kuījiǎ).</note></term>.

By covering the gap between the head and the torso armor, it secures one of the most vulnerable points on the human body. These pieces were often lacquered to prevent rust and decorated to signify the rank of the officer or the unit of the soldier.

## Tang-style Lion Helmet

<term>Tang-style Lion Helmet<note>original: 唐猊盔 (Tángní kuī). The term *Tangni* refers to the *suanni*, a mythical lion-like creature from Chinese mythology. In military contexts, this name implies that the armor is as fierce and impenetrable as the hide of a celestial lion. "Tang" likely refers to the "Tang Dynasty" style, which was often used in later eras to signify a classic, prestigious, or "golden age" design.</note></term>

The illustration on this page depicts a sophisticated evolution of the standard helmet. The **Tang-style Lion Helmet** features a robust, rounded crown designed to deflect downward strikes. At the peak is a decorative finial, which served the practical purpose of securing plumes or horsehair tassels to signify rank and maintain visibility on the smoke-filled battlefield.

The most notable feature of this particular model is the integrated protection for the neck and throat:

*   **The Crown:** Forged to withstand heavy impacts.
*   **The Scale-Patterned Neck Guard:** Unlike standard fabric or leather guards, this version utilizes a scale-like construction (resembling lion fur or scales), offering a superior balance of flexibility and defense for the wearer's neck and shoulders.

<note>In Ming Dynasty literature and military manuals, "Lion Armor" (Tangni kai) and "Lion Helmets" (Tangni kui) were often associated with elite units or high-ranking officers. The name served as a psychological deterrent, associating the soldier with the strength of the mythical beast.</note>

### Specifications for Helmet Manufacturing

For the construction of each helmet, use five or six <term>catties<note>original: 斤 (jīn). A traditional unit of weight. In the Ming period, one catty was roughly 600 grams, meaning a helmet crown used approximately 3 to 3.6 kilograms of iron.</note></term> of **pure iron**, reinforced with one catty of **steel**. The interior is finished by lining it with **fine leather**. If the helmet includes an attached <term>neck and head guard<note>original: 圍腦 (wéinǎo). Literally "surrounding the brain," this refers to the protective curtain of mail or fabric that drapes from the helmet to cover the back of the head and the neck.</note></term>, that component weighs an additional two catties.

The methods of construction follow these standards, though the styles of the helmet crowns vary. Notable designs include:

*   The **"Single-Piece Iron"** helmet <note>A helmet forged from a single sheet of metal rather than multiple plates.</note>
*   The **"Four-Opening"** helmet
*   The **"Six-Plate"** helmet
*   The **"Leather-Laced Willow Leaf"** helmet <note>A lamellar style where small, leaf-shaped plates are bound together with leather thongs.</note>

In the southern regions, <term>old cotton<note>original: 舊綿花 (jiù miánhuā). Thick, layered cotton was often used in southern China as a cost-effective alternative to heavy iron, which was prone to rusting in the humid climate.</note></term> is used to craft helmets. These are considered most advantageous when **soaked with water** <note>Wet cotton was surprisingly effective at absorbing the impact of projectiles and providing a measure of protection against early firearms and incendiary weapons.</note>.

# Rattan Helmets

**Rattan helmets** and **covers** are made from fine rattan. After weaving the frame with a sufficient quantity of rattan, a **cotton inner-cap** is used for the interior. The outer surface of this cap is made of two layers of cloth, while the inner lining is made of one layer. Between these layers, a certain amount of **silk floss** and <term>cocoon paper<note>original: 繭紙 (jiǎnzhǐ). A specialized, tough paper made from silk waste or mulberry fibers. When layered, it was remarkably durable and was frequently used in the construction of "paper armor" to resist arrows and blades.</note></term> are inserted. These components are then stitched together using **silk thread**.

The back of the inner-cap is not sewn shut; it features an opening three <term>inches<note>original: 寸 (cùn). A traditional unit of length. In the Ming Dynasty, three inches was approximately 9.6 centimeters (3.7 inches).</note></term> high. This is designed to accommodate different head sizes, allowing the soldier to personally attach and adjust the lining inside the helmet as needed.

On the peak of every helmet, a **red tassel** is attached. This is done for two reasons:
1.  To create a **grand and majestic** appearance for the military.
2.  To harmonize with the **symbolic color of the South** <note>According to the traditional "Five Elements" (Wuxing) theory, the South is associated with the element of Fire and the color red. This confirms the helmet's intended use by southern Ming forces, such as those fighting against the Wokou coastal raiders.</note>.

# Red Rattan Armor

<term>Red Rattan Armor</term> <note>original: 赤藤甲 (chìténgjiǎ)</note>: To create this armor, take fifty catties <note>approximately 30 kilograms or 66 pounds</note> of red rattan. Place the rattan inside a stone trough and soak it in water for half a month. Remove it and let it dry in the sun for three days, then return it to the trough and add fresh water. Repeat this cycle of soaking and drying until a full year has passed. 

Once thoroughly dried, weave and thread the rattan according to the established patterns. This quantity of material produces a total of twenty sets. Finally, coat the exterior of the armor with <term>tung oil</term> <note>a natural, water-resistant oil derived from the seeds of the tung tree, commonly used in historical Chinese woodworking and waterproof coatings</note>.

This armor is both lightweight and remarkably tough. It is capable of repelling arrows and blades, and it is particularly advantageous for use in deep or large bodies of water <note>referring to the rattan's natural buoyancy which prevents the wearer from sinking</note>. 

Furthermore, this same rattan is used to fashion wide-brimmed hats. These serve as helmets when facing the enemy in battle, and as protection against the rain during overcast weather. 

Additionally, soft rattan is woven to create <term>round shields</term> <note>original: 圓牌 (yuánpái)</note>. These shields are designed to be high in the center with raised edges. By donning this armor and wielding such a shield, a soldier is well-protected during both advances and retreats, making it highly effective for launching attacks.

# The Manufacture of Tang Suanni Armor

To create the plates for <term>Tang Suanni Armor</term> <note>original: 唐猊鎧 (Tángníkǎi)</note>, first take five catties <note>a "catty" or *jin* is a traditional unit of weight, approximately 600 grams</note> of <term>Bone-Penetrating Herb</term> <note>original: 透骨草 (tòugǔcǎo); a medicinal plant believed to have the power to soften or penetrate hard materials</note> and three catties of radish seeds. Coarsely grind these together and place them into one hundred catties of fresh water. Boil the mixture for a duration of "two hundred boils" <note>a traditional measure of time for decocting medicine</note>, then strain the liquid to remove the dregs.

To this decoction, add five whole <term>pangolin scales</term> <note>original: 川山甲 (chuānshānjiǎ); these scales were historically prized for their toughness and symbolic protective qualities</note>, three catties of <term>Datong salt</term> <note>original: 大同鹽 (Dàtóng yán); specialized salt sourced from the Datong region</note>, and three catties of <term>Glauber’s salt</term> <note>original: 皮硝 (píxiāo); a form of crude sodium sulfate used in leather tanning and metallurgy</note>. Furthermore, add five taels <note>a "tael" or *liang* is approximately 37 grams</note> of <term>saltpeter</term> <note>original: 硝石 (xiāoshí)</note> and half a catty of <term>borax</term> <note>original: 硼砂 (péngshā)</note>. Seal the cauldron tightly and boil the ingredients continuously for one full day and night.

Upon opening the cauldron, use a ladle to cast the molten or softened mixture into shapes with a thickness comparable to ox-hide. The patterns of these plates are diverse:
- **Spoon-head** shapes
- **Willow-leaf** shapes
- **Fish-scale** patterns
- **Square leaves** or **elongated rectangles**

Once formed, these individual plates are pierced and threaded together to construct the full suit of armor. This method results in equipment that is both light and highly efficient in combat. This particular style of armor is widely utilized in the Southern regions.

# The Necessity of Lightweight Protection

Armor is the fundamental basis for preserving one's life; it is that which allows a soldier to face the hail of **spears and arrows** <note>original: 鋒鏑 (fēngdí)</note> and maintain an invincible position.

In the southern regions of the empire, the terrain is treacherous and narrow, which necessitates the heavy use of infantry. When these soldiers must march or charge, it is difficult for them to carry heavy loads. Furthermore, the frequent rains and damp ground cause iron armor to rust and decay rapidly, making it utterly unusable in this climate.

The **Wokou** <note>A term referring to Japanese pirates who raided the Chinese coast during the Ming Dynasty</note> and local bandits predominantly utilize <term>muskets</term> <note>original: 火銃 (huǒchòng); early gunpowder firearms</note>. While armor made of rattan or horn is available and can be worn, **lead bullets** <note>original: 鉛子 (qiānzǐ)</note> are capable of piercing right through them. Moreover, the weight of such materials makes them difficult to endure during long campaigns.

In selecting the most advantageous equipment for current conditions, infantry should rely solely on <term>stitched armor</term> <note>original: 緝甲 (qìjiǎ); a form of protective clothing made by densely quilting layers of fabric and paper</note>. This is constructed using varying amounts of silk and cloth. If the layers of paper and cotton are too thin, even arrows will penetrate them, to say nothing of lead bullets.

This armor must be exactly one inch thick, using cotton that has been densely and tightly stitched. It should reach down to the knees. If it is too long, it will be cumbersome when maneuvering through the mud of the fields; if it is too short, it will fail to properly shield the body.

Heavy armor should only be used aboard ships. Since sailors do not have to march on roads or tread through muddy fields, and because the enemy can only reach them with bullets, nothing but the sturdiest protection will suffice to resist the assault.

<summary>This page explains the environmental and tactical superiority of "stitched" or paper armor in Southern China, arguing that iron is too heavy and prone to rust, while rattan is insufficient against musket fire.</summary>

### The Horse-Rejection Wood

<note>original: *jumamu* (拒馬木); literally "wood to rebuff horses." In Western military tradition, these are called *chevaux de frise* ("Frisian horses"). They were essential for Ming dynasty infantry to survive encounters with highly mobile steppe cavalry.</note>

The construction of the **Horse-Rejection Wood** uses either **bamboo** or **timber**. Its design consists of **three poles** that create **six ends**. These poles are joined by piercing through one another at a central intersection. Each of the six ends is fitted with a **sharp blade**. 

When placed upon the ground, the device stands upright immediately <note>due to the tripod-like geometry of the three intersecting poles</note>. At the point where the poles intersect, **iron chains** are used to link multiple units together in a continuous chain. 

Whether one is **deploying a battle formation**, **establishing a camp**, **defending a narrow pass**, or **blocking an open gap**, it is always appropriate to set these up. They are the primary means to defend against the enemy's **shock cavalry** <note>original: *tuqi* (突騎); rapid, hard-hitting cavalry units designed to break through infantry lines.</note>, as they prevent the horses from galloping freely. For this reason, the device is named the **"Horse-Rejection."**
