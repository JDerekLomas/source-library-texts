# PREFACE

The botanical studies of **Leonardo da Vinci** constitute one of the most captivating yet least explored branches of his universal genius. While the world celebrates the painter of the *Mona Lisa*, we find in these pages a man who looked upon the natural world with the rigorous eye of a modern scientist.

<margin>Leonardo's method: observation over dogma.</margin>

He did not merely seek to depict the outward beauty of a flower; he sought to understand the very laws of its growth. As he wrote in his notes: "The sun gives spirit and life to plants, and the earth nourishes them with moisture." <note>original: "Il sole dà lo spirito e la vita alle piante e la terra le nutre con l'umore."</note>

### The Windsor Collection

The drawings presented in this volume are reproduced with the utmost fidelity from the originals preserved in the <term>Royal Library of Windsor Castle</term>. These folios were once part of the vast mass of papers bequeathed by Leonardo to his faithful pupil, Francesco Melzi.

<column-break/>

Through the centuries, these sketches remained hidden from the public eye, known only to a few privileged scholars. By the grace of the <insert>Ministry of Public Instruction</insert>, we are now able to offer them to the artists and botanists of France.

### Classification of the Plates

The plates have been organized to show the progression of Leonardo's thought:

| Category | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Trees** | Studies of branching patterns and the effects of light on foliage. |
| **Flowers** | Detailed dissections of lilies, violets, and marsh marigolds. |
| **Fruits** | Observations on the internal structure of seeds and husks. |

## ON THE GROWTH OF BRANCHES AND THE NATURE OF LEAVES

The branch of the leaf <note>Leonardo often uses "branch of the leaf" to refer to the petiole or the small twig supporting the foliage</note> is always directed toward the sky, and never toward the earth.

The growth of the branches of trees is of two kinds: either the branches are opposite to one another, or they are arranged in a spiral.

### THE LAW OF THE SUN AND MOON

All the leaves which are on the side of the tree that is turned toward the north are larger than those on the south side. This happens because the leaves on the north side have more moisture, and the sun does not dry them out as much as those on the south side.

<margin>The sap flows more abundantly where the sun's heat is less intense.</margin>

The thickness of a branch is always equal to the sum of the thicknesses of all the branches that spring from it. <note>This is a famous observation now known as "Leonardo's Rule" for tree branching.</note>

<column-break/>

If you take a plant and turn it upside down, the leaves will immediately turn themselves back toward the sky. This is because the leaf always seeks the light and the air, which are its nourishment.

# THE ANATOMY OF PLANTS
### Observations on the Growth and Nutrition of Trees

---

The sun gives spirit and life to plants, and the earth nourishes them with moisture. Regarding this, I once made an experiment: I left only one small root on a gourd and kept this nourished with water; and the gourd brought to perfection all the fruits that it could produce, which were about sixty gourds of the long kind. And I set myself with diligence to write down this process. <note>Leonardo frequently used gourds in his experiments to study the movement of sap and water through the vascular system of plants.</note>

<margin>The sap that flows through the bark is the cause of the growth of the wood.</margin>

## ON THE BRANCHING OF TREES

The branches of trees, at every stage of their growth, are equal in thickness to the trunk from which they spring. <note>This is known today as "Leonardo's Rule" for trees; it suggests that the cross-sectional area of branches is conserved across different levels of branching.</note>

All the branches of a tree at every stage of its height, when put together, are equal in thickness to the trunk <insert>below them</insert>. 

Every year, when the leaves fall, the tree produces a new branch; that is to say, the branch that was at the end of the shoot last year now becomes the main stem. <gloss>The growth of the pith</gloss>

| YEAR OF GROWTH | THICKNESS OF THE STEM | OBSERVATIONS |
|----------------|-----------------------|--------------|
| First Year     | 1 unit                | The initial shoot emerges from the seed. |
| Second Year    | 2 units               | The first lateral branches appear. |
| Third Year     | 3 units               | The trunk thickens as the canopy expands. |

<column-break/>

The southern part of the plant shows more vigor and life than the northern part. <note>Leonardo observed that the rings of a tree are thicker on the side facing south, reflecting the influence of solar energy on growth.</note>

The bark of the tree on its southern side is much more cracked than on its northern side. And the wood of the tree, if you cut it, will show the circles which form its age to be much thicker on the side which was toward the south than on the side which was toward the north. <note>original: "tramontana"; referring to the north wind or the northern direction.</note>

<margin>Nature has so placed the leaves of the latest shoots of many plants that the sixth leaf is always above the first.</margin>

<term>Phyllotaxis</term> <note>The botanical term for the arrangement of leaves on a stem, which Leonardo was among the first to record systematically.</note>

## ON THE RINGS OF THE BARK AND THE AGE OF TREES

The circles of the branches of sawed trees show the number of their years, and which were wetter or drier according to their greater or lesser thickness. <note>original: "cerchi dei rami"</note> And so they show the direction of the world in which they were turned, because they are larger and thicker toward the south than toward the north; and thus the center of the tree is closer to its northern bark than to its southern bark.

<margin>The center of the tree is eccentric.</margin>

### OF THE ROOTS

The roots of trees do not increase in size in the same proportion as the branches above ground. <note>Leonardo notes here that the subterranean architecture of a tree follows different mechanical rules than the canopy.</note>

->**The Study of the Riverbank**<-

The roots of trees that are found on the banks of rivers show their nature more clearly than those in the middle of the woods. <insert>They cling to the earth like fingers</insert>. As the water washes away the soil, the roots are left bare and become as hard as the branches themselves, for they are no longer protected by the moisture of the earth.

<column-break/>

The bark of the roots is of the same nature as the bark of the branches of the tree. <gloss>The skin of the plant</gloss>

If you should see a tree that is thicker on its northern side, it is a sign that it has been nourished by the reflection of the sun from a nearby wall or some other heat source. <note>Leonardo accounts for anomalies in his "Southern growth" rule by considering the local environment and reflected light.</note>

The spirit of the plant flows from the leaves to the roots, just as the blood of animals flows through the veins. <note>While the circulatory system of plants was not fully understood in the 15th century, Leonardo correctly intuited a systemic flow of nutrients.</note>

## ON THE GROWTH AND NOURISHMENT OF THE TRUNK

The part of the tree which is most distant from the ground is that which first feels the cold of the night, and it is also the first to feel the heat of the morning sun. <note>Leonardo is observing the micro-climates surrounding a single organism.</note>

### THE PROPORTION OF THE TRUNK TO THE ROOTS

The thickness of the trunk of any tree is equal to the sum of the thickness of its roots. <note>This is an application of "Leonardo's Rule," which he usually applied to branches, suggesting that the total cross-sectional area remains constant.</note>

->**Observations on the Bark**<-

The bark of the tree, in its old age, becomes cracked and furrowed, just as the skin of a man becomes wrinkled with years. <margin>The skin of the earth</margin> This happens because the wood within increases in diameter more than the dry skin of the exterior can stretch. <insert>Nature always follows the shortest path</insert>.

<column-break/>

The sap that nourishes the tree flows between the bark and the wood. <gloss>The life-blood of the plant</gloss>

If you strip a circle of bark from a tree, the part above the wound will grow thicker than the part below. This occurs because the sap, descending from the leaves, is stopped at the edge of the cut and accumulates there. <note>original: "linfa"</note> <note>Leonardo correctly identifies the downward flow of nutrients through the phloem, even before the circulatory system of plants was fully codified.</note>

# On the Nature of the Bramble

### Observations on the Curvature of Thorns

The branch of the bramble <note>Rubus fruticosus, the common blackberry</note> does not grow in a straight line, but rather seeks the earth to take root once more. Observe how the thorns are always directed in opposition to the direction of the climber's progress. <margin>The hook of the thorn serves as a mechanical anchor.</margin> This is not by chance, but a necessity of its survival, for the plant uses these hooks to pull itself upward through the thicket.

<column-break/>

As the sap rises through the stalk, it distributes itself according to a law of proportion. <insert>See the diagram of the leaf nodes below.</insert> Where the stem is thickest, the thorns are most robust; as the wood softens toward the tip, the thorns become mere bristles.

->**The Geometry of Growth**<-

| Part of Plant | Form of Growth | Purpose |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Main Stem** | Pentagonal <note>Leonardo noted that many bramble stems have five distinct sides</note> | Structural strength |
| **Thorn** | Recurved hook | Defense and climbing |
| **Leaflet** | Serrated edge | Respiration and light |

<note>original: "le foglie non si cuoprano l'una l'altra"</note>

### THE LAW OF BRANCHING

->**The Rule of Proportionality**<-

All the branches of a water-bearing tree <note>original: "albero d'acqua" — likely referring to trees with high sap flow or those growing near water</note> at every stage of its height, when joined together, are equal in thickness to the trunk below them. 

| STAGE OF GROWTH | RATIO TO TRUNK | OBSERVATION |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Primary Limbs | 1:1 | Equal in total area |
| Secondary Shoots | 1:1 | Distribution remains constant |
| Final Twigs | 1:1 | Even the smallest tips follow the rule |

### OF THE BARK OF TREES

The bark of trees increases in hardness as the wood within it increases in thickness. ### ON THE RINGS OF THE WOOD

The circles of the branches of sawn trees <note>original: "circoli delli rami degli alberi segati" — referring to annual growth rings</note> show the number of their years, and whether they were more or less moist according to their greater or lesser thickness. # On the Growth of Plants and the Course of Sap

The sun gives spirit and life to plants, while the earth nourishes them with moisture. In this regard, I have observed that if you deprive a plant of one of these elements, its **vital force** <term>virtù: the inherent power or life-force of a natural object</term> will wither. 

<margin>The sap always flows to where it finds the greatest heat.</margin>

The water that rises from the roots to the highest branches is the same as the blood that rises from the heart to the head of man. It moves through the narrow vessels of the wood as if by a sort of attraction, which I call *capillarity* <note>Leonardo is describing the phenomenon where liquid flows in narrow spaces without the assistance of external forces, though he uses the term "attraction of the heat."</note>.

### Observations on the Bark and the Sun

It is a general rule that the bark of a tree will be thicker on its northern side than on its southern side. 

| Orientation | Bark Thickness | Branch Length |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **North** | Thicker | Shorter |
| **South** | Thinner | Longer and more robust |

This occurs because the sun, being in the south, draws the sap toward it with greater force, and thus the southern side of the tree grows more rapidly and produces more fruit. <insert>I have seen this even in the smallest shrubs of the field.</insert>

->**The Law of Branching**<-

All the branches of a tree at every stage of its height, when put together, are equal in thickness to the trunk. <note>This is known today as "Leonardo's Rule" for trees; it suggests that the total cross-sectional area of branches is conserved.</note>

<margin>Nature is a master of geometry.</margin>

# ON THE GROWTH OF TREES AND PLANTS

The sun gives spirit and life to plants, and the earth nourishes them with moisture. Regarding this, I once made an experiment: I left only one small root on a gourd and kept this nourished with water; and the gourd brought to perfection all the fruits it could produce, which were about sixty gourds of the long kind. I set myself with diligence to consider this process, and I perceived that the <term>dew of the night</term><note>Leonardo is likely referring to atmospheric moisture or transpiration cycles</note> was what entirely nourished it.

<margin>The sun's heat causes the sap to rise.</margin>

All the branches of trees, at every stage of their growth, when taken together, are equal in thickness to the trunk. All the branches of waters, at every stage of their course, if they have the same speed, are equal to the size of their main source.

The leaves of those plants that grow from the <insert>yearly</insert> shoots will all turn their <gloss>outer surface</gloss> toward the sky, so that they may better receive the nourishment of the dew.

# OF AQUATIC PLANTS AND THE REED

The nature of plants that grow in stagnant waters or slow-moving marshes is such that they possess a singular uprightness, for they are not buffeted by the great winds that strike the mountain forests. Here, the water provides a constant nourishment to the base, while the air above remains humid and heavy.

<margin>The root must be firmly fixed in the silt to resist the pull of the current.</margin>

I have observed the **Cattail** <note>original: "Typha" - commonly known as the bulrush or reedmace</note> and how its leaves are disposed. They do not grow at random, but follow a spiral necessity, winding upward so that each leaf may have its share of the sun and the falling moisture. 

<column-break/>

**The Flowering Spike:**
The head of this plant, which some call the <term>mace</term><note>A reference to the dense, cylindrical flower spike</note>, is composed of a most fine down. <insert>The stem is hollow like a pipe to allow the spirit of the plant to move freely.</insert>

->**On the Movement of Water Plants**<-

When the water moves, the plant bends with a graceful curve, for its fibers are supple. Unlike the oak, which resists the wind until it breaks, the reed yields to the flow and thus survives the flood. 

<gloss>The surface of the leaf is ribbed for strength</gloss>

The leaves of the <term>cattail</term> <note>original: "cattail"</note> are long and narrow, shaped like the swords of ancient soldiers, yet they possess a softness that allows them to ripple in the slightest breeze. One must note how the light catches the edges of these blades, creating a silvered appearance where the water has left a thin crust of salt or silt.

# On the Growth of Trees and the Proportion of Branches

The branch of the tree, or the plant at the top of its height, is as old as the root of the same plant. <note>Leonardo posits that the very first shoot produced by the seed remains the "tip" of the main trunk, making it chronologically the same age as the original root system.</note>
