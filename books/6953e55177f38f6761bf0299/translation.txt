# P R E F A C E

## In which it is demonstrated that what has been written until now concerning the movement and use of the heart and arteries is poorly grounded.

*When considering the movement, pulse, action, use, and benefits of the heart and arteries, it is a worthwhile task to examine what has previously been committed to writing by others, and to take notice of what is commonly circulated and handed down. This is done so that those things correctly stated may be confirmed, and those which are false may be corrected by anatomical dissection, repeated experience, and diligent, accurate observation.*

*Almost all anatomists, physicians, and philosophers up to this point suppose, along with Galen <note>Claudius Galenus (129–216 AD), the influential Greek physician whose theories dominated Western medicine for over a millennium.</note>, that the use of the pulse is the same as that of respiration, and that they differ in only one thing: that the former arises from the vital faculty and the latter from the animal faculty. In all other respects, whether regarding their utility or their manner of movement, they behave similarly. From this they affirm (as does Hieronymus Fabricius of Aquapendente <note>Girolamo Fabrici (1533–1619), a pioneering anatomist and Harvey's teacher at Padua.</note> in his recently published book on respiration) that because the pulse of the heart and arteries does not suffice for ventilation and cooling, Nature therefore fashioned the lungs around the heart. Hence it is clear that whatever the predecessors have said concerning Systole and Diastole <term>Systole and Diastole: the contraction and expansion phases of the heartbeat</term>, and concerning the movement of the heart and arteries, they have handed these down with the lungs in mind.*

# P R E F A C E

It is evident that when the windpipe <note>original: "tracheæ"</note> is cut, air enters and exits in two opposing motions. However, when an artery is cut <note>term: arteriotomy | the surgical opening or accidental cutting of an artery</note>, it is manifest that blood is immediately thrust out with force in one continuous motion, and air neither enters nor exits. If the pulsing of the arteries cools and ventilates the parts of the body—just as the lungs do for the heart—how can it be commonly said that the arteries carry vital blood from the heart to every part, filled with "vital spirits" <note>term: vital spirits | in early medicine, a refined substance in the blood thought to carry life and heat</note>? These spirits are supposed to nourish the heat of the parts, awaken it when dormant, and restore it when exhausted. Furthermore, how is it that if you tie off the arteries, the parts immediately become not only numb, cold, and pale in appearance, but eventually cease to be nourished? According to Galen <note>Claudius Galenus, a highly influential 2nd-century Greek physician</note>, this happens because the parts are deprived of the heat that flows down from the heart. From this, it appears more likely that arteries carry heat to the parts rather than cooling or ventilation.

Moreover, how can the expansion of the heart <note>term: Diastole | the phase of the heartbeat when the heart muscle relaxes and the chambers fill with blood</note> simultaneously draw spirits from the heart to warm the parts, and at the same time draw in cooling from the outside? Furthermore, although some claim that the lungs, arteries, and heart serve the same purposes, they also say the heart is the "workshop" of the spirits and that the arteries contain and transmit them. Yet, they deny that the lungs create or retain spirits, contrary to the opinion of Columbus <note>Matteo Realdo Colombo, a 16th-century anatomist who described pulmonary circulation</note>. Indeed, they assert with Galen—and against Erasistratus <note>an ancient Greek anatomist who believed arteries were filled with air</note>—that blood is contained in the arteries and not air. These opinions seem to conflict with and refute one another so much that they should all be viewed with suspicion.

That blood is contained in the arteries, and that arteries carry only blood, is manifest both from Galen's experiment and from instances of arterial cutting and wounds. <margin>Galen, in the book *Whether Blood is Contained in the Arteries*.</margin> Galen himself affirms in many places that if a single artery is cut, the entire mass of blood from the whole body would be exhausted in the space of half an hour by a great and impetuous profusion. Galen's experiment is as follows: "If," he says, "you tie an artery at both ends with a small cord <note>original: "funiculo"</note> and cut it lengthwise in the middle, you will find that what is contained in the artery between the two ties is nothing but blood." *it is permitted: If you find the same blood in the arteries—which I have very often experienced in the dead and in other animals—as is present in the veins when they are similarly bound and cut open, we are able to conclude by the same reasoning that the arteries contain the same blood as the veins, and nothing except that same blood. Some, while they attempt to resolve the difficulty by affirming that the blood is "spirituous" and "arterial," tacitly concede that the function of the arteries is to carry blood from the heart into the whole body, and that the arteries are filled with blood. For spirituous blood is no less blood: even blood as blood, and that which flows in the veins, no one denies is imbued with spirits.* <note>The "spirits" mentioned here refer to the "vital spirits" of Galenic medicine, believed to be a subtle substance that gave life to the body.</note> *But if the blood which is in the arteries swells with a more abundant supply of spirits, it must nevertheless be considered that these spirits are inseparable from the blood, just as those in the veins are, and that the blood and spirits constitute one body (as whey and butter are in milk, or heat in hot water). ### CHAPTER III.

#### *The nature of the motion of the arteries from the dissection of living animals.*

In the motion of the heart, the following points regarding the motion and pulsations of the arteries must be further observed.
I. At the same time that the tension, contraction, and striking of the chest by the heart occurs—which is entirely its <term>Systole</term> <note>The phase of the heartbeat when the heart muscle contracts and pumps blood from the chambers into the arteries.</note>—the arteries are dilated, produce a pulse, and are in their <term>Diastole</term> <note>The phase of the heartbeat when the heart muscle relaxes and allows the chambers to fill with blood; here, Harvey notes the arteries expand as the heart contracts.</note>. Similarly, at the time the right ventricle contracts and thrusts out the blood contained within, the <term>arterial vein</term> <note>original: "vena arteriosa"; this refers to what we now call the pulmonary artery.</note> pulses and is dilated along with the rest of the arteries of the body.
II. When the left ventricle ceases to move, pulse, and contract, the pulse of the arteries ceases; indeed, when it is tensed more feebly, the pulse in the arteries is scarcely perceptible; and similarly when the right ventricle ceases, the pulse in the arterial vein ceases.
III. Likewise, if any artery is cut or perforated, the blood is propelled out from the wound with force at the very moment of the tension of the left ventricle. Similarly, if the arterial vein is cut, you will see the blood leap forth from it with force at the same time that the right ventricle is tensed and contracted.
Likewise, even in fish, if the <term>pipe</term> <note>original: "fistula"; referring to the bulbus arteriosus or ventral aorta in fish.</note> which leads from the heart to the gills is cut, you will see the blood pushed through it with force at the same time the heart is seen to be tensed and contracted.
Finally, since in every <term>arteriotomy</term> <note>The surgical opening or cutting of an artery.</note> the blood comes out by leaping, sometimes further and sometimes nearer, you will find that the leap occurs during the Diastole of the arteries, at the time the heart strikes the chest; and this is surely at the time when the heart appears tensed and contracted, and is in its Systole and erection, and the blood is expelled by that same motion.
From these facts it seems manifest, contrary to common dogmas, that the Diastole of the arteries occurs at the same time as the Systole of the heart; and that the arteries are filled and distended because of the sending in and intrusion of blood by the constriction of the ventricles of the heart. Furthermore, the arteries are distended because they are filled like <term>leathern bottles</term> <note>original: "utres"; skin bags used for holding wine or water.</note> or a bladder, and are not filled because they are distended like <term>bellows</term> <note>Harvey is arguing against the traditional view that arteries expand to "suck in" blood; he argues they expand because blood is forced into them.</note>. And for the same reason, the arteries of the entire body pulse—namely, from the tension of the left ventricle of the heart—just as the arterial vein pulses from the tension of the right.

Whether the heart adds anything else to the blood besides its transposition, local motion, and distribution—be it heat, spirit, or some perfection—must be inquired into later and gathered from other observations. For the present, let it suffice that it has been shown clearly enough that in the beating of the heart, blood is transferred and drawn from the veins into the arteries through the ventricles of the heart, and is distributed to the entire body.

Indeed, everyone grants this in some manner, gathering it from the structure of the heart and the ingenious design, position, and use of the valves. # CHAPTER IX.

->*That there is a circulation of the blood, confirmed by the first supposition.*<-

**B**ut lest anyone should say that we are merely giving words, and making only specious assertions without foundation, or innovating without just cause: three things come to be confirmed, which being granted, I believe this truth necessarily follows, and the matter is made clear.

First, that the blood is continually and incessantly transmitted from the *vena cava* <note>The large vein bringing blood to the heart</note> into the arteries, in such great abundance, by the beat of the heart, that it cannot be supplied by the food consumed, and so much so that the entire mass of blood passes through there in a short time.

Second, that the blood is continually, evenly, and incessantly driven into every limb and part by the pulse of the arteries, and enters in much greater abundance than is sufficient for nutrition, or than the entire mass of blood could supply.

And likewise third, that the veins themselves perpetually return this blood from every limb to the location of the heart.

These things being established, I think it will be manifest that the blood goes around, revolves, is propelled, and flows back from the heart to the extremities, and from there back to the heart again, and thus performs a kind of circular motion.

Let us suppose (either by thought or by experiment) how much blood the left ventricle contains in its dilation (when it is filled), whether it be two ounces, three ounces, or an ounce and a half; I have found in a dead body more than two ounces.

The discoverer did not attain the true use of these valves, nor have others added to it. Their use is not to prevent the blood from falling downward by its own weight into the lower parts; for in the jugular veins <note>veins in the neck</note> they look downward, preventing the blood from being carried upward. They do not everywhere look upward, but always toward the roots of the veins, and everywhere toward the location of the heart. I, like others, have sometimes found them in the renal veins and in the branches of the mesentery <note>the membrane attaching the intestines to the abdominal wall</note> looking toward the vena cava and the portal vein. Now, finally, it is permitted to deliver our opinion on the circulation of the blood and propose it to all.

**I** do not find the heart to be a distinct and separate part in all animals. For some (which you might call) plant-animals <note>original: "plant-animalia"; referring to organisms like sponges or anemones that seem to bridge the gap between plants and animals</note> do not have a heart. This is because certain animals are colder, of small body size, of a softer texture, and of a uniform constitution—such as the genus of caterpillars and earthworms, and many which arise from decay and do not preserve their species. These have no heart because they have no need for an impeller to carry nourishment to their extremities; for they have a body that is joined and one, indistinct without members, so that by the contraction and relaxation of the whole body, they take in and expel, move and remove their nourishment. The plant-animals called Oysters, Mussels, Sponges, and all genera of Zoophytes <note>literally "animal-plants"</note> do not have a heart; for they use the whole body instead of a heart, and such an animal is, as it were, an entire heart.

William Harvey was born in Folkestone in the county of Kent in 1577. He first assumed the insignia of the Doctorate at Padua, where he had practiced in the study of Medicine for five years, and afterwards at Cambridge, where he had previously studied this art. He was the most wise Chief Physician <note>original: "Archiater"</note> to King James and Charles I, a Fellow and former President of the College of Physicians of London, and Professor of Anatomy and Surgery. He was the discoverer of the <term>Circulation of the blood</term> <note>The revolutionary theory that blood flows in a continuous loop through the body, rather than being consumed and recreated.</note> and he first revealed this new opinion in his anatomical lectures in the medical theater, and nine years later first published it at Frankfurt in 1628. Although he was most successful in establishing the circuit of the blood, he was nevertheless "blind" <note>original: "cecutiebat," meaning he was mistaken or lacked insight</note> regarding the lacteal vessels, affirming the transport of chyle <note>A milky fluid consisting of lymph and emulsified fats.</note> to the liver through the mesenteric vessels <note>original: "vasa meseraica"</note>. It was his intention to bless posterity with many more treatises, the loss of which—due to the civil wars infesting his fatherland—the entire literary and scholarly world deservedly mourns. He died in the year 1657 at the age of 80.
