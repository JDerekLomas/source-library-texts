->*Concerning the form of the Camp as a whole.*<-

<margin>⊢</margin>**I**f there were an equal number of men in every company <note>original: *vendel*</note>, an equal number of companies in every regiment, and always the same number of regiments in an Army—as was the custom *among the Romans—then His Princely Grace* <note>Prince Maurice of Orange (1567–1625), a Dutch stadtholder and innovator in military engineering and drill.</note> would always arrange the regiments of Infantry and Cavalry in a uniform manner and with equal space. He would lodge the Pikemen in one of the two rows of huts and the Musketeers in the other row, following the order the soldiers maintain in their ranks, giving the Camp always the same form. However, seeing as at the present time, neither in these Lands nor elsewhere as it seems, is there the same power or resolution as the Romans had to keep companies, regiments, and armies always orderly filled, a good rule for "imperfect" orders can now be of greater use than one for "perfect" ones. Therefore, alongside the preceding rules for the parts of a Camp, I shall also provide an explanation of the rule maintained for our Camps as a whole, despite the inequalities they may have, taking as an example the Army of the *High and Mighty Lords of the States-General* at *Jülich in the year 1610* <note>original: *Gulich*; refers to the Siege of Jülich, a major military operation where Stevin’s camp-planning methods were famously applied</note>, through which *the meaning can be explained.*

In such sections as this List of Artillery is divided, and such a sequence as the items have therein, it is also understood that they shall maintain this order in the Lists that are made thereafter every year, or as often as they are required. This is more convenient than arranging them without a fixed sequence, both for creating new Lists with less effort and more certainty of including everything, as well as for those concerned to easily review the correspondence of a current List with a previous one. Specifically, they can see whether all necessary items are included without anything missing, or whether too much has been listed, or whether the quantity of powder, shot, and other necessities is determined according to the requirements of the current pieces of artillery (as was done in previous years with good forethought). It also allows one to see if thought has been given to including those things found to be lacking during the previous campaign; all of which is much easier through such a consistent sequence of items than otherwise searching through difficult tallying <note>original: 'punctuering'; refers to the tedious process of checking off items one by one in an unorganized list</note> to see if one has everything.

Note also that although it is stated here that all subsequent Lists shall have an order like this one, it is not to be understood that they must contain all the items of the previous one. For although many items might be missing (as must necessarily be the case in several Lists), those that are present can maintain their sequence just like this one, as far as it goes. It is also understood that the improvement of the order of the sequence of the sections and their items is always permitted.

What has been said here regarding the List of Artillery also applies to the following Lists of munitions of war, wagons, ships, and others where many varieties occur.

## ARTICLE 2.

->*Concerning the measurement of the rectangular quarters of the soldiers in the field, which is performed by the Camp Measurer and his assistants.*<-

Once the ground plan on paper has determined what the layout on the land must be, one begins by marking the rectangles for the regiments or quarters of the infantry. For this purpose, approximately 150 <term>quarter-stakes</term> <note>original: "quartierbaken", literal beacons or markers used to denote boundaries.</note> are brought to the site in the field—or as many as are seen to be necessary—which are painted poles about 9 feet long, each with a small flag at the top, to be placed at the corners of the quarters. It is announced by drumbeat and forbidden under penalty of <term>corporal punishment</term> <note>original: "lijfſtraffe", which could range from physical discipline to the death penalty in a military context.</note> to pull these out; for some years ago, when this was not done, it was impossible to maintain the layout of the quarters, because everyone needing wood to build their huts would pull out the marking stakes. It is also forbidden for anyone to approach the site while the quarters are being marked, except for those specifically assigned, because in former times when this was not prohibited, one could not perform a proper layout due to the interference of people, wagons, horses, straw, and wood being brought in for the huts.

Note also that it has been found necessary to use a universal measure in the camp: a rod divided into its feet and inches, which is called the <term>field measure</term> <note>original: "veldtmaet". Stevin was a major proponent of standardized decimal systems and units.</note>. This is because the contractors, engineers, controllers of fortifications, carpenters, and others coming from various cities and provinces each brought a measure used in their own home city, which differed greatly from one another, resulting in various disorders.

Furthermore, the <term>Camp Measurer</term> <note>original: "Leghermeter", the officer responsible for surveying the camp site.</note> is followed by all the Quartermasters of the regiments, so that once a rectangle is marked, it can be immediately shown to each of them.

This being the case, the four stakes for each regiment are placed on the land according to the requirements of the aforementioned paper drawing, encompassing four-sided rectangles 200 feet long from front to back for the soldiers. This is done with the help of the <term>surveyor's cross</term> <note>original: "Meterſkruys", a tool used to establish 90-degree angles on the ground.</note> and the measurement of lengths in the common manner of land surveyors. As this is well known to engineers, it requires no further instruction here; I might only mention for the sake of ease that one only needs to measure one of the four sides of the rectangles—or two where a side of the camp begins—as the rest is more conveniently done through <term>sighting</term> <note>original: "driebaecking", a technique of aligning three stakes by eye to ensure a straight line.</note> and the use of the surveyor's cross, as is familiar to them.

## Division of the soldiers of the Romans.

The division of the Romans underwent some changes at various times, such that I shall only set down here the best known, namely that of the period of which Polybius <note>Polybius (c. 200–118 BC), a Greek historian whose work *The Histories* provides the most detailed description of the Roman military system during the Mid-Republic.</note> writes, in which a Roman legion consisted of 4,200 infantry and 300 cavalry. The infantry were divided into 10 regiments which they called <term>cohorts</term> <note>Latin: *cohortes*</note>, each consisting of 3 companies, called by them <term>maniples</term> <note>Latin: *manipuli*</note>, each of which had two captains called <term>Centurions</term>. The cohorts or regiments did not each have their own specific Colonel, but over the 10 regiments of the legion six officials commanded together, called <term>Tribunes</term> <note>Latin: *Tribuni militum*</note>. The first of the three companies of a regiment consisted of 120 <term>Hastati</term> <note>Spearmen; originally the first line of the Roman battle formation.</note>; the second of 120 <term>Principes</term> <note>Main-line soldiers; the second line of the formation.</note>; the third of 60 <term>Triarii</term> <note>Veteran soldiers who formed the third and final line.</note>, all being heavily armed, and each company had with it 40 light-armed hurlers and shooters, whom they called <term>Velites</term> <note>Skirmishers.</note>. This was organized much as companies are now made up of armed pikemen and musketeers. The companies were divided into files and ranks. The <term>files</term> <note>original: *rijen*</note> consisted of 10 soldiers (when the company was in battle order) standing on the lines from front to back, and the <term>ranks</term> <note>original: *leden*</note> were those standing on the lines from the right to the left side. Each of the ten soldiers was designated in his file as first, second, third, and so on according to his place. The files in each company were also designated as first, second, third, and so on to the last, which for the *Hastati* and *Principes* was the twelfth, and for the *Triarii* the sixth. The aforementioned 300 cavalry were divided into 10 groups of 30 each, called <term>Turmae</term> <note>Latin for "troops" or "squadrons."</note>. The maniples and cohorts had banners as markers, and the legions each had a silver <term>Eagle</term> <note>The *Aquila*, the primary standard of a Roman legion.</note>. The Roman army had two legions, and approximately as many again of those who were not Romans, such as <term>Allies</term> <note>original: *Socij*</note>, <term>Recalled Veterans</term> <note>original: *Evocati*</note>, <term>Picked Troops</term> <note>original: *Ablecti*</note>, and <term>Extraordinaries</term> <note>original: *Extraordinarij*</note>, so that it consisted of about 16,000 men, a ground plan of which can be seen in the 1st Member of the 1st Chapter. When more people were needed, there would be two armies, each with its Commander-in-Chief in the same form as the preceding, and arranged back-to-back against each other, which was done at the sides where the Commanders were lodged.

Against this, I say that one could answer each of these specifically, but seeing as it does not seem that such will soon be put into practice, I do not wish to lose time with unnecessary writing. I only say that it has been considered, and meanwhile one may take this admonition in a **theoretical** <term>spieghclincsche vvijse; literally "mirror-like manner," Stevin's term for a conceptual or mathematical model</term> way, so that it is possible to one day reach an improvement in **practice** <term>daet; the actual execution or deed</term>.

Eighthly, through this decimal division, such a convenience can occur in the matter of **Ships** as was said of the wagons; for by dividing them thus, and also appointing such officials over them, and the ships of each Ten-leader each carrying a flag by day with their numbers, as was said of the wagons, and lanterns by night, whose light showed the form of large letters (as I have seen such), and further following a rule both in sailing and at anchor as with the aforementioned wagons, one can sufficiently understand the advantages that would be gained thereby.

Ninthly, through decimal division of the **Pioneers** <term>Pioniers; military laborers who dig trenches and build fortifications</term> and **Sailors** or **Artillery-pullers** <term>Gheschuttreckers</term>, such good order can also be kept that more work would be done with fewer people than otherwise with many, each having moreover better satisfaction than with disorder, about which one could write more broadly, but...

<header>CAMP MEASUREMENT.</header>

...since such can be sufficiently understood from what has been said regarding similar matters of the soldiery, I will leave it at that.

Having explained my intention regarding the utility of a continuous **decimal progression** <term>tienige voortganc; an organizational system based on multiples of ten</term> starting with 10, someone might say that the above-described Greek division of continuous **duplicative progression** <term>tweeige voortganc; a system based on doubling, or base-2</term>, beginning with a **file** <term>rije; a single line of soldiers</term> of 16, has its own particular advantage highly praised by various authors. This advantage is that through continuous halving of the groups one arrives at unity, such that they can be divided into two equal parts; for example, 16,384 of the **Tetraphalangarchia** <term>Tetraphalangarchy; a large Greek unit consisting of four phalanxes</term> can be continuously halved down to the file, which is found to be 16, and by splitting that continuously in two, one finds halves of 8, 4, 2, and 1, ending in unity. To this, one may say that this halving does not proceed as perfectly, nor reach unity as is supposed, but stops at the commander over 2 files of 32 soldiers, for this reason: the purpose of the division is to have an appointed commander over each part. For example: over 16 files a **Syntagmatarch** <term>Sintagmatarcha; commander of 256 men</term> commands; over 8 a **Taxiarch** <term>Taxiarcha; commander of a taxis or division</term>; over 4 a **Tetrarch** <term>Tetrarch; commander of a quaternary unit</term>; over 2 a **Dilochite** <term>Dilochita; commander of a double-file</term>. But over the single file of 16 men, there is no commander appointed as before. It is true that the first man, namely the **File-leader** <term>Rijeleyder</term>, is taken for that role, but he commands only 15, as he himself is one of the 16. If the file is divided in two, each group consists of 8 men without commanders; or if one takes the first man for one half and the ninth man for the other (called a **Diomerite** <term>Diomerita; a subordinate officer in the Greek system</term>), they each command only 7. The same applies to the **Intergatores** <term>original: "Intergatores"; likely referring to the Greek "Enomotarchs" or similar sub-officers</term> who command 3 instead of 4, and the **Antistites** <term>Antistites; leaders or overseers</term> who command 1 instead of 2. Thus, such officials do not command groups of duplicative progression as the others do over groups of 2 files and upwards, as was intended to be explained.

If one should also think that the halving system—which has its appointed commanders from 16,384 down to 32—is better provided for having an appointed commander over all parts of the groups than the decimal progression, that is not the case. For example: should necessity require (as often happens there) a **Phalangarchia** <term>Phalangarchy; a division of the phalanx</term> of 16 Greek **companies** <term>vendels</term> to be divided into 9 and 7, or 10 and 6, or 11 and 5, or 12 and 4, or 13 and 3, or 14 and 2, or 15 and 1, there are no proper commanders for all these parts.

Beside the aforementioned inconveniences, there are also improper decimal names for groups and officials where no ten-fold nature exists. For instance, they call a group of 16 a **decuria** <term>Decury; historically a group of 10</term> as if it were of 10, and the commander a **decurio**. They call a group of 256 a **centuria** <term>Century; historically a group of 100</term> as if it were of 100, and the commander a **centurio**. They call a group of 1,024 a **chiliarchia** <term>Chiliarchy; historically a group of 1,000</term> as if it were of 1,000, and the commander a **chiliarcha**. This misuse of names suggests a great antiquity and dignity for the decimal progression, rather than it having been well-followed.

Regarding the Roman division mentioned earlier, which (besides the misuse of decimal names without a ten-fold structure) proceeds very inconsistently without any regular progression being observed, and is difficult to retain in the memory: it is true that it is better than following no certain rule at all, as is currently the case in the world, but it is not to be compared to the **Hebrew** <note>Stevin often cites the Biblical/Hebrew decimal organization as the ideal model of order.</note> system. Regarding what might be said here—that the Romans' military deeds sufficiently testified to the goodness of their system—one may answer that through a better division they could have achieved even more with better order and less effort.
