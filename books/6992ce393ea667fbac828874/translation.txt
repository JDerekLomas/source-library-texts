# Collection of Precious Elegance, Volume 1: Northern Pearls

2

Whenever observing the color of Northern Pearls, one must finish looking, close the eyes, and then blink and look again. If the color appears the same as before, then its quality is truly verified. 

Those pearls that are "blue" <note>original: "青" (Qing). A color term spanning blue, green, and black; here it refers to a prized celestial or azure tint.</note> appear like the sky at the end of summer or the beginning of autumn, when a sudden rain has just cleared and the clouds part to reveal a flash of the blue heavens. This "blue appearing within white clouds" is the premier authentic color. This blue should not be a deep, dark blue; it only requires a "tender blue" that is enveloped by a white outer layer. 

If the blue of the pearl only covers the top like a cap and does not drape down below the crown, it is called <term>Mohailuo'er</term> <note>original: "摩孩羅兒". A phonetic transcription, likely of a Manchu term or a reference to "Mahala" figurines, describing a specific "capped" color pattern.</note> "Top-Blue." If the blue reaches below the "waist" all the way to the drill hole, it is called <term>Turning-Body Blue</term> <note>original: "轉身青" (Zhuanshen Qing)</note>, which is the highest grade. If the blue stays above the waist, it is called <term>Shoulder-Drape Blue</term> <note>original: "披肩青" (Pijian Qing)</note>, also a first-rate grade. If there is only a tiny speck of blue on the crown that cannot cover the top, it is called a <term>Ghost Eye</term> <note>original: "鬼眼睛" (Guiyanjing)</note> and is not considered a rarity.

The formula for inspecting a large pearl’s <term>Form</term> <note>original: "身分" (Shenfen). Refers to the physical substance, stature, and grade of the pearl.</note>, color, and <term>Defects</term> <note>original: "節病訣" (Jiebingjue). A mnemonic or professional secret for identifying internal and external flaws.</note> states: when observing the <term>form</term> of a Northern Pearl, it must be rounded, using the drill hole as the point of reference. For the <term>form</term> of the pearl, colors such as bluish-white, greenish, yellowish, porcelain-white, or bone-white are considered lower-quality styles.

...the formula for a fine pearl is that it should be as **powdery white** as possible. This is particularly desirable in a Northern Pearl. It is best if the lower part of the body has "White Tower Banding" <note>original: "白塔搏" (Baitabo). Refers to a layered or tiered white luster resembling a pagoda.</note>, or if the upper part has "Trailing Tassels" <note>original: "牽孛落" (Qianbeiluo). A descriptive term for wispy, cloud-like patterns in the pearl's luster.</note>.

However, those where a yellow tint sits atop a blue color are **not up to standard**. Conversely, those where a blue tint sits atop a yellow base are **especially prized**. 

Regarding the holes: if the pearl has "Straight Eyes" <note>original: "直眼" (Zhizuan). Standard vertical drill holes.</note> or "Pit Eyes" <note>original: "竅眼" (Qiaoyan). Natural pits or cavities.</note>, or if the body is damaged by broken pits, or has been re-drilled with three or four holes, it is also **not up to standard**. 

Furthermore, when purchasing straight-drilled Northern Pearls, one should only buy those with a **high "belly"** <note>meaning the central rounded part of the pearl is plump and elevated</note>. If the area near the drill hole is pointed, it is called a "Bald Head." If the bottom is wide and flat, it is called <term>Cellar-Packed</term> <note>original: "窖裝" (Jiaozhuang)</note>, also known as a <term>Bottomless Pearl</term> <note>original: "無篤珠子" (Wudu Zhuzi)</note>. 

If one end is large and the other small, it is called a <term>Hammer</term> <note>original: "敲槌" (Qiaochui)</note>. If there is a single hole in the middle but both ends are rounded, it is called a "Cross-Drilled" pearl; these are also **not up to standard**.

### On Inspecting Pearls in the Box

To inspect the quality of pearls kept in a box, one must use silk cloth dipped in water to rub the surface. If the silk does not turn blue, the color is **authentic**. Those with fraudulent colors are often rubbed with rolls of high-quality blue paper to give the pearls a false blue tint. 

There are also pearls with a "bony" or oily yellow color. For these, fakers use rolls of bamboo paper with <term>Shao Powder</term> <note>original: "韶粉" (Shaofen). A white pigment made from lead, historically used in cosmetics and for whitening objects.</note> inside to rub the pearls, artificially brightening them.

**Gold flakes** are produced in Yizhou <note>modern-day Sichuan</note>. **Silver flakes** are produced in Yongchang <note>modern-day Baoshan, Yunnan</note>. Master Tao the Recluse <note>original: 陶隱居 (Tao Yinju). This refers to Tao Hongjing (456–536 CE), a renowned Daoist polymath and alchemist who categorized minerals for early Chinese medicine.</note> notes that while gold occurs in many places, the three provinces of Liang, Yi, and Ning are particularly abundant in it. It is found in the sand of riverbeds in the form of fine flakes; this is known as **raw gold**.

Silver is found in the same regions as gold, but it is always born within stone. ...**wrought iron** <note>original: shu tie, literally "ripe iron"</note>, also known as *li*-iron. When pig iron and soft iron are combined and forged to create the sharp cutting edges of knives and swords, the resulting metal is known as **steel** <note>original: gang tie</note>.

If one takes various pieces of iron, places them in a vessel, and soaks them in water for a long duration until the liquid turns green and a foam appears, it is called **iron liquor** <note>original: tie jiang</note>; this fluid is used for dyeing materials a deep black. There are specific methods for producing iron bloom powder, but the instructions are too lengthy to be fully recorded here. Generally, various forms of iron are not added directly into medicinal pills or powders; instead, only the liquid produced by boiling them is used. However, bloom powder is an exception: it should be processed by grinding it until it is extremely fine <note>original: yan zhi</note> before being blended with other medicines. 

Furthermore, iron objects such as horse bits, steelyard weights, cart linchpins, and bamboo-cutting saws are often heated until glowing and then quenched in wine for medicinal use. Even the water used for sharpening the blades of knives and axes is utilized as a remedy. These methods have all proven effective when applied according to the proper standards, and thus they are recorded here.

Blue Jadeite is produced in the marshlands of the Shu Commandery <note>modern-day Sichuan province</note>. Although it is similar to coral, descriptions vary so much that no one can identify it with absolute certainty.

We respectfully note that the "Tribute of Yu" chapter in the *Book of Documents* <note>one of the Five Classics of ancient Chinese literature</note> mentions that the tribute from Yongzhou included *qiulin* <note>a type of fine jade</note> and Jadeite. ## Cinnabar <term>Dansha: naturally occurring mercury sulfide, a heavy red mineral used in traditional medicine, as a pigment, and as a primary ingredient in Daoist alchemy.</term>

Cinnabar is produced in Chenzhou, Yizhou, and Jiezhou. Those specimens from Chenzhou <note>modern-day Huaihua, Hunan province</note> are considered the finest and are specifically called **Chenzhou Cinnabar** <term>Chensha</term>. It grows within the rocky cliffs of deep mountains. Local people gather it by burrowing dozens of feet into the earth. They first encounter what they call its "seedlings" <note>original: 苗 (miao); indicator minerals that signal the proximity of a larger deposit</note>, which are simply white stones.

These are known as the **Cinnabar Bed** <term>Zhusha chuang; the matrix or host rock in which the crystals are embedded</term>. The cinnabar grows upon these stones. The larger pieces are shaped like **hen's eggs**, while the smaller ones resemble **pomegranate seeds**. In form, they may appear like "hibiscus heads" <term>Furong tou</term> or "arrowheads" <term>Jianzhu</term>. Those that remain attached to the stone "bed" are a dark, dusky purple—almost the color of iron—yet they are brilliant and translucent. When broken apart, the fragments are jagged and sharp <term>Zhanyan</term>.

# Hollow Blue <term>Kongqing</term>

**Hollow Blue** <note>Identified by modern mineralogists as nodular azurite, a copper carbonate mineral known for its vibrant deep blue color</note> is produced in the mountain valleys of **Yizhou** <note>Modern-day Sichuan province</note> and the mountains of **Yuexi**, specifically in locations where copper is present. There are also secondary grades: larger specimens are about the size of a goose egg, while smaller ones are the size of beans. They are harvested at no specific time of year.

# Mercury and Cinnabar

**Mercury** <term>Shuigyin</term> <note>literally "Water-silver"; the liquid metal known in the West as quicksilver</note> is produced in the flat lands of Fuling. Nowadays, it is sourced from Qinzhou, Shangzhou, Daozhou, and the Shaowu Prefecture. The mercury from Qinzhou actually originates from the border regions of the **Western Qiang** <note>a historical ethnic group inhabiting the western frontiers of China</note>.

The Classics <note>referring to ancient pharmacological or alchemical texts like the *Shennong Bencaojing*</note> state: That which is derived from **Cinnabar** <term>Dansha</term> <note>the primary ore of mercury, valued both as a pigment and for its alchemical properties</note> is obtained by harvesting coarse or secondary-grade cinnabar found within mountain rocks. A **furnace** <term>lu</term> is constructed, and the cinnabar is placed inside. Below, it is supported by a vessel of water to receive the essence, while the top is covered with a basin-like apparatus. Fire is then applied to the exterior for **calcination and refinement** <term>duanyang</term> <note>a metallurgical process involving high heat to extract metal from ore</note>. 

During this process, the vapor flies upward, and the mercury trickles down into the water below. Its natural color is white and somewhat opaque.

# Native Copper

**Native Copper** <term>Ziran tong</term> <note>literally "Natural Copper." While the name suggests pure metallic copper, in historical Chinese pharmacy, this term primarily referred to iron pyrite or "fool's gold" (FeS2)</note> is produced within the mountain cliffs of **Yongzhou** <note>a historical prefecture centered in modern-day Nanning, Guangxi</note>, in the same areas where copper is found. Today, it is also found in **Xinzhou** <note>modern-day Shangrao, Jiangxi</note> and **Huoshanjun** <note>a historical administrative region in modern-day Shanxi</note>. 

It is collected from within copper pits and from the crevices between rocks. Its shape is irregular—sometimes appearing square and sometimes round—and its color is a greenish-yellow, much like refined copper. Because it is obtained directly from the earth without requiring **smelting or refining** <term>zuanlian</term> <note>the process of extracting metal from ore using heat and bellows</note>, it is called "Native Copper."

Currently, Xinzhou produces a variety that resembles a mass of tangled copper wires. It is said that within the copper mines, the "mountain vapors" <note>the subtle elemental energies believed to form minerals</note> steam and simmer, causing the mineral to flow out naturally. This variety is similar to **Native Silver** <term>Shengyin</term>, which can take on a form resembling an **old man's beard** <note>a description of dendritic or acicular crystal growth</note>. This filamentous variety is considered the highest quality for use in medicine.

# Jade

# Jade Fragments

**Jade** <term>Yu</term> originates in the mountain valleys of **Lantian** <note>a mountain range southeast of Xi'an, Shaanxi province, historically famous for producing a specific type of nephrite</note>. **Jade fragments** <term>Yuxie</term> also originate in Lantian. 

**Tao Yinju** <note>Tao Hongjing (456–536 CE), a renowned Daoist polymath and physician who significantly organized the Chinese pharmacopeia</note> states in his commentary: 
"Fine jade is produced in Lantian as well as within the administrative borders of **Xushanting** in **Nanyang** <note>modern-day Henan province</note>, and in the waters of **Lurong** in **Rinan** <note>a historical commandery located in what is now central Vietnam</note>. Foreign regions such as **Khotan** <term>Yutian</term> and **Kashgar** <term>Shule</term> <note>both major centers of jade trade on the ancient Silk Road in modern-day Xinjiang</note> also produce excellent quality jade."

Today, however, one no longer hears of jade being found in Lantian, Nanyang, or Rinan. Currently, the only notable source is the **Jade River** located outside the city of **Khotan**. Its source emerges from the **Kunlun** <gloss>mountains</gloss> <note>a legendary and geographic mountain range in Central Asia, long considered the primary source of the finest 'mutton-fat' nephrite jade</note>. The river flows westward for one thousand three hundred *li* <note>a traditional unit of distance, roughly 500 meters or 1/3 of a mile</note> until it reaches **Niutou Mountain** <note>literally "Ox-Head Mountain"</note> at the border of Khotan, where the flow branches into three distinct streams.

# Volume 8: Precious Treasures I

There are three rivers [in the region of Khotan]: The first is called the **White Jade River** <note>Yurungkash</note>, located thirty *li* <note>approximately 15 kilometers</note> east of the city. The second is the **Green Jade River** <note>Karakash</note>, twenty *li* west of the city. The third is the **Black Jade River**, located seven *li* west of the Green Jade River. Although these rivers share a single source, the jade changes in character according to the ground it passes through; therefore, the colors are not the same. 

Every year during the fifth and sixth months <note>early summer</note>, the waters rise in great floods, and the jade is carried down by the current. The quantity of jade found depends on the volume of the water. When the waters recede in the seventh or eighth month, the jade can then be collected. The local people refer to this practice as **salvaging jade** <term>laoyu</term>. 

According to the laws of that kingdom <note>Khotan</note>, until the government officials have finished their jade harvest, commoners are strictly forbidden from approaching the riverbanks. Consequently, the daily utensils and clothing within that country are frequently adorned with jade. Most of the jade currently found in China comes from this region.

**Tao Yinju** <note>Tao Hongjing (456–536 CE), a famous Daoist scholar and physician</note> states: "**Jade Spring** <term>Yuquan</term> is the very essence of jade. The white variety is clear and translucent in quality and can be dissolved into a liquid; hence the name 'Jade Spring.' People of the present age no longer possess a precise understanding of this substance; they simply refer to all varieties generally as 'Jade.'"

**Jade Fragments** <term>Yuxie</term> are simply jade that has been ground into a powder; they should not be considered a separate or distinct material.

# Malachite <term>Shilu</term>

**Green-blue mineral** <note>original: *luqing*</note> is what we now call **Malachite** <term>shilu</term>. ## Feldspar <term>Changshi</term>

**Feldspar** <note>literally "Long Stone"</note> is produced in the mountain valleys of **Zhangzi** <note>located in modern-day Shanxi province</note>, as well as **Mount Tai** and **Linzi** <note>in modern-day Shandong</note>. Today, it is primarily found in **Luzhou** <note>a historical region in modern Shanxi</note>. Its internal patterns resemble **horse teeth**; it is rectangular, smooth, lustrous, and has the color of fine jade. This stone is quite similar to **gypsum** <term>shigao</term>, but it is distinguished by being thicker, larger, and having a long, longitudinal grain. It can be mined at any time.

Respectfully according to the **Classic of Materia Medica** <note>the *Shennong Bencao Jing*, China's oldest pharmaceutical text</note>, **Striped Stone** <term>lishi</term> and **Feldspar** <term>changshi</term> were listed in the same entry, yet their flavors and medicinal effects are actually different. 

The text also states that **Striped Stone** <note>original: *lishi*</note> resembles gypsum, having a fine and straight grain. **Tao Yinju** <note>Tao Hongjing, a famous 5th-century alchemist and physician</note> says that Striped Stone is also called "Long-grain Stone." **Su Gong** <note>a Tang dynasty scholar-official and pharmacologist</note> notes that Striped Stone has a yellow-red outer skin and white "flesh" within, featuring a needle-like grain that does not actually resemble gypsum.

## Water-Cave Realgar <term>Shuiku Xionghuang</term>

**Realgar** <note>an arsenic sulfide mineral known for its bright orange-red color</note> is produced in the mountain valleys of **Wudu** <note>a region in modern Gansu province</note> and on the sunny southern slopes of **Mount Dunhuang**. Today, it is also found within the mountains of **Jiezhou** <note>also in modern Gansu</note>. It forms in clumps resembling **cinnabar** <term>dansha</term>; it is clear and translucent, not mixed with ordinary stone.

The foul odor can be eliminated by washing the mineral with vinegar. <gloss>The scent is enough to confuse it with the real thing, so one must distinguish it with great care.</gloss>

# Orpiment <term>Cihuang</term>

**Orpiment** <note>a bright yellow arsenic sulfide mineral, historically used as a pigment and in medicine</note> is produced in the mountain valleys of **Wudu** <note>a region in modern Gansu province</note>. It is found in the same mountains as **Realgar** <term>Xionghuang</term>. On the shady northern slopes of these mountains, gold is present; it is said that when the "essence of gold" <note>an alchemical vapor or vital energy</note> fumes, it gives rise to **Orpiment** <note>original OCR: "Realgar," likely a scribal error or referring to the pair's shared origin; the context of this entry is Orpiment</note>.

The finest quality specimens are those with a color like gold and a physical structure resembling **mica** <term>yunmu</term>, consisting of overlapping layers that can be easily peeled or separated. Those that are mixed with common stone or are as black as the color of iron are unfit for use. 

Some say that a single piece weighing four **taels** <note>a traditional unit of weight, approx. 150 grams</note> can be split into a thousand layers; such specimens are exceptionally rare and excellent. There is no specific season for its collection.

**Mica** <note>literally "cloud mother," a silicate mineral known for its layered, shimmering structure</note> is produced in the mountain valleys of **Mount Tai** <note>one of the Sacred Mountains of China, located in modern Shandong</note>, **Mount Lu** in Qi, and among the rocks of **North Ding Mountain** in **Langya**. # Stalactite <term>Shizhongru</term>

**Stalactites** <note>literally "stone bell milk," so named because they hang like bells and "milk" or mineral-rich water drips from them</note> are produced in the mountain valleys of **Mount Shaoshi** <note>located in modern Henan, the western peak of the sacred Mount Song</note> and **Mount Tai**. Today, they are also found in the mountains of **Daozhou**, **Tinghua County**, as well as **Lian**, **Ying**, **Shao**, **Jie**, and **Xiazhou**. 

They form in the shaded recesses of rocky caves, created by the dripping of mineral-rich mountain fluids. They are hollow through the center. The long ones reach six or seven inches and are shaped like the shaft of a **goose feather**. 

When crushed, the fragments resemble fingernail clippings. Those that are bright and translucent, with no **goose teeth** <note>original: "鴈齒" (yanchi); refers to jagged, coarse crystalline structures inside the hollow tube which were considered undesirable for medicinal use</note> inside, are of the highest quality. 

Their color is white with a subtle reddish tint. They may be harvested at any time. Older traditions state that there are three types of these "milk" minerals; where true stalactites are found, the mountain itself is said to be half-rock.

# Saltpeter <term>Xiaoshi</term> <note>Historically referred to as Nitre or potassium nitrate, though often confused with sodium sulfates in early texts</note>

**Crude Mirabilite** <term>Puxiao</term> <note>Sodium sulfate glauberite</note> and **Saltpeter** are produced in the mountain valleys of **Yizhou** <note>modern Sichuan</note>, as well as in **Wudu**, **Longxi**, and the **Western Qiang** regions <note>areas in modern Gansu and surrounding provinces</note>. **Glauber’s Salt** <term>Mangxiao</term> <note>refined sodium sulfate</note> is produced from Crude Mirabilite.

Today, these substances are found in both the North and the South, but those from **Xichuan** <note>Western Sichuan</note> are considered to be of the highest quality. Older accounts state that these three substances <note>Crude Mirabilite, Saltpeter, and Glauber's Salt</note> are of the same species. When first harvested, the "sprouts" <note>the initial mineral efflorescence or crystals found on the earth</note> are rinsed with water to extract their essence. This liquid is then boiled and refined to create **Crude Mirabilite**. It is also known by the name "Saltpeter Husk" <note>original: *Xiaoshipu*</note> because the refined Saltpeter is extracted from within it. Furthermore, when Crude Mirabilite or "earth-frost" <note>mineral crusts that form on the surface of the soil</note> is further refined until it becomes hard and white like stone, it is then called **Saltpeter**.

# Yu’s Surplus Grain <term>Yu Yu Liang</term>

**Yu’s Surplus Grain** <term>Yu Yu Liang</term> <note>identified by modern mineralogists as Limonite or brown hematite; legend says the mythical ruler Yu the Great threw his leftover rations into the water, where they petrified into these stones</note> originates in the ponds and marshes of the **East Sea** and among mountain islands. # White Quartz <term>Bai Shi Ying</term>

**White Quartz** <term>Bai Shi Ying</term> <note>identified as rock crystal or translucent quartz</note> produced in **Zezhou** <note>the same region in modern Shanxi province mentioned on the previous page</note> is considered the finest. Those that are long, white, lustrous, clear, and transparent with a brilliant shine are superior. Specimens with **six faces** <note>referring to the natural hexagonal prism shape of quartz crystals</note> that appear as if they were cleanly carved are suitable for use.

*   Those with a **golden-yellow** color at the tip are named **Yellow Quartz** <term>Huang Shi Ying</term>.

## Purple Fluorite <term>Zi Shi Ying</term>

**Purple Fluorite** <note>traditionally identified as fluorite, though sometimes associated with amethyst; it was used in "Five Stone Powder" for its warming properties</note> is produced in the mountain valleys of **Mount Tai**. Today, it is also found in the regions of **Lingnan** <note>the lands south of the Nanling Mountains</note> and the **Huiji Mountains**.

According to the **Materia Medica of Wu Pu** <note>an early pharmacological text from the 3rd century AD</note>, Purple Fluorite is produced in Mount Tai and Huiji. One should seek specimens that appear as if they were cleanly carved, with a deep purple color extending all the way to the tip. In shape, they should resemble a **Chupu** <note>an ancient game piece or die, suggesting a specific polyhedral crystal habit</note.

**Tao Yinju** <note>the honorary title of Tao Hongjing (456–536 AD), a renowned Taoist and physician who organized the pharmacopeia</note> states: "Stones from Mount Tai with a deep, clear color and a 'root' <note>the point of attachment to the matrix</note> at the base are the finest. Stones from Huiji, which resemble **pomegranate seeds** in both shape and color, are of the lowest quality."

In earlier times, these different varieties were used interchangeably, but currently, only the stones from Mount Tai are employed in practice.

## Gypsum
**Gypsum** <note>original: *Shigao*</note> originates in the valleys of **Mount Qi**, as well as the **Lu Mountains** of the Qi region and **Mount Meng** in the Lu region <note>these areas correspond to modern-day Shandong province</note>. Nowadays, it is also found in the prefectures of **Fen**, **Meng**, **Guo**, and **Yao**, as well as in **Yuan Prefecture**.

It forms upon mountain rocks. Its color is a brilliant, translucent white. Any specimens that are yellow in color are considered unsuitable <gloss>to be included in medical prescriptions</gloss>.

**Calcite** <term>Jieshi; a carbonate mineral often confused with gypsum in antiquity</term> was formerly classified as a "lower-grade" substance in the classic texts. **The Classic of Materia Medica** <note>original: *Benjing*; referring to the *Shennong Bencao Jing*, the foundational text of Chinese pharmacology</note> states that it originates on **Mount Fang**.

**Tao the Hermit** <note>original: *Tao Yinju*; the famous 5th-century scholar and alchemist Tao Hongjing</note> believed that Calcite was the same as **Feldspar** <note>original: *Changshi*</note>, which is also known as **Square Stone** <note>original: *Fangshi*</note>. Because their therapeutic effects on the body are so similar, he suspected they were actually the same substance.

However, the scholar **Su Gong** <note>a Tang dynasty official and editor of the Revised Materia Medica</note> stated that while Calcite's ability to treat internal heat is no less effective than <gloss>Gypsum—and if this is true, they might seem interchangeable—</gloss>it is not as effective as Gypsum for treating **Head-Wind** <term>Toufeng; a traditional term for chronic headaches, migraines, or dizziness caused by external "pathogenic wind"</term>.

# Magnetite (Lodestone)

**Magnetite** <term>Cishi; literally "the stone that attracts like a mother"</term> originates in the mountain valleys of **Mount Tai** <note>one of the Five Great Mountains of China, located in Shandong</note> and on the northern, shaded slopes of **Mount Ci** <note>Magnet Mountain</note> in areas where iron is found. Those specimens sent from **Ci Prefecture** as **annual tribute** <term>Suigong; high-quality goods delivered to the imperial court</term> are considered the finest.

The stones are capable of attracting iron, suspending a dozen needles in a continuous chain through the air. The most authentic specimens can even lift **knives and iron tools** <note>original: *Daoqi*</note> weighing one or two catties <note>a traditional unit of weight, approximately 1.3 pounds or 600 grams</note> and spin them around without the objects falling.

Inside the stone, there are small pores, and within these pores, the color is a yellowish-red. Upon the surface of the stone, there are fine hairs <note>this refers to the magnetic "fuzz" created when small iron particles or filings adhere to a strong natural magnet</note>. Its **nature is warm** <term>Xingwen; a pharmacological property in traditional medicine associated with restored circulation</term>, and its medicinal efficacy is considered superior.

### Black Stone (Xuanshi)

**Black Stone** <term>Xuanshi; likely a form of weakly magnetic magnetite or hematite</term> originates on the **Yang** <term>the southern, sunny side</term> of **Mount Tai** <note>one of the Five Sacred Mountains of China</note>. On the **Yin** <term>the northern, shaded side</term> of the mountain, there is copper. Within this mineral context, copper is regarded as **female** and iron as **male**. This stone is primarily used to treat restlessness and agitation. 

Its properties are quite similar [to magnetite], though they vary between **cold and warm** <note>referring to the stone's perceived "temperature" in traditional pharmacology</note>. Both the copper-associated and iron-associated varieties resemble **magnetite**; however, those specimens that lack internal pores and possess a pure, polished black luster are inferior in medicinal efficacy to true magnetite. Furthermore, they lack the power to **suspend needles** <note>a classic test for the strength of a lodestone</note>. 

Currently, the **Northern Tribes** <term>Beifan; historically referring to non-Han peoples to the north and west of China</term> offer "magnetite" as gifts, but those pieces are often highly lustrous while having almost no strength to attract needles. It is suspected that these are actually this **Black Stone**. Consequently, it is rarely used in medical prescriptions.

### Frozen-water Stone (Ningshuishi)

**Frozen-water Stone** <term>Ningshuishi; likely a form of calcite or gypsum</term> is the same substance as **Cold-water Stone** <term>Hanshuishi</term>. It originates in the mountain valleys of **Changshan** <note>modern-day Hebei Province</note> and is also produced in **Zhongshui County** and **Handan**. 

Nowadays, it is also found in the **Fen** and **Xi** prefectures of **Hedong** <note>modern-day Shanxi Province</note>, as well as within the territory of the **Deshun Army** <note>modern-day southern Ningxia</note>. 

There are varieties with a **vertical grain** <note>original: 縱理 (zongli), referring to the crystalline cleavage or striations</note> and those with a **horizontal grain**. When placed in water, it takes on the same color as the water itself <note>becoming nearly invisible due to its transparency</note>. Those specimens that cause the water to congeal into **slush** <term>si; ice crystals or icy fragments</term> are considered to be of the highest quality. 

Some claim that the variety with vertical grains is specifically **Cold-water Stone**, while the variety with horizontal grains is **Frozen-water Stone**.

# Fossilized Crab (Shihe)

**Fossilized Crabs** <term>Shihe; literally "Stone Crab"</term> originate in the **South Seas** <note>referring to the coastal waters of southern China</note>. Today, they are found throughout the coastal prefectures and districts of the **Lingnan** region <note>modern-day Guangdong and Guangxi provinces</note>.

While its physical substance is stone, it is in every way identical to a **sea crab**. Some suggest that these are actual crabs that, after many years of being encrusted and permeated by sea foam, have been transformed into stone. They are often cast ashore by the wind during surging tides, where they are gathered by locals. 

There is another variety found deep within mountain caves; those that have remained in such environments for a great length of time also undergo this transformation into stone. 

Regarding its medicinal use, when ground with vinegar and applied topically, it treats **swellings and carbuncles** <term>zhongyong; inflammatory skin lesions or abscesses</term>. It is also traditionally said to neutralize **poisoning from mineral medicines** <term>jinshidu; toxicity resulting from the ingestion of alchemical or metallic substances</term>. They may be collected at any time of year.

### Litharge

**Litharge** <term>Mituoseng; lead monoxide (PbO). The name is a phonetic transcription of the Persian word *mirdāsang*</term> originates in **Persia** <note>original: "Bosiguo"</note>. Today, it is also found in the **Lingnan** region <note>modern Guangdong and Guangxi</note> and central **Fujian** <note>original: "Minzhong"</note>. It is the byproduct of silver and lead smelting <term>yinqianjiao; literally "silver-lead feet" or residue</term>. 

When the ore is first mined, the silver and copper are mixed together. First, lead is added and the mixture is smelted; the silver then emerges along with the lead. 

Next, a furnace is dug into the ground and filled with ash, which is called an **ash pit** <term>huichi; a hearth used in cupellation, a process for separating precious metals</term>. The silver-lead mixture is placed upon the ash and subjected to intense, high-heat forging. The lead seeps down into the ash, while the silver is left resting on top of the ash. Once the fire is extinguished and the silver is removed after cooling, the ash pit—having absorbed the essences of the lead and silver over a long period of accumulation—is transformed into this substance.

# Stone Snake

**Stone Snakes** <term>shishe; traditionally identified by modern scholars as fossilized ammonites or coiled mollusk shells</term> originate from the **South Sea** <note>original: "南海" (Nanhai); referring to the coastal areas of southern China</note>, found among the rocks and mountains along the water’s edge. Their shape is coiled and winding like a snake, yet they possess no head or tail and are hollow on the inside. 

They are reddish-purple in color and also resemble **spiral shells** <term>chelue; literally "cart-wheel shells," likely referring to large gastropods</term>. They may be harvested at any time. The flavor is **salty**, its nature is **neutral** <note>referring to its balanced effect on the body's internal temperature</note>, and it is **non-toxic**.

## Alum Stone

**Alum Stone** <term>fanshi; a mineral salt, typically potassium aluminum sulfate, used historically in medicine, dyeing, and alchemy</term> is produced in the mountain valleys of **Hanzhong** <note>original: "漢中"; located in modern-day Shaanxi province</note> and **Shaoshi** <note>original: "少室"; a mountain in Henan province, part of the Songshan range</note>. When using this for treatment, one must obtain the genuine stone for it to be effective.

Ginger Stone is found nestled between layers of earth and rock. Those found to the east of **Licheng** in **Qizhou** <note>modern-day Jinan in Shandong Province</note> are of superior quality. While it can be found in various locations, it is currently primarily sourced from Qizhou.

# Flower-Milk Stone
<note>original: "花乳石"; Huarushi. In traditional Chinese medicine, this is typically identified as ophicalcite or serpentinite, a metamorphic rock often featuring white or colorful "flower" patterns against a darker base.</note>

Flower-Milk Stone is produced in **Wenxiang County** of **Shanzhou** <note>modern-day Lingbao in Henan Province</note>. Its substance is extremely hard and heavy, and its color is similar to **sulfur**. It occurs in blocks, some of which are found in very large sizes. People often use these stones to carve **vessels and utensils** <term>zhuoqi; the process of cutting, polishing, and refining stone into functional or decorative objects</term>.

This stone was not used in ancient medical formulas. In recent times, however, it has been combined with sulfur and **calcined** <term>duan; a pharmaceutical technique of heating minerals to high temperatures to make them brittle and chemically active</term> together. When ground into a fine powder and applied to **incised wounds** <term>jinchuang; literally "gold sores," referring to wounds inflicted by metal weapons like knives, swords, or arrows</term>, its effectiveness is truly miraculous.

Additionally, if a person is suddenly struck by a metal blade and there is no time to prepare the calcined mixture, one may simply scrape the surface of the stone to obtain a fine powder. Applying this raw powder to the wound is also effective. The stone may be harvested at any time of year.

<note>original: "不灰木"; Buhuimu. This substance is typically identified as fibrous asbestos or actinolite. It earned its name because its mineral fibers resemble wood grain but do not burn away in fire.</note>
