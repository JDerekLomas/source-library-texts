### Proclaimed Orders, Part 1
<note>original: 懸令 (Xuanling). These are the formal commands, military laws, and codes of conduct that were publicly posted or announced to the troops to ensure absolute discipline.</note>

Master Mao <note>referring to the author, Mao Yuanyi, using a respectful title</note> says: There are two principles regarding proclaimed orders. # Prohibitions

The *Essentials for the Marching Army* <note>original: 行軍須知 (Xingjun Xuzhi), a manual of military logistics and conduct</note> states, and the *Art of War* <note>original: 兵法 (Bingfa), a general reference to classical military strategy</note> also says: "Use rewards as the exterior, and law as the interior." It further says: "Command them through civility <note>original: 文 (wen); lead through education and moral example</note> and unify them through martial discipline <note>original: 武 (wu); lead through strength and regulation</note>; this is what is called 'certain victory.'" 

In commanding an army and leading the masses, if rewards are not clearly distributed, then the officers and soldiers will not exert their full effort; if punishments are not strictly enforced, then the soldiers will not maintain order. Therefore, it is said: "Rewards are the *Yang* <term>Yang: the active, bright, or positive principle in traditional Chinese philosophy; here it refers to the open, visible encouragement of merit</term>..."

<note>The text concludes mid-sentence, transitioning into the philosophical dualism of rewards and punishments as the 'Yang' and 'Yin' of military governance.</note>

...and punishments are the *Yin*. When rewards and punishments are balanced, then *Yin* and *Yang* are in harmony. When *Yin* and *Yang* are in harmony, the cycles of cold and heat do not err. When the seasons do not err, then the ten thousand things <term>Ten thousand things: a traditional Chinese expression (wanwu) referring to the entirety of creation</term> flourish in their proper time. 

In military matters, the highest value is placed on harmony between superiors and subordinates. When superiors and subordinates are in harmony, the Three Armies <term>Three Armies: a classical term for the entirety of a large military force, traditionally organized into center, left, and right divisions</term> act as one. When the Three Armies act as one, they are of one heart when facing battle. When they are of one heart, victory is certain. Therefore, Sun Wu and Rangju <note>Sun Wu is the personal name of Sun Tzu, the author of *The Art of War*; Rangju refers to Sima Rangju, another foundational military strategist of the Spring and Autumn period</note> taught that when rewards and punishments are clear, one's prestige and power are imposed upon enemy states. 

Furthermore, the Marquis of Wu <note>original: 諸葛武侯 (Zhuge Wuhou); Zhuge Liang, the most famous strategist of the Three Kingdoms period</note> said that there are seven types of prohibited conduct:

The first is called **Lightness toward the Army** <term>Lightness: referring to a lack of gravity or treating military duty with casual disregard</term>. This refers to: failing to arrive at the appointed time or meeting; hearing the drum but not advancing; taking advantage of laxity to linger behind; shirking duties while focusing on pleasing superiors; moving close initially but then distancing oneself again; not answering when one’s name is called; and having incomplete armor or weapons.

The second is called **Arrogance toward the Army**. This refers to: receiving orders but not passing them on; passing them on without accuracy, thereby confusing the officers and officials; not listening for the gongs and drums; and not watching the banners and flags.

The third is called **Thievery toward the Army**. This refers to: soldiers not receiving their grain rations; the army not being organized into proper units; distributing granted wealth unevenly; showing favoritism to those close to oneself; taking items that do not belong to one; and borrowing without returning...

In the military, unless the instruction comes from the **General-in-Chief** or a **Lieutenant General** <term>fujiang; a high-ranking officer serving directly under the commander-in-chief</term>, anyone who arbitrarily issues orders or changes the **banners** <term>jingqi; flags used for visual signaling</term> and **military signals** shall be severely punished. ### Fifth Prohibition: Against Discord

Whenever generals, officers, sentry leaders <term>shaoguan; an officer in charge of a "sentry" unit, roughly equivalent to a company</term>, or squad leaders fail to work in harmony, undermining or envying one another and thereby causing military matters to fail, they shall be dealt with according to **military law** <note>original: "junjia" (軍法); in the Ming military context, this usually implies severe corporal punishment or execution</note>. When deliberating on military strategy, the goal must be fairness and reaching an immediate decision. Those who violate this or remain stubborn and obstinate shall be punished.

### Sixth Prohibition: Against Evil Delusions

Those who spread rumors to deceive others, or who speak recklessly of **yin and yang** <note>here referring to occult arts and unauthorized divination</note>, divination, Daoism, Buddhism, ghosts, spirits, omens, or fortunes, thereby unsettling the minds of the masses, shall be severely punished. # 12. Clarifying Accountability

The regulations regarding **Accountability** <term>zecheng; the formal assignment of duty and the legal liability for its failure</term> apply regardless of whether it is a time of peace or during an active engagement on the battlefield.

In minor matters, this concerns the violation of any commands or the committing of crimes and legal infractions. In major matters, it concerns retreating from the enemy or causing the failure of military operations. The system of collective responsibility is as follows:

*   For every **two soldiers** [who offend], the **Squad Leader** <term>duizong; commander of a ten-man unit</term> must be held liable alongside them.
*   For every **two squads**, the **Ensign** <term>qizong; a "flag leader" commanding fifty men</term> must be held liable.
*   For every **two ensigncies**, the **Centurion** <term>baizong; commander of one hundred men</term> must be held liable.
*   For every **two bureaus** <term>ju; a unit of approximately 400-500 men</term>, the **Lieutenant** <term>bazong</term> must be held liable.
*   For every **two companies** <term>si; a unit of approximately 1,000 men</term>, the **Sub-Lieutenant** <term>qianzong</term> must be held liable.
*   For every **two battalions** <term>bu</term>, the case must be brought for deliberation regarding the **Sentinel Commander** <term>shaojiang</term>.

If a soldier or officer **reports or denounces the crime first**, they shall be **exempt from punishment** <term>mianzuo; a legal exemption from the shared penalty of a group</term>. In matters of **rewards**, the same hierarchical logic is applied. However, it should be noted: when it is said "from two squads up to two battalions," it does not mean that every single individual within those units has committed the crime; it refers to the cascading responsibility of the officers in charge.
