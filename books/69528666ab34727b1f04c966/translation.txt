# THE ORIGIN OF LETTERS AND NUMERALS ACCORDING TO THE <term>BOOK OF FORMATION</term> <note>original: "SEFER YETZIRAH"</note>

### BY PHINEAS MORDELL

There is hardly another book in Jewish literature, the Bible and the <term>Talmud</term> <note>The central text of Rabbinic Judaism, comprised of legal and ethical discussions; the name literally means "study" or "learning."</note> excepted, that has been so much commented upon as the <term>Book of Formation</term> <note>original: "Sefer Yetzirah"; this is an ancient and foundational text of Jewish mysticism which describes the creation of the world through the power of Hebrew letters and numbers.</note>. It has been the subject of deep study, not only to the <term>mystic</term> <note>In this context, a person who seeks to understand the hidden or spiritual nature of reality through traditional secret knowledge.</note>, who regarded it as the source of <term>esoteric lore</term> <note>Specialized, secret knowledge intended for a small group of initiated students.</note>, but also to the philosopher and the <term>Talmudist</term> <note>A scholar specializing in the laws and traditions found in the Talmud.</note>. And yet, despite all the efforts of a large number of scholars of repute for more than a thousand years, the <term>Book of Formation</term> remains a sealed book <note>The term "sealed book" indicates that the text is considered undecipherable or its true meaning remains hidden despite many attempts to explain it.</note>. The various commentaries upon it are more apt to bewilder the student than to enlighten him. Indeed, it would seem that every commentator endeavored to read his own views and theories into this little book, with hardly any concern whether they agreed with the text or not.

There is no book in Jewish literature that is so difficult to understand as the <term>Book of Formation</term>. For it was originally written in an obscure half-mystical style. To make matters worse, the commentators of the eighth or of the ninth century blended the original "Book of Formation" with an early commentary, which may be referred to as "<term>Book of Formation II</term>" <note>original: "Sefer Yetzirah II"</note>. Although the <term>Book of Formation</term> is exceedingly hard to understand, the solution of its many difficulties is not impossible. The reason why they have remained so long an unsolved problem is partly due to a lack of knowledge of Hebrew <term>orthography</term> <note>The standardized system of spelling and writing a language.</note>, on which the <term>Book of Formation</term> is based. The Hebrew <term>grammarians</term> <note>Scholars who study the rules and structure of language.</note> under Arabic influence came to believe that those rules of orthography which the Arab grammarians invented for the language of the <term>Quran</term> <note>original: "Koran"; the central religious text of Islam.</note> hold good also for the language of the Old Testament. When the Honorable <term>Mayer Sulzberger</term> <note>A prominent American judge and scholar (1843–1923) who was a major supporter of Jewish cultural institutions.</note> heard me expressing my views on Hebrew orthography, he advised me to make a study of the <term>Book of Formation</term> which in his opinion constitutes the earliest Hebrew grammar extant. Finding that my views on Hebrew orthography harmonized with those of the <term>Book of Formation</term>, I made an exhaustive study of it. After many years of study, I reached the conclusion that the <term>Book of Formation</term>, as the earliest Hebrew grammar, contains not only the fundamental rules of Hebrew orthography, but also an account of the origin of letters and numerals. This account it is my present purpose to set forth¹).

The first <term>Mishnah</term> <note>The basic unit of text in the Book of Formation, similar to a verse or paragraph.</note> reads as follows: <note>original Hebrew: "sheloshim u-shtayim netivot peli’ot hakak Yah YHVH Tzeva’ot be-sofer ve-sippur ve-sefer"</note>

> “Thirty-two mysterious ways has the Lord, the Lord of hosts, ordained through Scribe, Script and Scroll.“

The thirty-two mysterious ways are the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet, which represent thirty-two sounds.²)

In accordance with the belief of the ancients that the letters are of divine origin, the <term>Book of Formation</term> <note>original: "Sefer Yetzirah"</note> explains that the thirty-two ways of wisdom were ordained by God through:

(1) <term>Scribe</term> <note>original Hebrew: "sofer"</note>, the man whom God inspired to invent the alphabet;

(2) <term>Script</term> <note>original Hebrew: "sippur"</note>, the letters;

(3) <term>Scroll</term> <note>original Hebrew: "sefer"</note>, the material on which the letters were displayed.

In order to show how the twenty-two letters of the alphabet constitute the thirty-two ways of wisdom, the author proceeds to the division of the letters in the second and following Mishnahs. He divides them into simple and double sounds, and also into vowels and consonants. The simple letters he called <term>simple</term> <note>original Hebrew: "peshutot"</note>, and the double, <term>double</term> <note>original Hebrew: "kefulot"</note>; the vowels, <term>mothers</term> <note>original Hebrew: "imot," also known as "avot" or fathers</note>, and the consonants, <term>generations</term> <note>original Hebrew: "toledot"</note>. Altogether they form thirty-two sounds: the twenty sounds of the ten³) double letters, and the twelve of the twelve simple letters.

The <term>Book of Formation</term>⁴) <note>original Hebrew: "Sefer Yetzirah"</note> emphasizes that the number of the double letters is no less and no more than ten, and the number of the simple letters no less and no more than twelve. The <term>Book of Formation</term> urges us to investigate and examine the letters, that we may have a clear insight into the subject. This proves that, at the time when the book was written, the nature of the letters, or of some of them, was misunderstood. We know, indeed, that at the time when the Greek translation of the Bible <note>The Septuagint, the earliest Greek translation of the Old Testament from the 3rd century BCE.</note> was made, it was believed that the **ע** <note>The letter Ayin.</note>, for example, could be transliterated by *e*, *a*, or *g*, and the translators accordingly rendered it variously by one of these three sounds.

Arguments have repeatedly been advanced in favor of the view that the Hebrew **ע** <note>Ayin</note> had not only the sound of the Arabic **ع** <note>Ain, a deep voiced pharyngeal sound.</note>, but also of the **غ** <note>Ghayn, a voiced velar sound similar to a French 'r'.</note>. But according to the <term>Book of Formation</term>, the **ע** is a simple letter. If it has the sound of the Arabic **ع** it is impossible that it should also have the sound of **غ**. Moreover, if the **ע** was originally a vowel only and had no sound of *g*, as maintained by <term>Jerome</term> <note>Saint Jerome (c. 347–420 AD), the scholar who translated the Bible into Latin (the Vulgate).</note>, it can have only one vowel sound. If we ascribe to it the sound of *A*, it is impossible that it should have also the sound of *E* or *O*, etc. Furthermore, according to the <term>Book of Formation</term>, the letters **ח**, **ט**, **צ** <note>Het, Tet, and Tsade.</note> are also simple letters, and each must have had only one sound and not two as in Arabic.

The author of the <term>Book of Formation</term> apparently cautioned against the very errors and mistakes into which all writers on Hebrew grammar have fallen. -># THE ORIGIN OF LETTERS AND NUMERALS.<-

The Hebrew alphabet originates from the alphabet known as Arabic, which the Arabs themselves used to call *sūri*. By *sūri*, the term apparently meant Assyrian. The so-called Arabic alphabet originally consisted of only seventeen letters. It was seemingly invented to represent the Assyrian-Babylonian language, which consisted of seventeen or eighteen sounds; hence the name “*sūri*.” When the Arabs—whose original alphabet was the <term>Himyarite</term> <note>An ancient South Arabian script used in what is now Yemen.</note>, consisting of twenty-eight letters—adopted the “*sūri*” alphabet, they assigned two or even three sounds to some “*sūri*” letters, and such letters are now counted as two or three distinct letters.
