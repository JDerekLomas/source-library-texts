## Yellow Mastic Tree
<note>Original: *Huangdongshu*. Known today as the Chinese Pistache (*Pistacia chinensis*), it is valued for its timber and as a source of edible young shoots.</note>

The *Herbal for Famine Relief* <note>Original: *Jiuhuang Bencao*, a famous 15th-century text by Prince Zhu Xiao designed to help people identify edible wild plants during food shortages.</note> states that the **Yellow Mastic Tree** grows in the wild throughout the southern mountains of **Zhengzhou**. The leaves resemble those of a newly sprouted **Chinese Toon tree** <term>Chunshu (*Toona sinensis*), a tree whose leaves are still a popular seasonal delicacy in China</term> but are significantly smaller. They also resemble the leaves of the **Chinaberry** <term>Lian (*Melia azedarach*)</term>, though their color has a slight yellow tint. 

The tree produces purplish-red flowers and bears seeds about the size of a **garden pea**. These seeds are green when young and turn purplish-red when they ripen. 

The leaves have a bitter taste. To prepare them, gather the tender buds and leaves and **blanch** <note>Original: *zha*, a technique of quick-boiling to soften and prepare wild greens.</note> them until cooked. Change the water and soak them to remove the bitter flavor, then season with oil and salt to eat. Alternatively, the buds can be steamed and then **sun-dried**; prepared this way, they can also be used to brew a tea for drinking.

### Chengya Tree
<note>Original: *Chengyashu*. Literally the "Weighing-Scale Sprout Tree." Historically, this name has been associated with various species, including certain types of Spindle trees (*Euonymus*) or the *Mallotus repandus*.</note>

The *Herbal for Famine Relief* <note>Original: *Jiuhuang Bencao*. A Ming Dynasty text by Prince Zhu Xiao (1406) designed to help commoners identify edible wild plants during crop failures.</note> states that the **Chengya Tree** grows in the mountains and wilderness of **Hui County** <note>A region in northern Henan Province known for its diverse flora.</note>. 

The branches and stems of this tree resemble those of the **Pagoda Tree** <term>Huai (*Sophora japonica*); a common tree in China often associated with scholarship and officialdom</term>. The leaves on the branches are similar to those of the **Evergreen Holly** <term>Dongqing (*Ilex* species); a group of shrubs and trees characterized by their year-round green foliage</term>, though they are slightly longer. 

The tree produces white flowers and bears seeds that are greenish-white. 

The leaves have a sweet taste. To prepare them as food:
*   Gather the tender leaves.
*   **Blanch** <term>zha; a traditional culinary technique where greens are briefly boiled to soften them for consumption</term> them until they are thoroughly cooked.
*   Rinse them clean with water.
*   Season with oil and salt to eat.

# Moon Bud Tree

The **Herbal for Famine Relief** <note>Original: *Jiuhuang Bencao*. A Ming Dynasty manual for identifying edible wild plants to survive food shortages.</note> states that the **Moon Bud Tree** is also known as the **Sprouting Bud** <note>Original: *Xunya*. The character *Xun* refers to the budding of plants or the tip of a new sprout.</note>. It grows in the midst of fields and wilderness. 

Its stems resemble the branches of the **Pagoda Tree** <term>Huai (*Sophora japonica*); a common deciduous tree in East Asia with distinct green bark on young branches</term>. Its leaves are similar to those of the **Two-leaf Vetch** <term>Waitoucai (*Vicia unijuga*); a wild legume whose leaves grow in distinctive pairs</term>, though they are slightly shorter and somewhat stiffer. They also resemble the **Dogwood Bud** <term>Jiaya; referring to the edible young growth of *Cornus walteri*, the Walter’s Dogwood</term>, though the leaf tips of the Moon Bud Tree are quite long. 

The leaves grow in opposite pairs. Their flavor is sweet with a hint of bitterness. To prepare them for eating: 
*   Gather the tender leaves. 
*   **Blanch** <term>zha; a traditional culinary technique of briefly boiling wild greens to soften the fibers and remove any unpleasant bitterness or toxins</term> them until they are thoroughly cooked. 
*   Soak and rinse them clean in water. 
*   Season with oil and salt for consumption.

## Muslim Vinegar (Huihui Cu)

According to the <term>Famine Relief Herbal<note>original: "Jiuhuang Bencao" (救荒本草). A 15th-century medicinal and botanical text focused on identifying wild plants that could be eaten during food shortages.</note></term>, the **Muslim Vinegar** tree—also known as the <term>Linpuzha<note>A local dialect name for the Sumac tree.</note></term>—grows in the wilderness and mountains of **Shaohua Mountain** in **Mi County**. 

The tree reaches a height of over ten feet. Its leaves are similar to those of the **Doulu tree** <note>original: "兜櫨"; likely referring to the Smoke Tree (Cotinus coggygria) or a related species</note>, though they are thicker and larger, with prominent saw-tooth serrations along the edges. They also bear a resemblance to the leaves of the "Thick Toona" tree, being similarly large in scale. 

The leaflets are arranged in groups of three or five along a single stem. The tree produces white flowers and bears seeds about the size of a pea; when these seeds ripen, they turn the color of red lacquer. Both the seeds and the leaves have a sour flavor, though the leaves are only mildly so. 

**Instructions for consumption:**
Gather the leaves and <term>blanch them<note>original: "煠熟" (zhashu). This refers to the process of briefly boiling or scalding greens to soften them and remove unwanted flavors before eating.</note></term> in boiling water. Soak them in fresh water to remove the excess sourness, then rinse them thoroughly. They can then be seasoned with oil and salt to be eaten. The seeds can be used to season soups, as their flavor is much like vinegar.

# White Hibiscus Tree

The <term>*Famine Relief Herbal*<note>original: "救荒本草" (Jiu Huang Ben Cao). A seminal 15th-century botanical work by Zhu Xiao, a Ming Dynasty prince, created to help people identify wild plants that could be eaten during crop failures.</note></term> states: The **White Hibiscus Tree** grows in the mountain valleys of Liangjiachong in <term>Mi County<note>Modern-day Xinmi in Henan Province, China.</note></term>.

The tree grows to a height of five to seven feet. The leaves resemble **tea leaves**, but they are broader, larger, and more glossy. They also resemble the leaves of a newly sprouted <term>Qinggang<note>original: "青岡". A common name for species of evergreen oaks, typically *Cyclobalanopsis*.</note></term> but without flowers. ## The Doulu Tree <gloss>Also known as the Qi</gloss>

According to the *Famine Relief Herbal* <note>original: "救荒本草" (Jiùhuāng Běncǎo); a 15th-century guide to edible wild plants for use during famines</note>, the Doulu tree grows in the valleys of Liangjiachong in Mi County. The tree is quite tall and large. When its wood becomes old and decayed, it turns extremely porous and can be burned as incense. Its common name is "Qi Fragrance." The leaves resemble those of the "Hui-Hui Vinegar Tree" <note>original: "回回醋樹" (huíhuí cùshù); likely referring to a variety of Sumac</note>, though they are thinner and narrower; they also look like the leaves of the Rowan tree <note>original: "花楸" (huāqiū)</note> but with fewer serrations. The leaves grow in opposite pairs and have a bitter taste. To prepare them, harvest the young, fragrant leaves, scald them in boiling water, and soak them to remove the bitterness. Once washed clean, they can be seasoned with oil and salt and eaten.

According to the *Compendium of Materia Medica* <note>original: "本草綱目" (Běncǎo Gāngmù); the definitive 16th-century pharmaceutical text by Li Shizhen</note>, the Qi Fragrance tree is found in the mountains of the Yangtze, Huai, and southern ridge regions. Large specimens reach nearly ten feet in height, while smaller ones are frequently gathered by woodcutters. The leaves are green, long, and serrated, shaped like small perilla leaves but fragrant, growing opposite one another at the nodes. Its roots resemble those of the goji berry but are larger; when roasted, they are intensely aromatic. 

The *Shurangama Sutra* <note>original: "楞嚴經" (Léngyán Jīng); an influential Mahayana Buddhist scripture</note> states: "Place a small burner before the altar and use water boiled with *Douloupó* incense to perform ritual bathing." This tree is the source of that very fragrance. 

The roots have a taste that is bitter and astringent, a neutral "energy," and are non-toxic. They are primarily used to treat scalp sores <note>original: "頭癩" (tóulài); refers to fungal infections or scaly skin diseases of the head</note> and swollen toxins. Grind the root into a fine powder, mix it with sesame oil, and apply it to the affected area. Within seven days, the diseased skin will rot and fall away.

# The Linden Tree
<note>original: "Duanshu" (椵樹). In modern botanical terms, this refers to species of the genus *Tilia*, commonly known in English as the Linden, Lime, or Basswood tree.</note>

According to the *Famine Relief Herbal* <note>original: "Jiuhuang Bencao" (救荒本草), a famous 15th-century work documenting plants edible during times of crop failure</note>, the **Linden Tree** grows in the valleys of the Taihang Mountains within Hui County. The tree is exceptionally tall and large. Its wood is fine-grained and delicate, making it suitable for crafting **furniture and utensils**. 

The branches grow in opposite pairs. The leaves resemble those of the **Hibiscus** <note>original: "Mujin" (木槿)</note> but are longer, larger, and thinner, with a relatively pale color. The leaf margins are divided into five lobes and are lined with serrated teeth. It produces yellow flowers and bears seeds the size of peas, which are greenish-white in color. 

The leaves have a bitter taste. To prepare them as food, harvest the tender leaves and **scald** them <term>zháshú (煠熟): a culinary technique of quick-boiling or blanching to soften and prepare wild greens</term>. Soak them in water to remove the bitterness, then rinse them thoroughly. They may then be seasoned with oil and salt for consumption.

In the *Correct Meaning of the Erya* <note>original: "Erya Zhengyi" (爾雅正義), a classic dictionary commentary</note>, the **Linden** <gloss>also called the Shi</gloss> is noted <gloss>as the White Linden</gloss>. The tree is said to resemble the White Poplar. The *Zhengyi* further explains that the **Linden** <gloss>is also named the Shi</gloss>. In the *Tan Gong* <note>A chapter in the "Book of Rites" (Liji) concerning funeral customs</note>, it is mentioned that "one Linden wood coffin" was used. It is also referred to as a *bi* <gloss>meaning a Linden wood coffin</gloss>; *bi* <gloss>conveys the idea of being "firmly attached" or reinforced</gloss>.

Master Zheng observed a version of the *Erya* where the name "Shi" was written as "Er." The commentary <gloss>describing it from the White Linden to the White Poplar</gloss> in the *Zhengyi* cites the *Jade Chapters* <note>original: "Yu Pian" (玉篇), a 6th-century dictionary</note>, stating the Linden wood resembles the White Poplar.
