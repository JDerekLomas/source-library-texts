Socrates used to judge—as Xenophon records in his *Memorabilia*—that a noble mind, worthy of every favor, is one to which the political disciplines are dear. <note>Socrates was famously more interested in ethics and the "art of living" together in a city-state than in abstract physics.</note> And for that reason, as I now explain a certain principal head of Politics, I hope no one will stir up a dispute against me; especially since I repeat everything from the decrees of others, and I am ready to yield gladly to anyone teaching better things.

II. The supreme power in a Republic, the description of which completes the greatest part of Politics, is what Aristotle calls in the third book of his *Politics* the "governing power" <note>original Greek: κύριον πολίτευμα (kyrion politeuma)</note>, "absolute power" <note>original Greek: αὐτοκρατεία (autokrateia)</note>, "sovereign rule" <note>original Greek: κυρία ἀρχή (kyria archē)</note>, "power" <note>original Greek: δύναμις (dynamis)</note>, or "political authority" <note>original Greek: ἀρχή πολιτική (archē politikē)</note>, and "supreme power" <note>original Greek: ἄκρα ἐξουσία (akra exousia)</note>. The Spanish call it *Mayoria* <note>original: "Mayoria", implying seniority or superior power</note>. Commonly, among the Interpreters of the Law, it is the "greatest command" <note>original Latin: Imperium maxumum</note>, as noted by the Doctors of Law <note>The "Dd." refers to the "Doctores," the established legal commentators of the medieval and Renaissance periods.</note> in their commentary on the third law of the Digest regarding Jurisdiction. Bodin, *On the Republic, book 1, chapter 8, number 151, and book 3.*

<note>This continues a list of citations from the previous page</note> chapter 3, number 280; for instance, the *Praetor* <note>A high-ranking Roman magistrate</note>, in *the second to last law on justice and law, and the law "if a family" 9, on jurisdiction.* So too **Cicero** says: *Great is the name, great the hope, great the Majesty of the Consul.* And **Pliny**, in *Book 8, Letter 6 of his Epistles*, speaks of the *Majesty of the Senate and of the Judges*, which properly belonged to the people. And if we wish to speak accurately, those who think that the rights of Majesty consist merely in territorial right, <term>Territorial Sovereignty</term> <note>original German: *Landsfürstlicher Oberkeit*</note> or <term>Supreme Jurisdiction</term> <note>original German: *Oberbottmäßigkeit*</note>, are far from the truth. *Bornitius on Majesty, chapter 1 at the end; Thomas Michael, thesis 44, disputation on Jurisdiction.*

III. Now, **Majesty** consists in the Command <note>original Latin: *Imperium*</note> and all the dignity of the Roman people, or of any other body that has no superior. *Cicero in "For Cornelius Balbus"; Quintilian, book 7, chapter 3.* In this Majesty, the right of the entire state consists. It seems correctly divided into **Real Majesty**, or that of the <term>Empire</term> <note>original Latin: *Imperium*—referring here to the state's sovereign power</note> (which consists in the power to constitute or establish the state), and **Personal Majesty** (which consists in administering or governing the Republic). *Hermann Kirchner, Disputations on the Republic 2, thesis 3; Philipp Hoenon, political disputation 3, thesis 37; Johann Camman, disputation on the Rights of Majesty 1, theses 70, 75 and following; Bortius in Arumaeus, volume 9, discourse 30, chapter 1, theses 1 and 2; Mr. Daniel Otto, singular treatise on Majesty, chapter 1, number 8.* However, when they attribute Majesty to the "Empire," they do not assign it to an inanimate thing; rather, it belongs to the state because of the people within it.

IV. **Real Majesty**, or that of the <term>Empire</term>, is as old as the Republic itself; as long as the body of the state lasts, this Majesty remains, and it persists even during interregnums <note>periods between the reigns of rulers</note> and political changes. We can therefore call it the foundation of the Republic. *Christoph Matthias, Dithmarschen Political College 2, disputation 2, thesis 6; Heinrich Velsten, Century of political questions, decade 8, question 2 at the end.* **Personal Majesty**, however, falls with the person of the ruler and returns to the Republic. In view of this Real Majesty, it is said that even after the *Royal Law* <note>The *Lex Regia*, the law by which the Roman people supposedly transferred their power to the Emperor</note>, the Roman people retained some Majesty. *Section 4 of the Institutes on public judgments; Law 7, second to last section: "our Majesty" in the Digest on captives and postliminy; Law 1 and Law 3, the passage "The Julian Law, however," regarding "public Majesty" in the Julian Law of Majesty.* For the *Royal Law* should only be understood as a transfer of **Personal Majesty**; as I argued in my *Treatise on Monarchy, chapter 4, number 4*, the people transferred through it only the power of administration, not the power of constitution <note>the power to create the fundamental laws of the state</note>. It is clear from *Law 2, section 1 of the Digest on the origin of law* that the administration of the Republic—and nothing more—was given to the Emperor. And the Emperor Maximus (as recorded by Herodian) also acknowledged the Sovereignty of the people when he said:
> The Principate <note>the office of the Prince or Emperor</note> is not the private possession of just one man; rather, it has belonged from of old to the whole Roman people. Indeed, in that city [Rome] resides the fortune of the Empire; to us, however, only the distribution and administration of the Principate has been entrusted, together with you.
It should certainly never be thought that the whole and entire Republic is represented by the Prince. He is the head, not the whole body. And just as in the human body, the other parts also have their functions:

# A Politico-Legal Dissertation

...thus, even now, there remains some **Majesty** <note>original Latin: *Maiestas*—the supreme power or sovereignty of a state</note> belonging to the public body and the people. Indeed, no one but a flatterer would suggest that the name of the **Republic** <term>original Latin: *Respublica*—literally "the public thing," referring to the state or commonwealth</term>, which includes that most sacred title of "fatherland," was abolished when the rule of a prince began. Therefore, the **Crime of Majesty** <term>original Latin: *Crimen Maiestatis*—the crime of high treason or insulting the dignity of the sovereign state</term> is said to be committed as much against the people and the Republic as against the Prince himself. *See Book 1, Section 1 of the stated title; Lord Doctor Bocerus, Treatise on the Crime of Majesty, Chapter 1, Number 25, etc.; Vulteius, Roman Jurisprudence, Book 1, Chapter 47, at the beginning.* Today, the Emperors are accustomed to speak in this way regarding those accused of the Crime of Majesty against the Empire: <note>original German: *"Die wider vns vnd das H. Römisch Reich gehandlet"*</note> **"Those who have acted against us and the Holy Roman Empire."**

V. To **Real Majesty** <note>sovereignty that resides in the state as an institution</note> belong the privileges and the **Sovereign Charter of the Land** <note>original German: *Maiestätsbrief*—a formal document granting rights or religious freedoms</note>; to **Personal Majesty** <note>sovereignty exercised by the individual ruler</note> belong those powers which are typically granted to those who govern. Therefore, the **fundamental laws** <term>original Latin: *leges fundamentales*—the core constitutional rules of a nation</term> encompass this Majesty of the Republic (or Real Majesty). These are the laws under which the Republic was established and upon which it rests as if on a foundation, arising from common consent. If these laws are removed, the safety and the state—by which the Republic stands and persists—must necessarily be overturned, or at the very least, changed or altered into another form. *See Lambert Danaeus, Christian Politics, Book 1, Chapter 6; Johannes Althusius, Politics, Chapter 6; Keckermann, Politics, Book 1, Chapter 6; Börtius in Arumaeus, Discourse on Public Law 30, Chapter 2.* Of such a fundamental law, it can be truly said that it is a **common covenant of the state** <note>original Greek: *πόλεως συνθήκη κοινή* (poleos synthēkē koinē)</note>. Althusius looked to this in Chapter 6 of his *Politics*, asserting that the rights of a Kingdom are twofold: namely, the **Fundamental Law of the Kingdom** and the **Right of Majesty**. He adds a notable distinction: that a Republic or Kingdom can indeed be established, preserved, and passed down to posterity without fundamental laws, but not without the Rights of Majesty. However, I do not think this is without controversy; for every Republic, even an absolute lordship, seems to possess some form of Real Majesty. And so even **Samuel**, after the King was elected, wrote down the **Law of the Kingdom**. *1 Samuel 10:25.* A fundamental law was also made when we read in Holy Scripture that Kings made a **covenant** with their subjects at the beginning of their reign. *2 Samuel 3:21 and Chapter 5:3.*

In a **Democracy**, although Real and Personal Majesty appear to coincide (see Vulteius, *Roman Jurisprudence*, Book 1, Chapter 47), Majesty is nevertheless not held by the people in such a way that a majority part of the people can decide something against it. Rather, in the abolition of fundamental laws, the consent of **every individual** is required (if we look at the Law). For a fundamental law has the force of a **pact and a contract**; to rescind it, the consent of all parties is necessary. Thus, the **Religious Peace** <note>referring to the Peace of Augsburg (1555) or Westphalia (1648), which granted legal rights to different Christian denominations</note> in our Empire cannot be abolished by a mere plurality of votes, since in that matter, all individuals as well as the whole body can claim their legal right.

Now, regarding what I just said—that the consent of all is required in the repealing or establishing of a **Fundamental Law** <term>original Latin: *lex fundamentalis*—a foundational law or constitution that defines the core structure and succession of a state</term>—I mean those who represent the entire people, such as the **Estates** <term>original Latin: *Status*—the representative ranks or classes in a political assembly, like a parliament</term> in the **Holy Roman Empire**. However, the opposition of one or two individuals is not taken into account. Sometimes, change also occurs through revolution, where procedures are often extraordinary and violent.

VI. Since the **Majesty of the Ruler** is distinguished from the **Majesty of the Empire**, much like that which is founded is distinguished from the foundation itself, it follows that the Ruler’s Majesty is bound to the Majesty of the Empire as if to a superior. Even he who rules is bound to respectfully observe that Majesty and its laws, **"lest the Republic suffer any harm"** <note>original Latin: *ne quid Respublica capiat detrimenti*—a famous Roman legal formula used to charge leaders with the protection of the state's safety above all else</note>; and these laws ought not to be changed by any variation. 

Jean de Terrevermeille <note>original: *Ioh. de Terrâ Rubeâ*—a 15th-century French jurist</note> in his *First Treatise on the Law of Succession in the Kingdom of France, Article 1, Conclusion 24* and Bornitius in *On Majesty, Chapter 9* express this beautifully. Indeed, a ruler would be the murderer of his own Majesty if he attempted to overturn the Republic itself. For the Prince is bound by the laws of the Kingdom. Jean de Chokier writes in *Volume 2, Chapter 2, Page 55* that a custom which constrains the Prince himself is considered greater than the Prince. Moditius also notes this in his *Decisions, Section "the law is in doubt," 93, Number 4.* 

Since such laws possess the force of a contract and agreement, even the Prince, however supreme he may be, is bound by them. Hence Jean Bodin <note>a famous French political philosopher</note> writes in *On the Republic, Book 1, Chapter 8, Number 88, near the end*: "As far as the laws of the Empire are concerned," he says, "since they are joined with Majesty, Princes can neither repeal nor even diminish them." No dispensation can be granted against such laws. Gerlach Buxtorff, in his *Dissertation on the Golden Bull, Thesis 7.* Although, in the meantime, I am well aware that many are found who, through flattery, write the opposite.

Bartholomäus Keckermann correctly and prudently states in *Practical Disputations 29, Problem 1*: "Such things," he says, "are distinguished from the Right of Majesty—and thus from the power that is otherwise free from the laws—just as a cause is distinguished from its effect, and they take nothing away from it." 

For example: Is the rule of women appropriate? Should power be granted to those elected from the whole number, or to those succeeding by hereditary right? Should Majesty be offered to a native or rather a foreigner? These matters (if they have been decided by fundamental law) can no more be within the Prince's power than his own constitution or his introduction into the Kingdom; for indeed, they are not instruments of governance like other laws, but efficient causes and foundations, etc. Because of this Majesty, it seems correctly said that the Prince is not greater than the whole Republic.
