## The Spokes
There are thirty spokes to symbolize the days of the sun and moon.
<note>original: 輻三十以象日月. In ancient Chinese cosmology, the thirty spokes of a wheel were frequently used as a metaphor for the thirty days of a lunar month, a concept famously cited in the *Tao Te Ching*.</note>

## The Hub
The hub is situated at the center of the chariot wheel. On its exterior, it supports the spokes; in its interior, it receives the axle. It measures three feet and two inches in length, with a diameter of one foot.
<note>Units of measurement: The "foot" <term>chi</term> and "inch" <term>cun</term> used here refer to the historical Chinese system. During the Ming dynasty, one *chi* was approximately 31-32 centimeters.</note>

## The Wheel
The wheels of a war chariot <term>bingche</term> measure six feet and six inches in diameter. The wheels of a hunting or field chariot <term>tianche</term> measure six feet and three inches. These are positioned on the outside of the carriage body.
<note>original: 輿 *Yu*. This refers specifically to the box or frame of the chariot where passengers or soldiers stand.</note>

# Ornaments for Ceremonial Carriages

According to the regulations for ceremonial carriages <term>lu</term>, the ancient craftsmen <note>original: 罔厍氏 *Wangshe-shi*. Likely referring to the specialized artisans described in the "Rites of Zhou."</note> first established the basic form of the vehicle. During the era of Yu <note>The reign of the legendary Emperor Shun.</note>, the "Phoenix Carriage" <term>luanche</term> was created. The Xia dynasty adapted the Phoenix Carriage into the "Hooked Carriage," and the Shang dynasty further developed the Hooked Carriage into the "Grand Carriage" <term>dalu</term>. It was not until the Zhou dynasty that the system of the "Five Ceremonial Carriages" <term>wulu</term> was fully established. While the general regulations for these vehicles have survived, the specific details of their ornate colors and patterns can no longer be fully verified through historical evidence.

During the Qin and Han dynasties, the system of carriages and regalia <term>yufu</term>—the specific clothing and transport used by officials—was said to be at its most magnificent, yet the historical records lack specific details on their construction. It was only after Emperor Guangwu of the Later Han defeated the rebel leader Gongsun Shu that the imperial court acquired Shu’s collection of carriages and palanquins. Consequently, they adopted the old designs to create the "Golden-Root Carriage" <term>jingenche</term>, which was modeled after the "Jade Carriage" <term>yulu</term> of the Zhou dynasty.

Historical records describe these magnificent vehicles: the wheels were mottled with red, featuring double rims, reinforced hubs, and two linchpins. The handrails <term>shi</term> were decorated with intertwined tigers, the yokes <term>e</term> were capped with dragon heads, and other tiger-themed ornaments were used throughout. 

The object pictured here is curved into the shape of a mandarin duck’s head; it is recessed and square at the bottom to serve as a socket <term>jian</term>, allowing it to be fixed onto the railings <term>lanshun</term>. The upper portion features hornless dragon motifs <term>chiwen</term> and is shaped like the blade of an axe. However, because both sides are decorated with images of walking tigers and bears, it is suspected to be a component used for the "crouching tiger" handrest. It is a great pity that this specific find is incomplete, making it impossible to fully determine exactly how it was originally installed.

# Squatting Dragon

In ancient times, people often used various ornaments to decorate balustrades <term>lanshun</term>, tent-curtains <term>weiwo</term>, and ceremonial carriages <term>chelu</term>. These decorations sometimes took the form of water lilies <note>original: *shuizhi* (水芝), a common auspicious floral motif</note> or "fighting ducks" <note>original: *douya* (闘鴨)</note>. While there were many different varieties, upon examination, most of these motifs do not appear to have carried a specific or profound symbolic meaning.

However, ornaments featuring dragons were strictly forbidden for use by subjects or officials. For any clothing or utensils <term>qiyong</term>, the use of a dragon design served as a clear indicator that the object was reserved for the personal use of the sovereign. This particular object is crafted in the shape of a dragon in a squatting posture. It is suspected that it was used as a decorative fitting for palace temples or for the Emperor’s personal carriage <term>chengyu</term>. Based on an examination of its style and form, this is an object dating from the Tang Dynasty <note>618–907 CE</note>.

# Shaft Support

In the ancient system of chariots, a single-chariot setup utilized a curved pole <term>zhou</term> <note>original: *zhou* (輈), the central pole of an ancient chariot</note>. Those driven by a single pole were equipped with a crossbar <term>liang</term>. The "inner horses" <term>fuma</term> <note>original: *fuma* (服馬), the central pair of horses attached to the yoke</note> were positioned beneath this crossbar, while the two "outer horses" <term>can</term> <note>original: *can* (驂), the horses on the far left and right</note> served to assist the inner horses.

Therefore, on the single pole, there is a component called the **Shaft Support** <term>chengyuan</term>; it is the device used to grip the crossbar. This particular object is crafted in the form of a **tiger’s head**. At the rear, there is an opening or "mouth," which is designed to "bite" or hold the crossbar in place. 

Furthermore, the surface of the support is inlaid with gold and silver and further adorned with the figures of mandarin ducks and magpies in a whirling dance. From this, we can certainly see that the ancients followed strict regulations and aesthetic standards regarding carriages and ceremonial dress <term>yufu</term>. Although this is merely a decoration for a single shaft, it demonstrates how the finest craftsmanship was concentrated upon the chariot. From the quality of this one piece, the excellence of the rest of the vehicle can be inferred.

## The Design of the Comfort Carriage

<note>original: 安車 (Anche). Literally "Peaceful Carriage," this vehicle was designed for comfort and stability, allowing the passenger to sit or lie down. It was often reserved for the elderly, high-ranking officials, or scholars.</note>

The wheels of the **Comfort Carriage** should not be designed too high; if they are too high, the body of the carriage will shake and become unstable. The body itself is limited to a length of six feet <note>Approximately 1.8 meters; these are traditional Chinese feet, which varied by dynasty but were generally shorter than the modern English foot.</note>, which is sufficient for a person to lie down. Its width is designed to match the standard wheel track. 

The **carriage pole** <term>輈 (zhou): the main shaft extending from the carriage to the draft animal</term> is wrapped with cattail rope; the thickness of this rope should be roughly the size of a copper coin. 

Upon the carriage, four pillars are erected to support the roof. This roof is constructed from a **bamboo frame** <note>original: 簏 (lu). A basket-like lattice work.</note>, which is then treated with a dried-paste coating <note>original: 晞餬 (xihu). A method of stiffening and waterproofing fabric or paper over a frame.</note> and finished with black lacquer. The carriage box stands one foot and four inches high and is fully equipped with mattresses and cushions.

At the rear, there is a door. In the front, a **support board** <note>original: 扶板 (fuban). A multi-purpose adjustable board serving as an armrest, desk, or backrest.</note> is installed atop the carriage box. When placed at the front, one can lean upon it; when moved to the rear, it serves as a backrest. This board can be shifted as needed and is secured to the two sides of the box using **iron pins** <note>original: 銕距子 (tiejuzi). "Iron spur-pins" used as locking mechanisms.</note>. The board is over a foot wide, providing enough space to hold books, scrolls, and items such as wine vessels.

The floor of the carriage box is lined with boards to shield the passenger from wind and rain. Toward the rear of the carriage, windows are installed; because of the carriage's length, one can lie on one’s side and comfortably enjoy the **mountain scenery** through them.

An **oiled-cloth curtain** <note>original: 油幰 (youxian). A waterproof canopy or screen made of silk or cloth treated with oil.</note> is attached to the rear of the carriage, fitted with rollers at both ends. In the event of rain, it can be unfurled and fastened to the front pillars. To block the wind, it can be partially unfurled or draped over just one side, secured into place with iron pins to the roof slats and the lower box. When not in use, it is rolled up and stored upright at the back of the vehicle.

At the front of the carriage is a **footrest compartment** <note>original: 納陞 (nasheng). A recessed area or fold-out section for the feet.</note>. This allows the passenger to sit with their feet hanging down comfortably. If the passenger wishes to sleep, a board can be placed across this area to create a perfectly level surface for reclining.
