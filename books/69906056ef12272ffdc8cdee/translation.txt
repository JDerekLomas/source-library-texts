**I** have previously, in the preface of my book ***Micrographia*** <note>Published in 1665, this was Hooke's landmark work on microscopy, famous for its detailed illustrations of the natural world</note>, given the world an account of the founding of a **Physico-Mechanical Lecture** <note>A lecture series dedicated to the study of physics and mechanics</note> in the year 1665 by **Sir John Cutler**. This was established for the promotion of the **History of Nature** <note>The study of animals, plants, and minerals</note> and of **Art** <note>In the 17th century, "Art" referred to technology, craftsmanship, and human-made inventions</note>.

In pursuit of this goal, I have collected many observations of both kinds. From time to time—as I am obligated to do—I have informed the **Royal Society** at their **public meetings**, held at both **Gresham College** and **Arundel House**, regarding these findings through **discourses** and **lectures**.

Now, in order to further promote the goal and design of this lecture series, I have complied with the wishes of several of my **friends** to publish several of these **discourses**, even though I was not otherwise required to do so. These are, for the most part, incomplete in themselves; they are merely **essays** or **attempts** <note>Hooke uses the word "attempts" to mean preliminary research or trials rather than finished proofs</note> regarding various **subjects** that have no dependency or connection to one another. 

In doing this, I intend to avoid any kind of **method** or **order** that might require **apologies**, **prefaces**, or needless **repetitions** of what is already known, or what might have been said on those occasions. I also wish to avoid anything that might force me to follow one specific **subject** that does not, in some way or another, present itself and

...encourage me to **consider** <note>original: "consideration"</note> them. But because they may possibly be better organized in the future, I intend to print them all in the same **format** <note>original: "Volume"; referring to the physical size and layout of the pages</note>, so that when they are collected, they may be either stitched or bound together. This way, as the need arises, they can be referred to by their **number and page**.

I choose this method as the best for promoting the **purpose** <note>original: "Design"</note> of these **lectures**. There is hardly one subject in a million that could be chosen for which an exact and complete **account** <note>original: "History"</note> would not require a person's entire lifetime and thousands of **discoveries and observations** <note>original: "Inventions and Observations"</note> to achieve.

On the other hand, no one can say for certain that they will complete this or that **investigation** <note>original: "Inquiry"</note>, whatever it may be. The greatest part of **discovery** <note>original: "Invention"</note> is but a **stroke of luck** <note>original: "luckey hitt of chance"</note>; for the most part, it is not in our own power. Like the wind, the **spirit of discovery** <note>original: "Spirit of Invention"</note> blows where and when it **wills** <note>original: "listeth"; a reference to the biblical passage John 3:8</note>, and we hardly know where it came from or where it has gone.

It will be much better, therefore, to embrace the gifts of **Providence** <note>Divine guidance or natural fortune</note> and to be diligent in investigating everything we encounter. For we shall quickly find that the number of significant **observations and discoveries** collected this way will be a hundred times more than those found by **deliberate planning** <note>original: "Design"</note>. Everyone has some **fortunate insights** <note>original: "luckey hitts"</note> and useful thoughts on the subjects they are familiar with; recording and communicating these could allow other people to improve them greatly. 

Therefore, it is much to be wished that others would use this method in their **publications**, and not torment their readers with such **tedious** <note>original: "nauseous"</note> **repetitions** and frivolous...

# To the Reader.

...apologies, as systematic **methods** <note>original: "Method"</note> and large **volumes** <note>original: "Volumes"</note> often require. Instead, I would rather enrich the **storehouse of art and nature** <note>The collective body of human knowledge and natural phenomena</note> with choice and excellent **seeds of knowledge** <note>original: "Seed"</note>, freed from the chaff and waste <note>original: "Dross"</note> that would otherwise bury and corrupt them.

Communicating such fortunate thoughts and occurrences does not need to take up much of a person’s time to prepare them for the press; the account is much better the plainer it is. Since factual matter is the core <note>original: "Kernel"</note> that readers generally desire (at least in these subjects), it will be much easier to use if it is freed from the thick and hard shell of **irrelevant details** <note>original: "Impertinences"</note>. This approach is also more pleasant for both the writer and the reader, who can move on to variety with a fresh appetite <note>original: "stomach"</note>, but would be weary and bored if forced to dwell too long upon a single subject. 

There are other advantages in this method of communication that are just as significant as the former. Among these is the securing of **inventions** <note>original: "Inventions"</note> for their original authors, which is almost impossible to do by any other means. There are a certain type of people who make it their business to pry into and spy out the inventions of others so they can sell them to **publishers** <note>original: "Traders of that kind"</note>, who then think they are acting **honestly** <note>original: "ingenuously"; Hooke is being sarcastic, as these publishers claim ownership because they paid a middleman for stolen ideas</note> by printing them as their own since they have bought and paid for them. There have been some recent examples of this, and more may be expected if this method does not prevent it.

When things cannot be explained well by words alone (which happens frequently in **mathematical and mechanical discussions** <note>original: "Mathematical and Mechanical Discourses"</note>), I add **diagrams** <note>original: "Schemes"</note> and illustrations, as **descriptions** <note>original: "Descriptions"</note> of that kind are easier to create and understand. As much as I can, I omit repeating things already printed,

...and I endeavor to deliver those that are new and my own, as I am most pleased when other **authors** <note>original: "Authors"</note> treat me the same way.

I have begun with a **discourse** <note>original: "Discourse"; a formal lecture or written work</note> composed and read at **Gresham College** <note>The center for scientific research in London where Hooke lived and worked</note> in the year 1670. At that time, I intended to print it, but I was discouraged by the advice of some friends to wait until I could repeat the **observation** <note>original: "Observation"</note>, rather than publish it based on the **experience** <note>original: "Experience"; here meaning experimental data</note> of only one year. However, finding that **illness** <note>original: "Sickness"</note> has so far prevented me from repeating the **trials** <note>original: "Tryals"</note>, and that several years of observations have already been lost because of the first delay, I would rather rush it out now—even if it is imperfect—than hold it back for further completion. I hope it may at least serve as a **hint** to others to pursue and complete the observation, which I very much desire.

This first discourse is based on an observation of **nature**, and can therefore be properly categorized under that heading, although it also contains some discussion on the improvement of **art** <note>original: "Art"; here meaning technology, engineering, or human skill</note>. The second, which will follow quickly, will be more properly categorized as **artificial improvements** <note>original: "Artificial Improvements"; technological advancements or inventions</note>, though it will also contain many observations of nature. I intend to always have them follow each other in turns, interweaving them as it were. For on their own, they are like the **warp or woof** <note>The vertical and horizontal threads in weaving; without both, no fabric can be made</note> before they are woven together—unfit to clothe or adorn the body of **philosophy** <note>original: "Philosophy"; meaning Natural Philosophy, the early term for science</note>.

This discovery of the possibility and ease of seeing the fixed stars in the daytime while the sun shines is, I believe, the first instance of its kind ever given. I judge it will be a discovery of great use for perfecting astronomy. 

First, it will help in rectifying the true place of the Sun in the <term>Ecliptic</term> <note>the apparent path of the Sun across the sky</note> at any time of the year. Since by this means it is easy to find any star of the first, second, or third <term>magnitude</term> <note>a measure of a star's brightness; first magnitude stars are the brightest</note> at any time of the day (provided it is above the horizon and not too near the body of the Sun), and since by a method I shall shortly publish, any angle up to a semicircle in the heavens may be measured to the exactness of a second by a single observer, it will not be difficult for future observers to rectify the apparent place of the Sun among the fixed stars to within a second, or very near it. This is one hundred times greater accuracy than has been attained by the best astronomers until now. 

A similar use may be made of it for observing any notable <term>appulse</term> <note>the close approach or meeting of two celestial bodies in the sky</note> of the Moon, Jupiter, Saturn, Mars, and Venus to any significant fixed star that happens during the day. This may serve for discovering their true positions and <term>parallaxes</term> <note>the displacement or difference in the apparent position of an object viewed along two different lines of sight</note>. The second supposition is this: that all bodies whatsoever that are set into a direct and simple motion will continue to move forward in a straight line, until they are deflected and bent into a curved motion—describing a circle, an ellipse, or some other complex curve—by some other effective powers. <note>This is a clear early statement of the principle of inertia.</note>

The third supposition is that these attractive powers are much more powerful in their operation the nearer the body being acted upon is to their own centers. Now, what these several degrees of power are, I have not yet experimentally verified. However, it is a notion which, if fully pursued as it ought to be, will greatly assist the astronomer in reducing all celestial motions to a certain rule; I doubt this will ever be truly accomplished without it. 

He who understands the nature of the **circular pendulum** <note>a pendulum that moves in a horizontal circle rather than swinging back and forth; Hooke used this to model planetary orbits</note> and circular motion will easily understand the whole ground of this principle and will know where to find direction in Nature for the true stating of it. I heartily wish such people may be found, as I have many other things in hand which I would first complete, and therefore I cannot attend to it as well as I would like. But I dare promise the undertaker of this task that he will find all the great motions of the world to be influenced by this principle, and that the true understanding of it will be the true perfection of astronomy.
