# PROLOGUE.

## DON JOSÉ FERNANDEZ MONTAÑA, PRIEST, TO THOSE WHO SHALL READ.

### I.

We must call one of the most celebrated and precious manuscripts kept in the famous Library of San Lorenzo del Escorial (1) an inexhaustible mine of light and wealth for the sciences and the arts. Our historians hardly mention it, because they had no news of it other than the title with which antiquity baptized it, namely: *The Lapidary <note>A "Lapidary" is a book about the properties, virtues, and medicinal or magical uses of gemstones and minerals.</note> of King Alfonso the Wise of Castile*. And in past centuries there was no lack of those who named it, sometimes the "Lapidary of the Library of San Lorenzo," and other times the "Lapidary of Don Diego de Mendoza." Consequently, the well-known artist Don Antonio Selfa deserves the gratitude of the scientific world, having just reproduced the excellent *Book of Stones* with great fidelity and photo-lithographic mastery; and above all, Mr. Don Hipólito Rodrigáñez, the diligent and learned director of periodical publications, whose love and protection for the publishing enterprise was constant and resolute from the first thought of delivering the codex <note>A "codex" is an ancient manuscript in book form rather than a scroll.</note> to the public domain.

---

(1) Let the impartial and judicious reader remember here that the most celebrated and truly royal Library of the Escorial owes its birth and scientific and literary riches to the great Monarch Don Philip II, and how each of those codices and those incunabula <note>original: "incunables" — books printed before the year 1501, during the infancy of the printing press.</note> editions is crying out with a loud voice that the son of Charles V was a king who was a protector of letters, a friend of sciences, and a lover of all wisdom.

# PROLOGUE

The purpose of these pages is to briefly determine the origin of the Escorial Lapidary <note>A "Lapidary" is a treatise on the magical and medicinal properties of gemstones and minerals</note>; its history; its value and incalculable literary, artistic, and scientific merit; concluding with the proper historical and laudatory remembrance of the wise Prince who ordered its translation into the Castilian language and made it a precious heritage for the Spanish people.

The *Book of Stones* was born into the Castilian language in the second half of the 13th century. This is evidenced by the first page of the Index of the Lapidary itself, which features a most curious portrait of the Wise King and the following words, printed here with complete fidelity. They read as follows:

> "Here begins the book of the forms and images that are in the heavens, and of the virtues <note>In this context, "virtues" refers to the inherent powers or properties of the stones</note>, and of the works that proceed from them in the bodies that are below the heaven of the moon, which the very high and honored Don Alfonso, lover of sciences and knowledge, by the grace of God King of Castile, Toledo, Leon, Galicia, Seville, Cordoba, Murcia, Jaen, and the Algarve, ordered to be composed from the books of the ancient philosophers. And son of the very honored King Don Ferdinand and Queen Lady Beatrice. *And this book was begun in the 25th year of his reign. And in the Era of Caesar, in one thousand three hundred and fourteen years. And that of our Lord Jesus Christ, in one thousand two hundred and seventy-six years. And it was finished in the 27th year of his reign. And the Era of Caesar, in one thousand three hundred and 17 years. And that of our Lord Jesus Christ, in one thousand two hundred and seventy-nine years.*" <note>The "Era of Caesar" or Spanish Era was a dating system used in the Iberian Peninsula that begins in 38 BC; hence the discrepancy between the dates.</note>

The Index from which we have copied this paragraph—which establishes the date of our Lapidary—forms a separate codex, occupies a place immediately alongside the *Book of Stones* in the Escorial Library, and teaches us the parts of which it consisted, which were undoubtedly no fewer than eleven; today all, except one, are unknown or lost.

This very obscure manuscript gives a detailed account of the eleven parts, declaring the subjects contained in each and the names of the sages who composed them. The first "speaks of the images and of their works that are made in the stones." Its author was *Abolays* <note>Abolays is the Spanish transliteration of an Arabic name, likely a scholar of Chaldean or Arabic sources</note>, of whom we shall speak later. "And the second is by *Timtim*, who speaks of another manner of images." The third is attributed to *Pythagoras*, and likewise treats other classes of figures in stones.
