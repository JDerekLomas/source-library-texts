Western students are often somewhat confused in their ideas regarding the <term>Yogis</term> and their philosophy and practice. Travelers to India have written incredible tales about the hordes of <term>holy men</term> <note>original: "fakirs"</note>, <term>beggars</term> <note>original: "mendicants"</note>, and <term>tricksters</term> <note>original: "mountebanks"</note> who swarm the great roads of India and the streets of its cities, and who impudently claim the title of "<term>Yogi</term>."

# CHAPTER II.
## “BREATH IS LIFE.”

Life is absolutely dependent upon the act of breathing. “Breath is Life.”

However much they may differ on the details of theory and terminology, both the <term>East</term> <note>original: "Oriental"</note> and the <term>West</term> <note>original: "Occidental"</note> agree on these fundamental principles.

To breathe is to live, and without breath, there is no life. Not only are the higher animals dependent upon breath for life and health, but even the lower forms of animal life must breathe to live, and plant life is likewise dependent upon the air for its continued existence.

The infant draws in a long, deep breath and retains it for a moment to extract its life-giving properties; then the child exhales it in a long wail, and lo! its life upon earth has begun. The old man gives a faint gasp, ceases to breathe, and life is over. From the first faint breath of the infant to the last gasp of the dying man, it is one long story of continued breathing. Life is but a series of breaths.

Breathing may be considered the most important of all the functions of the body, for, indeed, all the other functions depend upon it. Man may exist for some time without eating; he can survive a shorter time without drinking; but without breathing, his existence may be measured by only a few minutes.

And not only is Man dependent upon breath for life, but he is largely dependent upon correct habits of breathing for continued vitality and freedom from disease. An intelligent control of our breathing power will lengthen our days upon earth by giving us increased vitality and powers of resistance. On the other hand, unintelligent and careless breathing will tend to shorten our days by decreasing our vitality and leaving us open to disease.

In his normal state, Man had no need of instruction in breathing. Like the lower animals and the child, he breathed naturally and properly, as nature intended him to do. However, civilization has changed him in this and other ways <note>The author suggests that modern lifestyle and "civilization" have caused humans to lose their instinctive, healthy habits.</note>

## THE EXOTERIC THEORY OF BREATH.
<note>original: "exoteric." This term refers to knowledge that is public or intended for the general population, contrasting with "esoteric" knowledge which is hidden or reserved for initiates.</note>

In this chapter, we will briefly explain the theories of the Western scientific world regarding the functions of the respiratory organs and the role played by the breath in the <term>human economy</term> <note>original: "human economy." A term used in the 19th and early 20th centuries to describe the organized system of the human body and its biological processes.</note>. In later chapters, we will provide the additional theories and established facts from the Eastern <note>original: "Oriental"</note> school of research and thought. The Eastern practitioner accepts the theories and facts of his Western counterparts—which have been known to him for centuries—and adds a great deal that Westerners do not yet accept. However, Western science will eventually "discover" these same facts in due time and, after renaming them, will present them to the world as a great truth.

Before exploring the Western perspective, it will perhaps be better to provide a quick general overview of the Organs of Respiration.

The Organs of Respiration consist of the lungs and the air passages leading to them. There are two lungs, which occupy the <term>pleural chamber</term> <note>The space within the chest cavity that contains the lungs.</note> of the <term>thorax</term> <note>original: Latin "thorax," meaning the chest.</note>. They are located on each side of the center line and are separated from each other by the heart, the major blood vessels, and the larger air tubes. Each lung is unattached in every direction except at the "root," which consists mainly of the <term>bronchi</term> <note>The main passageways that lead into the lungs.</note>, arteries, and veins connecting the lungs with the <term>trachea</term> <note>The windpipe.</note> and the heart. The lungs are spongy and porous, and their tissues are highly elastic. They are covered with a delicately constructed but strong sac known as the <term>pleural sac</term>. One wall of this sac closely adheres to the lung, while the other attaches to the inner wall of the chest. This sac secretes a fluid that allows these inner surfaces to glide easily against each other during the act of breathing.

The Air Passages consist of the interior of the nose, the <term>pharynx</term> <note>The throat.</note>, the <term>larynx</term> <note>The voice box.</note>, the windpipe or trachea, and the bronchial tubes. When we breathe, we draw in the air through the nose. There, the air is warmed by contact with the <term>mucous membrane</term>, which is richly supplied with blood. These in turn subdivide further and end in tiny sections within all the small air spaces in the lungs, of which the lungs contain millions. One writer has stated that if the air cells of the lungs were spread out over an unbroken surface, they would cover an area of fourteen thousand square feet.

The air is drawn into the lungs by the action of the diaphragm: a large, strong, flat, sheet-like muscle stretched across the chest that separates the chest cavity from the abdomen. The diaphragm's action is almost as automatic as that of the heart, although it can be transformed into a semi-voluntary muscle by an effort of the will. When it expands, it increases the size of the chest and lungs, and the air rushes into the vacuum created this way. When it relaxes, the chest and lungs contract, and the air is expelled from the lungs.

Now, before considering what happens to the air in the lungs, let us look a little into the matter of the circulation of the blood. The blood, as you know, is driven by the heart through the arteries into the capillaries, thus reaching every part of the body, which it vitalizes, nourishes, and strengthens. It then returns through the capillaries by another route—the veins—to the heart, from where it is drawn to the lungs.

The blood starts on its arterial journey bright red and rich, laden with life-giving qualities and properties. In considering the question of respiration, we must begin by looking at the mechanical arrangements that allow breathing movements to happen. The mechanics of respiration work through: (1) the elastic movements of the lungs, and (2) the activities of the sides and bottom of the thoracic cavity in which the lungs are contained. The thorax is the part of the trunk between the neck and the abdomen. Its space—known as the thoracic cavity—is occupied mainly by the lungs and heart. It is enclosed by the spinal column, the ribs and their cartilages, the breastbone, and, at the bottom, by the diaphragm. It is generally called "the chest." It has been compared to a completely sealed, cone-shaped box with the small end pointing upward. The back of the box is formed by the spine, the front by the breastbone, and the sides by the ribs.

There are twenty-four ribs—twelve on each side—which emerge from each side of the spine. The upper seven pairs are known as "true ribs" because they are fastened directly to the breastbone. The lower five pairs are called "false ribs" or **floating ribs** because they are not attached that way. The top two of these lower pairs are fastened by cartilage to the other ribs, while the remaining ones have no cartilages and their forward ends are free. <note>In modern anatomy, ribs 8, 9, and 10 are usually called "false ribs" because they connect to the cartilage of the rib above, while ribs 11 and 12 are specifically called "floating ribs" because they have no forward attachment at all.</note>
