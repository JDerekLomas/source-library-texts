# TO THE RIGHT HONORABLE,
## HIS SINGULAR GOOD LORD AND
## MASTER, SIR WILLIAM CECIL KNIGHT, BARON
### of Burghley, Master of the Court of Wards and Liveries, Chan-
### cellor of the *University of Cambridge, Knight of the most noble*
### *order of the Garter, one of the Lords of her Majesty’s*
### *most honorable Privy Council, and Lord*
### *High Treasurer of*
### *England.*

Among the manifold creatures of God (right Honorable and my singular good Lord) that have all in all ages diversely entertained many excellent wits, and drawn them to the contemplation of the divine wisdom, none have provoked men’s studies more, or satisfied their desires so much, as plants have done, and that upon just and worthy causes. For if delight may provoke men’s labor, what greater delight is there than to behold the earth apparelled with plants, as with a robe of embroidered work, set with orient <note>meaning lustrous or precious</note> pearls, and garnished with great diversity of rare and costly jewels? If this variety and perfection of colors may affect the eye, it is such in herbs and flowers that no Apelles, no Zeuxis <note>famous painters of ancient Greece</note> ever could by any art express the like. If odors, or if taste may work satisfaction, they are both so sovereign in plants, and so comfortable, that no confection of the Apothecaries <note>term: pharmacists or druggists</note> can equal their excellent virtue. 

But these delights are in the outward senses; the principal delight is in the mind, singularly enriched with the knowledge of these visible things, setting forth to us the invisible wisdom and admirable workmanship of almighty God. The delight is great, but the use greater, and joined often with necessity. In the first ages of the world they were the ordinary meat <note>meaning food in general</note> of men, and have continued ever since of necessary use both for foods to maintain life, and for medicine to recover health. # The Dedicatory Epistle

<margin>Pliny, Book 8, Chapter 27.</margin>
<margin>Ibid. Book 22, Chapter 2.</margin>
Even the brute beasts have discovered it; and (which is another use that he observes) from there the dyers took the beginning of their art.

Furthermore, the necessary use of these fruits of the earth is plainly apparent by the great expense and care of almost all men in planting and maintaining gardens—not as ornaments only, but also as a necessary provision for their houses. And here, besides the fruit, to speak again briefly of delight: gardens, especially such as your Honor has, furnished with many rare simples <note>original: "simples"; medicinal herbs used on their own rather than in a compound medicine</note>, do singularly delight when in them a man beholds a flourishing show of summer beauties in the midst of winter's force, and a beautiful spring of flowers when abroad a leaf is not to be seen. 

Beside these and other causes, there are many examples of those who have honored this science. For to pass by a multitude of the philosophers, it may please your Honor to call to remembrance that which you know of some noble princes who have joined this study with their most important matters of state. Mithridates the Great was famous 
<margin>Plutarch, *How to tell a flatterer from a friend*.</margin>
<margin>Pliny, Book 25, Chapter 2.</margin>
for his knowledge in this, as Plutarch notes. Euax also, King of Arabia (the happy garden of the world for principal simples), wrote on this subject, as Pliny shows. Diocletian might have had his praise, had he not drowned all his honor in the blood of his persecution. To conclude this point, the example of Solomon is before the rest and greater, whose wisdom and knowledge was such that he was able to describe the nature of all plants, from the highest Cedar to the lowest Moss. 

But my very good Lord, that which was once the study of great philosophers and mighty princes is now neglected, except by some few whose spirit and wisdom has carried them, among other parts of wisdom and counsel, to a care and study of special herbs, both for the furnishing of their gardens and the advancement of their knowledge. Among whom I may justly affirm and publish your Honor to be one, being myself one of your servants and a long-time witness of it. For under your Lordship I have served, and in that way employed my principal study and almost all my time now for the space of twenty years. 

To the large and singular collection of this noble island, I have added from foreign places all the variety of herbs and flowers that I might in any way obtain. I have labored with the soil to make it fit for the plants, and with the plants to make them delight in the soil, so that they might live and prosper under our climate as in their native and proper country. What my success has been, and what my collection is, I leave to the report of those who have seen your Lordship's gardens and the little plot of my special care and husbandry. 

But because gardens are private, and many times—finding an ignorant or a negligent successor—soon come to ruin, there are those who have urged me, first by my pen and after by the press, to make my labors public and to free them from the danger to which a garden is subject. When I was overcome by these requests and had brought this history, or report of the nature of plants, to a full volume, and had made it (as the reader may see by comparison) richer than former Herbals <note>original: "Herbals"; books containing the names and descriptions of plants with their properties</note>, I found it no question to whom I might dedicate my labors. For considering your good Lordship, I found no one on whose favor and goodness I might sooner presume, seeing I have ever found you my very good Lord and Master. Again, considering my duty and your Honor's merits, to whom may I better recommend my labors than to him to whom I owe myself and all that I am able in any service...
