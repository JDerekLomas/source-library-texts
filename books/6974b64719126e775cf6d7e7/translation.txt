<meta>This page continues the introduction to Geoffroy Tory’s 1549 edition, detailing the specific contents of the three books and the supplemental alphabets provided at the end of the volume.</meta>

The first book contains an exhortation to organize and regulate the French language by a specific rule for speaking elegantly in good and more wholesome <note>original: "plussain," meaning pure or correct</note> French.

The second treats the invention of Attic <note>Tory's term for classical Roman capital letters</note> letters and their proportional comparison to the natural body and face of the perfect human being, along with several beautiful designs and moralities <note>philosophical or symbolic interpretations</note> concerning these Attic letters.

In the third and final book, all the aforementioned Attic letters are drawn and proportioned according to their alphabetical order, in both height and width, each one individually, teaching their proper form and required Latin and French pronunciation, in both the ancient and modern styles.

At the end are added thirteen different styles of letters: specifically Hebrew, Greek, Latin, and French letters—the latter in four styles, which are *Cadeaulx* <note>large, decorative calligraphic capitals often used in legal documents</note>, the bastard form <note>a common hybrid of formal and cursive gothic scripts</note>, and *tourneure* <note>a rounded script</note>. Following these are Persian, Arabic, African, Turkish, and Tartarian letters, all five of which use the same alphabet style. Afterward come the Chaldean, the Gasses—otherwise called Imperial—and the Bullatic <note>letters resembling those used on papal "bulls" or seals</note>. Then, the Fantastic letters, the Utopian—which might be called "voluntary" <note>likely referring to letters created by whim or imagination</note>—and finally, the Flowered letters. Included also are instructions and the method for making letter-ciphers <note>monograms</note> for gold rings, tapestries, stained glass, paintings, and other things as may seem good and beautiful.

# Geoffroy Tory of Bourges, gives and offers humble Greeting to all true & devoted lovers of good letters <note>original: "bonnes lettres," a phrase referring to the humanities, classical literature, and the art of fine writing</note>.

<image-desc>A large decorative drop cap 'L' featuring intricate floral and vine patterns within a square border.</image-desc>

The Poets, the Orators, and the other scholars in letters and sciences, when they have created and compiled some work by their studious diligence and hand, are accustomed to present it to some great lord of the Court or of the Church, exalting him through writings and praises to the notice of other men. This is done to please him, and so that they might always be so welcome in his presence that he seems obligated and bound to give them some substantial gift, some Benefice <note>an ecclesiastical office providing an income</note>, or some Office in recompense for the Labors and Vigils <note>original: "Vigiles," referring to the practice of studying and writing late into the night by candlelight</note> they have put into creating and composing their said works and gifts.

I could easily do the same with this little book, but considering that if I presented it to one person rather than another, there might be some envious hesitation, I have decided that it would be more honorable for me to make a present of it to you all, O devoted lovers of good Letters, without preferring the great to the small: except in so far as one loves Letters more and is more steeped in virtue. In this way, the Prelates and great Lords, who are all excellent in beautiful and good virtues, will have their part, so that you will not lose yours. I am displeased that some have tried to discourage me from making public what I am writing to you in this, our complete work, and that they have tried to make me out to be an ungrateful man for not wanting to teach a thing so very beautiful and good.

He addresses the criticism he faced for choosing to share his specialized knowledge of letterforms and language with the general public.</summary>

They remind me of many people who, when they have a notebook or some unknown book, would not share it with their brother or even their father. I believe such men are so wicked and greedy that if all the flames and fire in the world were extinguished, except for one single candle that they had lit, and fire could only be obtained from their aforementioned candle, they would not even want their sweet mother to light one from it to make a fire to warm and feed their little brother.

In this way did the noble craftsman of the stained-glass windows of the Sainte-Chapelle in Bourges—which the Duke of Berry named Jean had made <note>Jean de Berry (1340–1416) was a great patron of the arts; the Sainte-Chapelle of Bourges was his palatine chapel.</note>—behave. Would to God that the inventor of gunpowder <note>original: "pouldre à canon"</note> had done the same, and that he had died without hands and with his mouth closed. A hundred thousand men would have been able to live longer...
