There are two types of pine. Only the variety with <term>five needles<note>original: 五葉 (wǔyè). Most likely referring to *Pinus armandii* or the Chinese White Pine, which is distinguished from the more common two-needle species by its edible seeds.</note></term> produces seeds. These seeds take three years to fully ripen.

Pines grow in the valleys of <term>Mount Tai<note>original: 泰山 (Tàishān). The most sacred of China's Five Great Mountains, often associated with longevity and the divine in historical texts.</note></term>. Their resin is known by the names <term>Pine Paste<note>original: 松膏 (sōnggāo)</note></term> or <term>Pine Fat<note>original: 松肪 (sōngfáng)</note></term>. It has a bitter yet sweet taste, is warm in nature, and is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat carbuncles, malignant sores, scalp rashes, <term>alopecia<note>original: 白禿 (báitū); literally "white baldness," referring to fungal infections or patchy hair loss.</note></term>, scabies, and <term>Wind-Cold humors<note>original: 風氣 (fēngqì). In traditional medicine, "Wind" is a pathogen responsible for many sudden-onset or wandering pains.</note></term>. It stabilizes the <term>Five Viscera<note>original: 五藏 (wǔzàng). The functional systems of the Heart, Liver, Spleen, Lung, and Kidney.</note></term>, removes heat trapped in the stomach, and addresses dry throat, <term>wasting-and-thirst disorder<note>original: 消渴 (xiāokě). A traditional diagnosis similar to modern diabetes, characterized by extreme thirst and weight loss.</note></term>, as well as <term>Wind-Numbness<note>original: 風痺 (fēngbì)</note></term> and dead skin. The red variety of resin is used specifically for severe numbness.

The fruit <insert>the pine nut</insert> is bitter and warm in nature and is non-toxic. It is used to treat Wind-Numbness, cold humors, and physical frailty or shortness of breath; it is used to supplement "deficiencies" in the body's constitution.

The needles are bitter and warm; they treat <term>rheumatism<note>original: 風濕 (fēngshī)</note></term> and <term>scrofula<note>original: 瘰癧 (luǒlì). Chronic swelling of the lymph nodes.</note></term>, promote hair growth, and stabilize the Five Viscera.

<term>Pine Knots<note>original: 松節 (sōngjié). The resin-rich joints or knots found in the wood.</note></term> are warm in nature. They are primarily used for all types of chronic joint pain caused by "Wind" and for numbness and pain in the legs due to "Wind-deficiency."

The yellow powder found on the flowers is called <term>Pine Yellow<note>original: 松黄 (sōnghuáng). Pine pollen.</note></term>. If one burns the branches and collects the liquid that drips from the ends, it is called <term>Pine Pitch<note>original: 松瀝 (sōnglì)</note></term>, which is used to treat skin sores on cattle and horses.

The green coating found upon the bark is called <term>Aina Incense<note>original: 艾納香 (àinàxiāng). In this context, it refers to the lichen or moss growing on the tree, used for its aromatic qualities.</note></term>. It is used when blending various aromatic incenses; its smoke is prized because it does not disperse easily.

# Chinese Gall
### (Galla Chinensis)

Chinese Gall <note>original: 五倍子 (*wǔbèizǐ*)</note> is found in many places, growing upon the leaves of the Sumac tree <note>The text refers to the "Skin-tree" (*fūmù*), a common name for *Rhus chinensis*</note>. It produces fruit in the seventh month without flowering; the wood of this tree is greenish-yellow in color. The galls themselves are green when young, turning yellow once ripe; the largest among them reach the size of a fist and contain many insects inside. In the ninth month, these galls are harvested and dried in the sun.

It is also known by the names **Clam Gall** <note>original: 文蛤 (*wéngé*), so named because its shape can resemble a clam shell</note> and **Hundred Insect Granary** <note>original: 百蟲倉 (*bǎichóngcāng*)</note>. 

In terms of its medicinal properties, it is bitter and sour in taste, neutral in its "temperature" or nature, and is non-toxic. It is used to treat:
*   Swelling and bleeding of the gums <note>original: 齒宣 (*chǐxuān*)</note>.
*   Chronic sores or dental decay <term>𤺊 (*nì*): a specialized term for corrosive ulcers or childhood malnutrition sores often attributed to "parasites" in ancient medicine</term>.
*   Wind-toxins originating in the Lung organ that overflow onto the skin, manifesting as wind-dampness, ringworm, itchy rashes, and weeping abscesses.
*   Persistent bleeding from the "five types of hemorrhoids."
*   Corrosive sores appearing on the faces and noses of small children.

# The Scholar Tree

**Sophora fruit** <note>original: 槐實 (*huáishí*)</note> grows in the level marshes of **Henan** <note>a province in central China, historically considered the cradle of Chinese civilization</note>, and is now found everywhere. The tree itself grows exceptionally tall and large. According to the **Erya** <note>the oldest surviving Chinese dictionary and encyclopedia, dating back to at least the 3rd century BCE</note>, there are several species of the Scholar Tree. 

Those with large, dark leaves are named the **Huai Scholar Tree** <note>original: 懷槐 (*huáihuái*)</note>. Those whose leaves close during the day and open at night are named the **Palace-Guarding Scholar Tree** <note>original: 守宮槐 (*shǒugōnghuái*)</note>. Those with fine, green leaves are simply called the **Scholar Tree** <note>original: 槐 (*huái*)</note>.

In the fourth and fifth months <note>of the traditional lunar calendar</note>, the tree blossoms. It bears fruit in the sixth and seventh months. In the tenth month, the mature fruits are harvested for use in medicine. The bark and roots can be gathered at any time. 

Its flavor is bitter, sour, and salty; its nature is cold and non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat "evil qi" <note>original: 邪氣 (*xiéqì*); pathogenic factors or harmful influences in traditional medicine</note> and heat within the five internal organs. It stops excessive salivation, mends severe injuries, and treats the **five types of hemorrhoids** <term>five types of hemorrhoids: a classical medical categorization including various presentations such as bleeding, swelling, or protruding types</term> and burns from fire. It is also used for women’s breast masses and acute pain in the uterus. 

The branches are primarily used for washing sores and treating dampness and itching of the scrotum. The bark treats festering sores, and the roots treat **throat obstructions** <term>throat obstruction: a condition, often called *houbi*, characterized by a swollen or blocked throat</term> and alternating chills and fever.

# The Cypress <note>original: 栢 (*bǎi*), a term covering various evergreen conifers like cypress and arborvitae</note>

Cypress fruit <term>Cypress seed kernels, often used in traditional medicine to calm the spirit</term> grows in the mountain valleys of Mount Tai. Today, it can be found everywhere, but the variety from Qianzhou <note>modern-day Qian County, Shaanxi Province</note> is considered the best. The tree flowers in the third lunar month and bears seeds in the ninth month. Its leaves are known as the **Flat Cypress** <note>original: 側栢 (*cèbǎi*), the Oriental Arborvitae</note>; those produced in Mizhou <note>modern-day Zhucheng, Shandong Province</note> are of particularly high quality.

The flavor of the seeds is **sweet** and **neutral** <note>in traditional Chinese medicine, "neutral" means it neither heats nor cools the body</note>, and they are **non-toxic**. They are primarily used to treat heart palpitations caused by fright, to calm the **five viscera** <note>the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys</note>, and to supplement **vital energy** <term>Qi</term>. They also eliminate numbness or pain caused by "wind and dampness," and treat mental confusion, physical exhaustion, and heavy, aching pain in the waist. Furthermore, they enrich the blood and help stop excessive sweating.

The leaves have a **bitter** flavor, are **slightly cold** in nature, and are **non-toxic**. They are used primarily to treat various types of internal bleeding, including vomiting blood, nosebleeds, bloody dysentery, and uterine bleeding. They also help to remove "damp-numbness" <note>a condition similar to rheumatism or arthritis</note>.

The **white inner bark** of the cypress is used to treat burns and scalds, as well as festering sores, and it is said to promote the growth of hair.

# The Elm

**The Elm** <note>original: 楡 (*yú*); specifically the Siberian elm (*Ulmus pumila*), a tree iconic to the Northern Chinese landscape</note> grows in the mountain valleys of Yingchuan <note>an ancient commandery in what is now Henan Province</note>, though it can now be found everywhere. In the third month of the year, it produces pods. The kernels within have a sweet flavor and a balanced nature, and they are non-toxic.

<column-break/>

It is primarily used to treat the blockage of both stool and urine, as it facilitates the <term>water passages</term> <note>the traditional Chinese medical concept of the body's fluid regulation and excretory pathways</note>. It eliminates <term>evil qi</term> <note>harmful environmental or internal influences believed to cause illness</note>, harmonizes the stomach and intestines, clears internal heat, and reduces swelling. 

Its fruit is used to treat scalp sores and scabs in children. The flowers <note>referring to the blossoms or the clusters of young seeds</note> are used to treat pediatric epilepsy, difficulty in urinating, and ailments caused by heat exhaustion. # The Cork Tree

The **Cork Tree** <note>original: 蘖木 (*nièmù*)</note> is the **Yellow Cork Tree** <note>original: 黃蘖 (*huángbò*); scientifically known as *Phellodendron amurense*</note>. # The Lacquer Tree

**Dried Lacquer** and **Raw Lacquer** <note>original: 乾漆 (*gānqī*) and 生漆 (*shēngqī*)</note> originate in the river valleys of **Hanzhong** <note>a region in modern Shaanxi province</note>. Today, they are found throughout the regions of **Shuhan, Jin, Xia, Xiang,** and **Qinzhou** <note>these names refer to historical administrative divisions across central and southern China</note>.

The tree reaches a height of two to three **zhang** <term>Zhang: a traditional Chinese unit of length; one *zhang* is approximately 10 feet or 3.3 meters</term>. Its bark is white, and its leaves resemble those of the **Chinese mahogany** <note>original: 椿 (*chūn*); *Toona sinensis*</note>. The flowers are similar to those of the **scholar tree** <note>original: 槐 (*huái*); *Styphnolobium japonicum*</note>, and its seeds are like those of the **buckthorn** <note>original: 牛李 (*niúlǐ*); *Rhamnus* species</note>. The heartwood of the tree is yellow. During the sixth and seventh lunar months, bamboo tubes are driven into the wood to collect the sap.

**Dried Lacquer** was traditionally said to be the substance that dries naturally within the lacquer collection buckets; in this form, it resembles a **honeycomb** <note>original: 蜂房 (*fēngfáng*)</note>. 

Its flavor is acrid, its nature is warming, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat severe traumatic injuries and to "supplement the middle" <note>a reference to tonifying the digestive system and internal energy</note>. It is used to knit together broken tendons and bones, replenish the marrow and brain, and harmonize the five internal organs. 

It addresses the "five laxities and six tensions" <note>original: 五緩六急 (*wǔ huǎn liù jí*); a traditional medical term referring to various states of constitutional weakness or acute spasmodic conditions</note>, as well as "wind-cold-dampness" blockages <note>commonly understood today as rheumatic or arthritic pains caused by environmental factors</note>. It is also used to treat coughing, dissipate blood stasis and abdominal masses, and relieve lower back pain and gynecological masses in women. It benefits the small intestine and helps eliminate parasites and pests from the body.

**Raw Lacquer** is used specifically to eliminate "longworms" <note>original: 長蟲 (*chángchóng*); usually referring to tapeworms or other large intestinal parasites</note>. If taken over a long period, it is said to "lighten the body" and help one resist the effects of aging.

# Five-leaf Ginseng Bark

**Five-leaf Ginseng Bark** grows in Hanzhong and Yuanju <note>historical regions in modern-day Shaanxi and Shandong provinces</note>; today, it is found throughout the prefectures of the Jiang-Huai region and Hunan. In the spring, it sprouts shoots where both the stems and leaves are green. Specimens that possess five leaflets are considered to be of the highest quality. In the third and fourth months of the lunar calendar, the plant blooms with white flowers that produce small, green berries. By the sixth month, these berries gradually turn black. The roots resemble those of the Chasteberry tree; the bark is yellowish-black, while the inner "bone" (the core) is white and very hard. The stems are harvested in the fifth and seventh months, and the roots in the tenth month; they are then dried in the shade for medicinal use. People in Qizhou call it <term>Wood Bone (Mugu)</term> <note>referring to the plant's remarkably hard, woody root core</note>. Its flavor is pungent and bitter; its nature is warm, though some describe it as slightly cold, and it is non-toxic.

It is primarily used to treat <term>Hernia (Shanqi)</term> <note>a broad term in traditional medicine for abdominal or groin pain and swelling</note> and general abdominal pain. It supplements <term>Qi (Vital Energy)</term> and treats lameness, particularly in children who are unable to walk. It is also used for malignant sores and genital erosion. For men, it treats impotence, dampness under the scrotum, and the dripping of residual urine. For women, it treats vaginal itching as well as pain in the lower back and spine, and pain or numbness in both legs. It is effective against "wind-weakness" <note>weakness or lack of coordination attributed to environmental or internal "wind" pathogens</note>, the <term>Five Loosenesses (Wuhuan)</term> <note>a traditional pediatric diagnosis referring to developmental delays in the neck, soft tissues, and limbs</note>, and general physical wasting or exhaustion. It tonifies the <note>middle (Zhong): the digestive and metabolic center of the body</note>, boosts vital essence, strengthens the tendons and bones, and fortifies the will and resolve.

# Maple
<note>original: 楓 (*Fēng*). While this character is now used for maples of the *Acer* genus, in historical Materia Medica, it almost always refers to the Chinese Sweetgum (*Liquidambar formosana*), known for its tri-lobed leaves and fragrant resin.</note>

**Maple trees** are found throughout all great mountains. They are particularly abundant in **Nangong** <note>A historical region in present-day Hebei province.</note> and the **Guan-Shan** <note>The regions of the Guanzhong plain and Shaanxi province.</note> territories. The tree resembles the **white poplar** and grows exceptionally tall and large.

The leaves are round in overall shape but split into segments to form three points <note>referring to a tri-lobed leaf shape</note>, creating a triangular appearance; they are also fragrant. In the second month of the lunar calendar, the tree produces white flowers. The fruit is large, roughly the size of a duck's egg. The fruit ripens during the eighth and ninth months. When dried in the sun, the resin can be extracted and burned to create **White Glue** <note>original: 白膠 (*Báijiāo*). This is the aromatic resin of the Sweetgum, used both as an incense and a medicinal salve.</note>. Its flavor is pungent and bitter, its medicinal "nature" is balanced, and it is non-toxic.

It is primarily used to treat **hives** <note>original: 癮疹 (*Yǐnzhěn*); itchy skin eruptions.</note>, wind-induced itching, edema <note>swelling caused by fluid</note>, and toothaches. The bark of the tree has a pungent flavor and a balanced nature, though it is considered slightly toxic. The bark is primarily used to treat systemic swelling and to expel **water-qi** <note>original: 水氣 (*Shuǐqì*); a traditional medical term for pathological fluid retention or bloating.</note>. For these conditions, the bark should be boiled into a liquid decoction for use.

# Cloves

**Cloves** <note>original: 丁香 (*Dīngxiāng*)</note> originate in the regions of Jiaozhou and Guangzhou <note>ancient administrative regions spanning Northern Vietnam and Southern China</note> as well as the southern foreign lands; today, they are found only in Guangzhou. The tree is similar to the cinnamon tree and reaches over ten feet in height. Its leaves resemble those of the oak and do not wither even in the depths of winter. The flowers are small, round, and yellow. Its fruits <note>referring to the dried flower buds used as the spice</note> grow upon the branches and leaves, shaped like nails, measuring three to four tenths of an inch <note>approximately 1 to 1.3 centimeters</note>, and are purple in color. Among these, some are swollen and large, resembling the fruit of the cornelian cherry <term>Cornus officinalis</term>; these are called **Female Cloves** <note>original: 母丁香 (*mǔ dīngxiāng*); these are the mature fruits of the clove tree, whereas ordinary cloves are the immature buds</note>.

The flavor is pungent and its nature is warming; it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to warm the Spleen and Stomach <note>In Traditional Chinese Medicine, these systems are responsible for digestion and the distribution of nutrients</note> and to stop sudden vomiting and diarrhea <term>huòluàn</term>, abdominal bloating, and various swellings caused by wind-toxins. It also treats gum disease and tooth decay <note>original: 𧏾 (*qǔ*), often associated with "tooth worms" in ancient medical theory</note>. Its roots are used to treat swellings caused by wind-heat toxins.

# Agarwood

**Agarwood**, **Green Laurel Incense**, **Chicken-Bone Incense**, **Horse-Hoof Incense**, and **Stacked Incense** <note>original: *zhànxiāng*; a lower grade of aromatic wood</note> all originate from the same botanical source. This tree is found throughout the various kingdoms of the Southern Seas <note>original: *Hǎinán*; referring to Southeast Asia</note>, as well as in the regions of Jiaozhou, Guangzhou, and Yazhou <note>modern-day northern Vietnam, Guangdong, and Hainan Island</note>. 

Its wood is similar to that of the Chinese Mahogany <term>chūn</term> or the Zelkova <term>jǔ</term>, and it possesses many knots. The leaves resemble those of an orange tree. Its flowers are white, and its seeds are like those of the betel nut—about the size of a mulberry and purple in color. 

The various names for the incense are determined by the quality and density of the resinous wood:
*   **Green Laurel Incense**: Small branches that are firm and have not yet undergone decay.
*   **Agarwood** <note>literally "sinking incense"</note>: Wood that is hard, black, and sinks completely in water.
*   **Chicken-Bone Incense**: Wood that half-sinks and half-floats, remaining level with the surface of the water.
*   **Stacked Incense**: The coarsest and most common grade of the wood.

In terms of medicinal properties, the flavor of **Agarwood** is "slightly warm" <note>a classification in Traditional Chinese Medicine indicating its effect on the body's internal energy</note>. It is used to treat "wind-water" <note>edema or swelling associated with fluid retention</note> and poisonous swellings. It is also highly effective at dispelling "evil qi" <note>noxious vapors, bad odors, or malevolent environmental influences</note>.

## Golden Cherry
<note>original: 金櫻子 *jīnyīngzǐ*; the fruit of the Cherokee Rose (*Rosa laevigata*), prized in traditional medicine for its binding properties.</note>

# Bamboo

Bamboo is found everywhere, and there are many varieties; however, only three kinds are used in medicine: **Grove Bamboo**, **Bitter Bamboo**, and **Mild Bamboo**.

**Grove Bamboo** <note>original: 篁竹 *huángzhú*; likely a variety of *Bambusa*</note> is firm with short joints; its body is round and its texture is strong. Its skin is white like frost. The leaves are bitter and neutral in flavor, yet "greatly cold" in nature and non-toxic. They are primarily used to treat <term>coughing with upward reversal of breath</term> <note>original: 欬逆上氣 *ké nì shàng qì*; a condition where breathing is difficult because the energy is moving the wrong way</note>, tension in the tendons, and malignant sores. They kill small parasites, remove irritability caused by heat, and treat wind-induced convulsions, <term>throat blockages</term> <note>original: 喉痺 *hóubì*; conditions involving swelling or pain that makes swallowing difficult</note>, and vomiting. The roots, when prepared as a decoction, <term>boost the vital energy</term> <note>original: 益氣 *yì qì*; strengthening the body's fundamental functional force</note> and stop thirst.

**Bitter Bamboo** <note>original: 苦竹 *kǔzhú*; *Pleioblastus amarus*</note> includes both white and purple varieties.

**Sweet Bamboo** <note>original: 甘竹 *gānzhú*</note> resembles the Grove Bamboo but is more lush; this is the same as **Mild Bamboo** <note>original: 淡竹 *dànzhú*; *Phyllostachys nigra* var. *henonis*</note>. It <term>supplements deficiencies</term> <note>original: 補虛 *bǔ xū*; a treatment strategy to nourish a weakened body</note>, <term>descends the qi</term> <note>original: 下氣 *xià qì*; calming upward-moving symptoms like hiccups or wheezing</note>, and eliminates toxins. Its sap is used primarily for wind-induced convulsions.

**Mild Bamboo Leaves** have an acrid and neutral flavor and are "greatly cold" in nature. They are used for phlegm-heat in the chest and coughing with upward reversal of breath. The <term>Bamboo Sap</term> <note>original: 瀝 *lì*; the liquid extracted from bamboo by heating it</note> is also "greatly cold" and treats sudden wind-stroke, numbness, and intense heat in the chest, stopping irritability and distress.

**Bamboo Shavings** <note>original: 皮茹 *pírú*; the thin, scraped inner bark or shavings of the bamboo</note> are slightly cold. They are used for vomiting, retching, chills and fever, vomiting blood, uterine bleeding, and spasms of the tendons.

**Bitter Bamboo Leaves** and their sap treat mouth sores and eye pain, and they benefit the <term>nine orifices</term> <note>The nine openings of the human body: eyes, ears, nostrils, mouth, anus, and urethra.</note>.

**Bamboo Shoots** <note>original: 笋 *sǔn*</note> have a sweet flavor. They are used for <term>wasting-and-thirst</term> <note>original: 消渴 *xiāokě*; a condition similar to diabetes characterized by intense thirst</note>, help clear the water passages, and boost the vital energy.

# Wu Zhu Yu (Medicinal Evodia)

Wu Zhu Yu grows in the river valleys of **Shanggu** <note>original: 上谷; a historical commandery located in modern-day Hebei province</note> and **Yuanju** <note>original: 寃句; an ancient county in modern-day Shandong</note>. Today, it is found growing everywhere, though it is particularly abundant in the regions of **Jiangsu**, **Zhejiang**, **Sichuan**, and the **Han River** basin.

The tree grows to a height of over ten feet. Its bark is a greenish-blue color, and its leaves resemble those of the **Chinese Toona** <note>original: 椿 *chūn*; *Toona sinensis*</note>, though they are broader, thicker, and have a purple tint. In the third month of the lunar calendar, the tree blossoms with reddish-purple flowers. During the seventh and eighth months, it bears fruit that resembles the **Sichuan Peppercorn** <note>original: 椒子 *jiāozǐ*</note>. When the fruit is young, it is a pale yellow, but it turns a deep purple upon reaching full maturity.

The fruit is harvested on the **ninth day of the ninth month** <note>The Double Ninth Festival, traditionally considered a potent time for gathering medicinal herbs</note> and is dried in the shade. 

### Flavor and Medicinal Properties

Its flavor is acrid and warm; its energetic nature is "greatly hot," and it contains a small amount of toxicity. It is primarily used to **warm the middle** <note>original: 溫中 *wēn zhōng*; a therapeutic method to dispel "cold" from the digestive organs</note>, **descend rebellious energy** <note>original: 下氣 *xià qì*; directing qi downward to stop vomiting or belching</note>, and to stop pain. It treats coughing with upward-surging breath, alternating chills and fever, and is used to eliminate dampness and <term>blood numbness</term> <note>original: 血痺 *xuè bì*; a condition of numbness or pain caused by stagnation in the blood vessels</note>. 

It also:
*   Expels "wind-evils" <note>External environmental factors that cause illness</note>.
*   Opens the <term>interstices</term> <note>original: 腠理 *còulǐ*; the pores and the spaces between the skin and muscles where energy and sweat flow</note>.
*   Clears cold-phlegm and twisting abdominal pain.
*   Treats indigestion caused by "cold accumulation."
*   Addresses **"evil-stroke"** <note>original: 中惡 *zhòng è*; a term for sudden, severe illness or loss of consciousness traditionally attributed to malevolent influences</note>, pain in the heart and abdomen, and counterflow energy. 
*   It is generally beneficial to the **five viscera** <note>The heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys</note>.

### Uses of the Root and Bark

The root of the plant is used to kill the **"three worms"** <note>original: 三虫 *sān chóng*; a traditional reference to various types of intestinal parasites</note>. 

The white bark of the root is used to treat:
*   **Throat obstruction** <note>original: 喉痺 *hóubì*; severe sore throat or swelling that makes swallowing difficult</note>.
*   Coughing with counterflow energy.
*   Chronic diarrhea.
*   Indigestion.
*   Lingering ailments in women following childbirth.
*   **Chronic abdominal masses** <note>original: 瘵日癖 *zhài rì pǐ*; refers to wasting diseases and hardened lumps in the abdomen</note>.

# Betel Nut
<note>original: 檳榔 *bīngláng*; the seed of the *Areca catechu* palm, widely used in Asia for its stimulating and medicinal properties</note>

<vocab>檳榔 (Betel nut), 三才圖會 (Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms), 草木 (Plants and trees)</vocab>

[Image: A woodcut illustration of a betel nut palm growing from a rocky outcrop. The image shows the characteristic ringed trunk, pinnate fronds, and hanging clusters of fruit.]

The **Betel Nut** tree grows in the regions of **Lingnan** <note>original: 嶺南; a historical term for the lands south of the Nanling Mountains, primarily modern Guangdong, Guangxi, and Hainan</note> and throughout the various countries of the **Southern Seas**. 

The tree reaches a height of fifty to sixty feet. Its bark is smooth, resembling that of the **Chinese Paulownia** <note>original: 青桐 *qīngtóng*</note>, while its trunk is marked by rings or segments similar to **mottled bamboo** <note>original: 斑竹 *bānzhú*</note>. The tree does not grow side branches; instead, all its leaves cluster at the very top of the trunk, spreading out like the fronds of a **palm tree** <note>original: 棕櫚 *zōnglǘ*</note>. 

The fruit grows in large, heavy clusters nestled among the base of the leaves at the top of the tree. A single cluster may contain several hundred individual fruits. When the fruit is young, it is green, but it turns a golden-yellow or reddish-brown as it ripens. The inner seed is hard and features a beautiful, marbled pattern when sliced.

### Medicinal and Cultural Significance

In the southern regions, the nut is traditionally chewed to ward off the **"poisonous mists"** <note>original: 瘴氣 *zhàngqì*; a traditional concept of miasma or pestilential vapors thought to cause illness in tropical climates</note>. Medicinally, it is valued for its ability to **dispel food stagnation** <note>original: 消食 *xiāo shí*</note> and **direct Qi downward** <note>original: 下氣 *xià qì*; a phrase referring to the relief of abdominal pressure, bloating, or nausea</note>. It is also frequently used in formulas to treat intestinal parasites and to regulate the "middle" <note>the digestive system</note> when it is burdened by dampness or congestion.

The **Betel Nut** <term>檳榔 *bīngláng*; the seed of the *Areca catechu* palm</term> grows in the regions of the **Southern Seas** and throughout the various prefectures beyond the **Ridges** <note>original: 嶺外 *lǐngwài*; the area south of the Nanling Mountains, including modern Guangdong and Guangxi</note>; it is found in all these locations.

Its bark resembles that of the **Chinese Paulownia** <note>original: 青桐 *qīngtóng*</note>, and its trunk is marked by segments or nodes similar to those of **cinnamon bamboo** <note>original: 桂竹 *guìzhú*</note>.

Beside these clusters are thorns that look like prickly needles, overlapping beneath the fruit. A single cluster may contain several hundred fruits, each shaped like a chicken egg.

In terms of medicinal properties, its flavor is acrid and warm, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to aid in the **digestion of grains** <term>消穀 *xiāogǔ*</term>, to **expel excess fluids** <term>逐水 *zhúshuǐ*</term> from the body, and to eliminate **chronic phlegm accumulations** <term>痰癖 *tánpǐ*</term>. People in the **Lingnan** region eat them as if they were common fruits.

There are three or four distinct varieties:
*   A small type with a pungent <note>The source text uses 其 (*qí*), which may be a transcription error for 苦 (*kǔ*, bitter) or 辛 (*xīn*, acrid)</note> flavor is called the **Mountain Betel Nut**. 
*   A large variety with an astringent taste and a large pit is called the **Pig Betel Nut**. 
*   The smallest variety is called the **Lotus Seed** <note>original: 藕子 *ǒuzǐ*</note>.

It is also said that those fruits which are pointed and long with purple patterns are specifically called ***Bing***, while those that are round and short are called ***Lang***. The medicinal potency of the *Bing* is considered slight, whereas the potency of the *Lang* is great. The variety known as the **"Big Belly"** <note>original: 大腹 *dàfù*; typically refers to the medicinal use of the Areca husk</note> is similar to the standard Betel Nut, though its stem, leaves, roots, and trunk differ slightly. When the fruit is harvested and preserved together with its husk, it is called the **Big Belly Betel Nut**.

<term>麒麟竭 *qílínjié*; Dragon's Blood resin, typically derived from *Dracaena cinnabari* or *Daemonorops draco*</term>

### Dragon's Blood

Dragon's Blood <term>騏驎竭 *qílínjié*</term> is produced in various countries of the Southern Seas <term>南番 *nánfān*; historically referring to regions in Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean</term> and in Guangzhou. 

The tree reaches a height of several tens of feet. Its leaves resemble those of a cherry tree but have three distinct corners. Its resinous sap flows out from within the wood, dripping down in a state similar to viscous maltose syrup. After a long period, it hardens and congeals into the resin known as *jie*. It is deep red and takes on the color of blood; for this reason, it is also called "Blood Resin" <term>血竭 *xuèjié*</term>.

Its flavor is sweet and salty, its nature is balanced <note>it does not lean toward "hot" or "cold" in the body</note>, and it is slightly toxic. It is primarily used to treat "evil qi" <note>harmful influences or pathogens</note> within the five viscera <term>五藏 *wǔzàng*; the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys</term>. It is also used to treat "below the belt" ailments <note>likely referring to leukorrhea or other vaginal discharge</note>, to stop pain, and to break up stagnant blood accumulations. Furthermore, it is effective in treating wounds caused by metal blades <term>金瘡 *jīnchuāng*</term>.

# Dragon-Brain Incense

Dragon-brain incense <note>original: 龍腦香 *lóngnǎoxiāng*; this is the crystalline resin of the *Dryobalanops aromatica* tree, highly valued for its purity.</note> originates from the Kingdom of <term>Barus *Pólǜguó*</term> <note>A historical port on the western coast of Sumatra, which was the primary source of this precious aromatic in pre-modern trade.</note>. The tree grows to a height of seventy or eighty feet and has a circumference of approximately six or seven arm-spans. Its branches and leaves grow from the sides; the leaves are perfectly round with white undersides. It bears fruit resembling nutmeg, with a skin that is scaly and rough.

The "incense" itself is actually the resin found within the wood. It resembles white pine resin and possesses a fragrance similar to cedar wood. The <term>balm *gāo*</term> is the clear liquid found beneath the roots; it is also referred to as <term>Barus Balm *Pólǜ gāo*</term>.

Regarding its medicinal properties, its flavor is acrid and bitter, and its nature is slightly "cold"—though some sources claim it is warm, balanced, and non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat malevolent energy <note>original: 邪氣 *xiéqì*; harmful environmental or internal influences that cause illness.</note> in the heart and abdomen, <term>wind-dampness *fēngshī*</term> <note>A traditional medical category often corresponding to symptoms of rheumatism or joint pain.</note>, and internal accumulations. It is also used to treat deafness and to "brighten the eyes" <note>This refers to improving vision or clearing inflammation of the eyes.</note>.

## Trifoliate Orange Tree

**Immature Trifoliate Orange** <note>original: 枳實 *zhǐshí*; the young, green fruit harvested early</note> grows in the rivers and marshes of Henan <note>original: 河內 *hénèi*; an ancient region north of the Yellow River</note>, while **Trifoliate Orange Peel** <note>original: 枳殼 *zhǐké*; the rind of the mature fruit</note> grows in the river valleys of Shangzhou <note>modern-day Shangdan region in Shaanxi</note>. The tree grows five to seven feet high. Its leaves resemble those of the common orange tree but are heavily guarded by many thorns. In spring, it produces white flowers which develop into fruit by autumn. The fruit is harvested during the ninth and tenth months. Its flavor is bitter and sour; its nature is slightly cold and non-toxic.

It is primarily used to treat "Great Wind" <note>a traditional term for severe skin conditions, including leprosy</note> that lodges in the skin, appearing like hemp seeds or beans and causing intense itching. It eliminates masses caused by cold or heat, stops dysentery, promotes the growth of muscle, and benefits the <term>Five Viscera: the core functional organs—Heart, Liver, Spleen, Lungs, and Kidneys</term>. It boosts the <term>Qi: the vital life force or energy of the body</term>, lightens the body, and clears phlegm accumulations and "fluid-habits" <note>original: 痰癖 *tánpǐ*; chronic masses caused by stagnant fluids</note> from the chest and flanks. It expels stagnant water, breaks up internal blockages, and dispels abdominal bloating and fullness. It is effective for acute pressure and "stuffed" pain below the heart, "rebellious Qi" <note>energy flowing upward improperly, causing distress</note>, and wind-related pain in the flanks. It settles the stomach energy, stops loose stools, and brightens the eyesight.

The **Peel** <note>Zhike</note> is also bitter and sour in flavor, slightly cold, and non-toxic. It is primarily used for wind-induced itching and numbness, and to ease the movement of the joints. It treats "Labor Qi" <note>original: 勞氣 *láoqì*; exhaustion or stagnation caused by overwork</note>, coughing, and a sense of heavy oppression or fatigue in the back and shoulders. It dissipates lingering blockages and phlegm stagnation in the chest and diaphragm, expels water, dispels bloating, treats "wind" in the large intestine, settles the stomach, and stops wind-related pain.

## Thick-Bark Magnolia

Thick-Bark Magnolia <note>original: 厚朴 *hòupò*; *Magnolia officinalis*</note> is produced in Jiaozhi <note>An ancient region covering northern Vietnam and parts of southern China</note> and Yuanju <note>An ancient district in modern-day Shandong Province</note>. The tree grows to a height of thirty to forty feet, with a trunk diameter of one to two feet. In spring, it produces leaves that resemble those of the willow; these leaves do not wither throughout the four seasons. It has red flowers and green fruit. The bark is remarkably scaly and wrinkled, and it is quite thick. Its flavor is bitter, its nature is warm, and it is non-toxic.

It is primarily used to treat wind-stroke, disorders caused by cold <term>Cold Damage: A category of febrile diseases known as *shānghán*</term>, headaches, alternating chills and fever, palpitations caused by fright, and numbness or "blockage" <note>original: 痺 *bì*</note> of the Qi and blood. It treats necrotic skin, removes the "three worms" <note>A traditional collective term for various internal parasites</note>, warms the middle <note>The "Middle Jiao," or the digestive center of the body</note>, and boosts the vital energy (Qi). It is used to dissolve phlegm, direct Qi downward, and treat sudden turmoil <note>original: 霍亂 *huòluàn*; characterized by acute vomiting and diarrhea</note>, abdominal pain, distention and fullness, cold-reversal in the stomach, and incessant vomiting. It also treats diarrhea, dysentery, and vaginal discharge. It eliminates fright, removes lingering heat and irritability in the chest, and strengthens <note>literally "thickens"</note> the intestines and stomach. 

The tree is also called *zhen* <note>original: 榛; not to be confused with the common hazelnut tree</note>; its seeds are called *zhuzhe* <note>original: 逐折</note>. These seeds are used to treat scrofula <note>original: 鼠廔 *shǔlóu*; chronic swelling of the lymph nodes in the neck</note>, improve eyesight, and boost vital energy.

# Ash Bark

<term>Ash Bark <note>original: 秦皮 *qínpí*; the bark of *Fraxinus chinensis*, also known as Chinese Ash. Furthermore, it treats low sperm count in men, vaginal discharge in women, and childhood convulsions accompanied by fever. It can also be prepared as a medicinal wash for the eyes.

It is also known as <term>Cen-Bark <note>original: 岑皮 *cénpí*</note></term> or <term>Stone Sandalwood <note>original: 石檀 *shítán*</note></term>.

# Umbrella Polypore <note>original: 猪苓 *zhūlíng*; the sclerotium of the fungus *Polyporus umbellatus*. The name literally translates to "Pig's Tuber," possibly because of its dark, clod-like appearance or a traditional belief that pigs favored eating it. In medicine, it is a key diuretic used to "leach out dampness."</note>

<note>The accompanying illustration depicts the Umbrella Polypore in two states: its growth habit near a garden rock and its harvested form. The foliage shown is likely the artist's interpretation of the surrounding vegetation or a misunderstanding of the fungus's parasitic relationship with tree roots, while the floating textured masses represent the harvested <term>sclerotia <note>A dormant, hardened mass of fungal mycelium that contains food reserves; this is the part used in medicine.</note></term>.</note>

<summary>This page presents the entry for the Umbrella Polypore (Zhuling), a significant medicinal fungus in the Ming Dynasty pharmacopeia. The woodcut illustrates both the natural setting and the harvested medicinal product used for regulating body fluids.</summary>
