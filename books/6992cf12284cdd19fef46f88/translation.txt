## Ginseng
### In the *Shuowen* dictionary it is written as 𧂄; in the *Guangya* it is 蔘; and in common usage it is 參. <note>These are variant characters for *Panax ginseng*. The author is citing ancient dictionaries to establish the plant's long history in Chinese scholarship.</note>

<margin>
Mountain Herbs, Volume 7
</margin>

Ginseng is listed as a "Superior Grade" herb in the *Classic* <note>original: "Benjing," referring to the *Divine Husbandman's Classic of Materia Medica* (Shennong Bencao Jing), the oldest foundation of Chinese herbalism.</note>. In ancient times, the varieties produced in Liaodong and Silla <note>An ancient kingdom on the Korean Peninsula.</note> were considered inferior to those from Shangdang <note>A region in modern-day Shanxi Province. In antiquity, this was the primary source of ginseng, though the local species was eventually harvested to extinction.</note>. Today, however, ginseng from Liaodong and Jilin is the most highly valued, with Silla ginseng ranking second. In the regions of Sanxing and Ningguta <note>Areas in modern-day Heilongjiang Province.</note>, experimental harvesting has been attempted, but the yield is not very large. Those plants transplanted from seedlings are called "Seedling Ginseng" <term>yangshen: seedlings moved to different soil</term>, and those grown directly from seeds are called "Seed Ginseng" <term>zishen: cultivated from seed</term>. The medicinal potency of both these cultivated types is considered weak.

What is currently known as "Dang-shen" <note>*Codonopsis pilosula*, or "Poor Man's Ginseng."</note> is a climbing vine that looks very much like the shoots of the Ladybell <note>original: "Shashen" (*Adenophora*).</note>, though its root grows to over a foot long. It is common practice to use this as a substitute for true Ginseng, but this lacks rigorous scholarly verification.

I respectfully note that our Dynasty <note>The Qing Dynasty (1644–1911), founded by the Manchus.</note> originated at Changbai Mountain. As the ancient poems say of the ancestral lands: "The plains were rich and fertile, where even the bitterest herbs grew sweet" <note>A classical allusion to the *Book of Odes* (Shijing), describing the blessed land of the Zhou ancestors; the author applies this sacred imagery to the Manchu homeland.</note>. It is truly a mysterious and sacred realm of Heaven and Earth, the most fertile soil in all the nine provinces. Deep within the vast forests and lush grasses, a light is said to glow in the middle of the night: that light is the Ginseng.

By Imperial decree, the private digging of ginseng is a crime; the goods are confiscated and the person is punished. The government issues merchant licenses <term>shangyin: official permits for regulated trade</term> to pass through the border checkpoints to harvest in designated areas. Upon their return, the harvesters must submit their find to the authorities. The officials assess the quantity and quality of the ginseng and levy a tax. Once the taxes are settled, the roots are stored in the Imperial Household Department <term>Neifu: the department managing the Emperor's personal treasury and palace supplies</term>. The Department then grades the quality; those of the very highest grade are reserved for the Emperor’s use.

# Astragalus (Huangqi)

Astragalus is listed as a "superior grade" herb in the *Classic of Materia Medica* <note>original: "Benjing" (本經), referring to the *Shennong Bencao Jing*, the foundational text of Chinese herbology.</note>. There are several varieties; those produced in Shanxi and Mongolia are of the highest quality. The variety produced in Yunnan <note>original: "Dian" (滇). An ancient name for Yunnan province.</note> has a foul, rank smell and is not suitable for medicinal use.

The Farmer of Yulou <note>original: "Yulounong" (雩婁農). The literary pseudonym of the author, Wu Qijun. He uses this persona to provide critical historical or moral commentary.</note> says: 

Astragalus is a product of the western regions. However, the *Gazetteer of Chun'an County* records that during the Jiajing era <note>The reign of the Ming Emperor Shizong, 1522–1566.</note>, someone claimed the local area produced Astragalus. The local authorities issued an official demand for it. Since the plant did not actually grow there, the people attempted to substitute it with the roots of what is vulgarly called "Horse-head Alfalfa" <note>original: "Mashou Muxu" (馬首苜蓿). A common plant substituted by desperate locals to satisfy unreasonable tax demands.</note>.

The physician sent to deliver the tribute was flogged and nearly died. In the end, they were forced to pay a bribe of thirty to forty gold pieces to settle the matter. Alas! The ancient kings assessed the natural suitability of the land to distribute resources for the people's benefit. How could later generations turn this benefit into a source of harm? 

The system of "paying tribute based on the soil" <note>original: "Rentuzuo’gong" (任土作貢). A principle from the *Book of Documents* where regions paid taxes in their unique local products.</note> has remained unchanged since the Three Dynasties <note>The Xia, Shang, and Zhou dynasties, regarded as the era of Sage Kings.</note>. However, when official demands are excessive and the culture of gift-giving is widespread, it remains a burden for the people. 

The "Flowers of the Children of Luoyang" <note>Referring to the famous peonies of Luoyang, often transported at great cost to the imperial court.</note> and the "Lychee Manuals of Putian" <note>Putian in Fujian was famous for lychees; the author cites these as examples of local delicacies that became tools for political flattery.</note>—transported across a thousand miles just to bring a moment's pleasure—leave wise men with lingering regrets. 

In the past, Yuanjiang in Yunnan produced lychees. Because the demands for them were so frequent, the local people eventually cut down the trees to escape the burden. In the Kunming Sea <note>referring to Dianchi Lake in Yunnan.</note>, there are also shrimp; fishermen there fear being required to provide them as tribute... <unclear>...</unclear>

Hiding their goods and not daring to sell them to city dwellers, the people’s fear of officials is truly like their fear of ghosts and gods! I have observed that when local gazetteers <term>zhisheng; historical records of a specific region</term> list local products, they either say the land is barren and produces nothing, or they say, "it existed in the past but is gone now." This is because they fear that higher-ranking officials will consult the gazetteer and demand these items as tribute. Their intentions are indeed born of hardship. 

However, I believe this does not address the root of the problem; it is like "giving up eating because of a choking hazard" <note>original: "yin ye er fei shi" (因噎而廢食). A Chinese idiom meaning to abandon a necessary task due to a minor risk.</note>. The "Local Products" section of a county gazetteer is not intended to be a mere commentary on the *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese encyclopedia, often used by scholars to prove their erudition.</note> to show off one's extensive research. Nor should it be like the "Poetic Expositions on the Capitals" <note>original: "Fu Jingdu zhe" (賦京都者). A reference to the Han dynasty literary genre that used grand, often exaggerated lists of resources to praise a capital city’s wealth.</note>, which borrow exotic items from other regions just to boast of local riches. 

By examining the forests, rivers, and plains, we learn what the land is suited for. By knowing what is suitable, we can understand whether the people are poor or wealthy, diligent or lazy. The *Bureau of Maps* <note>original: "Zhifang Shi" (職方氏). An office described in the *Rites of Zhou* responsible for mapping and regional production.</note> states: "Its benefits include gold, tin, bamboo, and arrows; its livestock is suitable for the six domestic animals; its soil is suitable for the five grains." These records were not meant to be diminished or hidden just because later generations might have corrupt officials <term>tanmo; literally "greedy for ink," a term for corruption</term>. 

Nevertheless, there have indeed been instances where gazetteers have brought burdens upon both officials and the people. Rice is the same all over the world, yet the *Gazetteer of Yiyang* claims that its rice is unique and cannot be found in other counties. In the past, this led to the heavy burden of officials specifically demanding "Yiyang Rice." Pigs are the same all over the world, yet the *Gazetteer of Ganzhou* claims that "Dragon Pigs" cannot be found in other prefectures...

...and so it was. In the past, the pursuit of rarities like the "Dragon-Grip" <note>This likely refers to a rare plant or local curiosity mentioned on the previous page that caught the eye of officials.</note> was considered an extraordinary event. The local chroniclers of the time would dip their brushes in ink, boasting of the names of such things to bring honor to their region. Those seeking favor in the district then used these "boasts" to flatter their superiors. Gradually, this became an established precedent. Forced levies and wide-ranging demands followed; those who had to provide were exhausted, yet those receiving were never satisfied. If a strong-willed official <term>qiangxiang li; literally "stiff-necked official," meaning one who resists unfair orders</term> delayed or failed to deliver, reprimands and punishments followed immediately. Therefore, the common social ills that plague the people and corrupt the officials are truly the fault of those who offer such flattery.

This being the case, the writer of a gazetteer must instead state: "In such-and-such a village, there are mountains and marshes; their grains, livestock, and vegetables are suited for such-and-such a species. In another village, there are plains and lowlands; their grains, livestock, fruits, and vegetables are suited for such-and-such a species." If a village is poor and the soil is barren, producing nothing of note, then the sources of the people’s food and clothing—even if meager—should be recorded so that their level of wealth is clear. 

If timber, reeds, and rushes <note>original: "huan wei" (萑葦). Common plants used for weaving mats and building materials.</note> come from a specific village, or if medicinal herbs and flower buds come from another, these are the resources the people rely on for "the nourishment of the living and the burial of the dead," <note>A classic Confucian phrase referring to the essential cycle of life and the duties of filial piety.</note> as well as for firewood, cooking, and the art of planting. For timber, one must record its specific use; for medicines, one must investigate their primary healing properties. By describing the labor required for cultivation <term>peizhi; the intensive process of nurturing plants</term> and recording the difficulties of transport by water or land, the cost and the difficulty of obtaining these goods will be made clear. As for metals like gold and tin, or decorative feathers, if they are not naturally suited to the land, one must clarify exactly how they were obtained...

# Licorice
<note>original: 甘草, Gancao; Glycyrrhiza uralensis</note>

Licorice is categorized as a "Superior Grade" herb in the *Classic of Herbal Medicine* <note>original: 本經, Benjing; referring to the Shennong Bencao Jing, the foundational text of Chinese pharmacology</note>. In the *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, dating to the 3rd century BCE</note>, it is referred to as *fan* or "Great Bitter"; the commentary by Guo Pu identifies this as the licorice of the present day. The *Dream Pool Essays* <note>A scientific and literary work by the Song Dynasty polymath Shen Kuo (1031–1095)</note> states that licorice resembles the **locust tree** but with pointed leaves—a description that is extremely accurate. The *Classic of Poetry* says: "*Gathering the ling herbs, gathering the ling herbs, upon the heights of Mount Shouyang.*" Mount Shouyang is located in what is now Puzhou Prefecture. In the local customs of the Jin region <note>Modern-day Shanxi province</note>, people pluck the tender buds, mix them with flour, and steam them for food. Their flavor is as sweet as malt sugar. I suspect that the "gathering of *ling*" in the poem also refers to harvesting it as a vegetable.

The agriculturalist Yuyu says: "Licorice is the **State Elder** <term>Guolao; a title of respect given to licorice because it harmonizes the various ingredients in a medical formula, much like a senior statesman mediates between factions</term> of medicines; even women and children are familiar with its taste." In Guo Jingchun's <note>The courtesy name of Guo Pu (276–324), a famous scholar and commentator</note> encyclopedic notes on the *Erya* entry for "Great Bitter," he states it is today's licorice. However, some sources claim it grows in trailing vines with leaves like a lotus, or that it resembles *Rehmannia* <note>original: 地黃, Dihuang; also known as Chinese Foxglove</note>. In truth, licorice does not trail at all, nor does it look like a lotus. These descriptions are likely based on conflicting rumors or errors in transcription. It is especially unlike *Rehmannia*; the use of the word "some say" indicates that the original authors themselves had doubts.

Tao the Recluse <note>Tao Hongjing (456–536), a renowned Taoist physician and alchemist</note> also noted that it came from the Hexi and Shangjun regions, but those trade routes are no longer open to the markets. Today, the herb comes from the Shu and Hanzhong regions; those that are firm and solid are considered the true variety. The "Locust-bud Herb" is the best. When the capital of the Jin Dynasty was moved and the western borders were cut off from the lands south of the river, scholars in the south could no longer verify the plant through direct observation...

# **Shu** <note>original: 朮, Shù; referring to the medicinal root of the Atractylodes plant</note>

**Shu** was categorized as a substance of the "Superior Grade" in the *Classic of Materia Medica* <note>original: 本經, Běnjīng; the Shénnóng Běncǎo Jīng, which lists non-toxic herbs that promote longevity</note>. The *Classic of Illustrated Materia Medica* <note>a Song dynasty work by Su Song</note> identifies the "Yangbao" variety as **White Atractylodes** <note>original: 白朮, Báishù</note>. It was only from the Song Dynasty onward that practitioners began to distinguish between the two varieties—**Black** <term>Cangzhu: Atractylodes lancea, typically used for drying dampness and digestive issues</term> and **White** <term>Baishu: Atractylodes macrocephala, used for tonifying the Spleen and Qi</term>—and apply them to different medicinal purposes.

Li Shizhen <note>author of the Compendium of Materia Medica</note> considered "Horse Thistle" to be the "Large Thistle," yet he also identified it as **White Atractylodes**. Since **Shu** is named "Mountain Thistle," how can one simply equate the common Thistle with **Shu**?

## **Yuanzhi** <note>original: 遠志, Yuǎnzhì; *Polygala tenuifolia*, commonly known as Siberian Milkwort. The name literally translates to "Far-reaching Ambition."</note>

**Yuanzhi** is listed as a "superior grade" herb in the *Classic of the Roots and Herbs* <note>original: 本經, Benjing; referring to the *Shennong Ben Cao Jing*, the oldest Chinese materia medica</note>. In the *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, dating to the 3rd century BCE</note>, it is referred to as *Yaorao* <note>original: 葽繞</note> or *Jiyao* <note>original: 棘葽</note>; the commentary confirms these are the modern **Yuanzhi**. It resembles **Mahuang** <note>original: 麻黃, Ephedra</note>, but with red flowers and leaves that are sharp and yellow. This description is brief, yet it captures the plant's appearance perfectly.

The *Shuowen* <note>original: 說文; the *Shuowen Jiezi*, an early 2nd-century dictionary</note> identifies *Yao* and *Jiyao* as the same plant in its commentary. It is also called the "Yao Grass." It is said that "in the fourth month, the Yao Grass flourishes." The scholar Liu Xiang noted that because this herb has a bitter taste, it was named *Yao* <note>A word often associated with lushness but here linked by Liu to medicinal properties</note>. Thus, *Yao* and *Yaorao* might actually be different things, though some commentators on the *Classic of Poetry* simply treat *Yao* as **Yuanzhi**.

The *Illustrated Classic* <note>original: 圖經, Tujing; the *Bencao Tujing* compiled by Su Song in 1061</note> records several varieties. One is described as "resembling **Daqing** <note>original: 大青; *Isatis tinctoria* or similar blue-dye plants</note> but smaller, with white flowers blooming in the third month," though its place of origin is unknown. The variety currently produced in Taiyuan matches the illustration found in the *Materia Medica for Relief of Famine* <note>original: 救荒本草, Jiuhuang Bencao</note>. The original illustrations of the Jiezhou variety of **Yuanzhi** should not differ significantly from those found in Taiyuan.

Li Shizhen <note>The renowned author of the *Compendium of Materia Medica* (Bencao Gangmu)</note> claimed there are two types: large-leaf and small-leaf. In Southern Yunnan <note>Diannan</note>, there is a "Sweet **Yuanzhi**" with large leaves and yellow flowers, but the local people do not use it in medical prescriptions. Generally, as this is a commonly used item, the specimens collected by modern pharmacies are more reliable than the depictions found on the old regional maps of the past.

# **Weirui** <note>original: 萎蕤, *Wēiruí*. This plant is now more commonly known in Chinese medicine as *Yuzhu* (Jade Bamboo) and in English as Solomon’s Seal (*Polygonatum odoratum*). The name *Weirui* poetically describes its drooping, lush foliage.</note>

**Weirui** is documented in the *Classic of the Roots and Herbs* <note>original: 本經, *Benjing*; the *Shennong Ben Cao Jing*</note> as a plant of the highest medicinal grade. 

<note>Based on the accompanying illustration:</note> The plant is characterized by a graceful, arching stem. # **Weirui** <note>original: 萎蕤, *Wēiruí*. Solomon’s Seal (*Polygonatum odoratum*).</note>

**Weirui** is indeed the same plant listed as <term>Nürui</term> <note>original: 女蕤; literally "Feminine Lushness." This name is considered an archaic synonym or scribal error for Weirui.</note> in the "Superior Grade" section of the *Classic of the Roots and Herbs* <note>original: 本經, *Benjing*</note>. The *Erya* <note>the oldest surviving Chinese dictionary</note> refers to it as *Ying* or *Weiwei*. It is likely that the *Classic of the Roots and Herbs* also originally used the name *Weiwei* <note>委萎</note>, but the upper portion of the character *Wei* [委] was lost during transcription, leading to the mistaken character *Nü* [女]. 

The *Materia Medica for Famine Relief* <note>original: 救荒本草, *Jiuhuang Bencao*</note> states that its root resembles that of <term>Huangjing</term> <note>original: 黃精; *Polygonatum sibiricum*, often called "Yellow Essence" or King Solomon’s Seal</note> but is smaller. 

Upon careful examination today, there are two distinct varieties: 
1. One has thin leaves, shaped like bamboo leaves but wider. Its roots resemble those of *Huangjing*—long, white, and very fibrous. This is the true **Weirui**. 
2. The other has thick leaves similar to those of *Huangjing*, but they are round and short. The roots do not grow deep but are also quite fibrous. Folk physicians consider this to be a different species. Both types are recorded in Li Yan’s *Manual of Bamboos*.

The name *Huangjing* appeared only in later records. Since various books categorize *Weiwei* as a relative of *Huangjing*, it stands to reason that ancient medical prescriptions likely used them interchangeably.

## **Weirui** <note>original: 委萎, *Wěiwěi*; a variant name for Solomon's Seal.</note>

People fully believe the legend of the **Hairy Woman** <note>original: 毛女, *Máo Nǚ*; a famous figure in Chinese folklore who fled to the mountains to escape the collapse of the Qin Dynasty. By eating herbs, she allegedly became immortal, grew hair to stay warm, and attained superhuman agility.</note> who ate **Yellow Essence** <note>original: 黃精, *Huángjīng*; King Solomon's Seal.</note> and became so light and agile that she could fly and leap like a mountain ape. Could **Weirui** not be of the same class of plants? 

It is truly strange that while "Lacquer Leaf" <note>original: 漆葉, *Qīyè*; a plant associated with the legendary physician Hua Tuo’s longevity formulas.</note> is known to everyone, it is no longer used in medical prescriptions. If **Hua Tuo** <note>the most famous physician of the Han Dynasty, often considered the "God of Surgery"</note> were here today to offer a formula, would people even be willing to believe in it fully? 

Generally speaking, those who live in the mountains and drink from the valleys are shielded from worldly affairs. They have no rich meats to cloud their digestive tracts, no seductive music or beauty to corrupt their ears and eyes, and no shifting joys or sorrows to disturb their spirits. Living in a wild, unrefined state, they remain as pure as the people of the primordial ages. To them, the fruits of herbs and trees are their natural "five grains." In Nanyang, those who drank from the "Chrysanthemum Spring" reached extreme old age; in Yazhou, those who ate sweet potatoes likewise lived long lives. 

Those who consume **Weirui**, even if they do not become "Earthly Immortals," should certainly ward off disease and resist aging. 

In later generations, when people reached the heights of status and wealth, they began to long for immortality. Putting aside the First Emperor of Qin and Emperor Wu of Han <note>both famous for their desperate and failed searches for elixirs of life</note>, even men like **Li Zhanhuang** <note>original: 李贊皇; referring to the Tang Dynasty statesman Li Deyu</note> and **Gao Pian** <note>a late Tang general</note> were deceived by wandering alchemists.

Skilled doctors then had to formulate medicines to "detoxify the minerals" just to save them. How truly unwise! 

I recall an anecdote from a story: after a sudden mountain flood, something came washing down along with the wood and stones...

## **Weirui** <note>Solomon’s Seal (*Polygonatum odoratum*)</note>

His hair and beard were like matted moss. His neighbors, seeing how tame and gentle he was, realized he was a man. He had apparently held his breath <note>practicing *bixi*, a Daoist breathing technique to achieve stillness</note> for an unknown number of years, yet he possessed no method for true ascension to the heavens. He sat there like a block of wood, devoid of conscious thought, yet his spirit remained pure and firm. When people from near and far heard of him, they believed he was an immortal and vied to welcome and provide for him.

At first, he practiced "internal vision" <note>a meditative practice of focusing one's gaze inward to cultivate the spirit</note>, but gradually he began to crave food and drink. Before long, he was consuming meat and wine, and soon after, he began to desire the "Human Way" <note>a polite reference to sexual relations</note>. When seekers asked him for the essential secrets of immortality, he had no teachings to pass on. Ultimately, he died from his own drunken lusts.

So then, if one lacks a "spiritual root" <note>innate virtue or spiritual capacity</note> yet obtains "mystical arts," could there really be such a thing as a "foolish and blind immortal" in the heavens? Alas! Could there ever be an immortal in the heavens who is not loyal or filial? The Sage <note>Confucius</note> said: "While you do not know life, how can you know death?" If one truly understands life, that is precisely how one avoids death.

According to recent observations, the plant currently used as **Weirui** is commonly called **Jade Bamboo** <note>original: 玉竹, *Yuzhu*</note> because its roots are long, white, and jointed like bamboo. # *Shengma* (Bugbane)

*Shengma* <note>Original: 升麻. Literally "Rising Hemp." Scientifically classified in the *Cimicifuga* genus, it is a staple of East Asian herbal medicine.</note> is listed as a "superior grade" herb in the *Classic of the Materia Medica*. <note>Original: *Benjing* (*Shennong Bencao Jing*), the foundation of Chinese pharmacology.</note> According to the *Illustrated Classic*, <note>Original: *Tujing* (*Bencao Tujing*), an 11th-century illustrated pharmacopeia.</note> its leaves resemble those of hemp. In the fourth or fifth month, it produces flowers like spikes of chestnut blossoms, white in color. Its seeds are black and its roots are purple. Today, in the Jiangxi and Huguang regions, there is a "local *Shengma*" that differs from the descriptions in the *Illustrated Classic* and is categorized separately as a folk herbal medicine.

The Farmer of Yulou <note>The "Farmer of Yulou" (Yulou Nong) is the pseudonym used by the author, Wu Qijun, when offering his personal observations and critiques.</note> states: The "Geographic Treatise" of the *Book of Han* mentions a plant called *Mumi* in Yizhou. <note>Original: 益州 (*Yizhou*), roughly modern Sichuan.</note> The commentator Li Qi noted that the word *mi* is pronounced like *ma* (hemp), and that this is indeed *Shengma*, a detoxifying medicine. The *Miscellaneous Morsels from Youyang* <note>Original: *Youyang Zazu*, a 9th-century collection of lore and natural oddities.</note> records that in Jianning Prefecture, there is a "Mumi Mountain." When birds eat the "Bird’s Beak" plant <note>Original: *Niaohui* (鳥喙), a name for Aconite or Monkshood, which is extremely toxic.</note> and are poisoned, they immediately fly to Mumi Mountain and peck at the *Mumi* grass to counteract the toxin. 

This confirms that *Shengma* is a native product of the Yunnan region. <note>Original: *Dian* (滇), the historical name for Yunnan province.</note> Yunnan has an abundance of Aconite; the *Shengma* used in the local prescriptions there is likely the "true" *Shengma*. Its leaves resemble hemp and its flowers form spikes, which matches the description of the Maozhou variety of *Shengma* in the *Illustrated Classic*. Since Yunnan and Sichuan share a border, their flora should naturally be categorized together. 

However, the *Illustrated Classic* also lists three other varieties from Chuzhou, Taizhou, and Hanzhou. The type produced in Hanzhou is shaped like a bamboo shoot. Local doctors in Hubei currently use this variety to "raise and express" smallpox pustules; <note>Original: *Douchuang* (痘瘡). In traditional medicine, *Shengma* was used to help "push" the rash to the surface to speed recovery.</note> its appearance is identical to the Hanzhou variety, and the branches and leaves are very similar.

He clarifies regional variations, noting that while the Yunnan variety is the "true" species, other regional types are effectively used in treating smallpox.</summary>

# Xu Changqing

<term>Xu Changqing</term> <note>Scientific name: *Cynanchum paniculatum*. Unlike most herbs named for their properties, this plant is named after a person, allegedly a physician or official from the Han or Tang dynasty.</note> is listed as a "superior grade" herb in the <term>Classic of Materia Medica</term> <note>Original: *Benjing* (Shennong Bencao Jing), the foundational text of Chinese herbal medicine.</note>. The <term>Tang Materia Medica</term> notes that it grows in various river valleys and marshes. Its leaves resemble those of a willow, growing in opposite pairs with a glossy surface. The roots are similar to <term>Asarum</term> <note>Original: *Xi Xin*, or Wild Ginger.</note> but are slightly coarser, long, yellow in color, and possess a strong, rank odor. The <term>Sichuan Materia Medica</term> states that the seeds resemble those of the <term>Broom-creeper</term> <note>Original: *Luo Mo* (*Metaplexis japonica*).</note> but are smaller and contain a kernel. Its appearance is likely what the folk healers of Hunan call "Earth Asarum" or "Nine-Headed Lion Grass." However, none of the various botanical texts have described its flowers in detail, which remains a point of uncertainty.

The **Farmer of Yulou** <note>The pen name of the author, Wu Qijun.</note> says: Laozi taught that "The Great Way has no name." Is Heaven not the Way? It is manifest and positioned above, yet do we not name it "Heaven"? Is a Sage not the Way? Great and capable of transformation, yet do we not name them "Sage"? However, the <term>Xiongnu</term> <note>A confederation of nomadic peoples anciently inhabiting the eastern Eurasian Steppe.</note> call Heaven <term>Tengri</term> <note>Original: *Chengli* (撐犁), a transcription of the Turkic/Mongolic word for Sky or God.</note>, so they do not use the name "Heaven" to refer to Heaven. Those in the West call the Sage "Buddha," so they do not use the name "Sage" to refer to the Sage. If one does not use a specific name to name a thing, are "Heaven" and "Sage" truly fixed names? The vinegar mites in a vat take the mouth of the jar to be their "Heaven"—is that not "Heavening" their Heaven? The heavy drinker calls clear wine a "Sage"—is that not "Saging" their Sage?

<note>Wu Qijun uses this philosophical digression to illustrate that names (like the person's name "Xu Changqing" given to a plant) are often arbitrary or culturally specific, yet they point toward a shared underlying reality.</note>

# Xu Changqing

And what of the Sages? When we descend to the level of individuals and objects, are their names not equally non-unique? Consider figures like <term>Ziche Zhenhu</term> <note>A nobleman of the State of Qin; his name "Zhenhu" literally means "Needle Tiger."</note>, <term>Shusun Bao</term> <note>A high official of the State of Lu; "Bao" means "Leopard."</note>, <term>Minzi Ma</term> <note>"Ma" means "Horse."</note>, or the Prime Minister <term>Zilan</term> <note>"Zilan" means "Purple Orchid."</note>. These are men, not objects or animals. When a human lacks a unique name and adopts the name of a thing, does the name of the thing turn the human into that thing?

Conversely, we have things like the <term>Jumping Spider</term> <note>Original: *Yinghu*, literally "Fly Tiger."</note>, the <term>Little Cuckoo</term> <note>Original: *Xiebao*, literally "Thank Leopard."</note>, the <term>Piebald Beast</term> <note>Original: *Boma*, literally "Piebald Horse."</note>, and the <term>Indian Aster</term> <note>Original: *Malan*, literally "Horse Orchid."</note>. Consider <term>Xu Changqing</term>, <term>Wangsun</term> <note>Literally "Prince" or "Grandson of a King"; also refers to several medicinal plants like *Paris polyphylla* or *Fritillaria*.</note>, <term>Duyou</term> <note>Literally "Postal Officer"; another name for the herb *Paris polyphylla*.</note>, and <term>Shijun</term> <note>Literally "Envoy" or "Lord"; the common name for the Rangoon Creeper, *Quisqualis indica*.</note>. And for those humans who are actually named Changqing, Wangsun, Duyou, or Shijun—is it because they are named after things that they must hide their status as humans?

Those who speak of clarifying "Names and Realities" say: "A bird is not a bird; a magpie is not a magpie." Does this imply that if you name a bird, it must strictly be a bird, and if you name a magpie, it must be a magpie? In the vastness of the world and the staggering diversity of the "ten thousand things," can every single one be named as clearly as "bird" or "magpie"? Or can we truly force the <term>Zhuli</term> <note>A classical term for foreign, "barbarian," or unintelligible languages.</note> tongues of distant lands to always call a bird "bird" and a magpie "magpie"?

Extrapolating from this, even the names of fiefs, prefectures, and nations are established simply to distinguish one territory from another...

Had Emperor Shenzong of the Song been content to be a ruler who maintained peace and followed the path of his predecessors—not greedily expanding the borders, hoarding wealth, or radically overturning the old laws—the Song dynasty might have remained militarily weak, but the hearts of the people would not have been lost. Perhaps the dynasty would have lasted for many generations without the tragedy of the southern migration <note>Refers to the fall of the Northern Song capital in 1127 and the court's flight to the south</note>. 

Consider the examples of the great ministers: Li Wenzheng <note>Li Hang (947–1004), a chancellor famed for his integrity and foresight</note> did not submit writings focused on the mere calculation of profit and harm. Sima Wenzheng <note>Sima Guang (1019–1086), the preeminent historian and statesman who advocated for traditional Confucian governance</note>, when discussing the desire to capture Liangzuo <note>The ruler of the Western Xia state (r. 1048–1067)</note> and force a surrender, argued that even if one Liangzuo were destroyed, another would simply rise in his place. # White Swallow-wort

White Swallow-wort <note>original: "Baiwei" (Cynanchum atratum). This plant is known in traditional medicine for its cooling properties and its ability to treat fevers and urinary tract infections.</note> is listed in the *Classic of Materia Medica* <note>The *Shennong Bencao Jing*, the oldest known Chinese pharmaceutical text</note> as a "Middle Grade" medicine <term>Zhongpin: The middle category of medicines in ancient texts, considered to have medicinal power to treat illness but potentially possessing some toxicity, unlike the "Superior" herbs meant for longevity</term>. According to the *Materia Medica for Famine Relief* <note>A 15th-century botanical work focused on edible wild plants for survival during crop failures</note>, the tender pods and tender leaves can all be blanched and eaten. 

The plants produced in Jiangxi and Hunan provinces are identical. The roots are long and numerous; for this reason, folk doctors call it "White Dragon Whiskers." 

I note that several medicines, such as Asarum <note>original: "Xixin" (Wild Ginger)</note> and Chloranthus <note>original: "Jiji" (Chloranthus fortunei)</note>, all utilize the root for their medicinal properties. These roots are generally long with many fibrous whiskers and are quite similar in appearance. Various medical authorities distinguish between them based on whether the root is yellowish-white, soft, brittle, thick, or thin. However, the seedlings and leaves of these various plants are completely dissimilar. 

Because many authors tend to omit descriptions of the foliage, folk doctors often have no standard to follow when identifying the plants in the wild. Ironically, it is because they rely solely on local common names for harvesting that they avoid making errors or causing confusion.

<summary>This page introduces White Swallow-wort (Baiwei), noting its classification in ancient texts and its use as a famine food. The author, Wu Qijun, observes that while many medicinal roots look similar, their leaves distinguish them—a fact often overlooked by scholars but preserved by folk practitioners.</summary>
