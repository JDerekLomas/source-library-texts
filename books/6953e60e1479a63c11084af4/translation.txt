The text used is (with a few unimportant modifications) that of Kühn (Volume II), as edited by Georg Helmreich; Teubner, Leipzig, 1893. <note>Karl Gottlob Kühn (1754–1840) produced the monumental twenty-volume edition of Galen's works which remains a standard reference for scholars.</note> The numbers of the pages of Kühn's edition are printed at the side of the Greek text <note>original: "Greek text"</note>, a parallel mark (||) in the line indicating the exact point of division between Kühn's pages.

Words in the English text which are enclosed in square brackets are supplementary or explanatory; practically all explanations, however, are relegated to the footnotes or introduction. In the footnotes, also, attention is drawn to words which are of particular <term>philological|The study of the history and development of languages</term> interest from the point of view of modern medicine.

My best thanks are due to the Editors for their courtesy and for the kindly interest they have taken in the work. I have also gratefully to acknowledge the receipt of much assistance and encouragement from Sir William Osler <note>A Canadian physician and one of the "Big Four" founding professors of Johns Hopkins Hospital; he was a great bibliophile and historian of medicine</note>, Regius Professor of Medicine at Oxford, and from Dr. J. D. Comrie, first lecturer on the History of Medicine at Edinburgh University. Professor D’Arcy W. Thompson of University College, Dundee, and Sir W. T. Thiselton-Dyer, late director of the Royal Botanic Gardens at Kew, have very kindly helped me to identify several animals and plants mentioned by Galen <note>Galen of Pergamon (129–c. 216 AD) was a Greek physician and philosopher whose theories dominated Western medical science for over 1,300 years</note>.

I cannot conclude without expressing a word of gratitude to my former biological teachers, Professors Patrick Geddes and J. Arthur Thomson. The experience reared on the foundation of their teaching has gone far to help me in interpreting the great medical biologist of Greece.

I should be glad to think that the present work might help, however little, to hasten the coming reunion between the “humanities” <note>The study of classical Greek and Latin literature and philosophy</note> and modern biological science; their present separation I believe to be against the best interest of both.

A. J. B. <note>Arthur John Brock (1879–1947), a physician known for his interest in sociology and the classics</note>
