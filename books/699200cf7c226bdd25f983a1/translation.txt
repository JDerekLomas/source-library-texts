# ERHARD RATDOLT

### AND HIS WORK IN VENICE, 1476 TO 1486.

Modern research has shed a great deal of light on the chronicles of the early Italian press. The facts relating specifically to printing in Venice have recently been clearly explained by Mr. Horatio Brown in his *History of the Venetian Printing Press*. Mr. Brown deals thoroughly with the incorrectly dated <term>colophon</term> <note>A colophon is a statement at the end of a book giving information about its publication, such as the printer's name and the date.</note> of the *Adornment of Girls* <note>original Latin: "Decor Puellarum"</note> by Nicolas Jenson. This date has caused much confusion regarding the earliest book printed in Venice. Brown's arguments, and his summary of the authorities who support or oppose the accuracy of the date given there—1461—leave little or nothing more to be said on the subject.

To my mind, a fact emphasized by Bernard¹—namely, that this work, on page 59, recommends that young girls study certain other books known to have been printed by Jenson—provides very strong evidence. These works, specifically *Devout Words*, *The Palm of Virtues*, and *The Glory of Women* <note>originals: "Parole Devote," "Palma Virtutum," and "Gloria Mulierum"</note>, were already printed when the *Adornment of Girls* was published. All these works, along with one other not included here, were first printed in 1471. The paper, the type, and the *format* are in all cases absolutely identical to those of the controversial edition dated 1461. Furthermore, Bernard points out that such errors are far from...

The interval between the arrival in Venice of the de Spira brothers and the arrival of the famous partnership headed by Ratdolt—whose work I propose to bring to your attention this evening—needs no special comments on this occasion. It was a period of immense activity throughout Italy in the progress of the art of printing, and Venice takes a proud position in the excellence and value of the work she produced. In the decade between 1470 and 1480, we find the names of no fewer than fifty <term>typographers</term> <note>Early printers who designed, cast, and set their own type.</note>, almost all Germans, who practiced their art in this city alone. Mr. Brown points out a probable reason for the stay of so many German printers in Venice. It was on the great highway between Germany and Italy over the Brenner Pass, and would be one of the first important cities a printer would reach on his southward journey. Moreover, as we know, at the close of the fifteenth century Venice contained a German exchange <note>The *Fondaco dei Tedeschi*, a famous center for German merchants in Venice.</note> greatly frequented by travelers and merchants, and it would thus be a place where newcomers would be likely to hear of employment.

We will move on, then, to the year 1476, the date of the first work by Ratdolt and his companions. In that year they completed in two different languages, Latin and Italian, the *Calendar* <note>original Latin: "Kalendarium"</note> of the eminent astronomer Johann Müller of Königsberg, better known by his Latinized name Regiomontanus (or in Italian, Monteregio). The choice of this work, as I shall attempt to show, is significant; Ratdolt, throughout his career in Italy, displayed a great fondness for books on astronomy and the mathematical sciences. On the title page of the *Calendar* we find the first mention of Erhard Ratdolt as a printer. He describes himself as "Erhard Ratdolt of Augsburg" <note>original Latin: "Erhardus Ratdolt de Augusta"</note>, and, associated with him, we have Bernardus Pictor (also of Augsburg) and Petrus Löslein of Langenzenn in Bavaria, who is sometimes called "corrector and partner" <note>original Latin: "corrector ac socius"</note>. Before I attempt to discuss the work of Ratdolt, I will pause to give a brief account of his career. His father was a carpenter and carver living in the imperial city of Augsburg, where his name is found in the tax records as early as 1439, residing in the street or quarter of St. Anthony. He appears annually in the tax books under various descriptions, still living in the same area, until 1462, when he evidently died. From then on, the tax was paid jointly by his elder son Hans and Erhard, the subject of this biography. The brothers lived together in their father's house until 1474, when Erhard’s name disappears from the tax records; no further mention of him is found there until 1486. This interval partly coincides with his stay in Venice, though it fails to account for a period of nearly two years before that.

Perhaps you will forgive me a short digression here, which will lead to a theory regarding Ratdolt's whereabouts between the time of his departure from Augsburg and the beginning of his career in Venice in 1476.

The famous astronomer Johann Müller—one of the most learned mathematicians of the fifteenth century—came to Nuremberg in 1471 after a long residence in Vienna (during part of which he studied under Georg von Peurbach) and a stay at the Court of Matthias Corvinus, King of Hungary. Here, with the help of Bernhard Welter, he established a private press in 1472 for the production of his *Calendar* and other works. This press was kept fully active until 1475, when Müller went to Rome at the request of Pope Sixtus IV to assist with the reform of the calendar. His subsequent election to the Bishopric of Ratisbon <note>The city of Regensburg, Germany.</note> and his death in 1476 ended his career as a printer. His workmen were likely scattered, and in this way Ratdolt—if not actually employed by Müller—may have at least become connected with some of his staff and gained knowledge of the publications issued from his press at Nuremberg.

Of Ratdolt’s life in Venice, I can find no trace beyond what is to be gathered from the dates and sequence of his books. No doubt his fame as a printer became widely known, and the quality of his productions was well known in his native city. So much was this the case that, as Zapf <note>Georg Wilhelm Zapf (1747–1810), a noted historian of early printing.</note>, the chronicler of the Augsburg press, informs us, upon the promotion of Count Friedrich von Hohenzollern to the Bishopric of Augsburg, Ratdolt was specially invited to return there in order to undertake the printing of the religious works for the diocese—a profitable and highly sought-after appointment, which he ultimately accepted. He departed from Venice, bringing back with him, as Dr. Muther points out, his decorated initial letters and woodcuts. These were the work of Italian artists and differed essentially in style and feeling from those in use at that time in Germany.

Ratdolt’s name reappears in the tax records of Augsburg for the first time after his long absence in 1486. For a period of twenty years, from 1486 to 1506, he lived on St. Katherine Street, his name being at first mentioned as Erhard Ratdolt, then as “Master Erhard Ratdolt,” and at times also as “Master Erhard Ratdolt (Book Printer).” In the year 1507, he seems to have moved to another house in the Frawengraben, and there he remained until 1527, as recorded in the various annual tax books. As late as November 9th of the year 1527, a certain Peter Kempter acknowledges his debt of fifteen gulden <note>A gold or silver coin used in parts of Europe; the term is often interchangeable with "guilder."</note> to Erhard Ratdolt in the judicial records. I will now move on to my main subject: an account of the works produced by his press during his stay in Venice. The *Kalendarium* (Calendar) <note>original: "Kalendarium"</note> by Johann Müller <note>Johann Müller von Königsberg (1436–1476), better known as Regiomontanus, a famous German mathematician and astronomer.</note>, in its original form as published by him from his press at Nuremberg around 1473, was a xylographic <term>printed from hand-carved woodblocks rather than movable type</term> production in quarto <note>A book size where each sheet of paper is folded twice to create four leaves (eight pages).</note> format, made from thirty-one woodblocks printed on both sides of the page. According to this writer, only three copies of this *Calendar* still exist: two are in Munich and one is in Dresden. From the facsimile provided in his *History of the Art of Printing* <note>original: "Geschichte der Buchdruckerkunst"</note>, the craftsmanship of the woodblocks appears to be extremely crude. However, according to Dr. Muther, the calendar of 1476—perhaps a later edition—contains several astronomical diagrams of solar and lunar eclipses.

The *Calendar* produced in Venice by Ratdolt is an exceptionally elegant work and is, in every way, a marvelous improvement over the crude prototype prepared in Nuremberg by Johann Müller. It is difficult to determine whether the Italian or the Latin edition was printed first, but Butsch <note>Albert Fidelis Butsch (1844–1899), a scholar of early book decoration.</note> favors the Latin one. The Italian version, which consists of thirty leaves <term>Leaves: In bibliography, a "leaf" is a single sheet of paper; one leaf equals two pages.</term>, begins with a poem as a preface or introduction. This seventeen-line poem gives an account of the contents and mentions the author; it also calls attention to the printers, whose names are said to be placed below in red ink. In accordance with this statement, after the imprint "Venice, 1476" <note>original: "Venetiis, 1476"</note>, the names Bernardus Pictor, Petrus Löslein, and Erhardus Ratdolt are printed in red. Their surnames are not capitalized. The poem begins with a boldly designed **Q** as an initial letter, which is four lines deep, and the entire page is surrounded on three sides by a graceful border made of three lines. The style of this ornament is typical of the Venetian school of this period. Renner’s book is, in many ways, a wonderfully close imitation of Ratdolt’s work. # PLATE XI.

**Dr. Burger** informs me that in the copy of this interesting book-list, which is preserved at Leipzig, there are a few wormholes, which may be observed in the present facsimile. One of these holes passes through the word forming the last line of the first column, "Salustius" <note>original: "Salustius"; the Roman historian Sallust</note>, which is rendered somewhat illegible because of it. Another wormhole has partially destroyed the letters *st* in "Astronomia" <note>original: "Astronomia"; Astronomy</note>, which was printed in red ink in the second column. **Dr. Burger** remarks that the list which forms No. 19 in Meyer's study (page 40 of the monograph) is printed with typefaces that often appear in the associated press of Johannes de Colonia <note>John of Cologne</note>, Nicolas Jenson, and others; this is a fact that escaped Meyer’s notice.

->G. R. R.<-
