<margin>The earliest history of the Greeks is not mythical.</margin>
One may believe, without being considered too gullible <note>original: "credulous"</note>, that the Greeks preserved very old memoirs in this manner—though perhaps not a formal history—stretching as far back as their gods, who were their first kings. These memoirs have been expanded and heavily corrupted regarding dates, people, and events; yet this does not destroy the historical root of the account. They held an ancient tradition that Uranus (also known as Caelus) was the sovereign of Greece and other lands; that he had sons called Titans; that Saturn was one of these and succeeded his father Uranus; and that Jupiter was the son of Saturn, who fought and defeated his uncles, the Titans, etc. This is the start of their history, and there is nothing absurd, inconsistent, or unbelievable about it; therefore, I think no serious objection can be made to its truth. However, the supernatural <note>original: "marvelous"</note> incidents and literary flourishes of the story were the work of later ages—the inventions of poets who, with their unique creative license, ignored all the (1) laws of history and chronology.

<margin>Why Hercules is joined with Bacchus.</margin>
