## I.

<margin>Who the Book **Theuerdanck** is / and what its Name means.</margin>
**T**he book called **Theuerdanck**, which we wish to bring to your attention, kind reader, explains in German metrical verse the arduous labors and overcome dangers of Maximilian I, the August Emperor. It is distinguished by this title: **The Adventures and a portion of the Stories of the Praiseworthy, Valiant, and Highly Renowned Hero and Knight, Theuerdanck.** <note>original: *Die Geuerlicheiten und eins teils der Geschichten des löblichen streitbaren und hochberümbten Helds und Ritters Tewrdannckhs.*</note> Among the common people, it is known by the simpler name **The Theuerdanck**, which it also bears in the privilege <note>A legal document granting exclusive printing rights.</note> granted by Maximilian to the printer. This fictional name is used to honor Maximilian throughout the work. Just as poets of old were accustomed to naming their poems after the illustrious persons whose fates they set forth, and just as even today the writers of romances have presented *Arminius*, *Octavia*, and *Argenis* <note>These were popular Baroque "romans à clef," or novels where fictional characters represented real political figures.</note> to the public light, so also for the same reason this book was titled **Theuerdanck**. We must, however, declare here what this name signifies regarding its etymology, because we observe that its genuine meaning is hidden from some. It should be known, therefore, that Maximilian was distinguished by the name **Theuerdanck** in this poem because from his earliest age he turned all his care and thought toward difficult matters and aspired to perform the greatest deeds. The <term>Pfintzingiana Key</term> <note>original: *Clavis Pfintzingiana*; a guide or "key" written by Melchior Pfinzing, the emperor's chaplain, to explain the allegorical characters in the poem.</note> declares this in these words:

> **He is called Theuerdanck for this reason: that from his youth he directed all his thoughts toward noble deeds.** <note>original German: *Ist darum Tewrdannckh genannt / daß er von Jugend auff all sein Gedancken nach theuerlichen Sachen gericht.*</note>

Therefore, those monstrous words—*Caraegrates*, or *Carigratius*, or even *Rarigratius*—only provoke laughter. These were concocted with misguided diligence by **VINCENTIUS PLACCIUS** <note>A 17th-century German scholar known for cataloging anonymous works.</note> in his *Theater of Anonymous Authors, chapter 11, page 478*, while he was striving to express the German word in Latin. From the authentic interpretation provided by the author himself, it is clear how absurd it is to wish to pretend that the name meant "Dear Thanks" or "Rare..."

## IX.

<margin>The standard for the Book was the **Books of Heroes** <note>German: *die Helden-Bücher*</note>.</margin>
Although our Melchior Pfinzing <note>The primary author/editor of *Theuerdanck*</note> ignored those rules of Heroic Poetry which the Ancients observed so accurately and the Moderns have extracted and imitated from their surviving writings—or rather, was ignorant of them in an age not yet so refined—he nevertheless directed his efforts in crafting the book **Theuerdanck** entirely toward the standard and constitution of the Old German epic poems. He confesses as much both in the Dedication and in the "key" to the Book. In this pursuit of imitation, he acted laudably, because he followed the honors of his homeland rather than following in the footsteps of poets of another language with foolish affectation—whose flourishes and embellishments the heroic simplicity of our mother tongue sometimes utterly rejects. 

Truly, those epic poems called **the Books of Heroes** owe their first origin to the earliest times of our nation’s founding. However, in those ancient centuries, when our Germans (according to the testimony of Tacitus <note>A Roman historian who wrote *Germania*, a primary source on early Germanic tribes</note>) were ignorant of letters, it was not books but heroic songs that were in use. For they sang of Tuisto and Mannus <note>Legendary figures described by Tacitus as the ancestors of the Germanic peoples</note>, the founders of the race. Even after the age of Tacitus, this custom did not expire; in the following times, the heroes who fell in war for their country or in some other way protected the liberty of the citizens were sung about by their people. 

Charlemagne <note>Latin: *Carolus Magnus*</note> collected songs of this kind and, since letters were either first introduced or became common in Germany at that time, he saw to it that they were transcribed. This was a significant benefit for those times, but of no use to our own age, since the stretch of centuries intervening between the Great Emperor and us has snatched these works away from us as well. Nevertheless, there lie hidden here and there more recent manuscript collections of such heroic songs.
