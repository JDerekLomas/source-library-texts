# Treatise on Armament Technology
<note>original title: 武備志 (Wubei Zhi)</note>

<term>Wubei Zhi<gloss>literally "Records of Military Preparation" or "Treatise on Armament Technology"</gloss></term> is the most comprehensive military encyclopedia in Chinese history. Compiled in 1621 by <term>Mao Yuanyi<gloss>a military scholar and official of the late Ming Dynasty</gloss></term>, this massive work consists of 240 volumes. It serves as a monumental record of Ming Dynasty military thought, covering subjects ranging from strategy and troop formations to weapons, defensive architecture, and maritime navigation.

### Assigned Numbering for the Inner Dike and Associated Stations

<insert>
The method of defense using "Grid Dikes" <term>Ge-ti<gloss>Transverse or "check" dikes built perpendicular to the main dikes. They create compartments that trap water if the main dike is breached, preventing the flood from spreading laterally along the entire riverbank.</gloss></term> is truly ingenious. Because the Inner Dike <note>original: "縷堤" (Lü-ti)</note> cannot be relied upon alone, should a breach occur, the "cross-flow" <note>water flowing out of the channel</note> will hit the Grid Dike and be stopped, preventing a general inundation. Once the water recedes, it can be drained back from the high ground. 

Currently, at the south bank locations of Fang Village, Dankou, Shuanggou, Majia, Danping’an, and Niu Mountain, these dikes have all been constructed within the Xuzhou border. This single line of Grid Dikes must be reinforced and repaired year after year; thus, there will be no concern about the river splitting its flow or "stealing" the channel <note>referring to the river carving a new, destructive path through farmland</note>. The labor required for each section is limited, but by continuing to add to the construction, the banks will be strengthened. In this matter, "the more, the better" truly applies.

### Chronicles of the Caoqing River

<insert>
The Caoqing River was excavated in the 32nd year of the <term>Jiajing era<note>1553.</note></term>. At the time it was opened, it provided a route to the Prefectural City, but it was later blocked. The transport canal winding north from Danyang originally lacked its own source of flowing water. It relies entirely on the connection between the rivers and lakes to facilitate transport via the tides; when the tide is high, the canal is full, but when the tide is low, it runs dry. There is the **Lian Lake** <note>original: "練湖"; historically a massive artificial reservoir used to regulate the Grand Canal’s water levels</note> district which might seem capable of being diverted into the canal, but it too lacks a natural spring source. During the heavy rains of autumn, the runoff collects to form the lake, but during a severe drought, the transport canal dries up and the lake dries up as well. Furthermore, when the lake water enters the canal, the current tends to rush eastward, offering no benefit to the depth of the transport canal.

### Page 23

The passage spans over one hundred *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters or 0.3 miles</note>, where the surging waves are as white as snow. At this point, set sail immediately, or perhaps turn back and head northeast. To the north lies **Saddle Mountain** <note>original: "馬鞍山" (Ma’anshan)</note> and **Bamboo Mountain Island** <note>original: "竹山島" (Zhushandao)</note>. North of these are the **Dry Gate Wide Shoals** <note>original: "旱門漫灘" (Hanmen Mantan)</note>; all of these locations are suitable for anchoring.

If the southeast wind is strong, do not attempt to strike against it. Heading north will bring you to **Mount Cheng** <note>original: "成山" (Chengshan), the prominent eastern cape of Shandong</note>. If you are within the **Official Green Water** <note>original: "官緑水" (Guanlüshui); traditional Chinese navigators used distinct water color changes—from black to green to yellow—to determine their distance from the coast and water depth</note> of the Northern Sea and see **Manifest Spirit Mountain** <note>original: "顯神山" (Xianshenshan)</note> and the **Peach West** <note>original: "桃西" (Taoxi)</note> range stretching out in a long, level line, you have likely sailed too far north. In that case, immediately turn back and head northeast.

After passing **Mount Cheng**, sail due west. Ahead lies **Cock-crow Islet** <note>original: "雞鳴嶼" (Jimingyu)</note>; there are submerged reefs nearby, so be sure to avoid them. To the west is **Lady Islet** <note>original: "夫人嶼" (Furenyu)</note>, which is unnavigable. You must reach **Liu Island** <note>original: "劉島" (Liudao), modern-day Liugong Island</note>, where the western side provides a safe anchorage.

From **Liu Island**, sail due west to reach **Zhifu Island** <note>original: "芝罘島" (Zhifudao), a landmark near modern Yantai</note>. There is a "gate" <note>original: "門" (men); a navigable channel between landmasses</note> to the northeast that you can enter. About one hundred *li* to the northwest of here, there is a **Black Reef** <note>original: "黑礁" (Heijiao)</note> about three or four *mu* in size <note>A *mu* is a unit of area; three or four *mu* is approximately half an acre</note>; avoid it.

To the southeast of **Eight-corners Island** <note>original: "八角島" (Bajiaodao)</note> is another navigable channel. With a favorable wind, it takes half a day to travel from **Zhifu Island** past **Mozhikou** <note>original: "抹直口"</note>. From the mouth of the **New River** <note>original: "新河" (Xinhe)</note>, one arrives at **Shamen Island** <note>original: "沙門島" (Shamendao); the main island of the Miaodao archipelago, which serves as a vital stepping stone across the Bohai Strait</note>. To the southeast, there are shallows. Hug the deep water to enter through the South Gate. There is an entrance to the east, but it contains submerged reefs. There is also an entrance to the northwest; one can anchor at **Shamen Island**.

Setting sail upon the open ocean and heading north, you will pass **Tuoji Mountain** <note>original: "砣磯山" (Tuojishan)</note>, **Qin Island** <note>original: "欽島" (Qindao)</note>, and **Ji Island** <note>original: "汲島" (Jidao)</note>. There are reefs to the southwest of **Huangyang Stream**. To the east, the route reaches **Ping Island Port**, where one may anchor inside.

In the South Sea and the Outer Sea, the mountain ridges all seem to terminate. Looking east, one sees the **Three Mountains** <note>original: "三山" (Sanshan)</note>; enter directly through the center. Inside, there are northern and southern sandbars that connect, where it is possible to anchor. To the west of the **Three Mountains** lies **South Mountain** <note>original: "南山" (Nanshan)</note>. This route reaches **Qingniwa** <note>original: "青泥窪" (Qingniwa); this swampy inlet was the original site of what is now the city of Dalian</note>.

Along the way, keep the islands in sight and reach **Yellow Island** <note>original: "黃島" (Huangdao)</note>. **Convenient Island** <note>original: "便島" (Biandao)</note> resembles **Iron Mountain** <note>original: "鐵山" (Tieshan)</note>. West of **Gaizhou** <note>original: "蓋州" (Gaizhou); modern-day Gaizhou in Liaoning Province</note>, sighting the **Pagoda Tower** <note>original: "寶塔臺" (Baotatai)</note> indicates the location of **Liangfang Mouth** <note>original: "梁房口" (Liangfangkou)</note>. Enter the **Sancha River** <note>original: "三叉河" (Sanchaché)</note> to dock at the **Niuzhuang Pier** <note>original: "牛壯馬頭" (Niuzhuang matou); Niuzhuang was a critical port for grain transport during the Ming dynasty</note> anchorage.

From **Zhigu** <note>original: "直沽" (Zhigu); the historical port area of modern-day Tianjin</note>, set sail upon the open ocean, heading east with a southward tilt <note>original: "挑南" (*tiaonan*); a navigational term for adjusting the compass heading slightly off a cardinal direction</note>. Sail in a <term>straight line <note>original: "一字行" (*yizi xing*); literally "one-character line," meaning to maintain a steady, direct course without tacking</note></term> for one day and night to sight **Niubian** and **Shamen Island**. 

If the southward tilt is too great, you will surely see the **Three Mountains** of **Laizhou**. If you then head northeast for half a day, you will see **Shamen Island**. If you tilt too far north, you will mostly see **Tuoji Mountain**. To the south, the route reaches **Dengzhou Guard** <note>original: "登州衛" (Dengzhou Wei); the primary naval headquarters of the Ming coastal defense in present-day Penglai</note>. 

Upon setting sail from **Shamen Island**, there are shallows at the southeastern mountain point. Hug the middle and sail east. With favorable winds, it takes one day and night to reach **Liu Island** <note>original: "劉島" (Liudao); modern Liugong Island off the coast of Weihai</note>. From **Liu Island**, set sail upon the open ocean, heading east with a northward tilt. Turn at **Chengshan Point** <note>original: "成山嘴" (Chengshanzui); the prominent easternmost tip of the Shandong Peninsula</note> and head due south. 

With favorable winds, in one day and night you will see **Green Water**; another day and night later, you will see **Black Water** <note>original: "黑水" (Heishui); ancient mariners used the color of the sea to estimate depth and currents; "Black Water" typically referred to the deep, dark waters of the open ocean or the Kuroshio current</note>. After another day, you will see the **Green Water** of the **South Sea**. After another two days and one night, you will see **White Water** <note>original: "白水" (Baishui); likely indicating shallower, sandy-bottomed waters as the ship approached the southern coasts</note>. Head south with a westward tilt, maintaining a straight course.
