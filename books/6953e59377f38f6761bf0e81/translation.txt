# TO THE SINCERE READER, AND THE STUDENT OF MAGNETIC PHILOSOPHY,
### PREFACE.

<image-desc>Large ornamental initial 'C' at the beginning of the text, decorated with elaborate floral and foliate motifs.</image-desc>

Since in the discovery of secrets, and in the investigation of the hidden causes of things, stronger reasons exist from certain experiments and demonstrated arguments than from probable conjectures and the opinions of common philosophers: Therefore, so that the noble substance of the great magnet, our Common Mother (the earth), <note>original: "telluris"</note> which has remained until now entirely unknown, and the excellent and eminent powers of this globe might be better understood: we have proposed to begin with the common magnetic, stony, and iron matter, and with magnetic bodies and those closer parts of the earth which we may handle with our hands and perceive with the senses themselves; to proceed by manifest magnetic experiments; and first to penetrate into the innermost parts of the earth. For after many of those things which have been dug up from high mountains, or the depths of the seas, or the lowest caverns and hidden metals, had been seen and examined by us, so that we might finally know the truer substance of the earth: we applied long and much care in investigating the powers of the magnet (those powers being indeed wonderful, and surpassing the virtues of all other bodies among us, even when the strengths of all other minerals <note>term: "fossils" - in the 16th century, this referred to anything dug out of the ground, not just prehistoric remains</note> are gathered together). Nor have we found this labor of ours to be idle or fruitless; since by daily experimenting, new and unheard-of properties shone forth for us; and philosophy grew so much from things diligently observed, that we have attempted to explain the interior parts and the genuine substance of the terrestrial globe by magnetic principles, and to point out the earth (our common mother) to men, as if with a finger, by true demonstrations and experiments manifest to the senses.

<summary>William Gilbert introduces his revolutionary scientific method, arguing that the study of the Earth's hidden nature must begin with hands-on experimentation with magnets and minerals rather than mere speculation.</summary>

-># TO THE READER<-

...we have become. And just as geometry rises from certain very small and easy foundations to the greatest and most difficult heights—by which the ingenious mind climbs above the heavens—so our teaching and magnetic science <term>scientia magnetica: the study of magnetic forces, which the author treats as a fundamental physical law</term> reveals, in a fitting order, certain less rare things first. From those, more remarkable things emerge, and finally, in a certain sequence, the greatest and most hidden secrets of the terrestrial globe are disclosed, and their causes are recognized—causes which, either through the ignorance of the ancients or the negligence of the moderns, have been unknown and overlooked.

But why should I add anything further to such a vast ocean of books, by which the minds of students are disturbed and fatigued? By these more foolish works, the common crowd and the most persistent men are intoxicated; they rave, they are puffed up, and they create literary tumults, professing themselves to be philosophers, physicians, mathematicians, and astrologers, while they neglect and despise truly learned men. Why, I say, should I add anything further to this so-disturbed literary republic? Why should I expose this noble philosophy—which is almost new and incredible because of its many unheard-of features—to be condemned and torn apart by the curses of those sworn to the opinions of others, or to the most absurd corrupters of the good arts: the literate idiots, the grammarians, the sophists, the brawlers, and the perverse common folk?

Instead, I have commended these magnetic foundations to you alone who truly practice philosophy <note>original: "Philofophantibus"</note>, you ingenious men who seek knowledge not from books alone, but from things themselves, through a new kind of philosophizing. But if it should not seem fit for some to agree with these opinions and paradoxes, let them nevertheless behold the great abundance of experiments and discoveries (in which all philosophy especially flourishes); these have been unearthed and demonstrated by our many cares, vigils, and expenses. Rejoice in them, and employ them for better uses if you can.

I know how arduous it is to give novelty to the old, luster to the obsolete, light to the obscure, grace to the loathed, and credit to the doubtful; so it is much more difficult to win and establish some authority for new and unheard-of things against all the opinions of everyone. Nor do we care for that: for we have judged that philosophy is for the few. To our discoveries and experiments, we have applied larger and smaller asterisks <note>The author uses these symbols in the margins of the main text to denote the importance or originality of a specific experiment</note>, according to the measure of their importance and subtlety. Whoever wishes to try these same things, let him handle the bodies not listlessly or clumsily, but prudently, skillfully, and aptly; lest he (when the matter does not succeed) ignorantly blame our discoveries. For nothing has been set forth in these books which has not been investigated and very often performed and accomplished by us. Many things in the reasons and hypotheses will perhaps seem harder at first glance, since they are foreign to common opinion; I do not doubt, however, that later they will gain authority from the demonstrations themselves...

<summary>The author introduces his "new kind of philosophizing" based on direct experimentation rather than book learning, warning that his revolutionary magnetic theories may challenge traditional beliefs. He explains his use of asterisks to grade the importance of his experiments and insists that readers must replicate his work with precision.</summary>
