### Preface to the third edition

This new edition reproduces the work exactly as it was published in 1889, without modifications. Undoubtedly, new studies have since completed and, on certain points, modified the opinions I expressed ten years ago. However, these new investigations are summarized in other works to which I ask the reader's permission to refer. The research conducted at the psychology laboratory of the **Salpêtrière** <note>A famous teaching hospital in Paris where Janet and Charcot studied hysteria and the mind.</note> has just been published in two volumes under the title *Neuroses and Fixed Ideas*. They present many observations and experiments regarding disorders of the **will** <term>will: the faculty of conscious choice and action</term>, attention, and memory, as well as **obsessive ideas** <note>original: "idées obsédantes"</note> and their treatment. One may consider these studies as verifications and supplements to the doctrines that were set forth in *Psychological Automatism*.

Paris, November 15, 1898.

## Preface to the Second Edition

This work, which was presented at the Sorbonne <note>The historic University of Paris, the center of French intellectual life</note> as a doctoral thesis in philosophy, was published for the first time in July 1889. We believe we should leave it as it was and print it again without serious changes. It would be necessary to modify it and, above all, to expand it greatly to bring it up to date with new research and discussions. Furthermore, most of these recent discussions have been studied in other works we have published since that time, which naturally serve to complete our first labor. On the other hand, some of the studies contained in *Psychological Automatism* <note>original: "l’Automatisme psychologique"; the author's seminal work on subconscious actions</note> would lose some of their interest if one forgot the date on which they were published. Presented by us for the first time in 1886 and 1887, and having had the honor of being reproduced since then by a great number of authors, they would appear a bit commonplace today. We therefore desire that our work retain its date, and that is why we have only allowed ourselves insignificant modifications in this second edition.

In this preface, we will limit ourselves to indicating briefly which points of our book seem to us today to require modifications and additions. In a general manner, one may remark that most of the descriptions contained in this work are very simple, while the phenomena one usually observes appear very complex. This is true, and this defect—which seems necessary to us—was largely intentional and sought after. No doubt, it is always a bit hypothetical to choose from among innumerable details those that one believes to be interesting and to neglect the others, but without this hypothesis, no presentation is intelligible. We have not described all these details because some seemed useless to us and others still incomprehensible. For example, in studying the movement disorders of an anesthetic limb <note>A limb that has lost physical sensation, often due to psychological rather than physical causes in these case studies</note> in a patient, Léonie, we described the facts that we thought we could classify and interpret. We passed over other bizarre phenomena in silence, such as those of <term>synkinesis</term> <note>Involuntary movements in one part of the body that occur simultaneously with voluntary movements in another part</note>, or we made only a brief allusion to them; this is because we had not observed a large enough number of these facts to be able to interpret them. Since then, we have had the opportunity to study this phenomenon more completely in other works. The simplicity of our descriptions also results from the choice of subjects. From among a great number of observations, we chose to present those that were simple and, in a way, typical. It is easy today—and we have done it ourselves—to speak of complications and irregularities, but we would not have been able to make ourselves understood if we had not first presented the most intelligible cases.

Here is an interesting text that proves our assertion and which we had neglected to cite. Biran <note>Pierre Maine de Biran (1766–1824), a French philosopher who emphasized the role of the "will" in consciousness.</note> <sup>1</sup>. We also regret not having cited more often the work of Dr. Gerdy, who expresses on several occasions ideas quite analogous to those of Maine de Biran. To study these facts, he says, "one must become accustomed to understanding that there can be sensation without perception of the sensation" <sup>2</sup>. Indeed, to interpret cataleptic attitudes <note>Catalepsy: a physical state of trance or seizure with loss of sensation and consciousness, where the body remains in a fixed posture.</note>, we have been led, like these authors, to admit the existence of elementary phenomena as simple as possible. These phenomena still had to have the character of psychological facts, but they were devoid of that reflective consciousness which consists primarily in the assimilation of phenomena into the personality.

Undoubtedly such phenomena are not absolutely simple, and one can decompose consciousness infinitely; undoubtedly one can, in a more or less theoretical way, find in these facts the essential elements that are attributed to consciousness—"the will-to-live, appetition <note>appetition: a natural desire or instinctive craving.</note>, etc." <sup>3</sup>—but, on the one hand, we did not have to depart from pure observation to seek to determine the essential nature of the facts of consciousness; on the other hand, it was enough for us to show the relatively simple character of such phenomena and the difference that separated them from the usually known facts of consciousness. Moreover, we are quite disposed to admit, with Mr. William James <sup>4</sup>, that such facts must be very rudimentary to remain thus impersonal; as soon as they become a little complicated, they "tend to take on the form of personality," which happens in somnambulisms <note>somnambulism: sleepwalking or a similar trance-like state.</note> or in subconscious writings, whether they be suggested or natural. As we remarked, words heard during catalepsy as simple sounds and which are not understood, can awaken in the form of memories in a later, more intelligent state. They will then be understood by a person and will have their suggestive power. The tendency toward synthesis and personality remains the general character of psychological phenomena.

Throughout the course of this work, we have insisted on the close relationship that seems to exist between psychological phenomena and physiological phenomena, in particular between thoughts and movements. We have tried to show that sensations and images were accompanied by movements of the limbs and that, on the other hand, the disappearances of the sensation or the image provoked a parallel suppression in movements, so much so that certain paralyses could be considered as amnesias (p. 350, 362): "There are not," we said, "two faculties, one that of thought, the other that of activity; there is at each moment only one and the same phenomenon always manifesting itself in two different ways." A study on **psychological automatism** <term>psychological automatism: the performance of actions without conscious intent or awareness, often repetitive in nature</term> led us to study primarily the phenomena of habit and the **association of ideas**, in which images follow one another in a regular sequence—in short, that activity which tends to preserve and repeat. But we have always sought to demonstrate that, for us, this category of phenomena and this form of activity did not exist alone in the human mind. "Thoughts that previously formed part of the same whole, of the same act of knowledge," we said, "are suggested by one another." "Automatism does not create new syntheses; it is merely the manifestation of syntheses that were already organized at a time when the mind was more powerful." In a word, this automatism is only the consequence of another entirely different activity which previously made it possible and which, moreover, almost always accompanies it today. Not only do these two activities—one which preserves the organizations of the past, the other which synthesizes and organizes the phenomena of the present—depend on each other, but they also limit and regulate one another. It is only the diminution of the current **activity of synthesis** <note>The mind's ability to combine individual thoughts into a coherent whole</note>, a weakening manifested by all sorts of symptoms, that allows for the exaggerated development of the old automatism. The conception of these two activities—a hypothetical conception, no doubt, but one that allows us to summarize and classify a great number of facts—has already been frequently expressed by philosophers whom we have often cited: Leibniz, Maine de Biran, Hamilton, Taine, Ferri, Fouillée, Paulhan, etc.; it has also been explained in a more or less clear fashion by **alienists** <term>alienist: an early term for a psychiatrist or psychologist</term>. These specialists were led to this psychological theory by the study of certain patients who seem agitated and active from one point of view, yet who, by a striking contrast, are inert and immobile from another. We have often cited Moreau (of Tours) in this work: he still seems to us to be one of those who best interpreted this philosophical thought from a medical perspective. We regret not having also pointed out other alienists who expressed similar ideas.

It is this opposition between the creative activity of the mind and the reproductive activity—which truly deserves the name <term>automatism</term> <note>The author refers to "psychological automatism," where the mind repeats actions or thoughts without conscious will or creative input.</note>—that we have attempted to clarify in our various works on <term>aboulia</term> <note>A clinical term for a pathological inability to make decisions or act, often described as a "paralysis of will."</note>, on suggestion, and on <term>fixed ideas</term> <note>Obsessive or recurring thoughts that dominate a patient's mental state, often linked to past trauma.</note>.

Our study on the modifications of memory appears very incomplete to us today. It only describes the alterations of memory in relation to somnambulistic states, and only the simplest of these alterations. Although the somnambulistic states described in this work already seem quite varied, it is easy to see that others still exist. For instance, it would be necessary to emphasize more than we have done the "simply reciprocal" somnambulisms; in these, the subject in a somnambulistic state remembers only previous somnambulisms, linking all these abnormal periods together to form a continuous existence, but possesses no memory of the waking state. ## Introduction

It is almost always the highest forms of human activity—will, resolution, and free will—that have been studied by philosophers. Naturally, interest was focused on the manifestations of activity that were most useful to know in order to understand human conduct, responsibility, and the moral value of actions. However, although this way of approaching the question is perhaps the most natural, it is nonetheless the most difficult and the most dangerous: the highest and most important phenomena are far from being the simplest; on the contrary, they present many modifications and incidental developments that prevent a clear understanding of their true nature. Today, the most elementary facts, in psychology as in other sciences, are sought out by preference, for it is known that knowledge of them—being easier to acquire—will greatly clarify the knowledge of more complex forms. *It is human activity in its simplest, most rudimentary forms that will be the subject of this study.*

This elementary activity, whether observed in animals or studied in humans by <term>alienists</term> <note>A historical term for early psychiatrists or doctors specializing in mental illness, from the idea that the patient was "alienated" from their reason.</note>, has been designated by a name that must be retained: *automatic* activity. This name, indeed, even according to its etymological sense (the Greek word *autos* [self] and *mat* [effort], from *mateuo* [to seek, to strive], according to Littré <note>Émile Littré, author of a famous 19th-century French dictionary.</note>) seems to apply quite well to the characteristics presented by these actions. Indeed, a movement is designated as automatic if it presents two characteristics. First, it must have something spontaneous about it, at least in appearance; it must take its source within the moving object itself and not originate from an external impulse. A mechanical doll that walks on its own will be called an automaton; a pump that is moved from the outside cannot be one. Second, this movement must nevertheless remain very regular and be subject to a rigorous determinism, without variations or whims. Now, the first efforts of human activity have precisely these two characteristics: they are provoked, but not created, by external impulses; they emerge from the subject itself, and yet they are so regular that there can be no question of the "free will" claimed by the higher faculties. 

However, another meaning is usually added to the word *automatic* which we do not accept as readily. For some authors, an automatic activity is not only a regular and rigorously determined activity, but also a purely mechanical activity that is absolutely without consciousness. This interpretation has been the source of numerous confusions, and many philosophers refuse to recognize an automatism in the human mind—which is nonetheless real and without which many phenomena are inexplicable—because they imagine that admitting automatism means suppressing consciousness and reducing man to a pure mechanism of extended and unfeeling elements. We believe that one can simultaneously admit both automatism and consciousness, and thereby satisfy both those who observe in man a form of elementary activity that is entirely determined, like that of an automaton, and those who wish to preserve for man, even in his simplest actions, consciousness and sensitivity. In other words, it does not seem to us that—

## Part One. Total Automatism

### Chapter I. Isolated Psychological Phenomena

Condillac <note>Étienne Bonnot de Condillac (1714–1780), a French Enlightenment philosopher who argued that all human knowledge and faculties derive from sensory experience.</note>, when he undertook to analyze the human mind, imagined an ingenious method to clarify and simplify the complex phenomena that present themselves to consciousness. He supposed an animated statue capable of experiencing every emotion and understanding every thought, but possessing none at the beginning; into this absolutely empty mind, he intended to introduce each sensation one after the other and in isolation. This was an excellent scientific method. The multiplicity of intersecting phenomena in the universe prevents us from discerning their relationships and dependencies; by a stroke of a magic wand, let us suppress all these phenomena and, in this absolute void, reproduce a single fact in isolation. Nothing would then be easier than to see the role and consequences of this phenomenon; they would develop before us without confusion. This is the ideal method of the sciences; Condillac hoped to apply it to the mind. Unfortunately, this method—so beautiful in theory—was completely impractical, for the philosopher did not possess the statue of which he spoke and did not know how to reduce a consciousness to its elementary phenomena. Thus, he performed his experiment in his imagination; instead of questioning nature and waiting for the answer, he provided both the questions and the answers himself, substituting a completely artificial construction for the analysis he claimed to be performing.

Well, the experiment that Condillac dreamed of and could not attempt, it is possible for us today to realize almost completely. We can have before our eyes true living statues whose minds are empty of thoughts, and into this consciousness, we can introduce in isolation the phenomenon whose psychological development we wish to study. It is thanks to a morbid state known for a long time by doctors, but little examined by philosophers, that we shall find this statue. It is the nervous illness most often designated by the name *catalepsy* <term>catalepsy: a physical state characterized by muscular rigidity and a lack of response to external stimuli, often associated with hypnosis or hysteria in 19th-century medicine</term> which will provide us with these sudden and complete suppressions, and then these gradual restorations of consciousness that we wish to utilize for our experiments. "Catalepsy," says Saint-Bourdin, one of the first authors to have made a precise study of this illness, "is an affection of the brain, intermittent, non-feverish <note>original: "apyrétique"</note>, characterized by the suspension of understanding and sensibility, and by the aptitude of the muscles to receive and retain all degrees of contraction given to them ^1." This definition, without being perfect, gives a fairly accurate general idea of a morbid state that occurs naturally in some predisposed individuals following a shock or an emotion, and which can be produced artificially in some subjects by various well-known procedures. # I. Description of phenomena provoked during the cataleptic state

It is regrettable to begin our description of states where psychological automatism <note>The performance of actions without conscious thought or intention</note> manifests itself with the description of a state that is quite rare, and with experiments that cannot easily be repeated. However, this should not be surprising: we wish to study very simple phenomena at the outset, and nature always presents complex things. Nothing is more complicated than a normal mind; nothing is more complicated, likewise, than insanity or an ordinary crisis of hysteria. We are forced to choose rare phenomena if we want them to be simple. Furthermore, although we have personally observed a fairly large number of people suffering from these nervous diseases where the cataleptic state may occur, we have never ourselves witnessed an absolutely complete crisis of natural catalepsy; those we have seen were only imperfect varieties. We have only collected descriptions of two natural crises: one observed in Paris at the Pitié Hospital by my brother Jules Janet, the other produced by a lightning strike on a subject I knew, but whom I was unable to see at that moment. I have been able to observe artificial catalepsies more frequently, but only in three subjects.

Catalepsy could sometimes be provoked in Lucie by suddenly showing her a bright magnesium light, or by lightly compressing her eyes during somnambulism <note>A state of sleepwalking or a hypnotic trance</note>. Catalepsy occurred naturally at certain moments during the induced somnambulism of Rose or Léonie. It was also produced, but in the latter case only, when her eyes were opened to the light during somnambulism.

## II. Mechanical or physical interpretation of these phenomena

Can the phenomena we have just described be of interest to psychology? This is the first question one must ask when considering cataleptic patients. These immobile women, like statues, without resistance of any kind and without speech—are they still thinking? Do they still possess some consciousness that brings them closer to us? One might reasonably doubt it and wonder if the organic life <note>The basic biological functions of the body, such as breathing and circulation, as opposed to mental or "psychic" life.</note> which seems to be all that remains would not suffice to explain all the observed phenomena. This is the explanation one would find in the works of Heidenhain <note>Rudolf Heidenhain (1834–1897), a German physiologist who viewed hypnosis as a purely physical inhibition of the brain.</note>. He explains cataleptic movements as reflex actions of the lower centers of the brain—actions that do not reach the higher centers where consciousness develops. It is also to this opinion that the English alienist <note>term: alienist (an early term for a psychiatrist)</note> Maudsley would subscribe. Finally, it is the doctrine found expressed and defended in the most complete manner in the works of Dr. Despine <sup>1</sup>. This author refuses to recognize any kind of consciousness, not only during catalepsy <note>A state of muscular rigidity and lack of response to external stimuli.</note>, but even during somnambulism <note>In this context, "somnambulism" refers to a deep hypnotic trance where the patient may act or speak, rather than simple sleepwalking.</note>. All acts performed during these abnormal states seem to him purely "organic," analogous to those that the heart and lungs perform constantly without our knowledge. They are indeed automatic acts, but "automatic acts of the spinal cord, the medulla, the hemispheres," for "one must not say unconscious and automatic activity of the mind, that is a contradiction; one must say unconscious activity of such or such a nervous center." "Carpenter is wrong," he adds, "to call these acts sensory-motor or ideo-motor, for there is neither sensation nor idea there; there are no truly automatic acts of the mind"; finally, "to ask psychology for an explanation of somnambulism would be to go the wrong way; physiology alone can give this explanation <sup>2</sup>." As our goal in this work, if we are not too ambitious, is precisely to demonstrate the contrary, we must insist on the study of Dr. Despine's opinions, which seem to halt our work from the very beginning. If one applied this thesis of absolute unconsciousness to somnambulists, as the author himself does, it would be absolutely unsustainable. To claim that a person who speaks, solves problems, spontaneously manifests likes and dislikes, acts as they please, and often resists our orders, has no more consciousness than a mechanical doll, is to go far back behind Descartes' famous theory of "animal-machines" <note>original: "animaux-machines" - René Descartes' 17th-century philosophical concept that animals are merely complex biological robots without souls or feelings.</note>. For the consciousness of a somnambulist is far more evident than the consciousness of a dog, and no one today doubts the consciousness of a dog. But, applied to cataleptic states, this theory does possess some force; and, as one must always present the theories one wishes to discuss in their best light, it is by placing ourselves at this latter point of view that we shall study Dr. Despine's thesis. We hope to show that, even in this last case, his arguments are not sufficiently demonstrative and leave the field open to other suppositions.

The arguments of Dr. Despine, scattered throughout a large number of studies on somnambulism <note>A historical term for sleepwalking or hypnotic trances</note>—all of which, it is interesting to note, are psychological studies—can be arranged into two groups:

1st *Most of the evidence is drawn from the fact of the amnesia <note>original: "l'oubli" (forgetting)</note> that characterizes the phenomena of somnambulism and especially those of catalepsy:* "By consciousness," says the author¹, "we designate the knowledge, the perception by the 'I' <note>The "ego" or self-aware subject</note>, by the being who feels themselves to be, of what is happening within their personality, of their own acts, of themselves; this work will only concern itself with this consciousness." From such a definition of consciousness, it follows that if there are acts that the "I" does not attribute to itself, which it does not recognize having done, these acts must not have been conscious. "When it concerns a serious act, capable of impressing the feelings to the highest degree, if the individual who performed it is completely unaware of this act, it would be against nature to attribute this ignorance to simple forgetting. It can only be explained by the non-participation of the 'I,' of the personal consciousness in this act, which is due entirely to the unconscious—that is to say, automatic—psychic activity of the brain during a momentary suspension of the conscious activity of that organ²." Now, there is no state after which this amnesia is more characteristic than after the cataleptic state <note>A physical state of trance or seizure involving muscle rigidity and loss of contact with the environment</note>. Somnambulists have sometimes been able to retain a portion of the memories of their actions; but cataleptics wake from their fit convinced that nothing abnormal has occurred. Furthermore, if, instead of waking up completely, the cataleptic subject merely passes into another abnormal state, appearing little different, such as the somnambulistic state, they still do not retain the memory of the preceding attitudes and movements. "This ignorance can only find its explanation in the non-participation of the 'I' in what the body has done, the cerebral activity that characterizes the 'I'—the conscious personality—having been paralyzed³."

This same character is found in individuals who have been subjected to inhalations of ether or chloroform. Whatever words the patient may have uttered, "their 'I,' their conscious being had not participated at all in what had happened, for the patient, soon having come to, affirmed having felt nothing, being completely unaware that they had been operated upon or had thought, that they had uttered words and that they had performed acts; the violent reactions of which one spoke to them, these various phenomena were therefore purely automatic." As the state of a cataleptic or somnambulistic individual infinitely resembles the state of a chloroformed individual (the demonstration of this point forms one of the most interesting parts of Dr. Despine's work), one can draw a conclusion from one to the other. Chloroform suppresses sensitivity and consciousness, and it is precisely for that reason that it is used; since somnambulism presents the same characteristics and in particular the same amnesia, we must believe that it brings about the same unconsciousness.

One only needs to look through these discussions and others of the same kind—where forgetting is always accepted as proof of unconsciousness—to be struck by the fragility of this argument. Is it then impossible for truly conscious acts to be forgotten? This forgetting would be inexplicable, says Despine <note>Prosper Despine (1812–1892), a French physician who studied somnambulism and argued that such states were purely automatic and lacked a conscious "I."</note>, when it concerns somnambulists <note>A term used in 19th-century psychology for people in a deep hypnotic trance, not just sleepwalkers.</note>. Very well, we must seek the reasons for this forgetting, which may perhaps be very difficult to find; but even if one could not always explain it, the forgetting of a thing that was truly conscious is nonetheless a possible and very often real occurrence. If, as an English author says <sup>1</sup>, a reader of the *Times* is suddenly killed after his reading, he will certainly have no memory [of it]; must we conclude that his entire reading was without consciousness? We could then never admit the consciousness of any man, not even our own; for nothing assures us that tomorrow an accident or an illness will not come to suppress our memory.

But let us admit for a moment—what seems inadmissible—that forgetting is sufficient proof of absolute unconsciousness; is it quite certain that no memory of cataleptic phenomena exists? It is true that, at least for the subjects I have studied, there is never any recollection when they return to the state that, by convention, we call the waking state or normal state. But a certain memory manifests itself first in subsequent catalepsies <note>A state of trance characterized by muscle rigidity and a lack of response to external stimuli.</note> through the habit the subject rapidly acquires of performing with greater perfection the acts they are made to do most often. Furthermore, and this is more important, there exist in these same individuals certain psychological states—certain "somnambulisms," since that is still the agreed-upon name—where the subject perfectly recovers the memory of the catalepsy. "You had placed my hand like this," Léonie <note>Léonie Leboulanger, a famous subject of early psychological research used by Janet and others for experiments in hypnosis.</note> told me then, "as if I were playing the flute; you closed my fists, etc." It is true that this memory is only found in very deep somnambulisms, which are sometimes so difficult to obtain that they were long ignored. We shall resume the study of these somnambulisms later <sup>2</sup>; it was good to know right now that a memory of catalepsy exists. But this memory, Mr. Despine will say, only exists in another abnormal state which is itself a state of unconsciousness. "If his paralyzed conscious activity has no knowledge of the time passed during the fit, because it received no impressions, the automatic activity that participated in these acts has preserved the impression of them, and the memory can reappear in another fit <sup>3</sup>."

Thus, memory, if it reappeared in the normal state, would be good proof of consciousness; but, since it reappears in another state, it is nothing more than proof of physical automatism <note>The theory that the body can perform complex actions without the involvement of the conscious mind or soul.</note>. Does this not prove that memory is a proof of neither consciousness nor unconsciousness, and that we must look outside of memory for indications regarding the state of cataleptics?

What is now only a hypothesis will be verified, we believe, more and more by other phenomena of the same kind.

### III. Psychological Interpretation. — Catalepsy assimilated to somnambulism

The acts performed during catalepsy are dependent on psychological phenomena: here is a proposition that seems very simple, but which is susceptible to very different interpretations. For psychological phenomena are of an extremely varied nature, and it is not a matter of indifference whether we explain the facts we have described by one or the other.

I will not speak of an easy interpretation, which was in fashion for a long time. It consisted of linking all the facts that were not understood to a voluntary and perfectly conscious simulation <note>The author is referring to the common 19th-century skeptical view that hysterical or cataleptic patients were simply "faking it."</note>. It is a completely false idea to believe that a psychological or even imaginary illness is always a simulated illness, and besides, catalepsy is of all abnormal phenomena the one that can least be simulated. But, without linking catalepsy to a complete intelligence calculating its ruses, one can explain it by a half-intelligence understanding the thoughts of the operator, realizing its acts, without having the strength to oppose them; in a word, one can compare catalepsy to somnambulism <term>somnambulism: here referring to a hypnotic trance state rather than simple sleepwalking</term> and explain all these acts by suggestion. ### IV. A rudimentary form of consciousness. – The isolated sensation and image

Certain philosophers, following the example of the Cartesians <note>Followers of the philosophy of René Descartes.</note>, have represented consciousness as something invariable and immutable, without nuances or degrees. Leibniz, on the contrary, in that profound philosophy to which all physical and moral sciences today seem to lead us back, had a completely different conception of consciousness. It is this latter theory that we must now recall in its main features, to understand the possibility of lower and rudimentary consciousnesses. Normal states do not, undoubtedly, present us with such clear examples, but we can nevertheless recognize that the idea of the "self" <note>original: "le moi"</note> does not always attach itself equally to all the sensations we experience. Most, on the contrary, awaken the idea of the self so little that we do not hesitate to attribute them to the outside world; instead of saying that it is we who experience the sensation of the color green, we say that it is the tree that possesses it. Often we no longer even make this distinction and, before a spectacle that fascinates us, we have nothing in our consciousness but the sensations of that spectacle without reflecting upon ourselves, without distinguishing what is internal and what is external. It is true that, in ordinary life, we can always recover ourselves, emerge from these absorbing sensations, and recognize that it is *we* who have had them. But we can conceive that certain beings, such as lower animals, can never disentangle their personality from these elementary sensations, and that other more complex beings are momentarily reduced to a purely *affective* life <note>A life based on raw feeling and emotion rather than logic or intellect.</note> without knowledge and without reflection.

### **V. The nature of consciousness during catalepsy**

It is precisely a consciousness of this kind—purely affective, reduced to sensations and images, without any of those connections or ideas of relation that constitute personality and judgment—that we believe is legitimate to assume during catalepsy <note>A nervous condition characterized by muscular rigidity and fixity of posture regardless of external stimuli.</note> and analogous states. Neither the nothingness of consciousness and pure mechanism, nor a knowledge capable of understanding and obeying, seem likely to us here; it is, on the contrary, a particular form of consciousness intermediate between these two extremes.

Very often this initial syncope <note>term: syncope (fainting or a temporary loss of consciousness caused by a fall in blood pressure)</note>, which lasts quite a long time in her case, leads to profound and dangerous respiratory disturbances. The breathing is sometimes rapid and panting, sometimes stops for an entire minute; the lips turn blue and let a little foam escape. But soon, movements begin. These are at first small tremors in each muscle, without overall movement, then movements of the limbs, but completely uncoordinated. I pass quickly over the details of these convulsions, which have been too well described by masters <note>Likely a reference to Jean-Martin Charcot and the Salpêtrière school of neurology</note> for me to allow myself to dwell on them. It seems to me that the movements, at first entirely isolated and incoherent, become increasingly general and systematic. For example, at the beginning, the muscles of the arm contract at random, one opposing the other, which simply produces a trembling of the arm and various flexions of the fingers. Now the muscles coordinate well enough so that both arms make large movements and come to strike blows with the fists on the same point of the chest. ## VII. Interpretation of the Specific Phenomena of Catalepsy

Guided by previous research into the general nature of consciousness during cataleptic states, let us return to the various phenomena we have described—namely, the continuation of a posture or a movement, the repetition of movements seen or sounds heard, the harmonious expressions of the entire body, and associated movements. Let us seek the simple hypotheses by which we can interpret each of these facts.

1st *Continuation of a posture or a movement.* — It is here that one clearly sees the superiority of actual experience, however imperfect it may be, over purely theoretical reasoning. Many philosophers, and Condillac <note>Étienne Bonnot de Condillac (1714–1780), a French philosopher who used the famous thought experiment of a "sentient statue" to explain how human knowledge derives from the senses.</note> especially, have wondered what happens when one introduces an isolated sensation into a statue void of thought. They have supposed a multitude of things more or less true; they have said that this sensation produced attention, memory, pleasure, pain, etc., but they did not guess the principal phenomenon that this sensation was going to produce; they did not tell us that at each new sensation, the statue was going to move. The simplest experiment immediately shows us this important phenomenon. Let any sensation whatever, produced by any process whatever, occur in an empty consciousness, and immediately there will be a movement. Such is the law manifested, we believe, by the simplest phenomena of catalepsy.

How, indeed, can we explain why the arm of a cataleptic patient <note>The author uses the feminine "cataleptique," likely referring to the female patients at the Salpêtrière hospital where these studies were common.</note> that I lift or set in motion maintains its posture or its movement? The physical forces of gravity would tend to make it fall: indeed, a delicately systematized contraction of all the muscles is required to maintain it. What can give these contractions their unity and their persistence? I see no other answer than this: it is a persistent sensation. When I lifted the arm, I provoked a certain conscious muscular sensation, entirely determined—that is to say, corresponding exactly to a specific position of the arm, the wrist, the fingers, etc. This sensation, being alone in the mind, encountered no antagonistic or reducing phenomenon; it did not disappear with the stimulus that produced it; it has remained and still lasts. But while it lasts, it maintains by its very persistence the position of the arm to which it is linked, or rather, from which it is inseparable.

Let us study the different points of this explanation separately. That the position of the arm moved by me can produce in the subject's mind a specific muscular sensation, different for each posture, is a proposition that is nearly indisputable. ## Chapter II. Forgetfulness and the various successive psychological existences

It is only in very rare and extraordinary circumstances that human actions are so isolated and impersonal; they usually appear to be the manifestation of a character and to depend on a personality. In order to pursue the study of automatic actions under more complex conditions that approach the normal state, we must seek psychological states susceptible to experimentation, but in which, nevertheless, character and personality begin to develop: it is the state known by the name of *somnambulism* <note>A 19th-century term for a hypnotic trance state, not merely sleepwalking</note> that best fulfills these conditions.

We see, in effect, among somnambulists, the automatic life of the mind growing and extending, forming a particular memory, and giving birth to a new character and personality. To discover the nature and the principal characteristics of this new form of psychological life is to see the activity of the elements of our thought from another perspective.

### I. The different characteristics that have been proposed to recognize somnambulism

This state is, in appearance, well known; sometimes, as we know, it occurs spontaneously in the middle of sleep, sometimes it forms an important part of a nervous crisis, and sometimes it is artificially induced by procedures that have been described too often for me to dwell upon them. However, it is very difficult to find a sign that characterizes it in a general way, and most of the characteristics that have been so designated seem to us insufficient; by reviewing them, we will see some accessory characteristics of this state, but it will remain for us to seek its distinctive sign.

Some of these characteristics have been called physical, not because they were truly visible modifications, but because they were observed by means of various experiments performed on the subject's body.

Most of the early magnetizers <note>Practitioners of "animal magnetism," the precursor to modern hypnotism</note> considered the absolute insensibility of the skin to be the constant rule and the indubitable sign of somnambulism. "There is no magnetic sleep," writes Baragnon <gloss>2</gloss>, "without complete insensibility of the body and the senses, such that we shall use, for the verification of sleep, everything that can convince us of this insensibility." ## II. Essential characteristics of somnambulism: Forgetfulness upon waking and alternating memory

The phenomena of memory are perhaps the most important of our psychological organization, and their slightest modifications have a considerable impact on our entire life. Now, in all of mental pathology, there is no modification of memory more complex and at the same time more regular than that of the somnambulist's memory. One regularly observes in the thoughts of individuals who, for one reason or another, have had periods of somnambulism, three characteristics or three laws of memory that are peculiar to them: 1st, complete forgetfulness during the normal waking state of everything that happened during somnambulism; 2nd, complete recollection during a new somnambulism of everything that happened during previous somnambulisms; 3rd, complete recollection during somnambulism of everything that happened during the waking state. The third law presents perhaps more exceptions and irregularities than the other two; therefore, in this study, which aims above all to give a general idea of somnambulism, we will insist a little less upon it. But the first two, despite the diversity that such complex phenomena always present, are so general and so important that they can be considered the characteristic sign of the somnambulic state.

The phenomenon of forgetfulness, upon waking, of everything that happened during somnambulism is so curious and so striking that it was noted from the very first studies of this kind.
> "When he returns to the natural state," says Deleuze <sup>2</sup>, "he loses the memory of all the sensations, of all the ideas he had in the state of somnambulism; so much so that these two states seem as foreign to each other as if the somnambulist and the waking man were two different beings... This character alone is constant and essentially distinguishes somnambulism."
> "The forgetfulness of everything that happened during magnetic sleep," writes Baragnon <sup>3</sup> also, "is an invariable effect without which there is no sleep."

We would respond first that, until now, we possess no better sign; and second, that this criterion is more reliable than one might suppose. Contrary to general opinion, I consider psychological phenomena to be much more difficult to simulate than physical phenomena, and I believe it would be easier to fake even an epileptic seizure than to feign insanity for several days before an alienist <note>A 19th-century term for a psychiatrist</note>. Regarding the subject at hand, a small amount of information and a little practice are enough to simulate a contracture <term>contracture: a state of prolonged muscle shortening or rigidity often observed in hysterical or hypnotic states</term>; it would require a great deal of intelligence, attention, and memory to never confuse memories acquired during somnambulism with memories acquired while awake, and to never be caught in an error. A more serious criticism can be made regarding the way questions are posed to verify the state of memory. It is indeed sometimes dangerous to question the subject directly. The question itself can, through a kind of suggestion, awaken a memory that would otherwise remain unknown. I do not believe this danger is generally very great, for suggestions relating to memory are rarely so easy, and one does not awaken a subject's memories simply by asking what they said or did while they slept. However, the danger exists, and Mr. Gurney <margin>Gurney. *The stages of hypnotic memory. Proceedings S. P. R.* <note>Society for Psychical Research</note>, 1887, 517.</margin>, who has often pointed it out, also indicates a fairly good means to avoid it: one must verify their memories through their conversation itself, without appearing to question them directly. "Ask them a seemingly indifferent question to which they will respond in a particular way if they have certain memories, and in another if they do not know what is being discussed." This procedure is excellent and easier in practice than it appears to be. But for this, as I have said, one must know the character and the entire life of the subject very well and resign oneself to spending a very long time with them. Psychological experiments require particular precautions and cannot be performed quickly. It is by this procedure that—at least whenever doubt was possible—we have carefully verified the state of memory in the persons we shall discuss; but we will not indicate here, for each one, the experiments and conversations that served us in making these verifications: that would needlessly lengthen this work.

### III. Varieties and complications of alternating memory

These lapses and recoveries of memory, so striking in somnambulism, sometimes present themselves with a greater degree of complication that is very useful to understand. The same subject does not always enter into the same somnambulistic state; they enter into varied states which are all indeed analogous to hypnotic sleep, but which are not identical to one another. It then happens that, depending on the state into which they have been brought, they present a different memory, remembering or not remembering this or that other state in which they were previously. In a word, the disturbances that ordinary somnambulism brings to the subject's memory when they have returned to the waking state, another somnambulism can produce equally when the subject returns to the first state.

Here is a very curious observation published first in the *Library of Magnetism*, then studied again in Bertrand's <gloss>1</gloss> treatise on somnambulism. A young girl of thirteen or fourteen years fell into different nervous states distinct from waking: nervous crises, natural somnambulism, and artificial or magnetic somnambulism <note>An early term for hypnosis, derived from "animal magnetism."</note>.

It is only fully recovered in the fourth period, during which the subject's memory no longer presents any particular gaps. This is a hypothesis which, until now, rests only on one characteristic—that of memory—and which our studies will justify more and more.

The description of these alternating memories <term>alternating memories: a phenomenon where a patient has two or more separate chains of memory that do not communicate with each other</term>, although it is done in a superficial way without entering into details, may appear complicated and obscure. We are convinced, however, that psychology must leave behind abstract generalities and enter into these details if it wishes to one day become a useful and practical science. It is thanks to the knowledge of these various psychological states of hysterics that one can cure their paralyses and their contractures <note>Physical symptoms like frozen limbs (contractures) were often treated via hypnosis in the 19th century.</note>, and it will be necessary to enter into much more arduous studies if we one day seek the true moral treatment of insanity, which is much more complicated than hysteria. ## IV. **Study on a Particular Condition of Memory and the Forgetting of Images**

Observing a phenomenon is not enough; one must also seek to explain it. Where do these changes in psychological states come from? Why these lapses and strange returns of memory? Every possible hypothesis has been proposed, and to review them all would be to traverse the entire history of animal magnetism <note>A 19th-century term for what we now call hypnosis, based on the theories of Franz Mesmer.</note>. Since most of these theories have already been summarized in the works of Maury, Despine, and Ribot, it will suffice for us to cite the most famous ones and show how little they take into account the true elements of the problem. Some claim that attention was too weak during somnambulism <note>The state of "sleepwalking" or deep trance induced by hypnosis.</note> and the psychological phenomena too slight ¹. Others say, on the contrary, that concentration was so strong and the phenomena so violent that the mind was exhausted and can no longer reproduce that same idea upon waking ². These two hypotheses refute one another; they take no account of the variety of somnambulistic phenomena—which are sometimes very strong and sometimes very weak—and finally, they would explain with great difficulty the perfect return of memory in a subsequent state of somnambulism. Mr. Despine, as we have already seen, attributes this forgetting to the total disappearance of the "ego" and of consciousness during the abnormal state:

> "It can only be explained by the non-participation of the ego and personal consciousness in this act, which is due to the unconscious psychic activity—that is to say, the automatic activity of the brain—during a momentary suspension of the conscious activity of that organ ³."

There is perhaps some truth in this theory of the disappearance of the ego; but to conclude from it that all consciousness is suppressed during somnambulism seems to us truly paradoxical and inadmissible. There is more to be gained, I believe, from the theories of Maury ⁴ on the role of the association of ideas in memory; but, as he himself remarks, the forgetting of somnambulists cannot be explained entirely by the breaking of the chain of associations. If, immediately after waking, they do not always have before their eyes an object or a movement associated with previous acts to remind them, it is nevertheless likely that, in the course of the day, they will happen to see objects or perform acts identical to those performed in somnambulism. Why does the power of association not exert itself at that moment to awaken the memories? Léonie <note>A famous subject of psychological study in the late 19th century, often used in experiments regarding multiple personalities and hypnosis.</note> in somnambulism picked a bouquet of flowers; when she is awake, I give her this bouquet to take away: why does she not understand where it came from? Why, by association, does she not remember having picked it?

Some of the early magnetizers also express a very accurate idea—one from which we shall profit—when they speak of the changes in sensitivity that occur during somnambulism.

This fortuitous observation led me quite naturally to suppose that there must be a relationship between the state of sensitivity and the state of memory. Memories acquired through a certain sensitivity seemed only capable of being recalled or reproduced if that sensitivity remained in the same state. To discuss the value of this hypothesis and to apply it to new cases, it seems necessary to me to distinguish between two cases and to study two species of memory separately. There is first the elementary or sensory memory, which consists simply of the recollection of this or that particular sensation considered in isolation; and there is then a complex or intellectual memory, which provides us with the recollection of complicated ideas and which can hardly exist in man except through language. Let us first concern ourselves only with the former memory and seek the conditions under which it is possible.

### **V. A condition of memory and forgetting for complex phenomena**

After this far too brief study of the conditions of recall for elementary memory, let us move on to complex or intellectual memory—that is, the complete memory of ideas and acts. Here, our task is singularly facilitated by the beautiful psychological works of Mr. Charcot <note>Jean-Martin Charcot (1825–1893), the famous French neurologist known as the "founder of modern neurology"</note> on language and different sensory types. It is known, in fact, that complex actions and ideas are hardly understood or preserved in memory except through language. To seek the conditions of the complex memory of ideas or actions is, in reality, to seek the conditions of the memory of language.

Language is formed by a large number of images associated with our ideas and our movements, and these images—as physicians have taught psychologists ²—are not the same in all individuals. Some, perhaps the majority, think through these motor or kinesthetic <term>kinesthetic: relating to the sensation of movement or the position of the body</term> images of which we have already spoken, and which have a tendency, when isolated, to translate outwardly into real movement or real speech. These people think by speaking aloud or under their breath, but always through the images of the movement of speech. Others think by means of auditory or visual images; their thought is formed by a sequence of images of words heard and not spoken, or by a sequence of images of writing—signs seen and not heard. How do these latter people speak and act? Do their visual and auditory images first awaken more or less weak motor images which then translate into movement? We have already discussed this question and concluded that it may have been so at the beginning of life, but that now things happen more simply. The sound of a word must translate into the movements that express that word exactly like the motor image itself. The habit of speaking and even acting by means of auditory or visual images must be added to the habit of thinking by means of these images, contributing to further separating these different types from one another. Since all thoughts and all actions thus depend on a single species of images, the result is that memories eventually all depend on the persistence of these same images.

## VI. Interpretation of Forgetfulness upon Awakening after Somnambulism

The application of the preceding remarks to somnambulism <note>A 19th-century term for a hypnotic trance state, not merely sleepwalking</note> seems, after all that has been said, so natural that it is enough to insist on a few details.

To understand the alternating memory of somnambulists, we have been led to suppose that it is due to a *periodic modification* (whether spontaneous or induced) in the state of sensitivity and, consequently, in the nature of the images that serve to form complex psychological phenomena, and language in particular. This modification occurs especially in subjects who are more or less anesthetic <note>Lacking physical sensation or "numb" to touch</note> in their normal state, and it then consists of *the momentary restoration of a certain category of images* of which the subjects have ordinarily lost possession. This modification can be more or less complete and, in certain subjects who are *distracted* rather than truly anesthetic, consist simply in the *momentary predominance* of certain images that are ordinarily neglected. Let us resume the examination of these various points in succession.

1° We have already had the occasion to show in most of our subjects *these periodic variations of sensitivity and images*; we have seen that one is a visual type in his normal state, then momentarily a motor type during somnambulism; that another is an auditory type while awake, then a visual type during a certain abnormal state: it is useless to return to it. Let us only remark that wherever, for one reason or another, analogous modifications of sensitivity occur, one will be able to observe memory phenomena analogous to those of somnambulism.

---

Who has not been struck by the fact that a hysteric <note>A historical diagnostic term for patients with various sensory or emotional disturbances, often treated via hypnosis</note>, anesthetic in the waking state, is no longer anesthetic in catalepsy? <note>A trance state characterized by rigid muscles and lack of response to stimuli</note> Close the left fist of Léonie or Lucie during their waking state, and she will not notice it; and yet, if I close their fist during catalepsy, even without them being able to see it, I will suggest to them a feeling of anger. If one puts a key in Léonie's left hand during the waking state, she will not know what it is; put the same object in the left hand during catalepsy and she will make the gesture of opening a door. There is therefore a tactile sensation during catalepsy that did not exist during the waking state. One should no longer be surprised, then, if these two women do not remember their catalepsy while awake, but do remember it in the second somnambulism, when they have recovered their tactile sensation.

A hysteric, like Lucie or Léonie, remembers her nighttime dreams upon awakening when they were visual, but does not remember them when they were motor-based and she rose from her bed—which she cannot do in the dark without the intervention of the motor sense <sup>1</sup>.

### **VII. The various successive psychological existences: spontaneous modifications of the personality**

When a certain number of psychological phenomena are gathered together, a very important new fact usually occurs in the mind: their unity, noticed and understood, gives rise to a particular judgment that we call the idea of the self <note>original: "le moi"</note>. This is, we say, a judgment and not an association of ideas: the latter reproduces phenomena one after another, juxtaposing them automatically and thereby providing us the opportunity to notice their unity and judge their resemblance; but it does not constitute this relationship of unity and resemblance by itself. Judgment, on the contrary, synthesizes different facts, establishes their unity, and—regarding the various psychological phenomena awakened by sensory impressions or the automatic play of association—forms a new idea: that of personality. We do not have to study this judgment of unity in this study, which focuses on the automatic part and not on the active part of the mind. Let us be content to show that the psychological phenomena filling the mind can be, as we have just seen, very different according to the various states; this judgment, this idea of personality, must undergo analogous modifications and vary in the same subject according to changes in sensations and memories.

That was real, however; if we do not recognize ourselves, it is because we have changed. Fortunately, these changes occurred little by little and they only actually affected the complex and secondary phenomena of our mind—our beliefs, our ambitions, our desires.
