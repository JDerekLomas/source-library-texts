**The Bronze Fagong Cannon**

Each unit weighs approximately 500 <term>catties (斤)</term> <note>Approximately 300 kilograms or 660 pounds.</note> and utilizes one hundred lead bullets, each weighing approximately four catties. This is an exceptionally effective weapon for besieging cities. When facing tens of thousands of enemy troops gathered together, one also uses this to attack them.

Its stone projectiles are as large as a small <term>dou (斗)</term> <note>A traditional dry measure roughly equivalent to 10 liters; the projectile would be roughly the size of a large melon.</note>. Anything struck or touched by these stones cannot survive. Upon meeting a wall, it pierces through. Upon meeting a house, it destroys it. Upon meeting a tree, it snaps it. Should humans or livestock encounter it, they are instantly reduced to a mangled slurry. When it strikes a mountain, it penetrates several feet deep.

It is not merely the stone projectile itself that is irresistible. Any object grazed or chipped by the stone is sent flying, colliding with other things, and those things are likewise destroyed. Even human limbs and flesh, if struck by the debris splashed up by the stone, will be mangled and ruined.

Furthermore, the danger is not limited to the physical impact of the projectile. After the gunpowder is discharged, its "breath" <note>The toxic fumes or gas produced by combustion.</note> can poison and kill people. Its "wind" <note>The concussive shockwave or blast radius.</note> can strike people dead. Its sound can shock and kill a person. 

Therefore, when one intends to fire the weapon, a pit must be dug in the ground at the firing site to allow the gunner to hide behind. When the fuse is lit, the fiery gases and the sound will surge upward, and the gunner may thus escape death.

These cannons are constructed from iron and measure five to six feet in length. They are characterized by a "large belly" <note>the breech section</note> and a "long neck" <note>the barrel</note>. Within the breech, there is a long opening designed to hold five "sub-cannons" <term>zichong</term> <note>removable breech chambers that allowed for rapid reloading</note> which are loaded with gunpowder and inserted into the breech one after another to be fired in rotation. The exterior of the cannon is encased in wood and bound with iron hoops to prevent the weapon from bursting under the pressure of the blast.

On naval vessels, four or five of these cannons are positioned on each side beneath the gunwales, allowing them to be fired discreetly from within the ship's cabins. If an enemy vessel draws near and is struck by a single projectile, its hull planks will be shattered, causing water to pour in and the ship to leak. Because of this weapon, these people roam the seas at will, and no other nation can oppose them.

At the time of a campaign against sea pirates, an interpreter presented one of these cannons to the authorities along with the formula for its gunpowder. The device was tested at the military training grounds, but its range was limited to only one hundred paces. Thus, while it is a powerful asset for naval ships and can be used effectively for defending city walls, it is of no use for mobile land battles.

Later, Wang Xuan <note>styled Chengzhai</note>, while serving as the Minister of War, petitioned the   Emperor <note>The original text uses "taitou," a traditional respectful spacing, before mentioning the Sovereign</note> to cast more than a thousand of these cannons to be distributed among the Three Frontiers <note>the strategic northern border regions of the Ming Dynasty</note>. One particular variety is mounted on a wooden frame that allows it to be lowered, raised, or pivoted left and right; this specific design was already an original Chinese invention and did not originate from the Franks <note>original: "Folangji" (佛郎機), the term used for the Portuguese and their artillery</note>.

# *Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms*: Equipment, Volume 8
Page: Great

Those who manufacture **<term>Lead-Tin Muskets</term>** <note>original: "Qianxichong." While literally "Lead-Tin Gun," this refers to the high-quality matchlock muskets of the Ming Dynasty, so named either for the alloy of their components or the nature of their ammunition.</note> must understand the **refining of iron**. Because the raw ore contains slag and impurities, it must be forged and refined without ceasing until the slag is completely melted away and the metal reaches a state of pure essence. Only then can one avoid the dangers of the barrel becoming brittle, snapping, or exploding. 

Therefore, refining ten catties of raw iron down to one catty of pure steel is considered superior; refining ten catties down to five is considered secondary. The barrel must be perfectly round and smooth on the inside, and its **<term>Owl</term>** <note>original: "Xiao." A technical term for the front sight of the musket, positioned near the muzzle.</note> must be upright and straight. 

Those who operate the musket must be chosen from among those with agile hands and feet. When facing the enemy, the process of loading the powder, inserting the wadding, aligning the **<term>Owl</term>**, and lighting the match must be performed without the slightest delay. 

If the gunner sees only the **<term>Owl</term>** (front sight) and not the barrel, the aim will err by being too high. If one sees only the barrel and not the **<term>Owl</term>**, the aim will err by being too low. In either case, the shot will not hit the mark. 

Today, people commonly stand in lines and use this weapon for long-range attacks, but this is incorrect. It must be used only when close to the enemy, interspersed with soldiers carrying long and short-range melee weapons. We must seize the momentum and advance rapidly, causing the rebels to dodge the shots; in the brief moment while they are blinking or dazzled by the flash, our troops will have already penetrated their ranks. 

The true ingenuity of the **<term>Lead-Tin Musket</term>** lies entirely in this tactic. If one relies on it merely to attack an enemy from afar, is that not a great mistake?

# Method for Internal Loading of the Musket

---

This musket is shaped like a **<term>Bird-Gun</term>** <note>original: "Niao chong." The Chinese term for a matchlock musket, named for its perceived accuracy in being able to hit a bird in flight.</note>. # **Refining the Gunpowder Barrel**

# The Leveling Instrument

The **Leveling Instrument** <term>shuiping<gloss>A traditional Chinese surveying tool used to establish a horizontal plane in construction and engineering.</gloss></term> is a fundamental device used in the layout of military defenses and civil works.

The instrument consists of a long, horizontal wooden trough that is precisely notched to allow for sightings. This trough is mounted upon a sturdy supporting frame. In practice, the trough is filled with water to provide a perfectly level surface for the surveyor’s eye. 

Suspended from the underside of the frame are three decorative **plumb bobs** <note>original: 墜子 *zhuizi*</note>. These weighted lines serve as vertical references; when the plumb lines align with the markings on the frame, the operator knows the instrument is stable and upright. This tool ensures that the foundations of walls, the height of battlements, and the floors of watchtowers are perfectly horizontal, which is critical for both structural integrity and the effective use of the defensive weapons described on the preceding pages.

These instruments are used in tandem with the leveling trough. To measure the height of a terrain or the intended slope of a rampart, a surveyor stands at a fixed point with the water level. An assistant moves some distance away and holds the **Measuring Pole** perfectly upright.

Attached to this pole is the **Target Board**. The board is adjusted up or down until the surveyor, looking across the level water in their trough, sees the edge or center-line of the board perfectly aligned with their eye. By reading the marks on the **Measuring Pole** at the point where the **Target Board** is fixed, the surveyor can calculate the exact elevation of that spot. 

In the construction of high walls and the digging of deep moats, such precision was essential. These tools ensured that even across uneven or hilly ground, the finished military structures would be structurally sound and properly aligned with the surrounding landscape.

# The Drop Gate and the Secret Gate

The **Drop Gate** <term>chaban<gloss>Literally "inserted board"; a heavy, reinforced timber barrier dropped into place behind the main gate to create a secondary line of defense, functioning similarly to a portcullis.</gloss></term> and the main city gate together form a "double gate" system. Its construction utilizes elm or locust wood <note>Both woods are prized for their hardness and durability in military engineering.</note>, and its width and height are calibrated to match the city gate itself. The surface is entirely covered with raw ox-hide and lined with iron plating. Iron rings are fixed to both sides, through which iron chains are threaded.

For all large city gates, two "cheek-posts" <note>Vertical side-frames</note> are erected five feet away from the main gate leaves. Grooves are carved into these posts to form a track, which is also lined with iron plating. If an enemy arrives, soldiers use a **windlass** <term>jiaoche<gloss>A mechanical winch or crane located on the gate tower used to raise and lower heavy objects with chains.</gloss></term> from the gate tower above to release the iron chains, dropping the **Drop Gate** into its tracks. The exterior is then packed with earth to protect against fire attacks, while the interior is braced with pillars to prevent the gate from being forced inward or collapsing.

Another tactical variation states that if a **Drop Gate** is not used, one may instead carve several dozen holes into the main gate leaves. When the enemy presses against the city gate, defenders can thrust spears and halberds through these openings or fire heavy crossbows. This is known as a **Perforated Gate Leaf** <term>zaoshan<gloss>Literally "carved fan/leaf"; a gate modified with defensive apertures.</gloss></term>.

# The Secret Gate

**Secret Gates** <term>anmen<gloss>Literally "dark gate"; a hidden sally port built into the wall for surprise counter-attacks or reconnaissance.</gloss></term> are further installed at locations convenient for the movement of troops. These are secretly carved into the wall, but on the exterior, about a foot of the wall's thickness is left untouched and not fully pierced. This ensures the gate remains invisible to the enemy until it is time to break through and launch a surprise ambush. 

The dimensions of this gate are seven feet high and six feet wide. Internally, it is fitted with "sand-discharging" support pillars <note>original: 排沙柱 (paishazhu); likely structural supports that could be quickly cleared of debris</note>, a horizontal crossbeam at the top, and gate leaves at the base. Defenders constantly monitor the enemy, waiting for a lapse in their guard to launch a sudden sortie.

### Ground Obstacles
**Ground Obstacle** <term>dise<gloss>Literally "Ground Roughness"; a spiked board or plank used as a hidden trap.</gloss></term>: These are made by fixing barbed nails onto wooden planks. The planks should be three inches thick, approximately three feet long, and two feet wide.

### Iron Tribulus (Caltrops)
**Iron Tribulus** <term>tie jili<gloss>Standard metal caltrops, often with a central hole for stringing them together.</gloss></term> are placed collectively along the essential paths that an enemy must take. They ensure that neither men nor horses can gallop freely. In ancient times, these were known as **Quda** <term>quda<gloss>An archaic term for caltrops found in early Chinese military texts like the Mozi.</gloss></term>.

### Hoof-Snare Gate
**Hoof-Snare Gate** <term>choutiguan<gloss>A square wooden trap designed to catch and puncture a horse's hoof.</gloss></term>: This device is constructed from four pieces of wood joined into a square frame, seven inches in diameter. Barbed iron nails are applied horizontally across the center. This is another implement used for blocking the paths of horses.

### Wooden Caltrops
**Wooden Caltrops** <term>mu jili<gloss>A budget-friendly or improvised version of the metal caltrop.</gloss></term> are fashioned from triangular pieces of wood.

Whenever the water in a moat becomes shallow due to drought, one should distribute **Iron Water-Caltrops** within the water. Outside the walls of a major city, **Deer-Horn Wood** should be planted extensively in all directions.

## The Striking Rolling Log

**The Striking Rolling Log** <term>bolei<gloss>Derived from 'bo' (to strike or grapple) and 'lei' (a heavy log used for defense).</gloss></term> [illustrated in the first diagram] is a formidable defensive weapon designed to be dropped from the height of a city wall. 

Unlike a simple smooth log, the **Striking Rolling Log** is characterized by its ridged or "toothed" surface. These ridges were often carved directly into the heavy timber or reinforced with iron bands. 
*   **Function:** When dropped onto a mass of attackers or scaling ladders, the uneven surface ensures a devastating impact, splintering wood and crushing armor more effectively than a flat surface.
*   **Deployment:** It was typically held in place by ropes at the top of the ramparts and released once the enemy reached the base of the wall.

## The Smooth Rolling Log

**The Smooth Rolling Log** <term>nilei<gloss>Literally "Mud Rolling Log." The name likely refers to its smooth, rounded surface which, when wet or coated in clay, would slide effortlessly over obstacles.</gloss></term> [illustrated in the second diagram] is a heavy, cylindrical beam of solid wood.

While simpler in design than the "striking" variety, its effectiveness lay in its sheer mass and momentum. 
<note>In historical siege warfare, these logs were sometimes coated in grease or mud to make them harder for the enemy to catch, redirect, or use as makeshift cover once they reached the ground.</note> 
These were the standard "rolling stones" of the Chinese arsenal, easy to manufacture in large quantities during a prolonged siege.

## The Cart-Wheel Rolling Log

**The Cart-Wheel Rolling Log** <term>chejiaolei<gloss>Literally "Cart-Leg Rolling Log," referring to the axle and wheel assembly.</gloss></term> [illustrated in the third diagram] represents a more advanced mechanical approach to wall defense.

As shown in the woodcut, this device consists of a central rolling log fitted with a **spiked wheel** or "cart-leg" at its center, mounted on an axle.
*   **The Axle and Rope:** A thick rope is wound around the central axle. This allowed defenders to:
    1.  **Control the descent:** The log could be lowered at a specific speed rather than just dropped.
    2.  **Recovery:** Most importantly, the rope allowed soldiers on the wall to winch the heavy log back up after it had done its damage, allowing it to be "reloaded" and used repeatedly.
*   **The Spiked Wheel:** The central wheel (often studded with iron spikes) acted as a pivot, allowing the log to "walk" or bounce over uneven piles of debris or bodies, ensuring it didn't get stuck before reaching the main enemy line.

# Construction of the Subterranean Tunnel

The **subterranean tunnel** <note>Original: 地道 (*dì dào*). In Western siege terminology, this is a "mine" or "sap."</note> described to the right is approximately seven feet five inches high and eight feet wide. 

Whenever an army attacks a city, they use a **lead chariot** <note>Original: 頭車 (*tóu chē*). This was a mobile, armored mantlet or shed that protected workers from projectiles while they reached the base of the enemy wall.</note> to reach the city wall. Under its protection, they begin to excavate the wall's foundation to create the tunnel. For every foot or so that is excavated, one must immediately install a **horizontal ground-sill**, erect **sand-stopping pillars**, and mount **roof beams**. 

These structures are essential to prevent the earth of the city wall from collapsing downward onto the workers. As the digging progresses deeper, the wooden supports must be installed continuously to match the depth. The laborers who transport the timber move back and forth through the interior and exterior of the **protective shed** <note>Original: 緒棚 (*xù péng*), referring to the covered gallery or extension of the lead chariot.</note> attached to the lead chariot.

When the workers have **hollowed out the wall base** <note>Original: 宂城 (*rǒng chéng*).</note> and are nearly ready to break through to the other side, they should leave approximately three to five feet of earth remaining as a temporary shell. Firewood is then piled high within the tunnel and set ablaze. When the wooden **support pillars** snap from the heat and fire, the city wall above, having lost its foundation, will collapse.

<term>Lead Chariot (頭車): A heavy, mobile shield used to protect sappers from arrows and stones while they work at the base of a wall.</term>
<term>Horizontal Ground-Sill (橫地柎): A wooden beam laid flat on the floor of the tunnel to provide a stable base for vertical posts.</term>
<term>Sand-Stopping Pillars (排沙柱): Vertical timber supports designed to hold back loose soil and prevent cave-ins.</term>
<term>Roof Beams (菴樑): Horizontal timbers that support the ceiling of the tunnel.</term>

<note>The tactic described here—burning the supports to collapse a wall—is known as "firing the mine." Before the widespread use of gunpowder explosives, this was the most effective way to breach heavy fortifications. The engineers would replace the dirt they removed with wooden props, then use fire to "withdraw" those props simultaneously, causing a catastrophic structural failure in the masonry above.</note>
