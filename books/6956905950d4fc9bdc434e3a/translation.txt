It is a dilemma <note>original: "Dilemma"; a situation where one must choose between two unfavorable options</note> frequently argued against translations of this kind, that when a subject lies far beyond common understanding, learned people will seek knowledge of it in the original scholarly languages, while others must remain equally strangers to it in any language. However, this argument loses its force depending on two things: whether the original work was written in an unfortunate style, or whether the improvements of a modern language have allowed it to explain the most difficult reasoning and to speak even of philosophy with the advantages of eloquence. 

As both of these conditions are met to justify this present attempt, the second condition—which may have been weakened by my own personal inability—seems to be made up for by a particular weight and importance in the first. For the praise of our author’s judgment has hardly been more universal than the criticism of his writing style. Although everyone acknowledges his writing is expressive, most find it so difficult and discouraging that they sometimes imagine the meaning has forced its way through the phrasing, leaving these irregularities behind as signs of its struggle. It is true that at the very beginning of his work, he did not fail to apologize for this lack of ornament. But the excuse he offers is one that better explains the necessary roughness of a difficult subject than the particular awkwardness of a writer. 

There was another visible disadvantage that required relief. # The PREFACE.

*The author was modern in his approach, yet he arranged the most distant examples and illustrations under the various headings of his work. Consequently, every page became burdened—not only with numerous lengthy citations, but with disorganized marks for additions, references, comparisons, and the like. It was as if the confusion of an overflowing margin had spilled over and discharged itself onto the main text. Thus, the sentences were frequently disjointed, and the argument was interrupted or obscured; the greatest part of the main body was forced into the unappealing shape of a commonplace book <note>A "commonplace book" was a personal journal where authors gathered miscellaneous scraps of information, quotes, and observations under general headings.</note>.*

*Some efforts have been applied here toward lessening both of these imperfections. Regarding the style, it is hoped that the metal—however rough and incapable of being polished to exact beauty—may appear less deformed after this second melting and may pass into more general use. However, it is beyond doubt that the other inequality has been further corrected. We have moved the unnecessary and misshapen mass of foreign additions to the bottom of the page. It might not have been an unpardonable boldness to have stripped these away entirely, but we must be cautious when altering an author’s work, taking care lest by cutting off an overgrowth, we should endanger the life of the overall design.*

*Custom has not so far prevailed over decency as to require the translators to proclaim the justice of their choice or the merit of their author. Much less would they base his character on the offensive argument of comparison. To compete with the admired names of Grotius <note>Hugo Grotius (1583–1645), a Dutch jurist who laid the foundations for international law.</note>, Selden <note>John Selden (1584–1654), an English jurist and scholar of Jewish law.</note>, and the Right Reverend the present Lord Bishop of Peterborough <note>Richard Cumberland (1631–1718), who wrote against Thomas Hobbes.</note>, is something he ought to decline out of modesty, if not for safety. Yet, this seems to have turned out to his advantage: none of those great men attempted a complete body of moral or political doctrine. The first [Grotius] chose to rely on authorities taken from historians, civil lawyers <note>original: "civilians"</note>, and experts in church law <note>original: "canonists"</note>, rather than on philosophical deductions drawn from the nature of men and the reason of things. The second [Selden] entered no further into this subject than the Jewish institutions directed his view. My Lord of Peterborough, having overthrown the deceitful fabric of a late treacherous builder <note>A reference to Thomas Hobbes and his work "Leviathan."</note> and settled the science of morality on its truest and deepest foundations, left the superstructure to be raised by inferior hands. Thus, while each had a particular boundary to his own inquiries, all contributed to the perfection of our author's system.*

*In this system, the author does not abstain from grateful remembrances of these and other learned benefactors. Likewise, he now teaches the publisher of this translation to most gladly acknowledge the favor and assistance of his friends. Two of them especially—the Reverend Mr. Percivale and the Reverend Mr. Itchiner—by entirely translating the fifth and eighth books, have kindly accepted a part of his burden and allowed him a share in their reputation and success.*
