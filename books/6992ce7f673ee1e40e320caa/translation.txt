# Pepper

->**Pepper**<-
<note>original: "椒" (Jiao). This refers specifically to Szechuan pepper (*Zanthoxylum* species), a native Chinese spice known for its "numbing" quality, distinct from the black pepper of India or the chili peppers of the Americas.</note>

Pepper grows in the river valleys of Wudu <note>an ancient commandery located near the border of modern Gansu and Sichuan provinces</note> and Ba Commandery <note>an ancient region covering modern-day Chongqing and eastern Sichuan</note>. The plant reaches four to five feet in height and resembles the <term>Dogwood<note>original: "茱萸" (Zhuyu), likely referring to *Cornus officinalis*. Ancient botanists often compared plants to the dogwood due to its similar pinnate leaves.</note></term>, though it is smaller and possesses needle-like thorns. Its leaves are firm and smooth; they can be boiled and consumed as food or drink. The stem is spicy and fragrant.

In the fourth month, it bears fruit without appearing to flower, the seeds emerging from between the leaves. These seeds are round and similar in size to small beans, with a purple-red skin. In the eighth month, the fruit is harvested and roasted until dry. Its flavor is spicy and its nature is "warm" and "greatly hot." It is considered toxic <note>In Traditional Chinese Medicine, "toxic" (you du) often refers to a potent pharmacological effect that requires careful dosing, rather than being purely poisonous.</note>.

It is primarily used to treat <term>Pathogenic Qi<note>original: "邪氣" (Xieqi). These are external environmental factors like wind, cold, or dampness believed to cause disease.</note></term> and coughing with rebellious breath. It warms the "center" <note>the digestive system</note>, expels cold and dampness causing numbness or pain in the joints and skin, and directs <term>Qi<note>the body's vital energy</note></term> downward. It removes cold from the <term>Six Viscera<note>The gallbladder, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, bladder, and the "Triple Burner" (San Jiao).</note></term>. It is further used for:
*   Cold-damage disorders and "warm malaria."
*   "Great wind" <note>a historical term often associated with leprosy or severe skin conditions</note> where the patient cannot sweat.
*   Fluid retention in the chest and abdomen, and chronic indigestion.
*   Dysentery and the "leakage of essence" <note>nocturnal emissions</note>.
*   Postpartum ailments in women.

It disperses "wind-evil," abdominal masses, edema, and jaundice. It is also used against <term>Ghost Possession<note>original: "鬼疰" (Guizhu). A historical diagnostic category for chronic, wasting diseases (like tuberculosis) that seemed to "spread" through families like a haunting.</term> and <term>Gu Poison<note>original: "蠱毒" (Gudu). A legendary and feared type of poison or sorcery involving venomous creatures.</note></term>. It can be used to kill parasites and neutralize toxins from fish. 

**Warning:** Those seeds that do not open their "mouths" <note>referring to the split in the husk of the peppercorn</note> are lethal to humans.

# Honey Locust

<note>original: "皂莢" (Zaojia). Also known as the Soap Pod tree, its fruit contains natural saponins and was traditionally used in China as both a laundry detergent and a powerful medicinal agent.</note>

The Honey Locust originates in the river valleys of Yongzhou <note>an ancient province covering parts of modern Shaanxi and Gansu</note> and Lu Zou County <note>in modern-day Shandong</note>. Today, it can be found in many locations, though those produced in Huaimengzhou <note>a region in modern Henan province renowned for high-quality medicinal crops</note> are considered the finest. 

The tree can grow to an immense height and size. There are three distinct varieties of this plant. *The Classic* <note>referring to the *Shennong Bencao Jing*, or "The Divine Farmer's Herbology Classic"</note> states that those shaped like "pig tusks" are of superior quality. The commentary by Tao <note>Tao Hongjing, a 5th-century scholar and alchemist</note> notes that pods measuring one foot and two inches in length are the best. However, the Tang Dynasty commentaries suggest that pods six inches long—round, thick, with short segments and a straight shape—are superior. These should have thin skin, abundant pulp, and a concentrated flavor.

Its flavor is pungent and salty; its nature is warm and "slightly toxic" <note>In this context, "toxic" refers to a potent pharmacological action that requires careful administration rather than being a deadly poison</note>. 

It is primarily used to treat <term>Wind-Paralysis<note>original: "風痺" (Fengbi). A traditional diagnosis involving numbness, pain, or loss of mobility caused by "wind" and "dampness" in the body's channels.</note></term>, deadened skin, and "evil qi" <note>pathogenic factors</note>. It addresses headaches caused by wind that result in involuntary tearing. It is effective in clearing the <term>Nine Orifices<note>The nine openings of the body: eyes, ears, nostrils, mouth, urethra, and anus; clearing them refers to restoring the body's natural passages and sensory functions.</note></term> and destroying "refined spirits" <note>likely referring to parasites or harmful internal influences</note>. It is further used to treat abdominal distension and fullness, aid the digestion of food, and relieve coughing or the formation of <term>encapsulated swellings<note>original: "囊結" (Nangjie). Refers to hard lumps, cysts, or localized fluid accumulations.</note></term>. Additionally, it is used for cases where a <term>placenta is not expelled<note>original: "婦人胞不落" (Furen bao bu luo). A serious postpartum complication where the afterbirth remains in the womb.</note></term> in women and for the purpose of brightening the eyesight.

# Haritaki

Haritaki <note>original: 訶梨勒 (*Hēlílè*); scientifically known as *Terminalia chebula*. It is a major medicinal fruit in both Chinese and Ayurvedic traditions, often called the "King of Medicines" in Tibetan culture.</note> grows in the Jiao and Ai Prefectures <note>ancient administrative regions in the far south, corresponding to parts of modern-day northern Vietnam and the borderlands of Guangxi</note>. Today, it can be found throughout the Lingnan region <note>the lands south of the Nanling Mountains, primarily Guangdong and Guangxi</note>, though it is most abundant in Guangzhou.

The tree’s form is similar to the *muwan* <note>original: 木梡; likely referring to a type of wild pear or a similar sturdy hardwood tree</note>. Its flowers are white, and the fruit resembles that of the gardenia. The fruit is greenish-yellow in color, with the skin and flesh tightly fused together. 

The harvest takes place in the seventh and eighth lunar months when the fruit is fully ripe. Specimens characterized by "six roads" <note>original: 六路 (*liù lù*); this refers to the six distinct longitudinal ridges or ribs found on the dried fruit, which were historically used to determine the highest grade of the medicine</note> are considered the finest quality. Its flavor is bitter, its nature is warm, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat "cold qi" <note>a traditional medical condition involving digestive stagnation and a sensation of cold in the torso</note>, to relieve abdominal distension and fullness, and to aid in the digestion of food.

# Croton Bean

The Croton Bean <note>original: 巴豆 (*Bādòu*); scientifically known as *Croton tiglium*. This plant is famous in East Asian medicine as a "drastic purgative," used to forcefully clear the digestive tract.</note> originates in the mountain valleys of Ba Prefecture <note>an ancient region encompassing modern-day Chongqing and eastern Sichuan; the plant is named "Ba-bean" because of its abundance in this specific territory</note>.

The tree reaches a height of ten to twenty feet. Its leaves are similar in shape to those of the cherry tree, though they are notably thicker and larger. When the leaves first emerge, they are green, gradually turning yellow as they mature. The fruit is ready for harvest during the eighth and ninth lunar months.

The fruit itself is approximately the size of a large soybean and is protected by a hard outer shell. This shell is naturally divided into three distinct valves or chambers, each containing a single seed kernel inside.

<note>Historically, the Croton Bean was regarded as a "fierce" medicine. Because of its high toxicity and the presence of croton oil—which can cause severe blistering and violent purging—it was often processed by being wrapped in paper and pressed to remove the oil (a method known as "making frost") before being administered in minute doses for extreme digestive blockages.</note>

# Croton Bean

The Croton Bean <note>original: 巴豆 (*Bādòu*); scientific name *Croton tiglium*</note> originates in the mountain valleys of Ba Prefecture <note>an ancient region encompassing modern-day Chongqing and eastern Sichuan</note>. Today, it can be found throughout Jia, Mei, and Rong Prefectures <note>modern-day Leshan, Meishan, and Yibin in Sichuan Province</note>.

The tree reaches a height of ten to twenty feet. Its leaves resemble those of the cherry or the gardenia, but they are thicker and larger. When they first emerge, they are green, gradually turning yellow or red as they mature. In the fourth lunar month, flowers bloom in spikes. In the fifth or sixth month, the plant bears fruit within a capsule. Each capsule is divided into three valves, and each valve contains a single seed, totaling three seeds per fruit.

The variety produced in Rong Prefecture is considered the finest. The shells of these beans have faint, raised vertical lines resembling threads—ranging from a single line to two or three. These are known as **Golden-Thread Croton** <note>original: 金線巴豆 (*Jīnxiàn bādòu*)</note> and are of the highest quality.

Another name for this plant is the **Ba Pepper** <note>original: 巴椒 (*Bājiāo*)</note>. Its flavor is acrid and its nature is warm. When raw, its property is warm, but when cooked, it is considered cold. It is **highly toxic**.

It is primarily used to treat:
*   Cold-damage disorders and "warm malaria" <note>an ancient term for a type of fever where the patient feels heat more than chills</note>.
*   Breaking up <term>Abdominal masses</term> <note>original: 癥瘕 (*Zhēngjiǎ*); refers to hard or movable lumps in the abdomen caused by stagnation</note> and accumulated fluids, phlegm, or chronic congestion.
*   Severe abdominal swelling and water retention.

It acts to scour and cleanse the <term>Five Viscera and Six Bowels</term> <note>a collective term for the primary internal organs in traditional medicine</note>, opening up blockages and clearing the passages for water and grain <note>the urinary and digestive tracts</note>. It removes "evil flesh" <note>necrotic or diseased tissue</note> and eliminates <term>Ghost toxins</term> <note>original: 鬼毒 (*Guǐdú*); sudden, severe illnesses or seizures historically attributed to malevolent influences</note> and <term>Gu-poisoning</term> <note>original: 蠱疰 (*Gǔzhù*); a category of lethal diseases traditionally believed to be caused by venomous parasites or toxins</note>. It also treats the cessation of the menses in women and can be used to expel a dead fetus.

# Willow

The willow grows in the rivers and marshes of <term>Langya</term> <note>original: 琅邪 (*Lángyá*); an ancient region in what is now Shandong Province, traditionally known for its waterways</note>. Today, it is found everywhere. It is commonly referred to as the "Poplar-Willow."

The <term>Classic of Materia Medica</term> <note>original: 本經 (*Běnjīng*), short for the *Shennong Bencao Jing*, the foundation of Chinese herbalism</note> identifies the <term>catkins</term> <note>original: 絮 (*xù*); the soft, fluffy masses of fibers associated with willow seeds</note> as the tree's flowers. However, <term>Chen Cangqi</term> <note>A renowned Tang Dynasty scholar and pharmacologist known for correcting earlier botanical errors</note> states that the "flowers" are actually the yellow stamens that first emerge, whereas the "seeds" are the flying catkins.

The willow is bitter in flavor, cold in nature, and non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat <term>wind-edema</term> <note>original: 風水 (*fēngshuǐ*); in a medical context, this refers to sudden swelling or fluid retention caused by external "wind" pathogens, distinct from the practice of geomancy</note>, jaundice, facial flushing, and <term>dark skin spots</term> <note>original: 黑皯 (*hēigǎn*); likely referring to melasma or other forms of hyperpigmentation</note>. It is also effective against scabies, malignant sores, and <term>metal wounds</term> <note>original: 金瘡 (*jīnchuāng*); wounds specifically caused by blades or weapons</note>.

The leaves are used to treat blood stagnation within the chest and abdomen and to relieve pain. The fruit <note>the mature seed-bearing catkin</note> is mainly used to treat ruptured abscesses, as it aids in expelling pus and blood. The juice of the seeds is used to treat thirst.

# Melia

The fruit of the **Melia** is the same as the *Golden Bell seed* <note>original: 金鈴子 (*jinlingzi*), so named for the fruit's shape and color</note>. It grows in the valleys of Mount Jingshan, though it is now found everywhere; those sourced from the Shuchuan region <note>modern-day Sichuan</note> are considered the finest. The tree grows to a height of over ten feet, with dense leaves that resemble the locust tree but are longer. In the third or fourth month of the lunar calendar, it produces reddish-purple blossoms whose fragrance fills the entire courtyard. The fruit is shaped like a small pellet, green when raw and turning yellow when ripe. The fruit is harvested in the twelfth lunar month, while the roots may be gathered at any time. There are male and female varieties of this species. The <gloss>male</gloss> variety has reddish roots, produces no seeds, and is highly toxic. The female variety has white roots, produces seeds, and is only slightly toxic; the female variety should be used for medicinal purposes. It is commonly known as the *Bitter Melia seed*. It is primarily used to treat febrile illnesses, "cold damage" disorders <note>a broad category of acute infectious diseases in traditional medicine</note>, and high fevers. It also kills the "three worms" <note>a traditional term for various internal parasites</note>, heals scabies and sores, and aids the flow of the urinary tract.

## Chinese Toon and Tree of Heaven

The **Chinese Toon** <note>original: 椿 (*chun*); *Toona sinensis*</note> and the **Tree of Heaven** <note>original: 樗 (*chu*); *Ailanthus altissima*</note>.

The leaves of the **Chinese Toon** have a bitter taste and are toxic. They are primarily used as a medicinal wash for scabies, sores, and wind-ulcers <note>original: 風疽 (*fengju*); a traditional term for skin eruptions or necrotic sores believed to be caused by "pathogenic wind"</note>. To use them, one should boil the leaves in water and apply the resulting juice.

The bark is primarily used to treat deafness. Regarding the **Tree of Heaven**, its roots and leaves are considered especially effective for medicinal purposes. The physical forms and trunks of these two trees are, for the most part, very similar to each other.

However, the **Chinese Toon** is solid and fragrant, and its young shoots are edible. In contrast, the **Tree of Heaven** is porous and coarse with a foul odor. People of the North refer to the **Tree of Heaven** as the "Mountain Toon," while those in the Jiangdong region <note>literally "East of the River," referring to the lower reaches of the Yangtze River</note> call it "Ghost Eyes."

This name is derived from the fact that where the leaves detach from the branch, the remaining scars resemble *chupu* <term>chupu (*chūpú*): an ancient Chinese board game; the "seeds" or dice used in the game were often made of wood and shaped like almonds</term> seeds or human eyes.

The *Erya* <note>the oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, dating back to at least the 3rd century BCE</note> states that the *Kao* <note>a species of evergreen tree, likely *Castanopsis* or *Mallotus*</note> is the "Mountain Chu." In his commentary, Guo Pu <note>a famous scholar and naturalist of the Jin Dynasty (276–324 AD)</note> notes that the *Kao* resembles the **Tree of Heaven**, but its color is a pale white and it grows deep in the mountains, hence its name. It also bears a resemblance to the lacquer tree.

## Yellow Yam
<note>original: 黃藥 (*huangyao*); *Dioscorea bulbifera*. Commonly known today as the Air Potato or Yellow Yam. In traditional medicine, it is valued for its ability to cool the blood and reduce swelling.</note>

The root of the Yellow Yam grows in the Lingnan region <note>A geographical area of southern China covering the Guangdong and Guangxi regions.</note>. Today, it is also found in the prefectures of Kuixia, as well as within the mountains of Ming, Yue, Qin, and Long. Those harvested from Zhong and Wan prefectures are considered the superior variety. It grows as a climbing vine, reaching three to four feet in height. Both its roots and stems resemble those of the small mulberry tree. The roots are typically harvested in the tenth month of the lunar calendar.

The variety produced in Qin prefecture is known as the "Red Medicinal Seed" <note>original: 紅藥子 (*hongyaozi*)</note>. Its leaves resemble those of buckwheat, and its stalks are reddish in color. It blooms with white flowers in the seventh month. When the roots are first harvested and still damp, they are a vibrant red; however, once they are dried in the sun, they turn yellow.

Kaizhou and the Yuan Prefecture produce another variety called the "Bitter Medicinal Seed" <note>original: 苦藥子 (*kuyaozi*)</note>, which is generally similar to the Yellow Yam.

The Yellow Yam root has a bitter taste, a balanced nature <note>In traditional pharmacology, a "balanced" (*ping*) nature means the herb is neither significantly heating nor cooling to the body's internal environment.</note>, and is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat various malignant swellings, sores, fistulas, throat obstructions <note>Likely referring to severe sore throats or goiters.</note>, and the venomous bites of snakes or dogs. To use it, take the root and grind it into a fine powder to be ingested. It can also be held in the mouth or applied topically to the affected area.

# White Poplar

White poplars <note>original: 白楊 (*baiyang*)</note> are found everywhere, but they are particularly numerous in the northern lands, where they are often planted among ruins and burial grounds. The tree grows to a large size with round leaves that resemble those of a pear tree. Because its bark is white and its wood is similar to that of other poplars, it is named the White Poplar.

There is also the Water Poplar <note>original: 水楊 (*shuiyang*)</note>, which has broad, round, reddish leaves and short twigs and stalks; it grows mostly along the banks of rivers and streams.

Regarding the Willow <note>original: 柳 (*liu*)</note>:
Cui Bao <note>A scholar of the Jin Dynasty (266–420 AD)</note> states in his *Notes on Things Past and Present*: "The leaves of the White Poplar are round; the leaves of the Green Poplar are long; and the leaves of the Willow are long and thin." 

The Pit Poplar <note>original: 核楊 (*heyang*)</note> has round leaves with weak stems that sway vigorously even in a slight breeze. Because of this, it is also known as the "High Flyer," the "Solo Swayer," or the "Rush Willow." It is further known by the names "Rush Poplar," "Pit Willow," or "Rush Pit."

The bark of this tree has a bitter taste and is non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat <term>Poisonous Wind Beriberi</term> <note>a traditional diagnosis involving swelling, numbness, or pain in the lower limbs attributed to environmental 'wind' and 'dampness'</note> and general swelling. It is also effective for limbs that are weak, sluggish, or paralyzed. When toxic energy <note>pathogenic factors</note> circulates through the skin or when there is an accumulation of phlegm-based ailments, the bark should be steeped in wine and consumed.

# Gomuti Palm

The seeds of the <term>Gomuti Palm</term> <note>original: 桄榔 (*guanglang*), scientific name: *Arenga pinnata*.</note> grow in the mountain valleys of <term>Lingnan</term> <note>original: 嶺南; the historical administrative region encompassing modern Guangdong, Guangxi, and parts of Vietnam.</note>. They are found throughout both the Guangdong and Guangxi regions. Its wood resembles the <term>Windmill Palm</term> <note>original: 栟櫚 (*binglü*); likely *Trachycarpus fortunei*.</note> but is significantly harder. When the trunk is cut open, it contains a flour-like substance <note>This refers to sago, a starch extracted from the pith of palm stems.</note>; in large specimens, one can harvest as much as several bushels. Eating this starch prevents hunger. The outer bark is extremely soft yet tough and resilient, and can be used to make <term>heavy ropes</term> <note>original: 綆 (*geng*); specifically durable ropes used for drawing water or on seafaring vessels.</note>. Its seeds form spikes that grow at the top of the tree, and they may be harvested regardless of the season.

# Oriental Blueberry

The <term>Oriental Blueberry</term> <note>original: 南燭 (*nanzhu*); *Vaccinium bracteatum*</note> grows on Mount Song; it is also found throughout the prefectures of the Jiangdong region. The plant grows to a height of three to five feet. Its leaves resemble those of the <term>Chinaberry</term> <note>original: 苦棟 (*kudong*); *Melia azedarach*</note> but are smaller. It does not wither even in the peak of winter. In the winter months, it produces red berries that grow in clusters, which is why the common folk call it the "Southern Sky Candle." Its branches and leaves may be harvested for use at any time of the year. Because this species is a woody tree that yet resembles a herbaceous plant, it is known as the "Nanzhu Herb-Tree." Its other names include: "Monkey Medicine," "Man’s Continuity," "Rear Carriage," "Weina Wood," and "King of Herbs and Trees." 

This tree is extremely slow-growing. In its first three or four years, its appearance is similar to varieties of <term>Chinese cabbage</term> <note>original: 菘菜 (*songcai*)</note> and it also closely resembles the <term>Gardenia</term> <note>original: 梔子 (*zhizi*)</note>. It takes twenty to thirty years to grow into a large specimen. Although it has eight different names depending on the regional dialect of the area where it grows, the formal name is Oriental Blueberry. Its berries are like those of the <term>Cornelian Cherry</term> <note>original: 茱萸 (*zhuyu*)</note>; they ripen in the ninth month and are tart, delicious, and edible. The leaves do not grow opposite each other; they resemble tea leaves but are rounder and thicker, with a slightly <gloss>sour</gloss> taste.

<gloss>It remains evergreen in the summer. The branches and stems are a faint purple color. The largest specimens can reach a height of forty or fifty feet, though they are quite brittle and easily broken.</gloss>

<gloss>The flavor is bitter and neutral, and it is non-toxic. It stops diarrhea, dispels drowsiness, strengthens the tendons, and boosts one's vital energy. If taken over a long period, it makes the body feel light and extends one's lifespan.</gloss>

# The Catalpa Tree

The white bark of the **Catalpa** <note>original: 梓 (*zi*); *Catalpa ovata*</note> grows in the mountain valleys of Henei <note>an ancient region north of the Yellow River in modern-day Henan Province</note>. Nowadays, it can be found everywhere along the common roads. The tree resembles the Paulownia <note>original: 桐 (*tong*)</note>, but its leaves are smaller and its flowers are purple.

Its flavor is bitter, its nature is cold <note>in Traditional Chinese Medicine, "cold" refers to the herb's cooling effect on the body's internal heat</note>, and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to clear heat, eliminate the "three worms" <note>a traditional term for various internal parasites or pathogens</note>, and treat ailments of the eyes.

# Photinia

**Photinia** <note>original: 石南 (*shinan*); specifically *Photinia serratifolia*, an evergreen shrub known for its bright red new growth.</note> grows in the valleys of **Mount Hua** <note>One of the Five Great Mountains of China, located in Shaanxi Province.</note>. Today, it is found in both the northern and southern regions. It grows upon rocky ground, and some specimens reach a very great height and size.

Those found in the regions of the rivers and lakes <note>original: 江湖 (*jianghu*); referring to the Yangtze and Huai River basins.</note> have leaves resembling the **loquat** <note>original: 枇杷 (*pipa*); *Eriobotrya japonica*.</note> but with small serrations <note>original: 刺 (*ci*); literally "thorns," referring here to the saw-toothed margins of the leaves.</note>. They do not wither even in the bitter cold of winter. In the spring, they produce clusters of white flowers, and in the autumn, they bear small fruits.

The variety found in the **Guanlong** region <note>The area encompassing modern Shaanxi and Gansu provinces.</note> has leaves similar to **Japanese star anise** <note>original: 莽草 (*mangcao*); *Illicium anisatum*.</note>. These leaves are greenish-yellow with purple spots on the underside. In seasons of heavy rain, they grow in pairs, reaching lengths of one, two, or even three inches. Their roots are horizontal, slender, and purple in color. This variety lacks flowers and fruit, but its foliage is exceptionally dense.

The leaves are harvested in the second and fourth lunar months, and the fruit is gathered in the fourth month; both should be dried in the shade.

The flavor is acrid and bitter, its nature is balanced <note>original: 平 (*ping*); a medicinal property that is neither heating nor cooling.</note>, and it is poisonous. It is primarily used to nourish **Kidney Qi** <term>Kidney Qi <note>In traditional medicine, the fundamental energy stored in the kidneys that governs growth, reproduction, and the strength of the skeletal system.</note></term>, and to treat internal injuries and the decline of **Yin** <note>The cooling, nourishing essence of the body.</note>. It benefits the tendons, bones, skin, and hair. It is also used to treat weakness in the legs, to expel pathogenic energy from the **five viscera** <note>The heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys.</note>, and to clear internal heat.

## Golden Rain Tree and Chastetree
<note>original: 欒荆 (*Luan Jing*); while these can be two different plants—the Golden Rain Tree (*Koelreuteria paniculata*) and the Chastetree (*Vitex negundo*)—they are presented here as a combined entry, likely due to their similar medicinal applications in the Materia Medica tradition.</note>

The **Golden Rain Tree and Chastetree** grow in the coastal regions of the Eastern Sea, as well as in the prefectures of **Zizhou** <note>modern-day Zichuan, Shandong Province</note> and **Fenzhou** <note>modern-day Fenyang, Shanxi Province</note>. Their nature is warm, their flavor is bitter, and they possess a slight toxicity.

Lord Su <note>original: 蘇公 (*Su Gong*); referring to Su Jing, the lead editor of the Tang Dynasty's official pharmacopoeia</note> states: "The stems and leaves are entirely similar to the **Photinia** <note>original: 石南 (*Shinan*)</note> and do not wither during the winter. They do not wither in either winter or summer.

The plant blossoms in the sixth month. The flowers come in two varieties: purple and white. The seeds resemble those of **hemp**. One should gather the shoots and leaves in the fourth month, and collect the seeds in the eighth month.

These plants are primarily used to treat "Great Wind" <note>a historical term often referring to leprosy or severe, spreading skin afflictions</note>, as well as various wind-related ailments affecting the head, face, hands, and feet. ## Wood Turtle Seed
<note>original: 木鼈子 (*Mubiezi*); the seeds of *Momordica cochinchinensis*, commonly known today as the Gac fruit.</note>

# Seed of the Maimu Tree
<note>original: 賣木子 (*Maimuzi*); literally "the seed of the tree that is sold." Historically, this plant has been associated with species like the *Celastrus* or certain woody shrubs used in traditional Chinese trauma medicine.</note>

The Seed of the Maimu Tree originates in <term>Jiannan Qiongzhou</term> <note>Modern-day Qionglai in Sichuan Province</note>; currently, it is found only in <term>Quzhou</term> <note>Modern-day Qu County in Sichuan Province</note>. The plant grows to a height of five to seven feet, with a trunk diameter of approximately one inch.

In the spring, it sprouts tender branches and twigs. The leaves are pointed and long, measuring one to two inches in length, and are entirely green. The tips of the branches are a pale purple. During the fourth or fifth month, it produces tiny, fragmented flowers. Over a hundred of these blossoms bunch together to form large, dense clusters of a scorched-red color.

Following the flowers, the seeds develop; they resemble <term>pepper seeds</term> <note>original: *jiaomu* (椒目); the glossy black seeds of the Sichuan pepper plant (*Zanthoxylum*)</note> and are nestled within the petals. These seeds are black, smooth, and glossy. Each plant produces only three to five of these large flower clusters.

In the fifth month, the branches and leaves are harvested for use. Its flavor is sweet and slightly salty; its nature is neutral and non-toxic.

It is primarily used for fractures and physical trauma, <term>internal blood pooling</term> <note>original: *xue neiliu* (血內溜); referring to internal bruising or stasis resulting from injury</note>, reconnecting severed tissues, and nourishing the bone marrow. It is also used to relieve pain and to <term>stabilize a pregnancy</term> <note>original: *antai* (安胎); a treatment used to prevent miscarriage or soothe the womb</note>.

## Lilac Daphne <term>Daphne genkwa</term>

The **Lilac Daphne** <note>original: *Yuanhua* (芫花)</note> originates in the river valleys at the source of the Huai River; today, it can be found growing almost everywhere. Its flavor is acrid and bitter, its nature is slightly warm, and it is known to be slightly toxic.

It is also indicated for <term>Gu poisoning</term> <note>an ancient category of ailments attributed to toxins or malevolent magic</note>, <term>Ghost infusion</term> <note>a traditional term for chronic, wasting, or infectious illnesses believed to be caused by spiritual influences</note>, <term>Shan-jia</term> <note>a general term for abdominal masses or hernia-like conditions</note>, and various abscesses and swellings.

It addresses the "Five Waters" <note>traditional categories of fluid accumulation related to the five internal organs</note> when they reside within the five viscera or the skin. Furthermore, it treats lower back pain and expels cold toxins as well as toxins derived from contaminated meat. 

The plant is also known by the name **Water Remover** <note>original: *Qushui* (去水)</note>, the name **Fish Poison** <note>original: *Duyu* (毒魚)</note>, and the name **Duyuan**. # Tea Tree

The **Tea Tree** <note>original: *Chashu* (茶樹)</note> is recorded in the *Materia Medica* <note>traditional Chinese pharmacological texts</note> under the name **Bitter Jia** <note>original: *Kujia* (苦檟)</note>. In terms of size, these trees are generally similar to the **Gardenia** <note>original: *Zhizi* (梔子)</note>.

In the early spring, the tree produces buds that can be prepared as a soup or a beverage. In common usage, those leaves gathered during the early harvest are called **Tea** <note>original: *Cha* (茶)</note>, while those taken later in the season are called **Ming** <note>original: *Ming* (茗)</note>. Another name for the plant is **Chuan** <note>original: *Chuan* (荈)</note>. In the region of **Shu** <note>modern-day Sichuan province</note>, it is referred to as **Bitter Jia**, though today it is universally known as **Tea**.

As a medicinal substance, it is both sweet and bitter in flavor, slightly cold in nature, and non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat fistulas and sores <note>referring to chronic or ulcerated skin conditions</note>, and it acts as a diuretic to benefit urination. It is effective at clearing phlegm-heat and quenching thirst, though it causes a person to sleep less. The **Bitter Jia** variety is particularly used for directing *qi* <note>vital energy</note> downward and aiding in the digestion of stagnant food <note>referring to chronic indigestion or food stasis</note>.

In the early spring, the tree produces buds that can be prepared as a soup or a beverage. In common usage, those leaves gathered during the early harvest are called **Tea** <note>original: *Cha* (茶)</note>, while those taken later in the season are called **Ming** <note>original: *Ming* (茗)</note>. Another name for the plant is **Chuan** <note>original: *Chuan* (荈)</note>. In the region of **Shu** <note>the historic name for the Sichuan province region</note>, it is referred to as **Bitter Jia**, though today it is universally known as **Tea**.

As a medicinal substance, it is both sweet and bitter in flavor, slightly cold in nature, and non-toxic. It is primarily used to treat fistulas and sores <note>referring to chronic skin ulcers or inflammatory conditions</note>, and it acts as a diuretic to benefit urination. It is effective at clearing "phlegm-heat" <note>a traditional Chinese medical concept involving congestion and internal heat</note> and quenching thirst, though it causes a person to sleep less. The **Bitter Jia** variety is particularly used for directing *qi* <note>the body's vital energy or breath</note> downward and aiding in the digestion of stagnant food <note>referring to chronic indigestion or the feeling of food being "stuck" in the stomach</note>.

## Silk Tree

The **Silk Tree** <note>original: *Yeheshu* (夜合樹), *Albizia julibrissin*. The name literally means "Night-closing Tree."</note> is named **Collective Joy** <note>original: *Hehuan* (合歡)</note> in the *Materia Medica* <note>original: *Bencao* (本草)</note>; another name for it is **Evening Union** <note>original: *Hehun* (合昏)</note>. Its wood resembles that of the **Chinese Parasol Tree** <note>original: *Wutong* (梧桐)</note>, but its branches are extremely supple and delicate. Its leaves are similar to those of the **Honey Locust** <note>original: *Zaojia* (皂莢)</note> and also resemble the **Scholar Tree** <note>original: *Huai* (槐)</note>, yet they are exceptionally fine and dense, intertwining with one another. Whenever a breeze blows, the leaves seem to untangle and separate, no longer clinging to each other. At nightfall, the leaves fold and close together, which is why it is called "Evening Union."

The flowers bloom in a reddish-white color, and the petals appear to be covered in a green, downy fuzz. By autumn, it bears fruit in the form of pods that are extremely thin and small. Regarding its medicinal nature, its flavor is balanced and it is non-toxic. It is primarily used to soothe the **five internal organs** <note>original: *Wuzang* (五臟); in traditional medicine, these are the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys</note> and to benefit the mind and will. It causes a person to feel joyful and free from sorrow. Taken over a long period, it makes the **body feel light** <note>original: *qingshen* (輕身); a traditional Daoist goal suggesting health and immortality</note> and **brightens the eyesight**.
