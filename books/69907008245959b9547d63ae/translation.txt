...when they bore into an amethyst and fill it with a stolen color <note>original: "furtiuo colore." *They are proven by testing* <note>original: "Tentata prob." This is a lemma, or a short excerpt from Marbodus’s poem being explained by the commentator.</note>) I find that experiments consist of four methods. First, by **weight**, for artificial stones are generally light, while natural ones are heavy—with the emerald being an exception. Second, all natural stones intended for recognition do not admit the **file** <term>file test (limam): a method of testing hardness; a metal file will scratch glass or soft fakes but not most genuine precious stones</term>, except for the emerald and the topaz. Third, a stone formed by nature delights and feeds the eyes the more you inspect it, but a fake does not. Fourth, **fire** is the "gnomon" <note>original: "gnomon," a Greek-derived term for a pointer or an indicator of truth.</note> and the most certain index. For adulterated stones are either consumed by fire or take on strange spots, while true stones emerge unharmed, with the exception of the <term>Chrysolectrum: likely a reference to yellow amber or a gold-colored mixture of gold and silver (electrum), which reacts to heat</term>. But for whoever these tests are not enough, let him read Pliny, Book 37, Chapter 13, and the *Natural Mirror*, Book 9, Title 33. <note>original: "speculum naturale." This refers to the massive encyclopedia by Vincent of Beauvais (c. 1184–1264).</note>

*Duly consecrated*) "Consecrated" is used here in place of "venerated," for stones demand their own veneration to such an extent that the <term>Diadochos: a stone traditionally used by magicians to summon spirits</term> (as Pliny believes) loses all its power if it is applied to a dead person.
