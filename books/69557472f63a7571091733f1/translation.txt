This is a digital copy of a book that was preserved for generations on library shelves before it was carefully scanned by Google as part of a project to make the world’s books discoverable online.

It has survived long enough for the copyright to expire and the book to enter the public domain <note>A legal status where a work is no longer protected by intellectual property laws and is free for public use.</note>. A public domain book is one that was never subject to copyright or whose legal copyright term has expired. Whether a book is in the public domain may vary country to country. Public domain books are our gateways to the past, representing a wealth of history, culture and knowledge that’s often difficult to discover.

Marks, notations and other marginalia <note>Notes written in the margins of a book by previous readers or owners.</note> present in the original volume will appear in this file - a reminder of this book’s long journey from the publisher to a library and finally to you.

### **Usage guidelines**

Google is proud to partner with libraries to digitize public domain materials and make them widely accessible. Public domain books belong to the public and we are merely their custodians. Nevertheless, this work is expensive, so in order to keep providing this resource, we have taken steps to prevent abuse by commercial parties, including placing technical restrictions on automated querying.

We also ask that you:

+ *Make non-commercial use of the files* We designed Google Book Search for use by individuals, and we request that you use these files for personal, non-commercial purposes.

+ *Refrain from automated querying* Do not send automated queries of any sort to Google’s system: If you are conducting research on machine translation, optical character recognition <note>The technology used to convert images of typed or handwritten text into machine-encoded text.</note> or other areas where access to a large amount of text is helpful, please contact us. We encourage the use of public domain materials for these purposes and may be able to help.

+ *Maintain attribution* The Google “watermark” you see on each file is essential for informing people about this project and helping them find additional materials through Google Book Search. Please do not remove it.

+ *Keep it legal* Whatever your use, remember that you are responsible for ensuring that what you are doing is legal. Do not assume that just because we believe a book is in the public domain for users in the United States, that the work is also in the public domain for users in other countries. Whether a book is still in copyright varies from country to country, and we can’t offer guidance on whether any specific use of any specific book is allowed. Please do not assume that a book’s appearance in Google Book Search means it can be used in any manner anywhere in the world. Copyright infringement liability can be quite severe.

# PREFACE.

---

The commentary of Theon of Smyrna <note>A 2nd-century philosopher and mathematician who wrote this "handbook" to help students understand the mathematical passages in Plato's works.</note> concerning mathematical matters useful for reading Plato has reached our age divided into two parts; the first of which (pages 1—119) exists in the Venetian manuscript 307 of the Marcian Library (**A**), and the second (pages 120—205) in manuscript 303 of the same library (**B**). I examined these books at Bonn, where they had been sent to me through the intervention of my most refined teacher, Otto Jahn; afterwards, I inspected many passages again in Venice.

Manuscript **A**, made of parchment and dating to the 11th or 12th century, is square in shape and contains nothing besides the first part of Theon. The scribe committed many errors in matters pertaining to prosody <note>The patterns of stress and intonation in language; here referring to Greek accents and breathing marks.</note>; for example, he often wrote—to cite a few instances—*νητή* [nete], *ὄγδοος* [ogdoos], *τετρακτῦς* [tetraktys], *ἶσος* [isos], *δυᾶδος* [dyados], *μεταβάσα* [metabasa] <note>The editor is noting incorrect accentuation in the original Greek technical terms for musical notes and numbers.</note>. Prepositions often lack accents. Where the form of a reflexive pronoun is required, either *ἑαυτοῦ* [of himself] or *αὐτοῦ* [of him] is placed; never, unless I am mistaken, *αὑτοῦ* [of himself]. The forms *αἰεί* and *ἀεί* [always] are read indiscriminately. The letter *iota* is never written underneath <note>Referring to the "iota subscript," a small mark under certain Greek vowels.</note>, though it is sometimes written alongside. Numbers are almost always rendered in red ink.

Those errors which that scribe committed in prosody were corrected for the most part, though not everywhere, by another hand (**A**^2), which also correctly restored *ἰδέα* [form/idea] for *εἰδέα*, *λεῖμμα* [remainder/limma] for *λίμμα*, and similar words. Several more serious changes are also found to have been made by the same hand. However, those readings were not from—

Furthermore, since even the scribe who wrote out the manuscript corrected many things and wrote over erasures, in several places it cannot be accurately determined whether a change should be attributed to the first hand or to a second hand.

Regarding manuscript B, compare what I have discussed in volume 31 of the journal *Philologus*, page 172 and following. It is made of paper, in the largest format, written in the 14th or 15th century. From folio 9a to 16b, it contains the second part of this work with figures <note>illustrations or diagrams</note> very carelessly executed.

The chapter headings provided by manuscripts A and B differ so greatly from a proper method of indicating the subject matter, and are sometimes placed so absurdly, that they cannot be attributed to the author himself, despite his mediocre talent. Therefore, I have decided that they should be assigned a place in the critical apparatus <note>the technical notes at the bottom of the page</note> rather than in the main body of the text. *Compare Martin, Theo on Astronomy, page 36.*

From manuscripts A and B are derived all the handwritten books of which I have knowledge that contain either the whole work or the first or second part; I will soon prove this to be the case with certain arguments in another place. Using copies derived from A or B, *Bullialdus* (Paris, 1644) edited the first part, and *Martin* (Paris, 1849) edited the second—the verses of Alexander (page 139 and following), which *Isaac Vossius* had transcribed from a copy of manuscript B, had already been printed several times before; see the note to page 139—and finally, *de Gelder* (Leiden, 1827) edited the first small section (pages 1 to 46, line 19). For my part, manuscripts of this kind had to be mentioned especially in those places where they show corrections of errors in the ancient writing. To avoid burdening the notes uselessly with a multitude of different signs, I did not wish to indicate individually those books from this group in which improved readings are found, but I have designated the manuscripts originating from A or B by the common name of *apographs* <term>apographum: a transcript or copy of an original manuscript</term>.
