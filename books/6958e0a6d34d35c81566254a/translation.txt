<margin>Copal.</margin>
**COPAL** *is a resin that is quite white, bright, and transparent, found in large fragments, not unlike very transparent* **Citron-sugar** <note>original: "Diacitri." This refers to a clear, crystalline medicinal confection made from citron.</note>, *quite fragrant, but not as much as* **Anime**. *The Indians used it in their sacrifices in place of incense* <note>original: "suffumigij." **IT IS USEFUL** *for cold diseases of the head* <note>In the humoral theory of the time, "cold" diseases were treated with "hot" medicines.</note>, *and it takes the place of* **Frankincense** <note>original: "Thuris."</note>, *or* **Anime**. *It is hot in the second degree and moist in the first. <header>COPAL AND ANIME.</header>

<margin>Amber and its origins.</margin>
...is believed, though nothing could be further from the truth: for **Charabe** <note>original: "Charabe," a Persian/Arabic term for amber, literally "attracting straw."</note> is a type of Bitumen, large fragments of which are hauled out of the German Sea with iron hooks. It flows from springs within the sea itself, much like Bitumen, and when exposed to the air, it immediately thickens and hardens. This can be inferred from the twigs and other debris of the sea that are seen trapped within those fragments. 
<margin>Amber is not a tree resin.</margin>
From this, we can see the error of those who thought it was the "tear" <note>Resins were often poetically called "tears" of trees.</note> of the Poplar or the Pine. Hermolaus Barbarus <note>A famous 15th-century Italian scholar known for his translations of Dioscorides and Pliny.</note>, a most learned man, says that **Oriental Anime** is collected in places near the village where Frankincense grows, and is named **Anime** after that village.

<margin>American Anime.</margin>
The **Anime** of New Spain <note>Modern-day Mexico.</note> is collected from trees of moderate size by making incisions in the bark, in the same manner as one collects Frankincense or Mastic.

<margin>The powers of Anime.</margin>
**ITS USE** is for many things, but especially for diseases of the head arising from coldness, and for the drainage of fluids <note>original: "defluxionibus," referring to catarrh or "runny" symptoms believed to be caused by excess cold humors.</note> that follows purgings. It is used in bedrooms throughout the winter (as it purifies the air) and to scent the head-coverings of those who wish to sleep at night; it is even used for the head itself by those suffering from chronic headaches and migraines, for it strengthens the head.

**IT IS MIXED** into plasters and Cerates <note>A medicinal ointment made of wax, oil, and medicinal ingredients.</note> where there is a need to strengthen the body and to dissipate cold humors and "winds" <note>original: "flatus," referring to gas or internal pressure.</note>. It serves as a substitute for Frankincense, both in incense and in the treatments already mentioned. It strengthens the brain when applied as a plaster, and even the stomach itself and the other nervous parts when applied as a Cerate (mixed with a third part of wax). If worn in this way for a long time and occasionally renewed, it draws out coldness from any part of the body. It is hot in the second degree and moist in the first <note>In Galenic medicine, substances were classified by "degrees" of heat, cold, moisture, and dryness to balance the body's humors.</note>.

<margin>Xolochcopalli. Copalcahuiel.</margin>
Gómara <note>Francisco López de Gómara, a 16th-century Spanish historian.</note> in his *History of Mexico* identifies two kinds of **Copal**; one is wrinkled, which he says is called **Xolochcopalli**, which is soft and similar to Frankincense; the other, much more excellent, is **Copalcahuiel**, which many have thought to be Myrrh. When the tree is wounded, a white liquid drips out drop by drop and hardens immediately.

<margin>Toçot-guebit</margin>
Fragoso <note>Juan Fragoso, a 16th-century Spanish physician and botanist.</note> reports that in the West Indies, a tree is found called **Toçot-guebit**, which is to say, "wood of desire" or "the longed-for wood." ### Tacamahaca.

<margin>Tacamahaca.</margin>
**THERE IS BROUGHT** likewise from New Spain <note>Modern-day Mexico.</note> another kind of gum or resin, called **Tacamahaca** by the Indians, which name it has retained among the Spaniards. It is collected by wounding the tree, which is large like a Poplar and very fragrant; its fruit is red, in the manner of a peony seed.

<margin>The powers of Tacamahaca.</margin>
**THE USE** of this resin is very frequent among the Indians, especially for all swellings: for it wonderfully dissolves, ripens, and removes them, as it also does for all pains proceeding from cold and flatulent humors <note>In the medical theory of the time, "humors" were bodily fluids that regulated health; "cold and flatulent" refers to conditions caused by an excess of cold or gas.</note>.

**IN COLOR** it is like Galbanum <note>A brownish-yellow aromatic gum resin.</note> (nor are there wanting those who think it is the same thing) with white "nails" <note>original: "unguibus albis," referring to white, tear-shaped streaks or inclusions within the resin.</note>, like Gum Ammoniac. It has a heavy odor and taste, such that if it is cast upon hot coals and held to the nostrils, it immediately relieves women suffering from "suffocation of the womb" <note>original: "vteri suffocatione," a historical medical diagnosis for various female ailments believed to be caused by the womb moving or "suffocating" the patient; strong-smelling substances were thought to drive it back to its proper place.</note>. Applied to the navel in the manner of a plaster, it keeps the womb in its place. Indeed, its use is so familiar to women that it is consumed for the most part by them, since they find its remedies effective both for uterine suffocations and for strengthening the stomach. Some of the more delicate women add ambergris and musk. It is most useful for dissolving and removing all pains proceeding from cold and flatulent humors when applied as a plaster. It has the same efficacy for cold tumors, as it resolves, ripens, and consumes them immediately. It does not melt, but always sticks so tenaciously that it cannot be removed until the work is finished.

*This method of extracting oils from seeds and fruits is very familiar to the Indians <note>Likely referring to the indigenous peoples of the Americas, given the context of the previous page's mention of Cartagena.</note>, as they are not acquainted with any form of pressing; however, even this specific oil is more easily obtained by that method than by expression <note>meaning mechanical pressing</note>.*

<margin>The properties of castor oil.</margin>
**THIS OIL** *is endowed with great powers, as has been proven by experience and extensive use, both in the Indies and in these regions. It heals all diseases arising from cold humors <note>In the humoral system of medicine, "cold humors" like phlegm were thought to cause sluggishness and swelling; castor oil's "heat" was the intended cure.</note>: it dissolves all tumors and disperses gas <note>original: "flatus"</note>, especially in the belly. For this reason, it is useful not only in cases of anasarca <note>generalized swelling of the body</note> but in all types of dropsy <note>abnormal accumulation of fluid, known today as edema</note>, if the belly is anointed with it and a few drops are swallowed in wine or another suitable liquid. Indeed, it evacuates fluids—a task it performs with even less harm when administered as an enema <note>original: "clysteribus"</note>. It clears the stomach of cold humors and gas, and is useful for the pains of colic if the affected parts are rubbed with it and a few small drops are taken internally. Experience has taught us that it helps even more effectively with the pains of ileus <note>a painful obstruction of the ileum or intestine</note>. It removes joint pains, provided they do not arise from a particularly hot cause, by evacuating the humor, if a few drops of this oil are taken dissolved in fatty chicken broth. It cures contracted parts of the body if they are anointed with it, by softening and stretching the nerves. By anointing, it frees the stomach, the womb, and the spleen from obstructions. It softens the bowels of children when smeared over the whole abdomen, and it expels intestinal worms, especially if one or two drops of it are given in milk or fatty broth. It is effective against itchy skin diseases <note>original: "psoras"</note> and running ulcers of the head; it is beneficial for earaches and all skin blemishes, and by application, it clears away the pimples <note>original: "varos"</note> that frequently plague women.*

**IT IS** *hot in the beginning of the third degree, and moist in the second.*

### Ambergris.

<margin>Gray Amber.</margin>
Florida, a province of the New World, now sends us what they call Gray Amber <note>original: "Ambar griseum." <margin>The properties of China root.</margin>
This water is useful for various kinds of diseases: it disperses the venereal plague <note>syphilis</note> of any kind, old wounds and ulcers, and hard tumors <term>schirrhosos: firm, non-inflammatory swellings or masses</term>; it relieves joint pain and all kinds of gout, but especially cures the hips <note>original: "coxendices", likely referring to sciatica or hip-joint pain</note>. <margin>
The origin of the name.
</margin>

When the Spanish first saw it, they gave it the name **Sarsaparilla** <note>original: "Çarça-parillæ"</note> because of its kinship with our own Sarsaparilla (which is *Smilax aspera* <note>the Mediterranean rough bindweed</note>). Certainly, I have found through experience that our local variety is endowed with similar powers, as it more closely resembles the type brought from New Spain than the one found in Honduras. Its taste is bland and without sharpness, and its "barley water" <note>a decoction or medicinal infusion</note> is no more flavorful.

The method of using it when it was first accepted into medicine was far different than it is now. Back then, the custom of the Indians <note>referring to the indigenous peoples of the Americas</note> was followed, which they are accustomed to using in curing their own diseases, for which it was found to be of the greatest efficacy in driving them out. However, the refinements <note>original: "deliciæ," implying the softer or more fastidious preferences of European society</note> of our age are the reason it is now prescribed in the same manner as the water of Guaiacum <note>a medicinal wood also used for treating syphilis</note>.

<margin>
The method of preparing Sarsaparilla among the Indians.
</margin>

Half a pound <term>selibra: approximately 160 grams or 6 ounces</term> of Sarsaparilla, cut into small pieces, was steeped in water and then pounded for a long time in a mortar until it was entirely resolved into a kind of mucilage <note>a thick, gluey substance</note>, which they then strained and squeezed out. They would drink a large enough cup of this expressed liquid while warm in the morning, then cover themselves with blankets and sweat for the space of two hours. If thirst pressed them during the day, the same slimy liquid was their drink. Toward nightfall, they would drink another warm cup of it and sweat for the same amount of time as in the morning. This routine was observed for three whole days, such that they ate or drank nothing else except that warm, thick liquid of squeezed Sarsaparilla.

They all died trembling and wasting away. Some suggested a natural cause: that since they had been raised in the very cold mountains where it rains every single day, they could not bear the heat of the plain, where no rain ever falls, and they died from that sudden change from extreme cold to extreme heat. Indeed, it is worthy of consideration that in that plain—which extends only eight miles to the mountains, but stretches more than a thousand miles in length—it has never rained, while in those neighboring mountains it rains daily.*
