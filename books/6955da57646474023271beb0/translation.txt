# ON COMEDY PAGE II

### The commentaries of Georgius Simler on the *Sergius* of Johannes Reuchlin of Pforzheim, Doctor of Laws.

<margin>Theophrastus</margin>
<margin>Donatus</margin>
¶ Since it is customary when explaining authors to investigate and seek out what kind of verse the poet has written, I believe I should remain silent regarding comedy rather than say too little. This is partly because the Greek author Theophrastus, a man of divine eloquence, composed a whole volume on that subject. Furthermore, regarding Aelius Donatus <note>A 4th-century Roman grammarian and teacher of St. Jerome</note> in his introduction to the reading of Terence, I have seen no one to this day who has more extensively traced the origin of that kind of poetry. He has recounted its beginnings so copiously that he alone ought to be thought to have perfectly satisfied the entire eager company of grammarians. Therefore, I judged it a fairer condition for the anxious reader to understand what Donatus left in writing, since that most learned commentator always wished his entire industrious career to be occupied in dispelling the errors of readers. Nevertheless, I am content to add a few things—as is customary to taste at the doors of books—concerning the subject matter of comedy, provided I first touch upon a brief little preface before I impart the explanation for each individual verse.

### ON COMEDY

<margin>Definition of comedy</margin>
<margin>Comedy and Tragedy</margin>
<margin>Emotions to be sought in comedy</margin>
<margin>The comic character</margin>
Comedy belongs to that genre of poem which is called by the Greeks *dramatikon* <note>original: δραματικὸν</note>, that is, "active." In this genre, the characters act alone without any interruption from the poet. By those Greeks it is defined thus: *komoidia esti idiotikon kai politikon pragmaton akindynos periochē* <note>original: κωμῳδία ἐστὶ ἰδιωτικῶν καὶ πολιτικῶν πραγμάτων ἀκίνδυνος περιοχή</note>. This roughly means: Comedy (for it is written with a long 'o' <note>omega</note> with an 'i' <note>iota</note> subscript in the second syllable, which letter is turned into the 'oe' diphthong) is, I say, a treatment of private and civil affairs without danger. It is named after the gatherings of rustics in the villages, which they call *komas* <note>original: κώμας</note>, that is, villas or villages. But how far it is removed from tragedy, he who does not know may learn from the words of Xenocrates of Chalcedon. When he was rebuked by Bion, he said, "I will certainly not answer you. For tragedy does not deign to answer comedy when it is provoked by it." Donatus teaches that comedy contains the emotions of civil and private life, through which one learns what is useful in life and what, conversely, should be avoided. From those words of Donatus, we elicit that the emotions in comedy must be sought and explained; the worthy interpreter himself everywhere skillfully searched these out, subtly discussing the inclinations of the heart (as they say) more than the mere inflections of words. For this reason, I cease to wonder why it is less taken up by "grammatists," that is, by elementary teachers. And when Marcus Cicero mentions the type of narrative which is placed in characters, he wants it to contain a diversity of minds, which he especially observes in the acts of comedies. Furthermore, in style, comedy is what they call a *charaktēra* <note>original: χαρακτῆρα</note> most similar to everyday speech: restrained, not in sublime oratory, but thin, light, common, and creeping along the ground. I see that Horace felt this when he said:

->For this reason, some have questioned whether or not comedy<-
->was a poem, because neither its words nor its subject matter<-
->possess a fiery spirit or force, and except that it differs<-
->from prose by a fixed meter, it is mere prose.<-

# ON COMEDY III

<margin>The plots of comedies</margin>
For this reason, comic poets have been called "popular" by many. In Old Comedy <note>The "Old Comedy" of ancient Greece, such as the works of Aristophanes, known for sharp political satire.</note>, the themes—which we call "arguments" or plots—were once actual historical events openly performed on stage. However, the abuse of this freedom led to a law being passed to restrain such defamation. Consequently, this genre of poetry was changed into Satire, but when that too began to fall under suspicion, it was likewise abolished. Finally, this "New Comedy" was invented, which uses a fictional premise <note>original: "hypothesi"</note>. Yet, these plots are not devised at random, as the unlearned mob constantly shouts; Cicero, in his speech *In Defense of Sextus Roscius*, most skillfully refutes such ignorance. There, he shows that these stories are fashioned by poets so that we might see our own morals portrayed in the persons of others, providing a vivid image of our daily life. I believe the purpose of comic writers can be perfectly examined through this little verse of Cicero's.

I suspect something similar has happened with our own author. He was incessantly reminded of that word from Ecclesiastes <note>The author refers to the biblical book of Ecclesiastes (Coheleth) 10:16.</note> crying out: *Woe to you, O land, whose king is a child.* <margin>The plot of the present comedy</margin> Therefore, I believe he turned his mind toward this goal: to provide wholesome counsel to princes through the warnings and persuasion found in the premise of this play. He suggests they must abstain from the "empty heads" to whom Plato forbade the administration of kingdoms. Lactantius—that "Christian Cicero"—introduced Plato's sentiment (which deserves eternal memory) in this most elegant phrasing: *Those states would be blessed where either philosophers ruled, or kings practiced philosophy.*

<margin>Alexander the Great</margin> Alexander the Great of Macedon also felt this, as he considered it better to excel in learning than in military glory. <margin>Zeno</margin> What do we think moved Zeno, or <margin>Aristotle</margin> Aristotle? They rendered the kingdom and the empire subject to no one except the wise. <margin>Socrates</margin> We read that Socrates pulled back Glaucon, the brother of Plato, when he wished to enter public life, on the grounds that he was unskilled and ignorant of affairs. <margin>Antisthenes</margin> Antisthenes, a philosopher famous throughout all Greece, advised the Athenians to vote that their donkeys were horses; when they replied that this was irrational, Antisthenes retorted: "What then are the generals among you, who have learned nothing and are merely appointed?" Does not Nestor, in the ninth book of the *Iliad*, argue that the kingdom was granted to Agamemnon by Jove so that he might consult for the others rightly and prudently? The Emperor Justinian thought the Republic ought to be armed with laws, not just decorated with weapons. Finally, Sacred History records that when the prophet Samuel sought counsel from God, the response was: *It is not physical beauty or appearance that makes a king, but true virtue without doubt.* <note>original: "facere regalem"</note> By that path, I say, Solomon succeeded David, and Joshua succeeded Moses as a leader for the people of Israel.

And indeed, Josephus left it most correctly written that those to whom the safety of subjects is committed ought to be full of every virtue. For if those who lead the people are ignorant of letters (which most become them) and neglect to gather the logic of good governance from the precepts of others (which cannot happen without learning and reading), they will be merely "stage-play" princes and imaginary ones. What else is an illiterate prince but the image of a lion ruling over other beasts? Therefore, one should not think it was by chance that the custom grew strong among the ancients for the elders to claim the kingdom for themselves, because counsel belongs more widely to that age...
