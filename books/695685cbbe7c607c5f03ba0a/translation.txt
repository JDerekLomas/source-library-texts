# TO THE READER
# PREFACE.

WHEN it seemed fit to the Curators of the *Oxford* Press to collect my Mathematical Writings (previously published separately) into one single Body, as it were, and to publish them together; I judged that some account of the whole Work, and of the order in which they now appear, should be rendered to the Learned Reader.

Most things (though not all) occur in the order in which they were first published; with the exception of several pieces which are either inserted into or annexed to the Second Volume, lest this First Volume should grow excessively large.

As to why I sent out that Second Volume first (already published two years ago), the reason is plainly this: because most of the contents therein had previously appeared only in the English Language, and had to be translated into the Latin Speech. These, therefore, required my own personal inspection more urgently; whereas those which had previously appeared in Latin could (if anything should happen to me in the meantime <note>A euphemism for his potential death</note>) be reprinted without much inconvenience, even after my death.

I took care, however, to ascribe to each work the time when it first appeared: lest I should seem to have borrowed from the writings of others (published later) those things which are common to both. Indeed, no one is unaware that after many of my works were published, <term>Mathematical Science</term> <note>original: "Doctrina Mathematica"</note> was advanced in no small measure: to what extent my writings served that end, let it be the judgment of others.

The *Arithmetic Work (or Universal Mathematics)* was adapted especially for the instruction of Beginners: yet so arranged that it contains things not to be despised even by the Expert. These things, if not entirely new, are at least delivered in a new way; and demonstrated from the first foundations. Some Philological or Philosophical matters are also intermingled, so that the taste of the Academics <note>The scholars and students at the University</note>, such as they were at that time, might be consulted.

For it must not be concealed that our Academics, before those times, were chiefly devoted to the so-called Peripatetic Philosophy <note>The traditional philosophy based on Aristotle</note> (which was then taught in the Schools, and to whose formulas of speech matters had to be accommodated), and they devoted themselves more sparingly to Mathematical affairs. There were indeed not a few among us sufficiently skilled in Mathematics (as their writings which still survive testify); yet fewer than nowadays; and those mostly living outside the Universities. And indeed, through the Civil Wars which occupied us for many years, Academic studies here, if not entirely interrupted, were at least greatly hindered, and had to be restored as if anew; since which time (regarding these matters) they have flourished more daily. So it is no wonder if we complained then of the neglect of Mathematics, concerning which there is not such great room for complaining now.

The things written against *Meibomius* <note>Marcus Meibom, a contemporary scholar with whom Wallis disagreed on musical and mathematical ratios</note> are not written with the intent that I wish ill to the man (otherwise well-deserving): but lest an Error should creep in (which had infected him and several others), while they had not well distinguished between a *Double or Triple Ratio* and a *Duplicate or Triplicate Ratio* <note>Wallis is distinguishing between simple multiplication (2x) and squaring or cubing a ratio (x²)</note>. Concerning which (I hope) there will not be much danger hereafter, the matter being well understood.

But even that Defense required a new Vindication: which I have now applied in the additions to the Second Volume, to which that prior Dissertation has now been transferred. This Doctrine is altogether Useful (not to say Necessary) for disentangling many Difficulties which tormented many before, and which (once this doctrine is established) now vanish, nor (I believe) will they torture anyone hereafter; at least there will be no need for anyone to be tortured by them.

Regarding the *Treatise on Conic Sections* <note>original: "Conicis Sectionibus Tractatus"</note> (translated from the Solid into the Plane), it is not necessary for me to say how clear, useful, and transparent it becomes (compared to the treatment of the same by the Ancients within the Cone). This approach was later imitated by *Mr. de Witt* <note>Johan de Witt, the Dutch statesman and mathematician.</note> in his Treatise inserted into the work of *Schooten* <note>Frans van Schooten, known for promoting Cartesian geometry.</note>; likewise by *Mr. De Chales* and *De la Hire*. By this method, the treatment of Conics (which was previously held to be so complex that most people were deterred by it) now becomes easy.

What follows, the *Arithmetic of Infinites* <note>original: "Arithmetica Infinitorum," Wallis's most influential work on early calculus.</note>, is a method of Investigation entirely New, and introduced (as far as I know) by me for the first time, and not without success. The greatest Men have approved of it; and the Illustrious *Bullialdus* <note>Ismaël Boulliau (1605–1694), a French astronomer.</note> deemed it worthy of an extensive Commentary. How useful it has been to me is evidenced by my other subsequent writings (on the *Cycloid*, on the *Rectification and Squaring of Curves*, on the *Calculation of the Center of Gravity*, etc.). Men of no small reputation have embraced and followed it (notwithstanding the minor objections of some), whether or not they have made mention of me. How much the promotion of Mathematical speculations has been advanced from this point, let it be declared by the judgment of others rather than my own.

The *Treatise on the Cycloid* which follows was written on the occasion of a *public Challenge* issued to all the *Mathematicians of Europe* by a certain anonymous Frenchman, who later assumed the name *Dettonville*, but who is otherwise known as *Pascal* <note>Blaise Pascal (1623–1662), who used the pseudonym Amos Dettonville for his mathematical contests.</note>. The work itself bears witness to how well I satisfied that Challenge. I have likewise pursued this matter more extensively (among other things) in the *Mechanics* (or *Treatise on Motion*), where the Calculation of the Center of Gravity is discussed in Chapter V, propositions 16 through 22.

To the same treatise on the *Cycloid* is joined another on the *Rectification of Curved Lines* and the *Squaring of Curved Surfaces*; that is, on assigning *Straight Lines* which are equal to the displayed *Curves*, and *Plane Surfaces* which are equal to the displayed *Curves*. I deduce this entire matter from the Principles handed down in the *Arithmetic of Infinites*; and I pursue it further than anyone had done before me, opening a Method by the help of which anyone (who so desires) will be able to advance the matter further at their own discretion.

In the final Treatise, titled *Mechanics*, or *Treatise on Motion*, I deal with *Machines* (or Mechanical Instruments) which writers on Mechanics usually treat. There, the whole Subject is deduced and demonstrated from the first principles of Motion. Likewise, the Doctrine of Percussions and Reflections is derived from its true and genuine principles. And the entire doctrine of Hydrostatics is deduced from the *Counter-pressure of the Air* <note>original: "Aeris Contrapondio"</note>, in place of the *Abhorrence of a Vacuum* <note>original: "Fuga Vacui," the Aristotelian idea that "nature abhors a vacuum."</note> held by the Ancients; (which I have pursued even more fully in the Treatise *On Weight and Gravitation*, which now appears in the Second Volume.) And indeed, in that part which concerns the Center of Gravity and its Calculation, it is marvelous how fruitful the Method of Corollaries sought from the *Arithmetic of Infinites* is in disentangling (beyond expectation) the most complex Questions, which had been regarded as hopeless.

The things contained in the Second Volume are described in the Preface prefixed to the same...

Fluxions was Newton's term for what we now call differential calculus, focusing on the "flow" or rate of change.</note> (as he calls it), which is of a similar nature to *Leibniz’s* (as he calls it) *Differential Calculus* <note>original: "Calculo Differentiali". Leibniz's notation and terminology are the ones primarily used in modern mathematics.</note>; (which anyone who compares both methods will sufficiently notice, even if they are under different formulas of speech). I described this (*Algebra Chapter 91* etc., especially *Chapter 95*) from two letters of *Newton* (or one of the two) dated *June 13 and August 24, 1676, sent to Oldenburg* <note>Henry Oldenburg was the Secretary of the Royal Society and acted as a hub for scientific correspondence.</note> to be communicated then to *Leibniz* (using almost the same words, or at least slightly changed, which are contained in those letters), where he explains this method to *Leibniz* as having been devised by himself ten years before, or even more. I mention this so that no one may claim that nothing has been said by us concerning this *Differential Calculus*.

Regarding the Method of Treatment: I have endeavored to deduce everything from first principles, and to deliver them in that Order in which they were Discovered; so that it may not only be clear to the Reader that they are true, but also how they were arrived at; so that the Method of Investigation itself (to be applied elsewhere as well) may become no less known. This is in contrast to what many complain about regarding the *Ancients* <note>The author refers to classical Greek mathematicians like Euclid or Archimedes, who often presented finished proofs without showing the "scratchpad" work of how they found the theorems.</note>, who were so intent on delivering and demonstrating their discoveries that they meanwhile concealed the Method of Finding them.

I have by no means sought after elegancies of words and phrases, being content with a simple style (provided it is not barbaric). I have taken the greatest care (both in the order and choice of words) that the sense should be as clear as possible and least subject to ambiguity; especially wherever there might be a fear that the matter proposed might either not be understood or be understood incorrectly.

And for that reason, I am accustomed to using accepted terms and formulas of speech (and indeed, sometimes, almost unwillingly) rather than disturbing the reader's mind by affecting innovation with unusual formulas when accepted ones are available. For instance, when it is said *Double Ratio, Triple Ratio, etc.* <note>original: "Ratio Dupla, Tripla". In Latin, this uses the adjective "double" to describe the ratio.</note> (because it is usually said so), although (if the matter were fresh) it ought rather to be said *Ratio of the Double, of the Triple, etc.* <note>original: "Ratio Dupli, Tripli". This would be more grammatically precise in Latin, using the genitive case.</note> Likewise, when it is said (in the feminine gender) *This Diameter, this Perimeter, etc.* (on account of the Greek *the diametron, the perimetron* <note>original: "τὴν διάμετρον, τὴν περίμετρον"</note>, because the word *line* <note>original: "γραμμὴν" (grammēn)</note> is understood), whereas it would be said more "Latinly" as *Diametra, Perimetra* (because the word *Line* is understood); or at least *Diametrus, Perimetrus* (which was said in the previous century), since no word (of this form) occurs to my memory ending in *er* that is of the feminine gender (which is why, when speaking of a male it is called *Socer* [father-in-law], but of a female it is called *Socrus* [mother-in-law]). Likewise I say *Triangle, Square, Plane, Parallelogram, etc.* (because the Greeks said *the trigonon, tetragonon, epipedon, parallelogrammon* <note>original: "τὸ τρίγωνον, τετράγωνον, ἐπίπεδον, παράλληλόγραμμον"</note>, because the word *figure* <note>original: "σχῆμα" (schēma)</note> is understood), whereas in Latin it would be better said *Triangular figure, square, plane, parallelogrammatic.* But accepted custom in speaking makes it so that either expression is considered sufficiently Latin. For that occurs in a thousand other cases. As when *The Honest* <note>original: "Honeſtum"</note> is said for *An honest thing*, because the Greeks had said *the prepon, the kalon* <note>original: "τὸ πρέπον, τὸ καλόν"</note>, on account of *thing* or *matter* <note>original: "χρῆμα vel πρᾶγμα"</note> being understood, etc.

Relying on this authority, I say indiscriminately *This Conoid, Spheroid, etc.* (understanding *Figure*), or *This Conoid, Spheroid* (because the Greeks said *the konoeides, sphairoeides* <note>original: "τὸ κωνοειδές, σφαιροειδές"</note>, and thus the Latin Translators have spoken, as if those were substantive nouns); but I would prefer the former as being more according to analogy <note>original: "ἀναλογικώτερον" (analogikōteron)</note>. Similarly, sometimes *as the first is to the second so the third is to the fourth* (understanding *Number* or *Term*); sometimes *as the first [line] is to the second so the third is to the fourth* (understanding *Line* or *Figure*); sometimes even, *as the first [neutral] is to the second so the third is to the fourth* (using the *Neuter* gender absolutely). And similarly in other things; sometimes I follow what I myself prefer, sometimes I yield to accepted formulas of speech. And in words which are written by others now in this way, now in that; I use the same liberty, not being anxious about these trifles. For I am not treating Grammar here, but Mathematics; and I wish to speak so that I may be best understood.

# TO THE READER, &c.

Perhaps someone will complain that the same matter is sometimes treated in one place and then again in another. And it certainly cannot be denied that this occasionally happens. Yet it should not be considered a fault, nor should I be accused of <term>tautology<note>original: "tautologias"; the unnecessary repetition of the same idea in different words.</note></term>. It must be considered that all these Treatises were not written in one single burst, or as a continuous work; rather, they were published separately, at different and widely distant times. And if something not unworthy of being known appeared in some earlier work in a suitable place, there is no reason why anyone should blame its publication then; nor is there reason why it should be deleted when that same work is reprinted, lest it become incomplete. Indeed, if in another work to be published later, the same matter should be taken up again—either to be enlarged, or advanced, or adapted to other uses, or to be defended from the slanders or objections of others—there is no reason why this also should not be done. For indeed, hardly anyone delivers a matter (at least a new one) so perfectly the first time that it cannot be improved, advanced, or further confirmed by second thoughts; or even cited, whether for the explanation or proof of other things, where it could not conveniently be omitted.

Certain small works written long ago against *Thomas Hobbes* (that <term>False-geometer<note>original: "Pseudo-geometram." Wallis had a long-running and bitter mathematical dispute with the philosopher Thomas Hobbes.</note>) are not included here, lest I seem to wish to triumph over a man now dead. For although, as things were then, it seemed entirely necessary to do so (when, under the pretext of being a Great Geometer, as he sold himself, he dared to supply the material for thinking wrongly in the business of Religion to incautious youths), there no longer seems to be any fear that this will result in damage to Geometry. And although in those works (besides the refutation of his own <term>false-writings<note>original: "pseudo-graphematum."</note>) many other things worthy of knowledge occur, most of these may be found in other places elsewhere.

There are likewise several other things (either my own, or those of others published by me) which are contained in neither of these Volumes; which the Reader (if he wishes) may seek elsewhere.
