# <insert>Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms</insert>

<term>Sancai Tuhui</term> <note>Original Chinese: 三才圖會. This title translates to "Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms," referring to the three traditional divisions of the universe: Heaven, Earth, and Humanity.</note> This monumental <term>encyclopedia</term> was compiled during the Ming dynasty by the scholar Wang Qi and his son Wang Siyi. Published in 1609, the work is celebrated for its hundreds of detailed woodblock illustrations covering subjects as diverse as astronomy, geography, biology, and historical costume. It remains one of the most significant visual records of knowledge and culture from late imperial China.

<term>Upright Melon-mace</term> <note>Original: *Ligua* (立瓜). A ceremonial mace with a melon-shaped head positioned vertically on the staff. These were carried by the Imperial Bodyguards during processions to signify the Emperor's power to punish.</note>

<term>Reclining Melon-mace</term> <note>Original: *Wogua* (臥瓜). Similar to the Upright Melon-mace, but with the head mounted horizontally. Together, the "Upright" and "Reclining" maces were standard regalia for the Ming court.</note>

<term>Bow Case</term> <note>Original: *Gongjian* (弓鞬). This refers specifically to a case for an unstrung bow. The gloss mentions a <term>Hulu</term> (gourd) style, which was a combined carrier designed to hold both the bow and a set of arrows in one unit.</note>

<term>Fish-skin Quiver</term> <note>Original: *Yufu* (魚服). A quiver made from the skin of large fish, such as sharks or rays. This material was prized for being waterproof and exceptionally durable, protecting the fletching of the arrows from dampness.</note>

<term>Archer’s Ring</term> <note>Original: *Jue* (夬). A thumb ring made of bone, horn, or jade. In the "Mongolian draw" used in China, the string is pulled with the thumb; the ring protects the skin and allows for a sharp, clean release of the arrow.</note>

<term>Arm Guard</term> <note>Original: *She* (拾). A leather bracer worn on the inner forearm of the arm holding the bow to protect the skin from the snap of the bowstring.</note>

<term>Large Shield</term> <note>Original: *Aipai* (挨牌). Literally "leaning shield." These were large, rectangular shields used by infantry to create a defensive wall. They were often tall enough to cover most of a soldier's body.</note>

<term>Swallow-tail Shield</term> <note>Original: *Yanweipai* (燕尾牌). A shield named for its distinctive shape, which narrowed or split at the bottom like the tail of a swallow, allowing for better mobility or specific defensive postures.</note>

<term>Rattan Shield</term> <note>Original: *Tengpai* (藤牌). A circular, convex shield woven from tough rattan vines. They were lightweight, extremely strong, and famous for their ability to deflect both blades and early firearms.</note>

<term>Pear-blossom Spear</term> <note>Original: *Lihua Qiang* (梨花鎗). A hybrid weapon from the late Song and Ming dynasties. It combined a traditional spear with a "fire tube" (bamboo or metal) attached near the head. When ignited, it sprayed flames and pellets—likened to "pear blossoms"—at the enemy before the user engaged in spear-fighting.</note>

<term>Horse-Toppling Thrusting Spear</term> <note>Original: *Daoma Tuqiang* (搗馬突槍). A specialized heavy spear designed for infantry to use against cavalry, intended to trip horses or unseat armored riders.</note>

<term>Iron Chain Flail</term> <note>Original: *Tielian Jiabang* (鐵鏈夾棒). Literally "Iron chain sandwiched staff." This is a two-sectioned weapon similar to a flail, used to strike over an opponent's shield or parry.</note>

<term>Caltrops</term> <note>Original: *Jili* (蒺藜). Small metal devices with four or more sharp spikes, designed so that one point always faces upward. <term>Iron Truncheon</term> <note>Original: *Tiejian* (鐵簡). A solid metal striking weapon, usually square or rectangular in cross-section with no edge. It was designed to crush bone and dent heavy plate armor that would deflect a sword's edge.</note>

<term>Garlic-head Mace</term> <note>Original: *Suantou* (蒜頭). A heavy mace named for its striking head, which was cast in a multi-lobed shape resembling a bulb of garlic.</note>

# Shield
<note>Original: *Gan* (干)</note>

The shield is a defensive screen. In the regions east of the Hangu Pass <note>a strategic gateway between the ancient Wei River valley and the North China Plain</note>, it is called either a *gan* or a *shun*. In the regions west of the Pass, it is primarily called a *shun*.

# Dagger-axe
<note>Original: *Ge* (戈)</note>

The dagger-axe is two inches wide. The tang <term>nei</term> is four inches long, the neck <term>hu</term> is six inches, the blade <term>yuan</term> is eight inches, and the shaft <term>bi</term> is six feet and six inches long. The "tang" refers to the part behind the neck that connects into the shaft. The "neck" refers to the part that extends out and downward from the side; its curve is like the dewlap <note>the fold of skin hanging from the neck</note> of a cow. The "blade" refers to the straight, pointed cutting edge. The "shaft" refers to the handle.

The utility of the dagger-axe relies primarily on the neck. If the blade is too straight, the weapon becomes sluggish and can only be used for thrusting. However, if the neck is too curved, it becomes a mere hook and can only be used to snag people. Only when the design strikes a perfect balance is the weapon truly effective in every movement, meeting with no disadvantage.

# Ancient Dagger-axe
<note>Original: *Gu Ge* (古戈)</note>

This specimen was recovered from the former palace of the Han dynasty Prince of Huainan <note>Liu An (c. 179–122 BC), a famous scholar-prince</note> in Shouyang. When compared to the measurements of the ancient crossbow trigger mechanism illustrated previously, the proportions of this piece precisely correspond to the descriptions in the *Artificers' Record* <term>Kao Gong Ji; a classical work on manufacturing and technology</term> regarding how the neck <term>hu</term> of a halberd passes through its mounting. 

Where the neck meets the blade <term>yuan</term> at a right angle, the outer hook forms an angle like that of a chime-stone <note>a *qing*, an L-shaped ritual lithophone; the term *qingzhe* implies a specific obtuse angle of roughly 135 degrees</note>. Master Zheng <note>Zheng Xuan, a prominent Han dynasty commentator</note> states: "The dagger-axe is called a 'hook-child halberd' <note>*juzi ji*</note>." Another name for it is the "crowing cock" <note>*jiming zi*</note>, because it is inserted horizontally and slants slightly upward. Yang Xiong’s *Book of Dialects* <note>the *Fangyan*, the first Chinese dictionary of regional terms</note> notes that the "hook-child halberd" is the term used in the state of Chu for the "dagger-axe."

# The Qiú Spear, the Yí Spear, and the Shū Staff

The **Qiú spear** <note>Original: *Qiumao* (酋矛). This was a standard spear used by soldiers stationed on the right side of a war chariot.</note> is two *zhang* in length <term>zhang; a traditional unit of measurement. During the Zhou dynasty, one zhang was approximately 2.3 meters or 7.5 feet</term>.

The **Yí spear** <note>Original: *Yimao* (夷矛). A longer spear used by the soldier on the left of the chariot.</note> is two *zhang* and four *chi* in length <term>chi; often translated as a "Chinese foot," where 10 chi make up 1 zhang</term>.

The **Shū** <note>Original: *Shu* (殳). Often called a "spear-staff," it is one of the "five weapons" of a chariot.</note> is the same as the weapon <insert>also written as</insert> **Shū** <note>Original: *Shu* (杸), written with the wood radical.</note>. It is two *zhang* long and has **no blade**. It is used primarily for **striking**. The *Books of Rites* <note>Original: *Lishu* (禮書); refers to the classical texts detailing Zhou dynasty ceremonies and equipment.</note> describe it as having an **octagonal shape** <note>Original: *Baguxing* (八觚形); a faceted, eight-sided shaft designed for a better grip and more impactful striking surface.</note>.

## The Feathered Standard &emsp;&emsp; The Yak-Tail Banner &emsp;&emsp; The Plumed Flag

**The Feathered Standard** <note>Original: *Yǔ* (羽). This refers to a ritual object or banner decorated with bird plumage, often used in dance and processions.</note>
This standard is distinguished by clusters of feathers arranged in tiers. Historically, these were used by dancers in the "Six Small Dances" <note>Original: *Liùwǔ* (六舞); ancient ritual dances performed at the Zhou court.</note> and functioned as a visual marker of rank within a procession.

**The Yak-Tail Banner** <note>Original: *Máo* (旄). A pole topped with the tail of a yak or a "wild ox."</note>
As seen in the central illustration, this banner features an ornate head—often shaped like a mythical creature—from which bells and decorative ornaments are suspended. The **Máo** served as a vital signaling device; in a military context, its movement directed the rhythm and direction of the troops.

**The Plumed Flag** <note>Original: *Jīng* (旌). A flag that combines silk pennants with feathered ornaments.</note>
The **Jīng** is the most complex of the three, featuring an ornate head with multiple hanging silk pennants <note>Original: *Yóu* (旒); the streamers or "fringes" of a flag.</note>. According to the *Rites of Zhou*, the addition of feathers to a standard transformed it into a **Jīng**, making it a symbol of high-ranking authority often seen flanking the royal carriage.

The **Shield** is a defensive implement. The **Feathered Standard** <gloss>also known as a screen</gloss> is held by dancers to promote and illustrate civil education. According to the *Rites of Zhou* <note>Original: *Zhōu Lǐ* (周禮); a foundational text on the ritual bureaucracy of the Zhou dynasty.</note>, the "Military Dance" specifically utilized the **Vermilion Shield** <note>Original: *Zhū Gān* (朱干); a shield painted with red lacquer, used in ritualized combat displays.</note>.

The people of the Zhou dynasty performed dances during sacrifices to the spirits of the mountains and rivers. The *Illustrations of the Three Classics of Rites* <note>Original: *Sānlǐtú* (三禮圖); a Tang-dynasty study of ritual objects.</note> states that the **Feathered Standard** is made by splitting white feathers; its shape resembles a ceremonial fan <note>Original: *Shà* (翣); a fan-shaped implement used in funerals and royal processions to screen the deceased or the ruler.</note>.

The **Yak-Tail Banner** resembles the **Plumed Flag**, but the **Plumed Flag** is crafted by splitting the feathers of a pheasant <note>Original: *Dí* (翟); specifically the Long-tailed Pheasant, whose feathers were highly prized for ritual use.</note> and mounting them at the top of a flagpole. The **Yak-Tail Banner**, conversely, is made from the tail of a yak <note>Original: *Niúwěi* (牛尾); literally "ox tail," referring here to the long-haired tail of the mountain yak.</note> and is attached to the head of the flagpole. Thus, the designs established by the ancient sages were not identical, and their practical applications were likewise distinct.

<note>This woodcut illustration displays two primary types of ancient standards. On the left is a multi-paneled banner with animal motifs; on the right is a simplified vertical standard.</note>

The **Bear Banner** <note>Original: *Xióngqí* (熊旗)</note>, shown on the left of the illustration, is composed of six horizontal panels. According to the *Rites of Zhou*, this specific banner was assigned to military commanders and regional officials. The bear imagery was chosen to represent strength and martial vigor, while the number of panels corresponded to the rank of the official holding it.

The **Solid-Color Standard** <note>Original: *Wù* (物)</note>, seen on the right, is a tall, vertical flag. While less ornate than the Bear Banner, it served a critical role in assembly. These standards were used to gather the common people or soldiers at specific locations during hunts, sacrifices, or military maneuvers. 

Both poles are topped with bird-shaped finials <note>These represent the "Vermilion Bird" or auspicious phoenixes, acting as spiritual guides for the assembly.</note> and are decorated with flowing tassels. These tassels served a practical purpose by indicating wind direction, but they also added a sense of "civilized majesty" to the harsh realities of military organization. 

The distinction between the **Flag** and the **Standard** was central to the ancient "System of Regalia" <note>Original: *Qíwù* (旗物)</note>. By varying the animal designs, the colors, and the way the fabric was attached to the pole, the rulers could coordinate vast numbers of people without the need for constant verbal commands.

## The Tasseled Standard
<term>Jing (旌): A staff-based standard traditionally decorated with pheasant feathers or yak tails.</term>

-> [Illustration of a Tasseled Standard] <-

The **Tasseled Standard** <note>Original: *Jīng* (旌)</note> represents a critical development in ancient Chinese vexillology <note>the study of flags and standards</note>. As depicted in the illustration, the *Jing* consists of a tall vertical pole topped with a sharp spearhead, signifying its dual role in military and ritual settings. 

What distinguishes the *Jing* from a simple spear is the secondary arm extending from the top of the shaft. From this arm, four large, ornate tassels hang vertically. According to the *Rites of Zhou*, these standards were given to officials as a symbol of the Emperor's trust. When a messenger or commander traveled with a *Jing*, it commanded the same respect as the ruler himself. Bows are constructed from flexible wood, wrapped tightly with silk, and bound together using a mixture of glue and lacquer. The bowstrings are made from strands of spun silk.

The **Dun bow** <note>original: 敦弓 (dūngōng)</note> is the ceremonial bow reserved for the Son of Heaven. The **Tong bow** <note>original: 彤弓 (tónggōng); literally "vermilion bow"</note> is the bow designated for the feudal lords.

Regarding the measurements of the bow:
*   A length of six *chi* and six *cun* is called the **Upper Standard**.
*   A length of six *chi* and three *cun* is called the **Middle Standard**.
*   A length of six *chi* is called the **Lower Standard**. <note>A Ming Dynasty *chi* was approximately 31-32 cm, making these bows quite large, ranging from roughly 1.8 to 2 meters.</note>

The fletching at the tail of an arrow is made from the feathers of the **sandgrouse** <note>original: 鵽鳥 (duóniǎo)</note>; these feathers are chosen so the arrow might catch the wind and be shot over great distances. Arrowheads are forged from metal so that they may effectively pierce a person's vital areas. Thus, it is said: "The arrow-maker's only fear is that his arrows will not harm people." <note>This is a famous philosophical observation from the *Mencius*, contrasting the arrow-maker's "harmful" intent with the armor-maker's "protective" intent as a matter of professional focus rather than personal malice.</note>

The **Shuowen Jiezi** <note>an early 2nd-century Chinese dictionary</note> states: "The character for arrow (*shi*) represents something level and straight; the character's form depicts the arrowhead, the nock, and the feathers."
The **Shiming** <note>a dictionary from the Han Dynasty</note> states: "The arrow (*shi*) is a pointer; it has a direction to which it points and travels with great speed."

The decoration of arrows varies, with distinctions such as black lacquer, yellow or white birch bark, or hemp-wrapped backs. Their strength and draw weight are graded by the units of **dan** and **dou** <note>traditional units of weight; *dan* is often translated as "stone"</note>.

Types of arrows include the **Copper-tipped**, the **Wooden-headed**, and the **Whistling Bone** <note>original: 鳴髇 (míngxiāo)</note>.
*   **Copper-tipped** arrows are actually made of refined iron.
*   The **Wooden-headed** arrow is utilized during military drills and inspections.
*   The **Whistling Bone** arrow is used for ceremonial or recreational shooting. <note>These arrows have perforated heads that whistle in flight, historically used for signaling or by the Xiongnu to designate a target for all to fire upon.</note>

There are also **Fire Arrows** <note>original: 火箭 (huǒjiàn)</note>, which have gunpowder attached to the head of the arrow; these are used interchangeably by both traditional bows and crossbows.

# **The Two Styles of Shields**

Shields come in two distinct styles. There is the **Hand Shield** <term>Shoupai</term>, also known as the "Swallow-tail Shield," and there is the **Leaning Shield** <term>Aipai</term>—the latter of which is distinguished by its use of ropes and wooden toggles <note>original: "mu ganlan" (木橄欖), literally "wooden olives." These were olive-shaped wooden beads or toggles used to secure the shield's harness</note>. 

The **Hand Shield** is designed for the easy application of strength and agility. The tactical approach is to first hurl javelins <term>biaoqiang</term> to disrupt the enemy's momentum. Once the enemy's front is broken, the soldier uses his saber. The shield allows the bearer to parry enemy weapons from the front, back, left, or right, providing the opening to strike with saber techniques. This versatility is the great advantage of the Hand Shield.

When using the **Leaning Shield**, the wooden toggle on the shield’s long rope is inserted into a rope loop and hung around the neck. The wooden toggle on the shorter rope at the bottom of the shield is then gripped between the middle fingers of the left hand. This specialized harness allows the left hand to remain free enough to grasp the front half of a spear, while the right hand manages the rear half. The spear can then be extended or retracted, lengthened or shortened, or thrust in circular motions to the left and right. This method does not only protect the bearer’s own body; it also provides cover for the soldiers following closely behind the shield.

It is also said that whenever troops charge forward, shields must be arranged at the front of the squad to provide cover against arrows and stones. The shield is the primary and most indispensable tool within a combat formation. This is what is meant by the classical descriptions of a protector as "the shield and wall of the lords" <note>original: "gonghou gancheng" (公侯干城), an idiom from the *Book of Odes* referring to a defender of the state</note> and the ritual display of "shields, spears, and axes" <note>original: "gange qiyang" (干戈戚揚)</note>.

## On Bone-Crushers, Iron Whips, and the Chain Flail

To the right are the **Caltrop** and **Garlic-head** varieties of the **Bone-Crusher** <note>original: 骨朶 (guduo)</note>. These two types use iron or wood to form a large head. If we trace the origin of the name, it was originally "Melon-Stomach" <note>original: 胍肫 (guazhun)</note>. "Melon-Stomach" refers to a large belly; the name was chosen because the shape of the weapon head resembles a large melon. Later generations mispronounced the words, turning "Melon" <note>gua</note> into "Bone" <note>gu</note> and "Stomach" <note>zhun</note> into "Cluster" or "Bud" <note>duo</note>. <gloss>The construction of the head is not fixed; some are shaped like caltrops and others like garlic bulbs, and the common people name them according to whatever is convenient.</gloss> Those with short handles or iron chains are all categories of the Bone-Crusher, differing only slightly in their specific physical form.

The **Iron Whip** and **Iron Mace** <note>original: 鐵簡 (tiejian)</note> are two distinct styles. For the whip, the size, weight, and length are determined by the physical strength of the person wielding it. Some makers create these with four distinct ridges; these are called "Iron Maces" because their square, straight edges resemble <term>writing slips<gloss>Called "jian" in Chinese, these were the narrow rectangular slats of bamboo used for books before the invention of paper.</gloss></term>. Both of these weapons belong to the general category of the "whip."

The **Iron Chain Flail** <note>original: 鐵鏈夾棒 (tielian jiabang)</note> originally came from the **Western Nomadic Tribes** <note>original: 西戎 (Xirong)</note>. It is used on horseback to combat the infantry of the Han Chinese. Its shape is identical to the <term>threshing flail<gloss>A traditional agricultural tool consisting of a long handle with a shorter swinging stick attached by a chain, used to beat grain from stalks.</gloss></term> used by farmers, but it is reinforced with iron segments. It is particularly effective for striking downward from a height <note>such as from the back of a horse</note>. Consequently, those Han soldiers who have mastered its use have become even more skillful with it than the nomadic tribesmen themselves.

-># The Heli Staff, the Hooked Staff, and the Lifting Staff<-

<note>The following sections describe the three distinct polearms illustrated on this page, which were used by infantry to combat armored opponents and cavalry.</note>

## The Heli Staff
<term>Heli Staff<gloss>Named after the *Heli* (訶梨) fruit—the Myrobalan or Haritaki—which features a distinctively ribbed or fluted shape.</gloss></term> <note>original: 訶梨棒 (heli bang)</note>
The head of this staff is crafted to resemble the ridged surface of the Myrobalan fruit. This design is not merely decorative; the ridges concentrate the force of a strike onto a smaller surface area, allowing the wielder to dent or crack armor that might otherwise deflect a smooth surface. It functions similarly to the "Caltrop" mace described previously but is mounted on a longer staff for greater reach.

## The Hooked Staff
<term>Hooked Staff<gloss>A heavy polearm equipped with a curved iron hook near the striking head.</gloss></term> <note>original: 鈎棒 (gou bang)</note>
The Hooked Staff is a multi-purpose infantry weapon. While the heavy head is used for bludgeoning, the side-mounted hook is specifically designed for tactical utility on the battlefield. It allows a soldier to snag the reins of a horse, hook the edge of an enemy's shield to pull it aside, or drag a mounted rider from their saddle.

## The Lifting Staff
<term>Lifting Staff<gloss>A weighted staff designed for powerful upward thrusts and parrying maneuvers.</gloss></term> <note>original: 提棒 (ti bang)</note>
The Lifting Staff is characterized by its balance and weight distribution, which favors "lifting" or upward-striking techniques. In defensive formations, it is used to parry spear thrusts or high-angle attacks, redirecting the opponent's momentum before delivering a heavy counter-blow to the legs or torso.

<margin>The dimensions and weight of these staffs are varied according to the strength of the soldier, but they are generally constructed from hardened wood with iron-reinforced heads.</margin>

-># Specialized Striking and Grappling Staffs<-

# The Pestle Staff
<term>Pestle Staff<gloss>A heavy-headed polearm shaped like a traditional mortar pestle.</gloss></term> <note>original: 杵棒 (chu bang)</note>
This weapon features a thick, rounded head made of hard wood or reinforced with iron. Unlike a spear which relies on a point, the Pestle Staff uses pure kinetic energy to crush bone and dent helmets. Its name derives from its resemblance to the tools used in kitchens and pharmacies to grind grain or herbs.

# The Plain Staff
<term>Plain Staff<gloss>Literally "White Staff"; a simple wooden pole weapon without iron spikes or elaborate heads.</gloss></term> <note>original: 白棒 (bai bang)</note>
The "Plain" or "White" staff is the foundation of all polearm techniques. Often made from resilient white wax wood, it is valued for its flexibility and strength. In a military context, it was used for training or as a versatile tool for pushing back enemy lines and defending fortifications where more complex blades might get stuck.

# The Claw Staff
<term>Claw Staff<gloss>A staff topped with a multi-pronged iron "claw" or "rake."</gloss></term> <note>original: 抓子棒 (zhua zi bang)</note>
The Claw Staff is a specialized tactical weapon. The iron prongs at the tip allow the wielder to catch an opponent's clothing, limbs, or equipment. It was particularly effective for pulling soldiers off defensive walls during a siege or dragging fleeing enemies to the ground so they could be captured or dispatched.

# The Wolf-Tooth Mace
<term>Wolf-Tooth Mace<gloss>A long-handled mace featuring a head densely studded with sharp iron spikes.</gloss></term> <note>original: 狼牙棒 (lang ya bang)</note>
Perhaps the most fearsome weapon in this category, the Wolf-Tooth Mace combines the blunt force of a club with the piercing power of multiple spikes. It was famously effective against the heavy armor of the Song and Ming periods, as the spikes could find gaps in plating or concentrate enough pressure to puncture metal.

<note>The term "Bang" (棒) is used here to describe a wide family of staff-like weapons, ranging from simple wooden poles to lethal, iron-studded maces.</note>

->### Various Staffs and Maces<-

The weapon shown to the right is constructed from hard, heavy wood, measuring four to five feet in length. It is known by four distinct names: the <term>Staff<gloss>A general term for a heavy club or stick used as a weapon.</gloss></term> <note>original: 棒 (bang)</note>, the <term>Heavy Club<gloss>An archaic term for a thick wooden staff.</gloss></term> <note>original: 棆 (lun)</note>, the <term>Pestle<gloss>Named for its resemblance to the heavy tool used for grinding grain.</gloss></term> <note>original: 杵 (chu)</note>, and the <term>Pole<gloss>A long, straight staff, often used as the foundation for other polearms.</gloss></term> <note>original: 桿 (gan)</note>.

Some varieties feature iron wrapping around the upper portion; these are commonly referred to as <term>Heli Staffs<gloss>A staff reinforced with iron bands; the term "Heli" is likely a phonetic transcription from a northern border language.</gloss></term> <note>original: 訶藜棒 (heli bang)</note>. Recently, officials stationed at the borders have modified these by adding a sharp blade to the head and placing a pair of inverted hooks just below the tip; this version is known as the <term>Hooked Staff</term> <note>original: 鉤棒 (gou bang)</note>. Those equipped with hooks but lacking a blade are also called <term>Iron Claws</term> <note>original: 鐵抓 (tie zhua)</note>.

When iron nails are embedded into the head so that it resembles the teeth of a predator, it is called a <term>Wolf-Tooth Staff<gloss>Commonly known as a Wolf-Tooth Mace; a devastating weapon used to crush armor.</gloss></term> <note>original: 狼牙棒 (langya bang)</note>. If the weapon is of uniform thickness from the base to the tip, it is classified as a <term>Pestle</term>. Those that are long and slender yet remain hard and heavy are called <term>Poles</term>. Some versions are also equipped with a <term>Bladed Counterweight<gloss>A metal spike or cap attached to the butt-end of the staff to balance the weapon and allow for secondary strikes.</gloss></term> <note>original: 刀鐏 (dao zun)</note>. In general, all of these are simply different types of the staff.
