### Return-Light Herb

The **Return-Light Herb** <note>original: "Huanliangcao" (還亮草)</note> is found throughout the mountain gardens of Linjiang and Guangxin <note>historical regions in modern-day Jiangxi Province</note>. It sprouts in early spring. The stem is square with five ridges, featuring a concave groove in the center; it grows to a height of one or two feet. The base of the plant is purple, while the tips are green. The leaves resemble those of the <term>Hogfennel</term> <note>original: "Qianhu" (前胡); Peucedanum praeruptorum</note> but are thinner.

Small, slender stalks emerge from the branch tips, holding purple flowers horizontally. These have long stalks and five petals; while the stalks stand upright, the flowers tilt, appearing exactly like soaring butterflies. The small inner petals are a particularly vibrant purple, slightly revealing yellow stamens. After the flowers fade, they form seed pods <note>literally "horns"</note> with tips that curve outward. Usually, one flower produces three pods, though occasionally there are four. 

It is also known as <term>Soul-Returning Herb</term> <note>original: "Huanhuncao" (還魂草)</note>, <term>Facing-Father Herb</term> <note>original: "Duifucao" (對父草)</note>, and <term>Butterfly Chrysanthemum</term> <note>original: "Hudieju" (蝴蝶菊)</note>. The stems may be boiled in water to create a wash for treating swollen sores and toxins.

According to the <term>Supplement to the Materia Medica</term> <note>A significant 8th-century medical text by Chen Zangqi</note>: 
The <term>Peach-Vermilion Herb</term> <note>original: "Taozhushu" (桃朱術)</note> grows in gardens and is as delicate as celery. It has purple flowers and produces pods. If one taps the side of the plant while holding a mirror toward it, the seeds will release on their own. If the seeds are collected on the fifth day of the fifth lunar month <note>The Dragon Boat Festival, a day of great ritual significance</note> and worn by a woman, they will cause her husband to love her. Its appearance is extremely similar to the Return-Light Herb.

### Tiankui (Celestial Mallow)

**Tiankui** is also known as "Summer Disappears Without a Trace" <note>original: *Xiawuzong* (夏無蹤)</note>. When it first emerges, it produces a single stem and a single leaf about the size of a copper coin. It looks very much like wood sorrel <note>original: *Sanyesuan* (三葉酸), likely *Oxalis*</note> but is slightly larger, with a green upper surface and a purple underside. The stems are as fine as silk, and the roots resemble those of the **Banxia** <term>Pinellia ternata; a common tuber used in phlegm-related medicine</term> but are smaller. 

In the spring, it sprouts extremely delicate branches. Each branch bears three leaves, and each leaf is divided into three lobes that turn backward and droop down. Small white flowers bloom at the tips of the stems, but the plant withers away immediately upon the **Beginning of Summer** <term>Lixia; one of the 24 solar terms, occurring around May 5th</term>.

According to the *Nancheng County Gazetteer*, the seeds of "Summer Disappears Without a Trace" are named **Tiankui**. This herb is found near the mountains of Fuzhou and Jiujiang in Jiangxi Province. It is what the scholar Zheng Qiao <note>A famous Song dynasty historian and encyclopedist (1104–1162)</note> referred to as the "Hare’s Mallow" <note>original: *Tukui* (菟葵)</note>, specifically the "Purple-backed Tiankui." 

Because it sends up its stems and flowers in the spring and withers by early summer, and because its physical substance is so fragile and its roots so tiny, it is exceptionally difficult to find. New sprouts appear again in the autumn and do not wither even during the depths of winter. Local physicians all call it **Tiankui**. Since the southern regions of Jiangxi border Fujian, Zheng Qiao <note>referred to in the text by his courtesy name, Yuzhong</note> was intimately familiar with it. This plant is small, not even reaching a foot in height, and it grows in the crevices of rocks or in the shade of walls. How then could it "sway in the spring breeze alongside wild oats"? <note>A rhetorical critique of other authors who may have confused this delicate plant with taller, field-growing species</note> In Jianchang, folk healers use it as a topical poultice for breast abscesses <note>original: *rudu* (乳毒), likely mastitis or related infections</note> with great effectiveness.

### Tiankui Grass

Tiankui grass <note>Scientific name: *Semiaquilegia adoxoides*</note> grows in the shady and damp soil of gardens and orchards in Jiujiang and Raozhou <note>Prefectures in modern-day Jiangxi Province</note>. It is also known as "Thousand-Year Rat Droppings" <note>original: *qiannian laoshushi*; a name referring to the small, dark, bead-like appearance of its tubers</note> and is also called "Firecracker Flower."

In the springtime, it sends out thin stems. Each stem bears three leaves, and each leaf is divided into three lobes, with a color resembling malachite green. At the tips of the branches, small purple flowers bloom horizontally; two petals are joined together while one petal turns upward. The flowers have long, arching stalks that appear to "fly" or tilt upwards, with the main stem positioned in the center of the bloom.

The root is reddish-brown and quite hard, adorned with short, fibrous whiskers. As summer begins, the plant immediately withers. Folk doctors use it to treat chronic injuries caused by overwork <term>laoshang: a traditional medical term for ailments resulting from long-term physical or emotional exhaustion</term>, which is prepared by simmering the herb in wine for consumption.

### Violet Flower Earth-Spike

The <term>Violet Flower Earth-Spike<note>Scientific name: *Viola philippica*. This name reflects its purple blooms and its habit of growing close to the ground.</note></term> grows upon the raised ridges between cultivated fields. ### Blood-Vitalizing Elixir

<note>original: 活血丹 (Huóxuè dān); likely *Glechoma longituba*, known today as Chinese Ground Ivy.</note>

The Blood-Vitalizing Elixir is produced in the regions of Jiujiang and Raozhou <note>historical prefectures in modern-day Jiangxi Province</note>. It can be found everywhere: in gardens, tucked into the corners of stone steps, and in the shade of walls. In the springtime, it grows in great abundance, reaching a height of six or seven inches. Its green stems are soft and delicate, with leaves growing in opposite pairs at each node.

The leaves resemble those of the mallow plant; when they first emerge, the small leaves have fine, serrated teeth and deep veins. Their stalks are long and flexible. The plant bears pale red flowers that slightly resemble those of Red Sage <note>original: <term>Danshen</term> (*Salvia miltiorrhiza*)</note>; they hang downward like the wings of a moth. Taking the stems, leaves, and roots to simmer as a medicinal drink has been proven effective for treating the vomiting of blood or blood in the stool. Once summer begins, the plant quickly withers and becomes difficult to find.

### Seven-Leaf Bramble

The **Seven-Leaf Bramble** <note>original: *Qiyejing* (七葉荆)</note> grows in the fields and wilderness of Nanchang, Jiangxi. It reaches a height of more than one or two feet. Both its leaves and stems are a pale green color. The leaves resemble those of the <term>Vitex bramble<note>A hardy shrub common in East Asia; its wood and leaves have been used for centuries in both medicine and craft.</note></term> and have serrated edges. 

Near the root, the leaves grow in a cluster of three. On the layer above that, there are four leaves; on the layer above that, there are five. This progression continues until the tip of the stem, which reaches seven leaves and then stops. 

Local people say that the seven-leaf variety is extremely rare and claim that it is feared by ghosts. Such talk is highly absurd; however, the *Botanical Accounts of the Southern Regions* <note>original: *Nanfang Caomu Zhuang* (南方草木狀), a 4th-century work often cited as the first botanical treatise of its kind.</note> already contains mentions of using this plant to identify illnesses. Master Tao’s *Secret Formula for the True Recluse* <note>original: *Taoshi Zhenyin Jue* (陶氏眞隱訣), a text likely associated with the 5th-century Daoist scholar and physician Tao Hongjing.</note> also contains passages regarding its ability to communicate with the divine. Thus, while popular rumors may be distorted, they are certainly not without some historical basis.

### Water Bayberry

The <term>Water Bayberry</term><note>Original: *Shui Yangmei* (水楊梅), likely referring to *Adina rubella*. The name derives from its fruit's resemblance to the edible bayberry.</note> is recorded in the *Compendium of Materia Medica*<note>The *Bencao Gangmu* (1578), China's most famous encyclopedic work on pharmacology and botany.</note> as growing by the water's edge. Its branches and leaves are very numerous, and its seeds resemble those of the bayberry.

According to my observations, this plant is very common near the ponds and marshes of <term>Jiangxi</term><note>A province in southeast China known for its wetlands.</note>. When the flowers grow old, they turn into a fluffy down. The locals call it the <term>Water Willow</term><note>Original: *Shui Yangliu* (水楊柳).</note>. It does not resemble the plant called "Ground Pepper" cited in the *Gengxin Jade Atlas*<note>A Ming Dynasty alchemical text that describes various minerals and plants used in "external alchemy."</note>, which is described as having yellow flowers.

### Wind-Dispelling Herb <note>original: *Xiaofengcao*</note>

The **Wind-Dispelling Herb** <term>*Xiaofengcao*; a medicinal name suggesting the plant's use in treating "wind-type" ailments such as skin rashes, tremors, or colds</term> is found in abundance throughout the level fields of **Nan’an** <note>a historical prefecture in modern-day Jiangxi province</note> and **Changsha** <note>the capital of modern-day Hunan province</note>. It possesses green stems covered in fine white hairs. Its leaves resemble those of the hemp plant, featuring branched veins that are extremely fine and intricate in their arrangement. The upper surface of the leaf is a deep, dark green, while the underside is white and hairy. Small, pink flowers with long stalks bloom from between the leaves. The plant produces round fruits divided into five lobes with dotted patterns, which slightly resemble hemp seeds.

### Henbit Deadnettle

Henbit Deadnettle <term>Bǎogài Cǎo: literally "Precious Cover Herb," referring to how the upper leaves shelter the flowers</term> grows in the damp, shaded areas of Nanchang, Jiangxi. It is also known as the "Pearl Lotus." This plant sprouts in early spring, featuring square stems that are purple in color.

The leaves resemble those of the Bird's Eye Speedwell <note>original: *pópónà* (Veronica polita)</note> but are slightly larger. They grow in opposite pairs and "clasp" the stem <note>the botanical term is amplexicaul</note>, forming a circular shape with deep veins. The plant grows in distinct layers; from within these circular leaf clusters, small, pale purple flowers bloom in groups. Local people harvest this herb to decoct in wine; it is used to nourish the sinews and invigorate the blood <term>yǎngjīn huóxuè: a Traditional Chinese Medicine concept referring to improving mobility and stimulating circulation</term>, and it serves to stop pain throughout the entire body.

### Ground Brocade

**Ground Brocade** <note>Original: *Dijin* (地錦)</note> is found in shady and damp locations. Its purple stems grow flat against the ground. The leaves resemble newly sprouted chrysanthemum leaves but are shorter, with deep "teeth" <term>serrations or notched edges</term> and a glossy luster. It produces small, pinkish-purple flowers that are only about the size of a millet grain. The fruit forms into a small ball. 

The plant has a slightly pungent taste. In Hunan province, it is also called the "Half-side Lotus" <note>Original: *Banbianlian* (半邊蓮). While this name is commonly associated with *Lobelia chinensis* today, regional variations in naming were common in historical texts</note>. It can be used as a remedy to treat injuries and bruises from falls. ### Thunder God’s Chisel

The Thunder God’s Chisel <term>Lei Gong Zao; likely a variety of wild garlic or lily</term> is found in the plains of Jiangxi. However, the *Gazetteer of Hunan* notes that during years of famine, some people who ate it died from excessive vomiting. I contend that the *Materia Medica for Relief of Famine* would certainly not suggest a poisonous plant to save the people from starvation; this is a mistake resulting from an incorrect citation within the *Compendium of Materia Medica*. 

Upon examining the *Materia Medica for Relief of Famine*, one finds no mention of the phrase “flowers and leaves never meet” <note>A hallmark of the Lycoris species is that the flowers bloom only after the leaves have died back</note>, nor does the illustration in that book show flowers or fruit. Its nature is non-toxic; I have tasted it myself and found the flavor mild. When people dug up plants for food during famine years, it must have been this species, and certainly not the toxic Stone Garlic.
