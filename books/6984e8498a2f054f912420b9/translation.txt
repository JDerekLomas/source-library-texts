Furthermore, those who trace the origins of such great evils and strive to gradually dismantle their accumulated strength (at which your piety is especially directed), they accuse, as if with one voice, the monstrous madness of **self-love** <note>original: "philautiæ rabiem" - self-love or vanity; a term often used in Renaissance theology to describe the root of pride and heresy.</note>. THIS age is a restless and inquisitive one <note>original: *curiosum*</note>, Reverend Father, which is so incapable of settling these long-standing upheavals that it stirs up new ones all the more quickly, and spreads movements that worsen day by day far and wide. Furthermore, those who track the origins of such great evils and strive to scatter their gradually gathered strength (at which your piety is especially aimed), accuse with one voice, as it were, the monstrous madness of self-love <note>original: *philautia*; a Greek term often used in theological texts to describe excessive self-regard as the root of heresy.</note>, and they suggest, not without reason, that this disease is both the head and the cause of all others. From the same fountain, it seems to me, flows the zeal for writing new books, which the Preacher <note>original: *Ecclesiastes*; likely a reference to the biblical book of Ecclesiastes 12:12, "of making many books there is no end."</note> refutes as a kind of vain curiosity. And from here (which is much more poisonous) comes both the ambitious display of learning and a certain stubborn presumption of wisdom, then that unbridled lust for inventing new dogmas, through which ancient lessons begin to be both loathed and rendered obsolete. 

Finally, through these steps, as if through all degrees of impiety, human recklessness has now reached such a point—alas!—that for most people, the dignity and authority of the most holy popes and the most distinguished fathers of the ancient Church are held in contempt, unless perhaps the new teachers find it convenient to misuse this authority to confirm their own errors. For thus far, they find value in Gennadius on communion and transubstantiation, Clement on the reservation of the Eucharist, Hilary on the justification of faith, Epiphanius on the destruction of sacred images, Prosper on confession, Gregory on the naming of the Ecclesiastical primacy, and Bernard on dispensation. But Jerome seems more than rigid—indeed, intolerable—no doubt because he cannot help but be unpleasant to the followers of Jovinian and Vigilantius <note>Jovinian and Vigilantius were 4th-century figures whom Jerome vigorously attacked in his writings for opposing monasticism and the veneration of relics.</note>. Cyprian is disliked everywhere. And no wonder, since he—being entirely "Evangelical" not just in name but in reality—diligently commends the merits of works and the practice of satisfaction to these boasters of faith. They claim that Augustine ignores the corrupt morals of his own time, simply because he gladly insists on the received discipline of the Church to these despisers. Now, for those who with equal modesty despise so many and such venerable decrees, laws, and institutions of our ancestors, I ask: will they listen to this Leo <note>A reference to Pope Leo the Great, whose writings Canisius is likely introducing.</note>, even though he thunders everywhere with a rare majesty of speech that is clearly apostolic? Will they look up to a Pope who is otherwise to be looked up to for so many reasons—for his dignity, gravity, wisdom, and eloquence?

# THE LETTER

...eloquence, so that by the highest right he received the surname **the Great**. Indeed, if only for this reason—that he is called Pope, and no less the Bishop of the universal Church than the Roman Pontiff, as he frequently writes of himself—they will undoubtedly receive such a great man with sneers and a Sardonic grin <note>A 'Sardonic grin' (*risus Sardonicus*) refers to a bitter or scornful laugh, originally associated with a plant from Sardinia that caused facial contortions.</note>. With what patience of stomach will they ever bear that the people of God are restricted to fasts on certain days, to nightly vigils, and to designated places and times of prayer? They will read here, whether they like it or not, that heavenly grace is to be earned by good deeds, and that the Christian heart is declared not by faith alone, but by the faithful pursuit of works. 

They will read repeatedly that sins are redeemed by alms, that an offended God is appeased by worthy fruits of penance, that internal filth is washed away by the power of tears, and likewise that the future reward from God is tempered to the magnitude of present labor and the fervor of our charity. They will also read that private laws of fasting are subordinated to common or public ones. They will read that the selection of foods and the yoke of Ecclesiastical obedience agree beautifully with Christian liberty, and have nothing in common with Jewish superstition <note>A common rhetorical point in the Counter-Reformation was to argue that Catholic fasting was a spiritual discipline of the New Covenant, distinct from the legalistic dietary laws of the Old Testament.</note>. They will read that many things have flowed down, without being written, from either the apostles or apostolic men to establish the order of religion. 

They will read that by the intercession of the saints, Christ the mediator of redemption is not only not obscured for us, but is even vehemently illuminated, and the whole Christian Church is aided at the same time. They will read, finally, that the most holy offering of the Mass and the sacrifice is repeated daily in the same basilica, and not without reason. I refrain from speaking of the primacy of Peter. Testimonies are everywhere at hand, confirmed even by sacred scripture, so that I need add nothing more. 

Being convicted, therefore—fine judges of war indeed!—both by the inviolable observation of such a long span of time and by the irrefutable authority of the most holy pontiff, let them (as much as their lust allows) slander such "papistic" doctrine. Since it was long ago rejected <note>original: *explosam*; literally "driven off the stage by clapping," meaning hissed off or discarded.</note> by them, let them, I pray, permit us to be those who desire to be seen as observant sons of Mother Church, and who do not cease to be so. 

Although it would not be very difficult to protect the innocence and cause of St. Leo <note>original: *D. Leonis*; the 'D.' stands for *Divi* or *Domini*, a title of respect for a Saint.</note> against those detractors. For what could they bring forward, however clever they may be, that would not be immediately subverted and collapse by a single response of this pontiff to Marcian the Emperor <note>original: *Marcianum Augustum*; Marcian was the Eastern Roman Emperor who convened the Council of Chalcedon in 451 AD.</note>? "This," he says, "is the best method of teaching: that the line of the fathers' sentiments be made known to the ears of the people and the clergy." And if there are any (let the adversaries note this at last) who despise our writings, let them at least acquiesce to those who agree with us in apostolic sentiments. 

But it is truly no wonder if they take up a censor’s rod <note>original: *virgam censoriam*; a reference to the authority of a Roman censor to judge and punish moral failings.</note> over Leo’s works, those who are not at all ashamed to break into even the secret and divine scriptures, bringing manifest violence to these passages, utterly breaking those, altogether denying others, and adulterating almost everything with new and hitherto unheard-of "little interpretations." For where, finally, does any of the <term>Theologians: Specifically referring to the great thinkers of the early and medieval Church.</term> exist who, in so many hundreds of years from then, ever even dreamed of such things as these men now bring forth, revealed to them by a new but not divine spirit? They bring forth, I say, things concerning the "doctrine of demons," the "leaven of the Pharisees," the "bones of the dead," the "marriage of priests," the prohibiti— <note>The text cuts off here at the catchword for the next page.</note>

# DEDICATORY LETTER

...been decreed regarding the devotion of vows and the disapproval of fasts; furthermore, concerning human strength, the kingdom of the Antichrist, Babylon, and the idols of the nations. 

Since it is easy for everyone to recommend the plausible dogma of justifying faith <note>original: "iustificantis fidei"; a reference to the Protestant doctrine of *Sola Fide* (faith alone), which Canisius is critiquing here.</note>, to spread the veneer of Christian liberty, to tear down the firm dogmas of the Church and Apostolic traditions in favor of petty human regulations, to mock the opinions of theologians, and to appeal to Scripture alone as judge—though it is a mute one—and finally to renew opinions previously condemned. For it is from here they chiefly begin, here they continue, and here they end—those who, by the boasting of new dogmas (no less dangerous than they are empty), stir up such great disturbances in our Germany. Nor do they cease even now (alas, the wickedness!) to draw this plague of souls, which infects so many, from such sources with all their strength and cunning.

For this reason, Reverend Father, your piety is all the more to be admired by us, and your diligence is always to be remembered with a grateful heart; for you, like a second and identical unconquered **Leo** <note>Canisius is making a pun on the name of Pope Leo the Great (the subject of the book) and the strength of the Bishop he is addressing, comparing him to a "lion" guarding the flock.</note>, have for so many years thrown your whole self against such ravenous wolves. You oppose them as a wall and a most strong bulwark for the house of Israel. Finally, you defend Christ's flock in this blessed metropolis of all Germany <note>Cologne was often referred to as the "Rome of the North."</note> as a Preacher with the sword of the Spirit, and as a Bishop you so guard and care for it, that in no neighboring towns has religion received less contagion, nor has ecclesiastical discipline preserved greater vigor until now. 

Certainly, with your eloquence and the power of your speech—like a living trumpet of the Gospel—you stir souls and fill Christian ears, and all the more effectively because your sincerity stands far apart from all pride, ambition, and pomp. This distinguished clergy and this by no means weakened people feel what we say and constantly approve of it. For this reason, both flock to you in crowds as you preach, even though you perform this duty weekly and indeed even more often; from this, they also receive with general applause that singular industry and vehemence in which you wonderfully excel in uncovering the frauds of heretics. 

The weak and the strong in faith alike know how much the integrity of the faith, confirmed through you, accomplishes. But it is better to set these things aside, lest I burden your modesty, especially since—as you truly and piously teach us—whatever we are or are capable of, it must all be credited to divine generosity. 

Therefore, we send forth into the light this book, adorned with the authority of your name—truly a most beautiful gift, and just as it has been most desired by the pious until now, so it is most useful in itself. It is most fitting both for me, your cleric, who gives it, and for you, my Bishop, who receives it. You treat divine dogmas with such admirable eloquence and amiable vehemence that, if one may speak freely, you could rightly be called and considered a "second Leo" in this Commonwealth. 

As for the rest, anyone will be able to recognize the course of Saint Leo's life and his extraordinary gifts, partly from those things added after the Index, and partly from his works themselves, no less than recognizing "a lion from its claws" <note>original: "ex unguibus"; from the proverb *ex ungue leonem*, meaning one can judge the quality of a whole work by a small sample.</note>, as they say. 

For what remains, I pray from my heart with all the pious that the Shepherd of shepherds may prosper your endeavors and preserve your health unharmed for a long time, for the benefit not only of this city but of the whole Church together. 

Given at the holy Cologne of the Ubians <note>original: "Ubiorum Coloniam"; the classical Roman name for Cologne, referring to the Ubii tribe.</note>, on the fourth day before the Ides of September, in the year of human salvation 1546.
