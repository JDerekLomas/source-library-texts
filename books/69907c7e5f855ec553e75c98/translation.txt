## CERBERUS IN CLASSICAL ART

Classical art has depicted Cerberus quite extensively; his portrayal, however, is not nearly as consistent as it is in the works of the Greek and Roman poets. Statues, sarcophagi <note>Stone coffins, often adorned with relief sculptures</note>, and vase paintings depicting Hades, or scenes set in the underworld, represent him as a ferocious Greek shepherd dog <note>original: "collie"; the author likely refers to the Laconian or Molossian breeds common in ancient Greece</note>. He is often shown encircled by snakes and with a serpent for a tail, but there is no certainty regarding the number of his heads. He is frequently shown with three heads in art, just as in literature; this can be easily seen in the illustrations in Baumeister’s *Monuments of Classical Antiquity* <note>original German title: "Denkmäler des Klassischen Altertums"</note>. A very well-known example is the statue in the Villa Borghese of Pluto <note>The Roman god of the underworld, often used interchangeably with the Greek Hades</note> sitting on his throne with the three-headed Cerberus at his side.¹ A Greek <term>scarab</term> <note>original: "scarabæus"; a gemstone carved in the shape of a beetle, used as a seal or amulet</note> depicts a pair of lovers, or a married couple, who have died—

## CLASSICAL EXPLANATIONS OF CERBERUS.

Such classical explanations of Cerberus’ shape as I have seen are feeble and foolishly reasonable. Heraclitus, in his work *On Unbelievable Tales* <note>original Greek: *Περὶ ἀπίστων*</note> (33 I), states that Cerberus had two pups. They always attended their father, and therefore he appeared to be three-headed. The mythographer <note>A collector or writer of myths</note> Palaephatos (39) states that Cerberus was considered three-headed because of his name *Trikarenos* <note>original Greek: *Τρικάρηνος*, meaning "three-headed"</note>, which he obtained from the city Trikarenos in Phliasia. And a late Roman rationalistic mythographer by the name of Fulgentius¹ tells us that Petronius defined Cerberus as the lawyer of Hades, apparently because of his three jaws, or the cumulative glibness of three tongues. Fulgentius himself has a story <note>original Latin: *fabula*</note> in which he says that Cerberus means *Creaboros*, that is, flesh-devouring <note>original: *Creaboros* is a Latinization of the Greek *kreoboros*</note>—

### CERBERUS AND COMPARATIVE MYTHOLOGY.

In conclusion, I would like to direct the attention of those scholars, writers, and journalists who have dismissed the methods and results of <term>Comparative Mythology</term> <note>Comparative Mythology: The study of myths from different cultures to identify shared themes and origins</note> to this current attempt to establish an Indo-European naturalistic myth. I would ask them to consider, in light of the <term>Vedas</term> <note>Vedas: The oldest sacred texts of Hinduism, written in ancient Sanskrit</note>, that it is likely the earliest ideas about life after death focused on the visible heavens—with the sun and the moon—rather than on geographically vague and hard-to-find caves and ravines that lead to the wide gates of <term>Hades</term> <note>Hades: In Greek mythology, the underworld home of the dead</note>. Therefore, the myth of <term>Cerberus</term> <note>Cerberus: The multi-headed dog that guards the entrance to the underworld</note> likely originated in concepts of heaven, not hell.

The <term>Vedas</term> declare that the sun and the moon are these two dogs. Over time, through gradual stages, this myth of heaven was transformed into a myth of hell...
