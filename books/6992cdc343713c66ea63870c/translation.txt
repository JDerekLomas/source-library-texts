# The Great Spinning Wheel

The design of the **Great Spinning Wheel** measures over twenty feet in length and approximately five feet in width. <note>The original units are *zhang* (丈) and *chi* (尺); a *zhang* is roughly ten feet.</note> First, construct a wooden ground-frame. At the four corners, erect pillars, each five feet high. Horizontal crossbeams are pierced through the center, and square timber beams are laid on top. The two ends of these square beams feature V-shaped notches <note>original: "mountain mouths" (山口)</note> to horizontally support the iron axle of the long reel which receives the wound yarn.

Next, on the front of the ground-frame, erect a long wooden base. Upon this base, arrange rows of sockets to support the iron pins at the base of the <term>spindles (xian)</term>. 
<gloss>The large spindles are made by turning wood into cylinders, each one foot two inches long and one foot two inches in circumference. There are thirty-two in total, designed to hold the wound yarn.</gloss> 
At the top of the spindles, iron rings are used to secure the spindle axles. Furthermore, in front of the top crossbeam, arrange a row of small iron forks to separate and guide the strands of prepared fiber.

<gloss>Pronounced in the rising tone</gloss>

Above the spindles and the long reel, separate frames are built on the left and right to hold two large drive wheels. Leather driving cords are connected through these, passing under the row of pulleys and over to turn the rotating drum of the reel. The machine is powered by either **humans or animals**. 

<gloss>Pronounced in the rising tone</gloss>

When the large wheel on the left is turned, the driving cords move with the wheel, and all the mechanical parts move in unison. The upper and lower components correspond to one another, with the speed of the motion properly regulated. This causes the fiber strands to become tightly twisted 
<gloss>Pronounced in the departing tone</gloss> 
as they are wound onto the reel. Operating day and night, this machine can produce a hundred catties of yarn. <note>A "catty" or *jin* is a traditional unit of weight, roughly equivalent to 1.3 pounds or 600 grams.</note>

<term>Great Spinning Wheel (Dafangche)</term>: A massive, multi-spindle machine that represented a peak of pre-industrial textile technology, allowing one power source to drive dozens of spindles simultaneously.
<term>Reel (Kuang)</term>: The large frame onto which the finished, twisted yarn is wound.
<term>Spindle (Xian)</term>: The vertical rod that twists the raw fiber into thread.

### The Silk-Twisting Spindle
The **Silk-Twisting Spindle** <note>original: 撚綿軸 *nianmianzhou*; from *nian* (to twist), *mian* (silk wadding or soft cotton), and *zhou* (an axle or spindle).</note> is a tool used to twist raw fibers—most commonly silk wadding—into a consistent thread. By rotating the spindle, the artisan imparts a twist to the fibers, which provides the strength and tension necessary for the thread to be used in weaving or sewing.

### The Winding Brush
The **Winding Brush** <note>original: 纏刷 *chanshua*; from *chan* (to wind or bind) and *shua* (a brush).</note> is an implement used during the winding process. In traditional Chinese textile production, brushes like these were often used to smooth the fibers as they were wound onto a reel, or to apply a thin layer of starch or sizing to the yarn to prevent it from fraying or tangling during the high-tension environment of the loom.

## The Silk-Twisting Spindle
To construct a **Silk-Twisting Spindle** <note>original: 撚綿軸 *nianmianzhou*</note>, one uses a small base made of either wood or stone. A thin axle, approximately one *chi* <note>A Chinese foot, roughly 32 centimeters or 12.6 inches.</note> in length, is inserted into the base. To begin, take the end of the fiber <note>original: 纩頭 *kuangtou*; referring to the fine silk wadding or cotton starter.</note> and hook the raw material onto it. The artisan holds a fork-like tool in the left hand while the right hand draws the fiber upward onto the spindle. By suspending and rotating the device, the fibers are twisted into a continuous thread <note>original: 綿絲 *miansi*</note>, which is then wound directly onto the spindle to form a fine strand. This tool is primarily used by women and young girls in the household, as it can effectively replace the more laborious work of large-scale spinning wheels.

## The Warping Brush
The **Warping Brush** <note>original: 繮刷 *jiangshua*</note> is an implement used to separate and align the strands of cloth. It is made by bundling the roots of hardy grasses together. Including the handle, the tool is about one *chi* long and over one *chi* wide. 

Once the warp threads <note>The longitudinal threads in a loom that are held in tension.</note> have been wound onto the **warp beam** <note>original: 扞軸 *hanzhou*; the roller or axle that holds the warp threads.</note>, the assembly is mounted upon a wooden frame. Heavy weights are attached below to pull the threads taut. Once the warp threads are evenly spread out, the artisan holds this brush, applies **sizing paste** <note>original: 漿糊 *jianghu*; a starch-based liquid used to strengthen and smooth the yarn.</note>, and brushes downward along the length of the threads. This process increases the luster of the fibers and prepares them to be cast into the loom for weaving. Although this is considered a "minor tool" in the manufacture of cloth, it is absolutely indispensable to the process.

<note>The 'Tiangong Kaiwu' (1637) emphasizes that even these simple handheld tools, often made of organic materials like grass and stone, were vital to the Ming dynasty's textile economy.</note>

For farming families, the work of spinning and weaving during the spring and autumn seasons is the most vital of tasks, making this loom the most essential of all tools.

The **Rope Machine** entwines <gloss>the original pronunciation is indicated by the characters for "ancient" and "dark"</gloss> taut strands to make rope. 

Regarding the construction of this machine: first, set up a winder frame and plant wooden stakes to secure it. Atop the frame, add a single horizontal board, approximately five feet in length and four inches in width. In the center of this horizontal board, carve a row of eight or six apertures. Within each aperture, place a "swinging branch" <note>original: 掉枝 (diaozhi). These are the rotating hooks or crank handles used to twist the fibers.</note>, made of either iron or wood, each curved like an ox’s horn. Additionally, construct a single horizontal beam with holes aligned to fit these swinging branches. Furthermore, create a second machine using the exact same method.

Place the two machines facing one another at a measured distance. Take the prepared taut strands <gloss>pronounced in the departing tone</gloss> and tie each to the feet of the swinging branches on both machines. One person stands at the head of each machine, and together they turn the horizontal beams that pass through the swinging branches. Wait until the individual strands are evenly tightened. Then, gather three or four strands together into one, and tie each group to the foot of a single swinging branch; this will result in the production of two ropes.

Afterward, take a separately crafted "claw-wood" <note>original: 爪木 (zhuamu). In Western rope-making, this is known as a "rope top" or "lay top"—a notched wooden block that regulates the twist and prevents the strands from tangling before they are combined.</note> and place it at the head where the strands are to be combined <gloss>pronounced in the entering tone</gloss>. Turn the swinging branches once more to entwine the strands tightly into rope. The claw-wood will move forward on its own as the twisting progresses; once the rope is finished, the process stops.

# The Skein Frame and the Spinning Spindle

The **Skein Frame** <note>original: 綛車 (rènchē)</note> and the **Spinning Spindle** <note>original: 旋椎 (xuánzhuī)</note> are fundamental tools in the traditional Chinese textile workshop, used for the preparation and refinement of thread. 

The **Skein Frame** <term>Skein Frame<note>Also known as a yarn winder or reel; it is a wooden apparatus used to wind finished thread into organized loops called "skeins" or "hanks."</note></term> is a structural frame designed to catch thread as it is pulled from a spindle or bobbin. The **Spinning Spindle** <term>Spinning Spindle<note>A weighted tool, often called a drop spindle, used to twist raw fibers (such as silk, hemp, or cotton) into a continuous, strong thread through the use of centrifugal force.</note></term> is a simpler, more ancient tool. It typically consists of a central shaft and a weighted whorl at the bottom. The spinner twists the fibers and drops the spindle, letting its rotation provide the necessary "twist" to turn loose fiber into durable yarn.

# The Twine Machine
<gloss>The character 'Rèn' is pronounced as a combination of 'ni' and 'lin'</gloss> This is a device used for twisting rope and twine. # The Spinning Spindle
This is a tool for spinning hemp yarn. It is made from a section of wood approximately six inches long, with a diameter at the head of about three inches. # The Silkworm Ladle

The **Silkworm Ladle** <term>cánbiāo<note>A specialized long-handled tool, resembling a flat wooden scoop or paddle, used for the delicate task of moving silkworms or redistributing mulberry leaves without touching them directly with the hands.</note></term> is described in the *Collected Rimes* <note>original: 集韻 (Jíyùn). A major Chinese rime dictionary published in 1037 during the Song Dynasty.</note> as being synonymous with a measuring vessel. The *Rites of Zhou* <note>original: 周禮 (Zhōu lǐ). A ritual text of the Zhou dynasty that outlines governmental and social structures.</note> notes that such a vessel holds one *sheng* <note>Approximately one liter or a pint.</note> and is used for pouring <gloss>pronounced jǔ-zhū; meaning to ladle out or pour</gloss>.

The *Explanation of Graphs and Analysis of Characters* <note>original: 說文 (Shuōwén Jiězì). The first dictionary to analyze Chinese characters, compiled in the 2nd century AD.</note> states that the character *biāo* (ladle) refers to a tool for scooping things. To create a **Silkworm Ladle**, a piece of wood is cut and carved. # The Silkworm Net
<note>original: 蠶網; cánwǎng. A mesh tool made of rope, used to move silkworms without touching them directly.</note>

The **Silkworm Net** is a tool used in conjunction with the **Silkworm Rack** <term>qíng<note>Original: 檠. In this context, it refers to the stand or frame that supports the trays.</note></term>. It is constructed by knotting rope together, following the same design used for fishing nets. The length, width, and overall size of the net are determined by the dimensions of the silkworm rack it is intended to fit. 

By treating the rope with **lacquer oil** <term>qīyóu<note>Original: 漆油. A protective coating made from the seeds of the lacquer tree, used to waterproof and strengthen fibers.</note></term>, the net becomes smooth, tight, and highly resistant to decay. When the net is threaded with supporting cords, it becomes very convenient to manage and maintain. 

When it is time to "change the bed" <note>A term for removing the silkworms from their accumulated waste and old leaves to a clean tray.</note>, the net is first spread over the silkworms. Then, fresh **mulberry** <term>sāng<note>Original: 桑. The white mulberry (Morus alba), the primary food source for the Bombyx mori silkworm.</note></term> leaves are scattered on top of the net. Upon smelling the fragrance of the leaves, the silkworms all crawl up through the mesh openings to reach the food. Once the silkworms have finished climbing onto the leaves, workers can lift the net together by hand and **transfer** <term>yízhì<note>Original: 移置. Literally "move and place."</note></term> them to a separate rack. 

The waste and debris left behind on the original tray can then be easily gathered and discarded. Compared to the method of transferring silkworms by hand—which is tedious and can damage the insects—this technique is more than twice as efficient. This method is widely used for **Southern silkworms** <term>náncán<note>Original: 南蠶. Referring to the sericulture traditions of the Yangtze River Delta regions like Zhejiang and Jiangsu.</note></term>, and it is also highly suitable for use with **Northern silkworms** during their early stages of growth when they are small and most fragile.

# The Heated Basin

**Qin Guan** <note>A famous Song Dynasty poet and scholar (1049–1100 AD) who wrote the "Can Shu" (Book of Sericulture), one of the earliest specialized works on the subject.</note> stated in his *Book of Sericulture*: "To reel silk, one uses the **yu** <term>yú<note>Original: 禺. An ancient type of deep vessel or basin used specifically for heating cocoons.</note></term>; the silk is drawn from it and led directly through the 'coin-eye' <note>A small hole, often the size of a copper coin, used to guide and consolidate the silk filaments.</note>." This confirms that the *yu* vessel is essential for the reeling process. 

Today, farming families imitate the depth and breadth of the ancient *yu* by placing a **basin** <term>pányí<note>Original: 盤匜. A combination of a shallow tray (pan) and a water vessel (yi).</note></term> atop a **cauldron** <term>fǔ<note>Original: 釜. A large metal pot or cauldron used for boiling or steaming.</note></term>. This setup serves as a practical substitute for the *yu*. 

Therefore, the *General Discourse on Agriculture and Sericulture* says: The cauldron must be large and set firmly upon the stove. 

<gloss>Using a method similar to steaming yeast for fermentation, this setup can be used to reel coarse silk.</gloss>

Place the large basin atop the cauldron. 

<gloss>This setup is suitable for both single-person and double-person reeling operations.</gloss>

Add water at the joint where the basin meets the cauldron until the basin is eighty percent full. This arrangement provides enough space for two people to sit opposite each other and reel silk together. The water must be kept constantly hot. It is best to drop the cocoons into the water gradually as you reel; if too many cocoons are added at once, they will be overcooked and the silk will be damaged. Whenever there is a large quantity of cocoons to process, this "Heated Basin" method should be used to increase speed.

An imperial poem <note>original: 勅詩; chìshī. Refers to a poem of official or instructional nature, often included in agricultural manuals to help farmers memorize techniques.</note> says:

*When the looms are idle, the household takes up the bamboo tubes and hurries to the reeling,*
*Carefully maintaining the fire to keep the water at the 'crab-eye' boil.* 
<note>original: 蟹眼湯; xièyǎn tāng. "Crab-eye soup" is a traditional culinary term for water that has just begun to bubble, where the bubbles are small, like the eyes of a crab. This indicates the precise temperature needed to soften the silk gum without destroying the fiber.</note>

### The Round Frame
To build a frame, one must first establish the "frame core." This is done by taking five long rafters, gathering them at the top, and tying them securely. Wormwood stalks are then distributed evenly and firmly around this core. Once the silkworms are placed on the stalks, the entire structure is enclosed with more mats, and the top is covered to resemble a round pavilion. This is known as the **Round Frame**.

### The Horizontal Frame
There is also the **Horse-Head Long Frame**. This is constructed by planting two pillars in the ground with a horizontal beam suspended between them. Thin rafters are leaned against both sides of the beam to form the core. The remaining steps follow the standard method. These are the "Horizontal Frame" methods typically used in Northern China.

I have observed that silkworm frames in the South are simply arranged using short grass placed atop <term>silkworm stands<note>Original: 蠶檠 (*cán qíng*). # Storing Cocoons in Jars
<note>Original: 甕繭 (*wèng jiǎn*). This term refers to the method of placing silkworm cocoons into large ceramic vats to kill the pupae inside, ensuring the silk thread remains unbroken.</note>

**Jar** <note>Original: 甕 (*wèng*). A large, wide-mouthed ceramic vessel used for storage and processing.</note>
**Cocoon** <note>Original: 繭 (*jiǎn*). The protective silk casing spun by the silkworm.</note>

One figure carries a large, flat tray overflowing with the harvest, while another uses a <term>steelyard scale<note>A traditional Chinese balance scale consisting of a wooden beam with a sliding weight.</note></term> to weigh the silk before it is processed.

# Management of Silkworm Egg Sheets

**Silkworm Egg Sheets** <note>Original: *canlian* (蠶連). These are the specialized papers upon which silkworm eggs are deposited and stored.</note> are the papers used for silkworm eggs. In former times, two large sheets of paper were joined together. After the moths lay their eggs, a long thread is used to connect them into a single continuous "link," which is why they are called "links" (*lian*). Specialized artisans produce and sell these sheets.

The *New Book on the Fundamentals of Agriculture* <note>Original: *Wuben Xinshu* (務本新書), an important Yuan Dynasty agricultural manual.</note> states: "Thick paper is superior for silkworm sheets; thin paper cannot withstand the process of soaking and bathing. Using 'small ash paper' <note>A type of absorbent, sturdy handmade paper treated with alkaline plant ash.</note> is even more wonderful. The links must be bathed at the appropriate times. After the bathing is finished and it is time to hang them, ensure the silkworm eggs are facing outward, for fear that the wind might cause friction and damage them."

On the day of the Winter Solstice and the eighth day of the twelfth lunar month <note>The eighth day of the twelfth month is known as *Laba* (臘月八日), a traditional time for various ritual and agricultural preparations.</note>, when it is time to bathe the eggs, do not let the water be extremely deep. After soaking and bathing, take them out. By the time of the full moon, roll several sheets into a single scroll and bind them securely with a mulberry bark rope.

<gloss>The *New Book on the Fundamentals of Agriculture* says: Silkworm egg sheets must not be tied or hung with hemp rope. If this taboo is ignored, many will later dry up and die and fail to hatch. The scholar Chen Zangqi <note>A famous Tang Dynasty pharmacologist and physician.</note> says: If ramie <note>A plant in the nettle family used for fiber, similar to hemp.</note> is brought near the eggs, they will not hatch; one must keep it far away.</gloss>

Stand a pole in the front courtyard and hang the sheets high to receive the cold winter air of the twelfth month. After the New Year festival, stand the sheets upright inside a ceramic jar, ensuring they are arranged loosely and with enough space to be "exquisite and airy" <note>Original: *linglong* (玲瓏); here referring to a structure that allows air to circulate freely.</note>. Keep them there for ten-plus days. When the sun is high, take them out once. After every period of cloudy weather or rain, immediately sun and air them.

<gloss>This is for fear of damage from dampness or stuffiness.</gloss>
<gloss>Exposure to wind should also not last for too long.</gloss>

This is the method for bathing and nourishing the silkworm sheets, lasting until the time for "warming the seeds" <note>Original: *nuanzhong* (暖種); the process of incubation to trigger hatching.</note> and the emergence of the larvae.

# The Pruning Axe

The pruning axe is a specific tool for the mulberry tree. The socket <gloss>pronounced *qiong*</gloss> of this axe is thick and the blade is broad, which makes it different from a common woodcutter’s axe. It is used during the "silkworm month" <note>The third month of the lunar calendar, the peak season for silkworm rearing and leaf harvesting.</note> for pruning mulberry branches. This is the meaning behind the ancient verse: "Take up the axes and hatchets to prune those long, high-reaching branches." <note>A reference to the *Shijing* (Book of Odes), the oldest existing collection of Chinese poetry.</note> 

Farmers find this tool essential. It is said that by turning the body and swinging the axe, the branches and leaves fall outward; this is precisely what is meant by "pruning the long branches." Generally, an axe that can strip and chop without requiring its edge to be sharpened again is considered of high quality. Furthermore, if it can strike dead branches or tough knots without being deflected or stopped, it is also superior. If the steel is hard without chipping, and sharp without dulling, it is of the highest excellence.

However, there is a specific technique for using the axe: one must turn the wrist and angle the blade upward when chopping. In this way, the branch stubs <term>zhicha<note>The knot or remainder of a branch after it has been cut.</note></term> will be smooth and the sap-veins will not <gloss>leak out. If the sap is preserved, the leaves will surely grow back thicker; thus, a farming proverb says: "The axe-head itself brings a double yield of leaves."</gloss> From this, we understand that while the benefits of harvesting and pruning depend on the quality of the tool, the true success lies in the skillful use of the axe.

## The Mulberry Hook

The **mulberry hook** is a tool used for harvesting mulberry leaves. Whenever a harvester needs to reach branches and leaves that are spreading far out or high up, drawing them closer for picking, they use this curved wood to replace the labor of using one's fingers and arms to pull <gloss>pronounced *ban*</gloss> and haul them down.

In ancient times, from the Empress and the wives of the high officials <note>Referring to the *shifu*, a rank of palace women in the Zhou dynasty who assisted the Empress in ceremonial duties.</note> down to the common women, all who personally tended to the silkworms used baskets and hooks to harvest the mulberry leaves. In the first year of the Shangyuan era of Emperor Suzong of the Tang Dynasty <note>Approximately 760 CE; Suzong was the tenth emperor of the Tang dynasty.</note>, thirteen "National Treasures" were obtained, among which was a "mulberry harvesting hook." From this, we know that in antiquity, the harvesting of mulberry leaves always involved the use of such hooks.

However, customs in the North tend toward felling the mulberry branches <note>Cutting entire branches to bring the leaves to the ground.</note> with very little picking, whereas people in the South pick the leaves and rarely fell the branches. If a tree is felled year after year, the <term>shumai<note>Literally "tree-veins" or "pulse," referring to the internal circulation of sap and the tree's overall vitality.</note></term> will easily decline. Conversely, if leaves are picked from the same branches for a long time, the branches will develop many knots and become stunted. 

For those who wish to adapt their methods to the specific needs of the North or South—using picking or cutting as appropriate—the **mulberry axe** and the **mulberry hook** each have their specific applications. Therefore, both tools are presented here together so that neither method is neglected or favored over the other.

# The Mulberry Clamp
<note>original: *Sangjia*. A guide or clamping device used to hold mulberry leaves steady while they are chopped into fine strips for young silkworms.</note>

The **Mulberry Clamp** is a tool for grasping and holding mulberry leaves. One uses a wooden block as a base, upon which fork-like legs are set upright, standing about two to three feet high. A steel blade is aligned along the top. With the left hand, the worker bundles the leaves, and with the right hand, they press the knife down to cut them. This is the "small" version of the clamp.

In households that rear a vast number of silkworms <note>original: *can'e*; literally "silkworm moths," referring to large-scale production</note>, they use a long rafter or beam placed vertically against a wall. The space in the middle is about a foot wide, and it is packed tight with mulberry leaves to a height of up to ten feet. The worker then climbs a ladder and, with both feet braced against the wall for leverage, leans forward to press the leaves down. With both hands, they firmly press <gloss>the knife downward to perform the cutting. This is the "large" version of the mulberry clamp. In the southern regions, a cutting frame is used with a block placed beneath it. The leaves are accumulated and then processed all at once to maximize efficiency.</gloss>

# The Warping Frame
<term>Warping Frame: *Jingjia*. A large wooden structure used to arrange hundreds of individual silk threads in parallel order before they are mounted onto a loom.</term>

The **Warping Frame** is a device used for drawing out and organizing silk threads. 

To use it, one must first arrange the **silk reels** <note>original: *zhuo*; the bobbins described on the previous page.</note> at the base of the frame. On the upper part of the frame, horizontal bamboo poles are installed, set with a series of rings. These rings serve to guide the numerous thread ends, gathering them all together at the front of the frame at the **warping guide** <gloss>The character *pang* used here is synonymous with *jia*, a boat-shaped guide</gloss>.

Once this process is complete, the organized threads are handed over to the **loom and shuttle** <note>original: *jizhu*; a general term for the weaving apparatus itself</note> to begin the cloth-making process.

# The Weft
<term>Weft: *Wei*. In weaving, the weft (or woof) refers to the transverse threads drawn through the longitudinal warp threads. In ancient Chinese thought, *Jing* (Warp) and *Wei* (Weft) also metaphorically represented longitude and latitude, or the fundamental structure of the universe.</term>

<note>The following description is based on the accompanying illustration of the winding process.</note>

The preparation of the **weft** is a crucial step that follows the setting of the warp. While the warp threads are held under high tension on the loom, the weft must be wound onto smaller bobbins or pirns that can fit inside a handheld shuttle.

As seen in the illustration, a woman is seated at a **winding wheel** <note>original: *fangche*; a common household machine for spinning or winding</note>. # The Weft Wheel
<gloss>used for connecting silk threads</gloss>

The ancient dictionary *Fangyan* states that in the regions of Zhao and Wei <note>territories in northern China</note>, this device is called the **"Liluche"** <note>a term suggesting a mechanical pulley or winding wheel</note>. In the coastal regions of Eastern Qi and Haidai <note>modern-day Shandong province</note>, it is known as the **"Daozhi."** Today, it is also called the **"Wei Wheel"** <gloss>pronounced *zuo*</gloss>.

The text *Tongsuwen* <note>a collection of popular terms from the Han Dynasty</note> explains that the act of weaving fine silk is called *wei* <gloss>pronounced *sui*</gloss>. The component that receives the weft thread is called a **"chu"** <note>the spool or bobbin</note>.

The construction of the machine is as follows:
On its base frame, an upright pillar is erected to support a large wheel. Near the top of the wheel, an iron rod is used to pierce through the center of a slender tube <note>the spindle</note>. A driving rope is then looped around the circumference of the wheel and the tube. When the operator’s right hand turns the wheel, the tube rotates in synchronization with the wheel's motion. Simultaneously, the left hand guides the silk thread onto the tube. In this manner, the wound silk is prepared to serve as the **weft** <note>the horizontal thread in a loom</note> for the weaving process.

# The Weaving Loom

The **weaving loom** is the tool used for weaving silk. According to the *Lushi* <note>original: *Lushi* (路史), "History of the Routes," a Song Dynasty history by Luo Mi</note>, it is said that Leizu <note>original: *Xiling-shi* (西陵氏), also known as倮祖/嫘祖. She was the primary consort of the legendary Yellow Emperor and is traditionally deified as the inventor of sericulture.</note> was the first to labor at the cultivation of silkworms. 

When the moon reached the **Great Fire** <note>original: *Dahuo* (大火), the star Antares. Its appearance in the sky traditionally signaled the proper season to begin silkworm rearing.</note>, she would wash the silkworm seeds. Dressed in her ceremonial robes, the Empress personally tended to the mulberry leaves. She then presented the silk cocoons, and thus the merit of **textile production** <note>original: *zhiren* (織紝), specifically referring to the weaving of silk threads</note> was established. Following her example, weaving was expanded to provide the garments for suburban sacrifices and ancestral temples. 

The text *Fu Zi* <note>original: *Juanzi* (傳子), a work by the Jin Dynasty philosopher Fu Xuan</note> states: 

"In the looms of old, those with fifty **heddles** <note>original: *zong* (綜); the loops that hold warp threads</note> had fifty **treadles** <note>original: *nie* (躡); the foot pedals used to raise and lower the heddles</note>, and those with sixty heddles had sixty treadles." 

**Master Ma** <note>original: *Ma Sheng* (馬生), referring to Ma Jun, a famous mechanical engineer of the Three Kingdoms period (3rd century CE)</note> was the most renowned craftsman under heaven. He was concerned that these ancient designs wasted time and stifled ingenuity, so he modified them to use only twelve treadles. 

Today, in **women's needlework** <gloss>the character *hong* (紅) is pronounced *gong* (工) in this context</gloss>, those who weave silk fabrics use only one or two treadles. This has become even more simplified and essential. Every piece of clothing or covering that people wear upon their bodies originates from this device.
