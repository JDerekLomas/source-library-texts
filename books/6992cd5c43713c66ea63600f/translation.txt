# Collected Illustrations of the Three Talents

</insert>

<term>Collected Illustrations of the Three Talents</term> <note>original title: "Sancai Tuhui"</note> is a vast, foundational encyclopedia compiled by the scholar Wang Qi and his son Wang Siyi, first published in 1609 during the Ming Dynasty. The title refers to the <term>Three Talents</term> <note>original: "Sancai"</note>, a traditional philosophical concept representing the three essential components of the universe: Heaven, Earth, and Humanity. This work was intended to provide a comprehensive visual and textual record of all known things within these three realms, featuring thousands of illustrations ranging from celestial maps and geographical charts to depictions of foreign peoples and mythical creatures.

# Portrait of Emperor Wen of Han
<note>original: 漢文帝像 (Hàn Wéndì xiàng)</note>

<term>Emperor Wen of Han <gloss>literally "The Civil Emperor of Han"</gloss></term> <note>Born Liu Heng (r. 180–157 BCE), he was the fifth emperor of the Western Han dynasty. He is historically revered as one of China’s most benevolent and frugal rulers.</note>

This portrait is featured in the *Sancai Tuhui* <note>original: 三才圖會; an expansive Ming-dynasty encyclopedia whose title translates as "Illustrated Assembly of the Three Realms" (Heaven, Earth, and Humanity).</note> within the section dedicated to historical figures. 

Emperor Wen’s reign is remembered as a period of recovery and peace. Following the internal strife of the Lü Clan disturbance, his ascent marked a shift toward <term>Huang-Lao</term> <gloss>a school of thought combining Daoist philosophy and political art</gloss> governance, which emphasized non-interference and tax reduction. 

In this woodcut illustration, the Emperor is shown wearing a traditional <term>mianguan</term> <gloss>a flat-topped ceremonial crown with hanging beads</gloss> and flowing imperial robes. The Ming-era artist emphasizes a calm and dignified countenance, reflecting the historical reputation of a ruler who famously abolished "three generations" of collective punishment and personal corporal penalties, opting instead for mercy and social stability. This image serves as a moral exemplar for subsequent generations, representing the Confucian ideal of a ruler who governs through virtue rather than force.

## Portrait of Emperor Jing of Han
<note>original: 漢景帝像 (Hàn Jǐngdì xiàng)</note>

<term>Emperor Jing of Han <gloss>The "Exalted Bright Emperor"</gloss></term> <note>Born Liu Qi (r. 157–141 BCE), he was the sixth emperor of the Western Han dynasty. He is celebrated for maintaining the peaceful policies of his father, Emperor Wen.</note>

The <term>Han Dynasty</term> <gloss>202 BCE – 220 CE</gloss> reached a peak of domestic tranquility during the reign of this monarch. This woodcut portrait captures the Emperor in formal imperial regalia, consistent with the iconographic style of the Ming dynasty's *Sancai Tuhui* encyclopedia.

He is shown wearing the <term>mianguan</term> <gloss>a ceremonial flat-topped crown</gloss> featuring strings of beads that traditionally hung before the eyes to remind the ruler to remain focused on virtue and avoid being distracted by minor faults in others. His robes are adorned with the symbolic patterns of a high sovereign.

Historically, Emperor Jing is noted for his adherence to <term>Huang-Lao</term> <gloss>a Daoist-influenced school of political thought</gloss> philosophy, which advocated for "governance through non-action" (*wu wei*). Under his rule:
*   Land taxes were reduced to a record low of one-thirtieth of the harvest.
*   The power of regional princes was curtailed, most notably after the <note>Rebellion of the Seven States in 154 BCE</note>, which strengthened the central government.
*   The population flourished, and the imperial granaries were said to be so full that the grain rotted before it could be eaten.

This depiction emphasizes the Emperor's maturity and dignity, portraying him with a mustache and long goatee, symbols of wisdom and authority in the traditional Chinese visual canon. He stands as the bridge between the founding era of the Han and the later expansionist era of his son, the famous Emperor Wu.

<summary>This page presents a woodcut portrait and biographical context for Emperor Jing of Han, highlighting his role in the "Rule of Wen and Jing" golden age.</summary>

# Portrait of Emperor Wu of Han
<note>original: 漢武帝像 (Hàn Wǔdì xiàng)</note>

<term>Emperor Wu of Han <gloss>The "Martial Emperor"</gloss></term> <note>Born Liu Che (r. 141–87 BCE), he was the seventh emperor of the Han dynasty. His fifty-four-year reign was one of the longest and most consequential in Chinese history.</note>

This portrait from the <term>Sancai Tuhui</term> <gloss>Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms</gloss> depicts the monarch who transformed the <term>Han Dynasty</term> <note>202 BCE – 220 CE</note> into a vast empire. Unlike the "non-action" approach of his predecessors, Emperor Wu was a man of immense energy and ambition.

He is depicted wearing the traditional imperial <term>mianguan</term> <gloss>a ceremonial crown with a flat board and hanging beads</gloss>. This attire signifies his role as the "Son of Heaven," the mediator between the cosmic and earthly realms. The woodcut captures the Emperor with a full beard and a stern, commanding presence, reflecting his historical reputation for strength and decisiveness.

Key aspects of his reign include:
*   **Ideological Shift:** He officially adopted <term>Confucianism</term> <gloss>a system of ethical and social philosophy</gloss> as the foundation of the imperial state, establishing a civil service system based on scholarly merit.
*   **Military Expansion:** He launched massive campaigns against the <note>Xiongnu nomads of the north</note>, significantly expanding China's borders into Central Asia, the Korean peninsula, and Northern Vietnam.
*   **The Silk Road:** His efforts to find allies in the West led to the travels of <note>Zhang Qian</note>, which effectively opened the Silk Road, linking China with the Mediterranean world for trade and cultural exchange.
*   **State Monopolies:** To fund his military endeavors, he established state monopolies on essential goods like salt and iron, fundamentally altering the Chinese economy.

While his reign brought great prestige and expanded the empire to new heights, the constant warfare and high taxes placed a significant burden on the populace toward the end of his life, leading to his famous "Repenting Edict of Luntai," where he expressed regret for his earlier military excesses.

This page highlights his transition from the era of stability into an era of imperial growth and the opening of the Silk Road.</summary>

# Portrait of Emperor Xuan of Han
<note>original: 漢宣帝像 (Hàn Xuāndì xiàng)</note>

<term>Emperor Xuan of Han <gloss>The "Proclaiming Emperor"</gloss></term> <note>Born Liu Bingyi, later renamed Liu Xun (r. 74–48 BCE), he was the tenth emperor of the Western Han dynasty. His life is one of the most dramatic "rags-to-riches" stories in Chinese imperial history.</note>

This portrait, sourced from the <term>Sancai Tuhui</term> <gloss>Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms</gloss>, depicts a ruler whose reign is often considered the peak of the <term>Han Dynasty</term> <note>202 BCE – 220 CE</note> in terms of economic prosperity and military power. Because his grandfather (the crown prince) was purged, Emperor Xuan grew up as a commoner and even spent time in prison as a child, an experience that gave him a deep understanding of the hardships faced by ordinary citizens.

The woodblock <term>portrait</term> <gloss>huàxiàng</gloss> shows the <term>Emperor</term> <gloss>huángdì</gloss> in full ceremonial regalia. He wears the traditional <term>mianguan</term> <gloss>the beaded imperial crown</gloss>, a symbol of his divine authority and his role in maintaining the cosmic order. The artist portrays him with a dignified expression, reflecting his historical reputation as a pragmatic and diligent administrator.

Key aspects of his reign include:
*   **The Han Restoration:** He is credited with reviving the dynasty after the excesses and internal strife of the late reign of Emperor Wu. He reduced taxes and emphasized the selection of officials based on their practical abilities and integrity.
*   **Legal and Judicial Reform:** Having personally experienced the cruelty of the prison system, he reformed the laws to be more lenient and appointed "circuit officials" to investigate judicial abuses.
*   **Foreign Relations:** Under his rule, the <note>Xiongnu confederation</note> finally split and surrendered, acknowledging the Han as their overlords, which brought lasting peace to the northern frontiers and the Silk Road.

<summary>This page features a portrait of Emperor Xuan of Han, a ruler who rose from commoner status to lead the Han Dynasty to its height of administrative and economic success.</summary>

# Portrait of Emperor Guangwu of Han
<note>original: 漢光武像 (Hàn Guāngwǔ xiàng)</note>

<term>Emperor Guangwu of Han <gloss>The "Radiant and Martial" Emperor</gloss></term> <note>Born Liu Xiu (r. 25–57 CE), he was a distant descendant of the Han imperial line. He led the restoration of the Han Dynasty, establishing what historians call the Eastern Han, after the brief and chaotic Xin Dynasty.</note>

This woodblock <term>portrait</term> <gloss>xiàoxiàng</gloss> captures the likeness of <term>Liu Xiu</term> <note>the personal name of Emperor Guangwu</note> in full imperial splendor. Having spent his early years as a farmer and student of the classics, Guangwu's rise to power is often cited as a return to the legitimate and virtuous rule of the Han ancestors.

The <term>Emperor</term> <gloss>huángdì</gloss> is depicted wearing the <term>mianguan</term> <gloss>beaded ceremonial crown</gloss>. This crown, with its distinctive flat board and hanging strings of jade beads, symbolized the emperor’s role as the pivot between Heaven and Earth. The artist emphasizes his calm and resolute demeanor, which historically reflects his reputation for being a "civilian-minded" military leader who preferred diplomacy and efficient administration over continued warfare.

Key aspects of his legacy:
*   **The Restoration of the Lineage:** He successfully reunited the fractured empire and re-established the Han social order, moving the capital to Luoyang.
*   **Humane Governance:** Influenced by his Confucian education, he was known for his thriftiness, his release of slaves, and his efforts to lighten the tax burden on the peasantry.
*   **Scholarship:** His reign saw a great revival in classical studies, as he personally favored the company of scholars and encouraged the establishment of the Imperial Academy.

# Portrait of Emperor Ming of Han
<note>original: 漢明帝像 (Hàn Míngdì xiàng)</note>

<term>Emperor Ming of Han <gloss>The "Bright" or "Illustrious" Emperor</gloss></term> <note>Born Liu Zhuang (r. 57–75 CE), he was the second emperor of the Eastern Han Dynasty. He is historically regarded as a diligent and capable ruler who maintained the stability established by his father, Emperor Guangwu.</note>

This woodblock <term>portrait</term> <gloss>xiàng</gloss> illustrates Emperor Ming in traditional imperial attire, including the ceremonial <term>mianguan</term> <gloss>beaded crown</gloss>. His reign is characterized by a strict adherence to law and a deep devotion to classical learning. The artist depicts him with a composed and authoritative air, reflective of his reputation for being a hands-on administrator who personally oversaw the functioning of government departments.

Under his rule, the Han Empire saw significant expansion and intellectual growth. He is perhaps most famous in Chinese history for his "Dream of the Golden Man," which is traditionally credited with the official introduction of <term>Buddhism</term> <gloss>Fójiào</gloss> to China.

Key aspects of his legacy:
*   **The Advent of Buddhism:** Legend states that after dreaming of a flying golden deity, the Emperor sent envoys to the West. They returned with the *Sutra of Forty-two Chapters* and two Indian monks, leading to the establishment of the White Horse Temple in the capital, Luoyang.
*   **Expansion into the West:** He dispatched the famous general Ban Chao to the "Western Regions" (Central Asia), successfully re-establishing Chinese control over the Silk Road trade routes.
*   **Confucian Piety:** Emperor Ming was a staunch supporter of Confucianism. He notably performed the <term>Biyong</term> <gloss>Imperial Academy</gloss> rituals, where he personally lectured on the classics to thousands of scholars and officials, emphasizing the moral foundations of the state.
*   **Internal Stability:** By maintaining low taxes and continuing his father's policies of irrigation and agricultural reform, he ensured that the "Restoration" of the Han remained prosperous and secure.

### The Western Jin Dynasty

The **Western Jin** began in the *Yiyou* year <note>265 AD</note> when **Emperor Wu** <note>Sima Yan</note> usurped the throne from the Wei dynasty and established his own rule. It ended in the *Bingzi* year <note>316 AD</note> with **Emperor Min**. Across four emperors, the dynasty lasted a total of fifty-two years before it was destroyed by **Liu Cong** of the "Pseudo-Han" <note>originally called Han Zhao, one of the states founded by non-Han peoples</note>, representing the **Five Barbarians** <term>Wu Hu</term> <note>a collective term for various non-Han nomadic tribes who settled in northern China</note>.

The family's ancestor was **Sima Yi**, a man from Wen County in Henei. Their lineage traces back to **Zhongli**, the son of **Gaoyang** <note>the legendary Emperor Zhuanxu</note>. Zhongli served as the "Fire Official" <term>Zhurong</term> under the **Ministry of Summer**. By the time of the Zhou dynasty, the Ministry of Summer was designated as the **Sima** <term>Minister of War</term>, and the family adopted this title as their surname. During the contention between the states of Chu and Han, they were descendants of **Sima Ang**.

**Cao Cao** <note>the powerful warlord of the late Han</note> recruited **Sima Yi** to serve as a Literary Assistant to the Chancellor. During the reign of **Emperor Wen of Wei** <note>Cao Pi</note>, Yi served as the General Who Sustains the Army and Manager of the Imperial Secretariat, receiving the late Emperor's deathbed decree to assist in governing the state.

Under **Emperor Ming**, he was promoted to Grand General. During the reign of the **Prince of Qi** <note>Cao Fang</note>, he was elevated to Grand Marshal and Chancellor, and was granted the **Nine Bestowments** <term>Jiu Xi</term> <note>a set of nine ceremonial gifts bestowed by the Emperor upon high officials to signal they had achieved the highest possible status below the throne itself</note>. 

Upon his death, his son **Sima Shi** continued as Grand General and Manager of the Imperial Secretariat; he deposed the Prince of Qi and enthroned the **Duke of Gaoguixiang**. When Shi died, his younger brother **Sima Zhao** inherited his positions as Grand General and Manager of the Imperial Secretariat. Zhao assassinated the Duke of Gaoguixiang and enthroned the **Prince of Chenliu**. He was promoted to Chancellor of State, enfeoffed as the **Duke of Jin**, granted the Nine Bestowments, and oversaw all administrative affairs of the realm. He eventually advanced to the rank of King. After his death, his son **Sima Yan** succeeded him as the Chancellor of State and **King of Jin**.

The ruler was styled the "Youngest Son." He was Murong De, the younger brother of Murong Chui. <gloss>Murong De</gloss>. <note>original: "少子" (shaozi). Murong De was the last surviving son of Murong Huang to rule.</note> The state of Northern Yan was later destroyed by Feng Ba. The King of Fanyang, Murong De, along with <unclear>Du Cheng</unclear> and Si Deng, fled in the fifth month. He subsequently declared himself the King of Yan. He occupied Guanggu in the Qing-Qi region <note>Modern Shandong province</note> and ascended the imperial throne. The state began in the second year of the Long’an era of the Eastern Jin (the year of Wuxu) and ended in the sixth year of the Yixi era.

## Northern Yan
The state lasted through two rulers over eleven years. <note>This likely refers to the reign of the Feng family specifically.</note> Feng Ba was a native of Xindu in Changle. He and Gao Yun killed Murong Xi <note>The last emperor of Later Yan</note>. Liu Yu eventually destroyed the state. In the year of Gengxu, they promoted Gao Yun as the leader. When Yun was assassinated, the masses promoted Feng Ba, who then ascended the throne as the <term>Heavenly King</term> <note>A title used by some 16 Kingdoms rulers that claimed imperial authority without using the specific title of "Emperor"</note>. The state was named Yan. It occupied Changli, which is modern-day Pingzhou. The state began in the fifth year of the Yixi era of the Eastern Jin (the year of Jichou) and ended in the thirteenth year of the Yuanjia era of the [Liu] Song dynasty (the year of Bingzi). Over two rulers and twenty-eight years, it lasted until Emperor Taiwu of the Northern Wei destroyed it.

## Former Qin
The founder was Fu Jian <note>original: 苻健. Not to be confused with his nephew, the more famous Fu Jian 苻堅</note>. He was of the Di people from Lüeyang. His family had been chieftains of the Western Rong <note>A general term for non-Han tribes to the west</note> for generations. His father, Fu Hong, occupied Lüeyang during the chaos of the Yongjia era in the Jin dynasty. Later, during the era of Shi Hu <note>ruler of Later Zhao</note>, he held ambitions for the Central Plains. He surrendered to Shi Hu and was granted the surname Fu. <note>original: 苻 (Fu). They originally used the surname 蒲 (Pu)</note>. He styled himself the Great Chanyu and King of the Three Qins. The territory was eventually taken by the Yan and Di peoples. Fu Jian then officially changed the family name to Fu, entered the Pass <note>The Guanzhong region</note>, established his capital at Chang'an, and took the titles of Heavenly King and Emperor. The state began in the sixth year of the Yonghe era of the Eastern Jin (the year of Gengxu). It lasted through seven rulers over forty-five years, ending in the first year of the Yixi era (the year of Jiawu). Yao Xing of the Later Qin eventually destroyed it.

## Later Qin
The founder was Yao Chang, a Qiang tribesman from Chiting. His family had been Qiang chieftains for generations. He served Fu Jian <note>The later, famous Fu Jian of Former Qin</note> as the <term>General of the Dragon Soaring</term> <note>Longxiang Jiangjun; a prestigious military title</note>. He later killed Fu Jian and declared himself the King of Qin, occupying Chang'an. The state began in the ninth year of the Taiyuan era of the Eastern Jin (the year of Jiashen) and ended in the thirteenth year of the Yixi era (the year of Dingsi). It lasted thirty-three years over three rulers. In the fourteenth year, Liu Yu of the Jin dynasty destroyed it.

## Western Qin
The founder was Qifu Guoren, a Xianbei tribesman from Longxi. His father, Sifan, had surrendered to Fu Jian, who then ordered him to garrison Yongshichuan.

# The Yuan Wei Dynasty
## <note>original: 元魏; also known as the Northern Wei. It was founded by the Tuoba clan of the Xianbei people. It is called "Yuan Wei" because the imperial family later changed their surname from Tuoba to Yuan.</note>

The Yuan Wei descended from Changyi, the youngest son of the Yellow Emperor, who was enfeoffed in the northern lands. There, a kingdom was established. The lineage rose to prominence under Jifen, who fathered Liwei. Liwei’s grandson, Yilu, was granted the title Prince of Dai by the Jin Dynasty. The rule passed down to his grandson Shiyijian, whose territory was eventually annexed by Fu Jian <note>leader of the Former Qin dynasty</note>. Shiyijian’s grandson, Tuoba Gui, re-established himself as the Prince of Dai. He moved the capital to Shengle in Dingxiang and changed the dynastic name to Wei. Later, he moved the capital to Pingcheng and ascended to the imperial throne, ruling under the "Earth Virtue." 

Emperor Xiaowen later changed the imperial surname to Yuan and moved the capital to Luoyang. He decreed that the dynasty would henceforth rule under the "Water Virtue," following the "Metal Virtue" of the Western Jin. The dynasty began with Emperor Daowu’s accession in the year of the Fire Dog <note>386 AD</note> and ended when Emperor Xiaowu abandoned the capital and fled west to Chang’an in the year of the Wood Tiger <note>534 AD</note>. In total, there were thirteen rulers over a period of one hundred and forty-nine years before the state split into the Eastern and Western Wei.

**Emperor Daowu** <gloss>His surname was Tuoba and his name was Gui. He was the grandson of Shiyijian and ruled by the Earth Virtue.</gloss>
<gloss>He was murdered by his son, Shao, the Prince of Qinghe. His son, Si, then ascended the throne. He reigned for twenty-eight years and lived to the age of thirty-nine. He used four era names: **Ascending the State** (Dengguo), **Imperial Beginning** (Huangshi), **Heavenly Rise** (Tianxing), and **Heavenly Gift** (Tianci).</gloss>

**Emperor Mingyuan** <gloss>His name was Si and he was the eldest son of Emperor Daowu. He reigned for fifteen years and lived to the age of thirty-two. He used three era names: **Everlasting Prosperity** (Yongxing), **Divine Omen** (Shenrui), and **Great Continuity** (Taichang).</gloss>

The Tang ruling family, surnamed Li, were people of Chengji in Longxi <note>modern-day Xizhou</note>. Their ancestors were the descendants of the mythical emperor Zhuanxu and the progeny of Li Hao, Prince Wuzhao of the Western Liang. Following the "Fire" virtue of the Sui dynasty, they rose to power through the "Earth" virtue <note>referring to the Five Phases theory of dynastic succession</note> and established their capital at Chang’an. The dynasty began in the <term>Wuyin</term> year <note>618 CE</note> when the High Progenitor usurped the Sui throne, and ended in the <term>Dingmao</term> year <note>907 CE</note> with Emperor Ai. Including the reign of Empress Wu, there were twenty emperors in total, spanning two hundred and ninety
## years, until Zhu Wen usurped the throne and destroyed the dynasty.

# Gaozu
<gloss>His personal name was Yuan. He avoided the use of the word "Tiger" <note>original: 避虎 — this refers to the naming taboo for his grandfather, Li Hu; during this time, the word for tiger was often replaced with other terms</note>. In the Northern Zhou dynasty, he was enfeoffed as the Duke of the State of Tang. His father, Li Bing, and Yuan himself held the hereditary title of Duke of Tang. The Sui dynasty appointed Yuan as the garrison commander of Taiyuan. While Emperor Yang of Sui was on a tour of the south, Yuan and his son Shimin raised an army and entered Chang’an. They initially welcomed and enthroned the Prince of Dai as emperor, while Yuan was promoted to the rank of Prince of Tang. He eventually usurped the Sui throne and established himself as Emperor. He later abdicated the throne to the Crown Prince and took the title <term>Taishang Huang</term> <note>Retired Emperor</note>. He reigned for nine years and lived to the age of seventy. He used one era name: Wude, which lasted nine years.</gloss>

## Taizong
<gloss>His personal name was Shimin. He was the second son of Gaozu and was originally enfeoffed as the Prince of Qin. He killed the Crown Prince, Li Jiancheng <note>during the Xuanwu Gate Incident</note>, and subsequently became the Crown Prince and then the Emperor. He reigned for twenty-three years and lived to the age of fifty-three. He used one era name: Zhenguan, which lasted twenty-three years.</gloss>

# Gaozong
<gloss>His personal name was Zhi. He was the ninth son of Taizong. He reigned for thirty-four years and lived to the age of fifty-six. He changed his era name fourteen times: Yonghui (6 years), Xianqing (5 years), Longshuo (3 years), Linde (1 year), Qianfeng (2 years), Zongzhang (2 years), Xianheng (3 years), Shangyuan (2 years), Yifeng (3 years), Tiaolu (1 year), Yonglong (1 year), Kaiyao (1 year), Yongchun (1 year), and Hongdao (1 year).</gloss>

## Empress Wu
<gloss>Her personal name was Zhao. She was the daughter of Wu Shiyue and was originally a <term>Cairen</term> <note>original: 才<unclear>人</unclear> — a "Talented Lady," a mid-ranking palace consort</note> of Taizong.</gloss>

The Empress <unclear>Wu</unclear> was established as Empress by Gaozong. After Gaozong passed away, she presided over the court and issued edicts. She deposed her son, Zhongzong, making him the Prince of Luling. She resided in the inner palace and favored men such as Wu Sansi <note>original: 武三思; though actually her nephew, the text associates him with her inner circle</note>. Zhang Yi-zhi and others also gained her favor. She then used the "Great Cloud" <note>referring to the *Dayun Jing* or Great Cloud Sutra, used to justify a female ruler</note> to bolster her position. While Wu Sansi and the officials offered titles, she was called the "Holy Mother and Divine Emperor." She changed the dynastic name to **Zhou** and established the "Zi" month as the start of the new year <note>a calendar reform shifting the start of the year</note>. It is said she committed many errors. Her reign lasted through the transition from Tang to Zhou and back, until the Shenlong restoration. She held power for twenty-one years and lived to the age of eighty-one. Her era names were changed ten times: 

**Tiance Jinlun** <note>Heavenly Appointment of the Golden Wheel</note>, **Shengshen** <note>Holy Spirit</note>, **Shengchang** (1 year), **Zaochu**, **Tianshou** (2 years), **Ruyi**, **Changshou** (2 years), **Yanzai**, **Tiance Wansui** (1 year), **Wansui Dengfeng**, **Qilin Tongtian**, **Shengong** (1 year), **Shengli** (1 year), **Jiushi** (1 year), **Dazu**, and **Chang'an** (4 years).

### Zhongzong
<gloss>Son of Gaozong</gloss>

He was the seventh son, born in the <term>Jiashen</term> year. Empress Wu deposed him, making him the Prince of Luling. He was restored to the throne in the <term>Yisi</term> year <note>705 CE</note>. He reigned for twenty years <note>including his time in exile</note> until he was poisoned by his wife, Empress Wei, and his daughter, Princess Anle. He lived to the age of fifty. He had two era names: <insert>Shenlong (2 years) and Jinglong (2 years)</insert>.

### Ruizong

His name was Dan, and he was the second son of Gaozong. He was initially enfeoffed as a Prince. After Li Longji <note>the future Emperor Xuanzong</note> executed the Wei clan to settle the internal crisis, Dan was supported to take the throne. He reigned for three years before abdicating to the Crown Prince, Longji. He lived to the age of fifty-five. He had four era names: <insert>Jingyun (2 years), Taiji, which changed to Yanhe</insert>, and then **Xiantian**. In the third month of that year, he passed the throne to his third son in the seventh month.

### Xuanzong

His name was Longji. 
As the second son <note>historically the third son</note>, he executed Empress Wei, settled the internal crisis, and was established as the Crown Prince. He accepted the abdication of the throne. Later, when An Lushan rebelled, he fled to Shu <note>modern-day Sichuan</note> and passed the throne to the Crown Prince, taking the title of Retired Emperor. He reigned for forty-four years and lived to the age of seventy-eight. He had three era names: **Xiantian** (upon ascending), **Kaiyuan** (29 years), and **Tianbao** (14 years).

### Suzong

His name was Heng, the seventh son of Xuanzong. Following his father's command to suppress the rebels, he declared himself Emperor at Lingwu. He reigned for seven years.
