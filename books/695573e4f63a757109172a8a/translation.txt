# Introduction.

In the history of music, the name of Guido of Arezzo has attained such fame that it must be of the greatest interest for every musician to become more closely acquainted with his writings. Alongside the "Letter of Guido to the monk Michael concerning an unknown chant" <note>original: "Epistola Guidonis Michaeli monacho de ignoto cantu directa"</note>—which offers a particular interest insofar as it sheds the most light on Guido’s person and life circumstances apart from its musical content—the most significant of Guido’s writings is his *Micrologus*. Of his remaining works, the treatises "Guido's Rhythmic Rules of Music, presented in the prologue to his Antiphonary" <note>original: "Musicae Guidonis regulae rhythmicae in antiphonarii sui prologum prolatae"</note> and "Other Rules of Guido concerning unknown chant, likewise presented in the prologue to his Antiphonary" <note>original: "Aliae Guidonis regulae de ignoto cantu identidem in antiphonarii sui prologum prolatae"</note> merely reproduce the content of the *Micrologus* in a different form. Meanwhile, regarding the essays "Guido's Corrective Treatise on many errors which occur in many places in Gregorian chant" <note>original: "Tractatus Guidonis correctorius multorum errorum, qui fiunt in cantu gregoriano in multis locis"</note> and "How music proceeds from arithmetic" <note>original: "Quomodo de arithmetica procedit musica"</note>, the latter likely does not originate from Guido at all, while the former is frequently provided with additions from a later period. For us, however, gaining knowledge specifically of the *Micrologus* holds a much higher interest, as Guido himself, at the conclusion of the aforementioned Letter, directs his students toward the study of it as a more detailed manual on the theory of music <term>music theory: the study of the practices and possibilities of music</term>; we therefore find in it a reliable explanation of the...

The state of the <term>musical art</term> <note>original: "Tonkunst"; refers to the science and practice of music</note> in the 11th century has been preserved by a man who was regarded by his contemporaries as the most prominent and significant expert and promoter of the musical arts. As such, he was as much honored and praised as he was, on the other hand, envied and persecuted. — For our translation, we have used the text as it was first published by Prince-Abbot Gerbert in his work: *Ecclesiastical Writers on Sacred Music Above All* <note>original Latin: "Scriptores ecclesiastici de musica sacra potissimum"</note>. This was based on manuscripts from the library of his monastery, St. Blaise, compared with two manuscripts from St. Emmeram, one from Admont, and fragments from Ottobeuren and Monte Cassino. Through the kindness of our highly esteemed and dear friend, the Ecclesiastical Councilor Mr. R. Schlecht in Eichstätt, we have been enabled to add the variant readings from the Latin Codex 9921 (Ottobeuren), Codex 14,663 of the Munich Court and State Library, and Codex 14,965a and b in the footnotes. These variants contribute significantly in many places to the clarification of the meaning, and we therefore feel deeply indebted to the aforementioned gentleman.
