## AND ILLUSTRIOUS SENATORS
### OF THE REPUBLIC OF NUREMBERG

It is a well-known saying of Plato, most distinguished Fathers, that Republics will only be happy if either philosophers rule or kings philosophize. <note>original: "Philosophi regnent, vel reges philosophentur." This is a famous quote from Plato’s *Republic*.</note> For since the correct administration of the State <note>Latin: *Politia*</note> depends entirely on the perfect use of reason, and according to the opinion of Aristotle, the one who most excels in virtue and wisdom is, by the silent consensus of all nature, the most worthy of ruling over all. While this honor belongs by its very nature only to God and to the Man-Christ, the Best and Greatest, it is also shared by a certain participation with those whom He willed to be His vicars on earth, honoring them with the name of "Gods." <note>This refers to Psalm 82:6, "I said, 'You are gods,'" a common biblical justification for the authority of civil magistrates in the early modern period.</note>

Just as He has been accustomed to stir up, call, and instruct these men for right governance by a certain special destiny—as the examples of Moses, Joshua, Gideon, Jephthah, Samson, the Maccabees, and similar figures teach us—so too, by a more general method, that good Author of a good nature is accustomed to unfold and illuminate the rules of right living inscribed on the minds of all men. He does this by the efficacy of the Spirit in certain people born to command, who possess "golden souls" <note>A reference to Plato’s *Republic*, where he suggests that those fit to lead are endowed by nature with "gold," while others are made of silver or bronze.</note> in Plato’s view. He makes them participants in truth and virtue, as much as is divinely permitted, just as they share in His own essence. 

Indeed, there are two norms of virtue and truth: **Sacred Authority**, consisting of eternal reason, and **Profane Reason**, relying on mortal authority. The former, enjoying the unpretentious name of **Theology**, is like a most brilliant sun that can dispel and illuminate all the shadows of the human mind by itself. However, this is granted only to those who, through the singular kindness of the Divine Power, have received the revelation of the divine oracles—first outlined in the **Mosaic Law**, and subsequently explained by the **Gospel Law**. 

The latter norm, which men have called **Philosophy** in imitation of the wise God, gathers the remnants of the "human shipwreck" <note>A common theological metaphor for the state of human nature after the Fall—the idea being that while the "ship" of perfect human nature was wrecked by sin, some fragments (reason) still float on the surface.</note> and investigates the law of uncorrupted nature. While it understands and deplores the accidental foulness of man, it strives to reach the idea of native beauty, if not by action, then at least by contemplation. But even in that effort it is frustrated; while it recognizes what it lacks, it remains ignorant of the cause of the disease and sees no way to dispel it. Thus, it turns entirely to the good Author of nature—whose goodness envies no one—so that, as it owes the beginnings of its birth to Him, it may also humbly and devoutly seek from Him the aid for its restoration and preservation. # PREFACE

Socrates, judged by the oracle of Apollo to be the wisest of all mortals, established this principle when he taught that all things should be referred to that "highest good and beautiful," and defined philosophy as a "preparation for death." <note>original: "philosophiā mortis commentationem esse." This is a classic Platonic definition found in the *Phaedo*.</note> Indeed, he did not hesitate to assert that philosophy holds the primary place among all things we have received from the Gods. In this one thing, however, he was deceived: through his ignorance of Divine Authority (even if he perhaps silently suggested that this authority was placed beyond the reach of human gifts), he assigned the first rank to reason—which, in truth, ought only to be numbered among the secondary gifts.

Now, indeed, human reason itself—for divine reason completely dims and obscures the eyes of men, much like the sun's brilliance blinds owls—deduces universal theory and contemplation from the history and observation of individual things. Yet, such observation cannot be performed in a short time, nor by a single person. Therefore, to provide the "equipment" of reason, so to speak, the constant order of nature persuades us that experience must serve as a handmaid and midwife. Consequently, we must place the highest value on the authority of those who have found things through observed reason, established what they found, and taught what they established. For this reason, that admirable initiate and interpreter of nature, Aristotle—who asserted that nothing is in the intellect which was not first in the senses—pronounced that "the learner must believe." <term>τὸν μανθάνοντα πιστεύειν <note>original Greek: "ton manthanonta pisteuein." A core pedagogical principle in Aristotelian thought: a student must first trust the teacher's authority to eventually reach independent understanding.</note></term>

For although the freedom of reason and speech, beholden to no authority and devoted to truth alone, is becoming of a philosopher, such a state can only be attained by those few whom "impartial Jupiter has loved" <note>A reference to Virgil's *Aeneid*, describing those rare individuals capable of heroic or divine insight.</note> and who have surpassed all authority through the piercing clarity of a divine mind. Meanwhile, for the average person—who makes up the largest part of humanity—it is much more advisable to serve the opinions of their predecessors than to seize the tyranny of <term>vainglory <note>original Greek: "κενοδοξίας" (kenodoxias), meaning empty pride or conceit.</note></term> through a reckless zeal for liberty that repudiates the authority of the elders.

We might set aside the "Sarmanes" <note>original: "Germanas." Likely referring to the Shramanas, ancient Indian ascetic practitioners.</note> and Brahmins of the Indians, the priests of the Egyptians, the Chaldeans of the Babylonians, the *Tarabosteseos* <note>A term for the noble, hat-wearing class of the Scythians or Getae.</note> of the Scythians, and the Druids of the Germans and Gauls. Certainly, the two most famous schools of the ancient Greek philosophers—the Ionic school, stretching from Thales to Epicurus, and the Italic school, from Pythagoras all the way to Socrates—granted a great deal to authority. While they followed the tracks of reason, they frequently turned into the "territories of authority." This is more than proved by that famous Pythagorean saying: "He himself said it." <term>ἀυτὸς ἔφα <note>original Greek: "autos epha." Known in Latin as *ipse dixit*; it refers to an assertion accepted as true simply because a master said it.</note></term>

Socrates, indeed, when he had determined to restore a mutilated philosophy to its wholeness—investigating not only the obscurity of nature but also the corruption of morals and seeking a way to repair them—brought forth various "colonies" of later philosophers from himself, thanks to his elegant method of philosophizing and the holiness of his life. These skillful disciples strove to either understand more rightly or cultivate more diligently the natural seeds of virtue and truth they had gathered from him.

From this point, the sect of the Academic philosophers was established by **Plato**, and that of the **Cynics** by **Antisthenes**, which soon also produced the **Stoic** school from within itself. **Aristotle**, the disciple of **Plato**, led the **Peripatetic** colony into the Lyceum; a school by no means inferior to its "mother city" <note>Metropolis</note> due to the clarity of its order and method, which has continued in an unbroken succession for so many centuries. Meanwhile, **Speusippus** preserved the name and dignity of the Old Academy with his successors, while **Arcesilaus** introduced the Middle Academy, and **Lacydes** the New.

Later, after the constitution of the Athenian school was disturbed along with the fall of the empire, the Academy began to spread its branches before all other sects—first through all of Greece, and then even through Italy. Such was the kindness of the climate and the soil that these living roots grew into the widest forests. And after "captured Greece took captive her fierce conqueror," <note>original: "Græcia capta, ferū victorē cepit." A famous quote from the Roman poet Horace, referring to how Greek culture dominated Rome after the Roman military conquest of Greece.</note> as the saying goes, the harvest of **Platonic** philosophy was especially abundant in Rome. This was attested and celebrated wonderfully, not only through living tradition but also by the writings of most learned men.

This is best demonstrated by **Plotinus of Lycopolis**, whom we here bring forth onto a more refined and elegant literary stage. From the age of twenty-eight, when he first approached philosophy, he was a diligent pupil for eleven years under **Ammonius of Alexandria**, the greatest of philosophers, and a fellow student of **Origen** and **Erennius**. <note>The "Origen" mentioned here is the Neoplatonist philosopher, a contemporary of Plotinus, though early modern scholars often debated if this was the same person as the famous Christian Church Father.</note> It had long been agreed among them that they would not publish the <term>acroamatic <note>Oral teachings intended only for an inner circle of advanced students, rather than for the general public.</note></term> doctrines of their teacher, but would protect the authority of the school by communicating them only by word of mouth to legitimate listeners. 

But after **Erennius** first broke this faith, and then **Origen** followed suit—and also because the students of **Plotinus** (of whom he had many learned followers in Rome over twenty-six years) were following only a disorganized oral tradition without a written reading of the doctrines—**Plotinus** finally applied his mind to writing. This occurred when he was over forty years old, continuing until his sixty-sixth year, which was the second year of the Emperor **Claudius II**—a year that proved fatal to both. 

Since **Porphyry** has followed the details of his life diligently and at length, there is no reason for us to "do what has already been done." <note>original: "actū agamus."</note> But let us observe this one thing: **Plotinus** lived in those times when secular philosophy—provoked by the rivalry of the **Christian** religion, which was then emerging far and wide, and further compelled by the truth of its doctrines—could have most easily submitted to the yoke of Christ. This would have happened had it not been for the ambition and wickedness of those who practiced philosophy only as far as their beards and cloaks, <note>A reference to those who took on the outward appearance of a philosopher without the inward virtue.</note> and who, swollen with the reputation of their wisdom, thought it most disgraceful to obey those they considered unlearned and common. For while they strove to defend the authority of secular wisdom, they observed in the meantime that Christian piety relied not so much on the subtlety of arguments as on the holiness of character and the frequency of miracles. They did not hesitate to "stir up Hell itself," <note>original: "Acheronta mouere." A classical idiom meaning to resort to any means possible, even the most underworldly or forbidden, to achieve a goal.</note> that is, to call upon **Magic** for assistance under the pretext of human philosophy. Thus, after **Apollonius of Tyana** (whose wonderful illusions the most cunning <term>evil spirit <note>original: "cacodæmon."</note></term> used to mock the miracles of Christ the Best and Greatest)...

But since the more excellent a thing is, the worse its abuse tends to be, these things should have been carefully distinguished from one another, so that what was the fault of human morals was not attributed to the art itself. More rightly did those—among whom were Melito, Bishop of Sardis (a disciple of the philosopher Fronto), Pantaenus of Alexandria the Stoic, with his disciples Clement of Alexandria and Alexander, Bishop of Jerusalem, as well as Quadratus, Aristides of Athens, Justin Martyr, **Cyprian** of Carthage (who was a magician before becoming a bishop), and **Origen** the Egyptian, along with many others—who, having rejected philosophical vanity, forced philosophical truth to serve as a handmaid to revealed truth. Thus, they turned the otherwise forbidden use of "Egyptian gold" <note>A common metaphor in early Christian writing for taking the "wealth" of pagan learning and repurposing it for the worship of God, based on the Israelites taking gold from Egypt during the Exodus.</note> toward the worship of the true God. This was done with such success that **Julian the Apostate** <note>The Roman Emperor Julian (reigned 361–363 AD), who attempted to revive paganism and famously banned Christian teachers from using classical Greek texts.</note> thought the only way to uproot Christianity was to bar the godly from the literature of the heathens, because <note>original: "q," an abbreviation for "quia."</note> he realized that if they were instructed in these things, they would defend the truth with much greater probability and effectiveness, defeating the impious with their own weapons.

Indeed, although it seems absurd that men turned away from God and who are professed enemies of true piety could arrive at any knowledge of sincere (though profane) truth, yet great is the indulgence of that eternal Giver of all good things. ...are more easily passed down. Therefore, it was not without divine providence—which clearly wishes to wonderfully recall all people to itself according to their individual talents—that a certain pious philosophy was born long ago, appearing both among the Persians under **Zoroaster** <note>The legendary ancient Persian prophet, seen by Renaissance scholars as a source of primordial wisdom.</note> and among the Egyptians under **Mercury** <note>Referring to Hermes Trismegistus, the mythical Egyptian sage.</note>, consistent with itself in both places. It was then nurtured among the Thracians under **Orpheus** and **Aglaophemus**, and soon reached its youth under **Pythagoras** among the Greeks and Italians, until finally it was brought to perfection in Athens by the divine **Plato**. 

However, it was the ancient custom of these theologians to shroud divine mysteries in mathematical numbers and figures, as well as in poetic fables, so that they would not be easily accessible to just anyone. Finally, **Plotinus** stripped these theological veils; as **Porphyry** and **Proclus** testify, he was the first and only one to divinely penetrate the secrets of the ancients. Yet, because of his incredible brevity of speech, his abundance of thoughts, and the depth of his meaning, he requires not only a translation into another language but also commentaries. We have therefore worked on translating and explaining the earlier theologians found in Plato and Plotinus, so that as this theology comes to light, poets may cease to count the sacred deeds and mysteries of piety among their impious fables. 

Furthermore, many **Peripatetics** <note>Followers of Aristotle’s philosophy.</note>—that is, nearly all philosophers—should be warned that they must not think of our common religion as if it were a collection of old wives' tales. Indeed, almost the entire world is now occupied by the Peripatetics and divided into two main sects: the **Alexandrian** and the **Averroistic**. <note>The Alexandrians followed Alexander of Aphrodisias, believing the soul was mortal; the Averroists followed the Muslim philosopher Averroes, believing there was only one "intellect" shared by all humans. Both views were seen as threats to Christian doctrine on the individual soul.</note> The former think our intellect is mortal, while the latter contend it is one single entity for all. Both equally destroy all religion at its foundation, especially since they seem to deny divine providence regarding human affairs; in both cases, they have even abandoned their own teacher, Aristotle. Today, few interpret his mind with that same piety shown by the sublime **Pico**, our fellow Platonist <note>original: "Picum Complatonicū," referring to Giovanni Pico della Mirandola.</note>, or as **Theophrastus**, **Themistius**, **Porphyry**, **Simplicius**, **Avicenna**, and recently **Plethon** once did.

If anyone thinks that such widespread impiety, protected by such sharp intellects, can be erased among men solely through a simple preaching of faith, he is straying far from the truth; he will undoubtedly be proven wrong by the reality of the situation. There is a need here for a much greater power: either through divine miracles appearing everywhere, or at least through a certain **philosophical religion** <note>Ficino's concept of a philosophy that leads the mind to religious truth through reason.</note> that will eventually persuade philosophers, who would listen to it more willingly. It pleases divine providence in these times to confirm the nature of its religion through philosophical authority and reason, until at a set time, it may confirm the true form of religion among all nations with manifest miracles, as it once did. 

Led therefore by divine providence, we have interpreted the divine Plato and the great Plotinus. We sent Plato to you long ago, so that in him **Cosimo** <note>Cosimo de' Medici, Lorenzo's grandfather and Ficino’s first patron.</note> might live again, in whom he did indeed live again, and, reborn, grew according to our wishes and now happily flourishes in his maturity. As for Plotinus, although we are now rightfully sending him to you, we do not so much "send" him as watch him hurrying toward your house of his own accord and with great eagerness. It is as if he were drawn from Plato himself, like iron by a **Herculean stone** <note>original: "lapide Herculeo," a classical term for a magnet.</note>, so that he may live most happily alongside his Plato in your care, O magnanimous **Lorenzo**, the unique patron of the learned. 

Listen happily, therefore, to Plotinus as he speaks with Plato in your home about all the mysteries of philosophy. But before you listen to him, you must listen to **Porphyry**, his pious disciple, who narrates the life, character, and deeds of his master both briefly and most truly. Our **Angelo Poliziano**, your protégé and a man of the sharpest judgment, considers this history to be as oratorical as it is philosophical; for that reason, it will please you greatly. Finally, not only listen happily, but live most happily. And as much as you love us, my dearest Lorenzo, I pray that you love our **Valori**—I mean **Filippo**, an excellent man, a student of Platonic wisdom, and one who loves you ardently.
