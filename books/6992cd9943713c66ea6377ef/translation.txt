The **Hall of Conjunction** was established by the **Yellow Emperor**. According to *Master Su’s Exposition*, the word "palace" <note>original: 宮 (gong)</note> signifies being "in the center," referring to its position at the heart of the capital city. Additionally, a palace is defined by its "squareness," built with gate-towers and battlements to ensure a perfectly rectangular and upright structure.

The *Comprehensive Meaning of Customs* <note>original: 風俗通 (Fengsu Tong), a Han dynasty text on social norms and linguistics</note> notes that "palace" and "house" were originally one and the same. Since the Qin and Han dynasties, however, the term was adopted as a formal title for the most honorable personages <note>the Emperors</note>, and those of lower status were thereafter required to avoid using it to describe their own dwellings. 

The *Classic of Divine Marvels* mentions a "Palace of Heavenly Grace." The text *Master Guan* records that the **Yellow Emperor** possessed the **Hall of Conjunction**, the legendary King Yao had the "Secondary Palace," the Zhou dynasty had the "Azure Palace," the Qin dynasty had the "Palace of Ten Thousand Years," and the Han dynasty had the **Weiyang Palace**, among others.

The *Record of Rites* states that for those of the rank of <term>Commissioned Officer</term> <note>命士 (mingshi), the lowest rank of the aristocracy</note> and above, fathers and sons resided in separate "palaces." It also says that a scholar might have a "one-acre palace" or a "room with walls of one *du*" <note>A <term>du</term> is an ancient unit of height, roughly ten feet; this refers to a modest, simple dwelling</note>. This shows that in ancient times, the term "palace" was a designation shared by both officials and commoners alike.

The **Front Hall** <note>original: 前殿 (Qiandian)</note> was first constructed by **Qin Shi Huang** <note>The First Emperor of China, r. 221–210 BCE</note>; this is the origin of the term "Hall" <note>original: 殿 (dian)</note> as a specific architectural designation. The *Extended Meanings* <note>original: 演義 (Yanyi), likely referring to a philological commentary</note> states: A "Hall" <term>dian <gloss>to bring up the rear</gloss></term> is so named because it is supported and surrounded by a multitude of buildings, much like the rear guard <note>original: 殿 (dian)</note> that protects an army.

**Zhi Yu** <note>A scholar and official of the Jin Dynasty, c. 250–311 CE</note>, in his *Essential Annotations on Deciding Meanings*, states:
"A **Hall** <note>dian</note> is a structure that possesses both stairs and a raised imperial dais. A **Court** <note>original: 堂 (tang)</note> possesses stairs but lacks the raised dais."

The *Spring and Autumn Annals* <note>one of the Five Classics of Chinese literature</note> refers to this as the **Grand Chamber** <note>original: 路寢 (luqin), the primary living and working quarters of a ruler</note>. Both the *Book of Rites* and the *Comprehensive Discussions in the White Tiger Hall* <note>a record of a major 1st-century CE scholarly conference</note> call it the **Sovereign’s Court**.

In the *Book of Han*, it is recorded that **Huang Ba** <note>An official famous for his administrative skill, d. 51 BCE</note> issued an order to the officials from the various commanderies and kingdoms. He decreed that those who had nominated filial sons, devoted younger brothers, or virtuous women should be formed into a single group to be the first to ascend the **Hall**. **Yan Shigu** <note>A renowned 7th-century commentator on the Han histories</note> notes: "This refers to the building where the **Chancellor** <note>The highest-ranking civil official</note> sat."

Under the ancient system, any building that was high and imposing was commonly called a **Hall**; the term was not necessarily restricted to buildings located within the imperial palace.

### The Structure and Origin of the Hall

In ancient times, what was called a **Hall** <note>original: 堂 (Tang)</note> was defined by its layout: the area from the midpoint forward was left open and vacant, which was called the **Hall**; the area from the midpoint backward was enclosed and solid, which was called the **Chamber** <note>original: 室 (Shi)</note>.

The word "Hall" <note>original: 堂 (Tang)</note> is synonymous with "Facing" <note>original: 當 (Dang)</note>, referring to a building that directly faces the **sunlight** <term>Yang (陽): the bright, active principle; in architecture, this refers to the traditional south-facing orientation designed to catch the sun</term>. 

The <gloss>Sea King</gloss> <note>original: 海王 (Haiwang), a chapter within the *Guanzi*, an ancient philosophical and political text</note> states that "Hall" also means "Enlightenment" <note>original: 明 (Ming)</note>, signifying that it is the place where **ritual and righteousness** are made clear.

# Towers

The tradition of building towers <note>original: 樓 (lou). In Chinese architecture, this refers specifically to multi-storied buildings or pavilions elevated above the ground.</note> began with the Yellow Emperor <note>The legendary ancestor of the Chinese people, credited with numerous inventions and the establishment of civilization.</note>. According to the *Records of the Grand Historian* <note>original: 史記 (Shiji). The foundational text of Chinese history, completed around 94 BCE by Sima Qian.</note>, an alchemist <note>original: 方士 (fangshi). Specialists in occult arts, medicine, and the search for immortality.</note> told Emperor Wu of the Han Dynasty that the Yellow Emperor built "five cities and twelve towers" to await the arrival of divine beings. Inspired by this, the Emperor erected the Jinggan Tower <note>original: 井幹樓 (Jinggan Lou). A massive structure built around 115 BCE, named for its "well-frame" construction style involving crisscrossed logs.</note>.

Furthermore, the *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary/lexicon, dating back to the 3rd century BCE.</note> states: "A structure that is narrow, tall, and winding is called a *lou*." 

The *Shuowen Jiezi* <note>original: 說文 (Shuowen). An early 2nd-century dictionary that explains the components of Chinese characters.</note> defines it simply: "A *lou* is a multi-storied building." 

The *Shiming* <note>original: 釋名 (Shiming). A dictionary from the Han Dynasty that explains words through sound associations and etymology.</note> explains that a tower used for guarding over the vegetation in marshlands is also called a *lou*. It further notes that because a tower has doors, windows, and many other openings, it appears "looming and vast" <note>original: 僾然 (airan). This suggests a structure that is partially obscured by its height or the surrounding atmosphere, giving it a majestic, ethereal presence.</note>.

# Gate Towers

<term>Que <note>original: 闕. Pronounced "ch-weh." These are freestanding, paired towers that flank the entrances of imperial palaces, temples, and tombs. They served as watchtowers, status symbols, and places for posting official decrees.</note></term>

A **Gate Tower** is a lookout structure for a gateway. Because the center is left open or "deficient" <note>original: 闕 (*que*) is phonetically and semantically linked to the word 缺 (*que*), meaning a gap, vacancy, or deficiency.</note> to provide a path for travelers, it is called by this name.

It was here that the government would suspend official edicts and laws on the first day of the first month so that the common people could observe and follow them.

**Zheng Xuan** <note>the most influential commentator on the Confucian Classics, 127–200 CE</note> further stated that the Zhou dynasty had Yuxiang academies in each of the four suburbs. **Wang Su** <note>a rival scholar of the Three Kingdoms period who often disagreed with Zheng Xuan</note> argued that the Biyong was actually the **Mingtang** <note>Hall of Distinction, a central ritual space for the Emperor</note>. These matters are ultimately unverifiable. 

Generally speaking, for the Son of Heaven, the Lesser Learning was on the outside while the Great Learning was on the inside. This is because when the Son of Heaven selects scholars, they rise from the outside to the inside to eventually reach the imperial court. In the schools of the Feudal Lords, the Lesser Learning was on the inside and the Great Learning was on the outside. This is because the Feudal Lords select scholars to be promoted from the inside to the outside <note>to be sent beyond their local fiefs to serve the central authority</note>.
