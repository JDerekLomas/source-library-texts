# Preface.

The first edition of this volume has been both commended and criticized by the public. It was admitted to be a valuable compendium of literary curiosities in general, and especially of those pertaining to magic; however, it was simultaneously accused of being designed to foster superstition and thereby promote evil. This is a repetition of the charge previously made against the honorable Horst <note>Georg Conrad Horst (1767–1832), a German theologian and scholar of the occult</note>, the publisher of a library of magic.

In our enlightened age, the unprejudiced observer will see in the publication of such a work only what the author claims: a contribution to the study of literature and culture of no small merit. Furthermore, for the believer, the release of a cheap edition will be more useful than the formerly expensive productions on sorcery, which were only circulated in summarized forms and sold at extortionate prices. What other practical value the aforementioned edition may possess is not the question at hand. Let us not, therefore, underrate this branch of popular literature. These authors wrote according to a system which was—or at least seemed—clear to them. Illustrious persons in all ages and climates have not considered the labor required to fathom the mysteries of magic as labor spent in vain; and although they condemned the methods, they could not deny the possibility, or even the fact, that gifted men of inherent worth could accomplish such wonderful things.

Regarding the present edition, it can only be said that the so-called *Sixth and Seventh Books of Moses*, which have attracted popular faith for several centuries, are presented here in accordance with an old manuscript (the most legible among many). They are given word for word, divested only of the spelling errors <note>original: "orthographical errors"</note> that the best interests of literature demand be corrected—yet with unerring fidelity. The publisher guarantees that not one syllable has been added.
