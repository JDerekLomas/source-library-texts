# Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms

<note>original: "Sancai Tuhui" (三才圖會). This is the title of a monumental pictorial encyclopedia compiled by the scholar Wang Qi and his son Wang Siyi during the Ming Dynasty, first published in 1609. The work is famous for its vast collection of illustrations covering all aspects of knowledge known at the time.</note>

<term>Three Realms (Sancai): A traditional Chinese philosophical concept referring to the three components of the universe: Heaven, Earth, and Humanity. The encyclopedia is organized into these three overarching categories.</term>

<note>original: "Sheji Tan" (社稷壇). The "Soil and Grain" altars were among the most important ritual sites in China, symbolizing the state's foundation and its reliance on agriculture. Every administrative level, from the capital down to the county, was required to maintain one.</note>

<term>Bay (Jian): The standard unit of Chinese architectural space, defined by the interval between four pillars. A "three-bay" hall is a medium-sized structure, while a "one-bay" hall is a smaller, simpler shrine.</term>

<note>The "Altar of the First Farmer" (Xiannong Tan) was dedicated to Shennong, the mythical inventor of agriculture. Each year, the Emperor (or local officials) would perform a ritual plowing ceremony here to encourage the nation's farmers.</note>

<term>Gate Bolt (Guan): In this ritual context, "shaking the bolt" means to rattle the door handle or the locking mechanism. It is a gesture of politeness intended to notify those inside the hall of an arrival, preventing a sudden or startled encounter.</term>

# The Temple of the First Sage

The **Temple of the First Sage** <note>The "First Sage" is a primary title for Confucius (Kong Fuzi), the philosopher whose teachings formed the bedrock of Chinese social and political life.</note> is located two *li* <note>A traditional unit of distance; two *li* is approximately one kilometer.</note> west of Xianyuan Township in Yanzhou. To the west, it borders the site of the ancient city of Lu, sitting just over two hundred paces from the Master's old residence.

Directly facing the outer gate are the **Front Three Gates** <gloss>this gate holds the plaque personally inscribed by Emperor Renzong</gloss> <note>Emperor Renzong (r. 1022–1063) was a Song Dynasty monarch known for his devotion to Confucian scholarship.</note>. Behind the Three Gates is the **Library Tower** <gloss>where imperially bestowed books are stored</gloss>. Behind the Library Tower, along the Imperial Path, stand two pavilions to the east and west. To the east is the **Stele Pavilion for the Restoration of the Temple** by the current dynasty. To the west is the **Stele Pavilion for the Tang Dynasty’s Enfeoffment of Confucius as the Grand Preceptor** <note>original: *Taishi*. This refers to the high honorary rank granted to Confucius by the Tang emperors centuries after his death.</note>.

Next, within the gates of the palace courtyard, is the **Hall of Imperial Eulogy**. Beyond this lies the **Apricot Altar** <note>original: *Xingtan*. Tradition holds that this is the spot where Confucius taught his students under the shade of apricot trees.</note>. Behind the Apricot Altar is the **Main Hall of the First Sage** <gloss>the plaque for this hall was personally inscribed by Emperor Renzong; the transcription reads "Libo Hall," likely an error for "Proclaiming the Sage Hall"</gloss>.

Directly behind the Main Hall is the **Hall of the Lady of the State of Yun** <note>This shrine is dedicated to Confucius’s wife, Lady Qiguan.</note>. In the eastern wing of this rear palace is the **Hall of the Marquis of Sishui**, and in the western wing is the **Hall of the Marquis of Yishui** <note>These titles were posthumously granted to Confucius’s son, Kong Li, and his grandson, Kong Ji (Zisi).</note>. 

Outside the west gate of the Ancestral Hall wings is the **Hall of the Duke of the State of Qi** <note>This hall is dedicated to Confucius’s father, Shuliang He.</note>. Directly behind it is the **Hall of the Grand Lady of the State of Lu** <note>Dedicated to Confucius’s mother, Yan Zhengzai.</note>. From the Grand Lady’s hall, proceeding north through the eastern wing, one finds the **Hall of the Five Sages**.

Outside the Chen Gate of the Ancestral Hall wings is the **Abstinence Hall** <note>original: *Qi Ting*. This was a place for ritual purification where participants would fast and meditate before ceremonies.</note>. Outside the eastern wing gate of the Abstinence Hall are the **Guest Quarters**, which face the hall directly.

At the northeast corner of the hall's foundation is the **Hall of Twin Cassias** <note>original: *Shuanggui Tang*. In Chinese culture, "plucking the cassia" is a metaphor for passing the difficult imperial examinations.</note>. During the Huangyou era <note>1049–1054, under the reign of Emperor Renzong of the Song Dynasty.</note>, brothers of the forty-fifth generation of the family were awarded their degrees in the same year; they both studied together at this location, which is why it was given this name.

The various residential quarters are all arranged behind the **Ancestral Hall**, as well as to the east and west of the **Hall of Grace and Celebration**. From the rear of the Ancestral Hall through these various residences, the structures were formerly designated as <term>Imperially Maintained</term> <note>original: *Chixiu*. This term refers to buildings constructed or repaired by direct decree of the Emperor using state funds.</note>. In recent times, those supervising the renovation of the Ancestral Temple have not dared to use public funds to build or decorate private residences. Excluding these residential quarters, the courtyards, halls, and corridors of the Ancestral Temple total three hundred and sixteen gates <note>In traditional Chinese architecture, "gates" or "bays" (*men*) are used as a standard unit to measure the size and scale of a building.</note>.

### Ritual Architectural Terminology

<term>**Renzu** (Benevolent Ancestor): The posthumous title for the father of the Ming founder. In Chinese imperial tradition, honoring one's parents and grandparents with temples and specific rites was a foundational act of "filial piety" (xiao) that legitimized the dynasty.</term>

<term>**Miaodian** (Temple Hall): The central architectural focus of an ancestral complex. Unlike a "palace" for the living, the "temple" was a palace for the spirits, designed to facilitate the descent of the ancestor's soul during the sacrifice.</term>

<term>**Shengzai** (Sacrificial Slaughter): Ritual purity was paramount. The slaughtering of animals was not merely food preparation but a consecrated act that took place within the temple walls in a specific area to ensure the blood and remains were handled according to protocol.</term>

<term>**Pao/Zhuan Shen** (Kitchen and Food Deities): These shrines indicate that every step of the ritual—from the raw animal to the cooked dish—was under the protection and observation of specific spiritual overseers.</term>

<term>**Dongwu** (East Gallery): Part of the traditional four-sided courtyard layout (*siheyuan*). The east and west galleries mirrored each other, creating a sense of balance and enclosure for the sacred central space.</term>

<term>**Zhishi Fang** (Attendants’ Room): Rituals involved a large staff of musicians, chanters, and assistants. This room served as their administrative and preparatory headquarters during the multi-day sacrificial cycles.</term>

### The People’s Community Altar

In ancient times, the <term>Village Shrines</term> <note>original: "Lishe" (里社) — the basic unit of local community organization and worship in ancient China.</note> featured a central shrine tree, chosen from among the species most suited to the local soil. For instance, the people of the <term>Xia Dynasty</term> <note>The Xia (c. 2070–1600 BCE) are traditionally considered China's first dynasty.</note> used the pine; the people of the <term>Yin</term> <note>Also known as the Shang dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE).</note> used the cypress; and the people of the Zhou used the chestnut. The philosopher **Zhuangzi** <note>A foundational Daoist thinker (4th century BCE).</note> famously encountered a massive sacred oak at a village shrine. **Emperor Gaozu of Han** <note>The founder of the Han Dynasty, reigned 202–195 BCE.</note> established the Altar of the White Elm at Fenyu, and the Tang dynasty maintained the Maple Forest Altars. All of these traditions focused on the tree as the primary object of the shrine.

From the imperial court down to the prefectures and counties, the systems for the <term>altars and enclosures</term> <note>original: "Tanwei" (壇壝) — the raised platform (tan) and the low surrounding walls (wei).</note> are governed by fixed official regulations. However, it is only in the people’s local community altars that a "Divine Tree" is established. During the spring and autumn periods of prayer and thanksgiving, the entire community gathers here to perform sacrifices.

Upon examining the ritual ceremonies of recent times, the procedure is as follows:

One day prior to the ceremony, the <term>Shrine Master</term> <note>original: "Shezheng" (社正) — the local headman responsible for overseeing the community altar.</note> and all members of the shrine community must undergo a period of ritual fasting and purification. 

On the day of the sacrifice, three quarters of an hour before dawn, the livestock are slaughtered and cooked in the kitchen. The food stewards then fill the ritual vessels. The officiants enter the grounds with mats and arrange them as follows:
*   The mat for the **God of Soil** is placed directly beneath the Divine Tree.
*   The mat for the **God of Grain** is placed to the west of the Divine Tree.
*   Both mats must face North.

At <term>daybreak</term> <note>original: "Zhiming" (質明) — the moment when light first appears in the sky.</note>, the Shrine Master and all those ranked below him perform two ritual bows. The prayers are read, and once the ceremony is complete, they withdraw.

### Exposed Star Room

> A ruined house that leaks badly and contains holes [in the roof] is called an "Exposed Star Room." This primarily results in <term>official disasters: "Guanzai," referring to legal troubles, lawsuits, or unwanted attention from government authorities.</term> and unexpected misfortunes. The family population and <term>livestock wealth: "Xuecai," literally "blood wealth," referring to the health and profit gained from farm animals.</term> will not flourish. It is fortunate to complete repairs and make the house whole.
<note>original: "露星房" (Luxingfang). The name suggests that the roof is so damaged that the stars can be seen through it, inviting "leakage" of the home's prosperity.</note>

> When a room at the eastern end of the main hall borders a hill or a narrow side-alley, it is called a "Single Side-Ear." This primarily leads to unexpected disasters and <term>disputes: "Shifei," literally "right and wrong," referring to social conflict, gossip, or litigation.</term>. One must demolish it or <term>ritually suppress it</term> to ensure good fortune.
<note>original: "單側耳" (Dance er). This continues the anatomical metaphor from the previous page, where side-rooms are viewed as "ears" that must be balanced.</note>
