# Essential Summary of River Management Through the Ages

**Jia Rang’s Three Strategies for Managing the River** notes that the construction of dikes began relatively recently, during the **Warring States period** <term>Warring States period (c. 475–221 BCE): A foundational era of Chinese history characterized by intense warfare and significant technological and philosophical growth.</term>. The states of **Qi, Zhao, and Wei** used the Yellow River as their mutual border. Because the land of the Qi state was low-lying, they constructed dikes twenty-five *li* <note>Approximately 12.5 kilometers or 7.7 miles.</note> away from the river's edge. Although this was not the river's main channel, the distance allowed the waters space to meander and spread out. When the floods receded in due time, they left behind **fertile silt** <note>original: "填淤" (tianyu). Siltation was often viewed as a "blessing from the river" for farmers despite the danger of the floods.</note>, which the people used for farming. However, over a long period of time without major disasters, people gradually began to build houses and drain the marshes to dwell there; in such a situation, being submerged by floods was only to be expected.

Today, the dikes are narrow; in some places they are only a few hundred paces from the water, and even in the far reaches they are only a few *li* away. These are all areas that were cleared by previous generations.

Now, to implement the **Upper Strategy**, we should relocate the residents of **Jizhou** <term>Jizhou: One of the traditional "Nine Provinces" of ancient China, encompassing parts of modern-day Hebei, Henan, and Shanxi provinces.</term> who are currently in the path of the floodwaters, and allow the river to flow northward into the sea. Once this work is established, the river will be settled and the people will be at peace, remaining free from disaster for a thousand years. This is called the "Upper Strategy."

The **Middle Strategy** suggests digging numerous irrigation and transport canals. During droughts, one would open the eastern sluices to irrigate Jizhou. During floods, one would open the western "High Gates" to divert the river’s flow. This would enrich the nation and bring peace to the people, creating benefits and removing hazards for several hundred years. ### Classification of Dredging and Structural Controls

To restrain the violence of the River by choking its flow is called **blocking**. In contrast, the methods of **dredging** are distinguished into four categories: those applied to **new ground**, to **former channels**, to the **riverbed itself**, and to **diversion channels**.

When dealing with **new ground**, the path may be straight or winding; one must excavate and carve the channel according to the terrain.

**Former channels** <note>original: 故道 (gudao) - abandoned paths previously taken by the river.</note> vary in elevation. The high sections must be leveled to encourage the current to flow through, while high and low points must be balanced against one another. In this way, the high sections will not become blocked, and the lower sections will not become stagnant. This prevents the danger of excessive congestion, which leads to breaches, or the formation of pools, which leads to siltation.

Regarding the **riverbed**, although the water already passes through it, the channel varies in width. Where it is narrow, it is difficult for the channel to receive the volume of water, making the current increasingly fierce; therefore, narrow sections must be widened according to a calculated plan. Where it is wide, it is difficult to maintain stable banks, as they are prone to collapse; therefore, wide sections must be reinforced with defensive works.

**Diversion channels** <term>water reduction rivers (jianshui he): auxiliary channels designed to draw off excess water during floods</term> are used to regulate the river's "madness" when the flow becomes unrestrained and to diminish its "fury" during dangerous surges.

# Survey of River Strategies: Illustrated

### Materials and Categories of Breaches

The methods for **blocking the river** <note>original: 塞河 (saihe) — the process of damming a breach or closing a channel.</note> utilize earth, stone, iron, grass, wood, machinery, and heavy ropes; these all constitute a single category of work. 

Breaches are classified into three types: the **gap**, the **spillway**, and the **dragon’s mouth**. 

*   A **gap** <note>original: 缺口 (quekou)</note> is a breach that has already matured into a flowing stream. 
*   A **spillway** <note>original: 豁口 (huokou)</note> is a place where the water has previously carved a path; when the water recedes, the mouth sits lower than the embankment, and when the water rises, it overflows through this opening. 
*   The **dragon’s mouth** <term>Dragon's Mouth (longkou): the final, most difficult gap to close during dam construction, or the point where a new channel meets the old source.</term> is the point of convergence where the water enters the source of the old channel from the new river.

The term **balancing** <note>original: 折 (zhe) — a technical surveying term.</note> refers to the use of ancient calculation methods to infer one value from another. By understanding the relative heights and momentum of the terrain, one can **balance and adjust** the flow to ensure it is uniform and steady.

It flows south for one thousand *li*, entering the head of **Longshou** <note>original: 隴首; the "Head of the Long Region," referring to the Gansu/Shaanxi borderlands.</note> and reaching **Longmen** <note>original: 龍門; the "Dragon Gate," a famous narrow gorge where the river's power is concentrated.</note>. This geographic location is called the "**Root**" <note>original: 根 (Gen)</note>. Corresponding to it is the **Guanshi Star** <note>original: 官室星; the "Official Chambers Star."</note>. This is the **fourth bend**. 

It flows south for one thousand *li* to reach **Juanzhong Mountain** <note>original: 卷重山</note>. This location is called the "**Land Throat**" <note>original: 地咽 (Diyan)</note>. Corresponding to it is the **Juanshe Star** <note>original: 卷舌星; the "Rolled Tongue" constellation within the Pleiades mansion.</note>. This is the **fifth bend**. It then flows east, passing through the **Shizhu** <note>original: 砥柱; the "Pillar Rock," a famous geological feature in the middle of the river that resisted the current, symbolizing strength.</note> and striking **Liushan** <note>original: 流山; literally "Flowing Mountain."</note>. This location is called the "**Earth Larynx**" <note>original: 地喉 (Dihou)</note>. Corresponding to it is the **Shu Star** <note>original: 樞星; the "Pivot" star, known in the West as Dubhe in the Big Dipper.</note>, which serves to revolve the **Seven Regulators** <note>original: 七政 (Qizheng); refers to the Sun, Moon, and the five visible planets: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn.</note>. This is the **sixth bend**. 

To the west, it is one thousand *li* from Juanzhong Mountain; to the east, it reaches **Luohui** <note>original: 雒會; the confluence of the Luo and Yellow Rivers.</note>. This location is called the "**Land Spirit**" <note>original: 地神 (Dishen)</note>. Corresponding to it is the **Ji Star** <note>original: 紀星; the "Regulation" or "Record" star.</note>. This is the **seventh bend**. Flowing east, it reaches **Dapi** <note>original: 大伾; a prominent hill in modern-day Henan province.</note>. This location is called the "**Land Arm**" <note>original: 地肱 (Digong)</note>. Corresponding to it is the **Fu Star** <note>original: 輔星; the "Assistant" star, known as Alcor, which sits beside Mizar in the Big Dipper.</note>. This is the **eighth bend**. This is the **ninth bend**.

## On Water Transport <term>Caoyun (漕運): The state-managed system of transporting grain from the fertile south to the capital and northern borders via the Grand Canal.</term>

From Guazhou <note>A strategic canal junction near modern Yangzhou.</note>, passing through the Transport Administration Office, it is two hundred *li* <note>A *li* is a traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters or one-third of a mile.</note> to reach the Nankou Sluice-gate. From Nankou, it is nearly another two hundred *li* to the mouth of the Huai River at Qingjiang. From Qingjiang, it is three hundred *li* to the Luliang Rapids; along the way, one passes through several shallow areas including the Huangjia, Lijia, and Wujiagou channels.

The waters of the Luliang Rapids are the most treacherous; after seventy or eighty *li*, one passes the Xuzhou Rapids. From Xuzhou, it is approximately two hundred *li* to Xiegou, where the route joins the waters of Lake Zhaoyang. After another five hundred *li* or so, one reaches Shawan and Zhangqiu. From Zhangqiu, it is nearly three hundred *li* past Linqing. From Linqing, it is about four hundred *li* passing through the territory of Cangzhou, then another three hundred-plus *li* slightly upstream to the Wei River. From Tianjin, it is roughly three hundred-plus *li* slightly downstream to Zhangjiawan <note>The final inland port before the capital.</note>.

The total distance is over four thousand *li*. Because the water is often shallow, the transport ships must move carefully and usually do not reach the Capital until the fourth or fifth lunar month. The total annual quota of tax grain collected from across the empire and transported to the granaries of the Capital and Tongzhou for distribution is 4,008,998 *shi*, 9 *dou*, 9 *sheng*, and 3 *he* <note>These are traditional units of dry volume for grain; a *shi* (picul) is roughly equivalent to 100 liters.</note>.

The Han Dynasty established its capital in the "Region Within the Passes" <note>Guanzhong (關中), the fertile area surrounding modern-day Xi'an</note>, and while all supplies and logistics relied on resources from the eastern plains <note>Shandong (山東), referring generally to the lands east of the Xiao Mountains and Hangu Pass</note>, the expenses were initially quite modest.

The Tang Dynasty’s capital was in the same location as the Han, but its grain was sourced primarily from the Yangtze and Huai River regions. Since this is a region accessible from all directions, <term>Water Transport (Cao): The state system for moving tribute grain from the provinces to the capital via waterways.</term> was convenient. What is regrettable, however, is the change from the "transfer transport" method to the "direct transport" method. <note>Under "transfer transport" (zhuan yun), grain was moved in stages by different regional teams; "direct transport" (zhi da) forced local laborers to travel the entire distance to the capital, which was far more exhausting and expensive for the common people.</note>

When the Yuan Dynasty established its capital in Yan <note>modern-day Beijing</note>, grain transport was conducted via sea routes. In the twenty-sixth year <note>1289 CE, during the reign of Kublai Khan</note>, the Huitong Canal was first excavated. However, although the Wen River canals were opened, sea transport continued as before; thus, the Yuan Dynasty’s reliance on sea transport remained significant.

At the beginning of our current dynasty <note>the Ming Dynasty</note>, the capital was established at Jinling <note>Nanjing</note>. At that time, only the military provisions for Liaodong were sent via sea transport. The Huitong Canal remained navigable until the twenty-fourth year <note>1391 CE, during the reign of the Hongwu Emperor</note>, when the Yellow River breached its banks at Yuanwu. The floodwaters overwhelmed Anshan Lake, and the Huitong Canal subsequently became silted and blocked.

During the Yongle era <note>1402–1424 CE</note>, when the new capital was being built at Beijing, the grain route ran from the Yangtze River into the Huai River, and from the Huai into the Yellow River as far as Yangwu. From there, the grain was moved to Jinan, where laborers from Shandong performed overland transport to Weihui. **The Count of Pingjiang, Chen Xuan** <note>original: 平江伯陳瑄; Chen Xuan (1365–1433) was a crucial Ming military commander and hydraulic engineer who oversaw the Grand Canal's reconstruction.</note>, continued the management of these works and refined the system. This dam spanned five *li* <note>approximately 2.5 kilometers or 1.5 miles</note> and blocked the old course of the **Wen River** where it used to flow eastward into the sea. Instead, the dam forced all the water to flow toward **Nanwang** <note>the highest point of the canal, often called the "Water Divide" or the "Suez of China" in historical contexts</note>. 

From there, the water was divided: one-third flowed south and two-thirds flowed north. He dredged the rivers at **Jining** and **Linqing**, and constructed dikes at **Pingwang** and **Gaoyou**. He also cut through the massive boulders at the two Great Rapids of **Xu** and **Lu** <note>original: 徐吕二洪; these were notorious stretches of the Yellow River and canal system where jagged rocks and swift currents frequently wrecked grain barges</note>.

Furthermore, he dredged the tidal inlets at **Yizhen** and **Guazhou**, opened the **Baita River**, and cleared the docks at **Qingjiang**. From the region of Xu <note>modern Xuzhou</note> to Linqing, forty-seven <term>Locks (Zha): Water control gates used to maintain navigable depths and manage the flow of the canal.</term> were constructed to store and release water as needed. 

From the **Huai River** all the way to **Tongzhou** <note>the terminus of the canal near Beijing</note>, five hundred and sixty-eight granaries were established, with troops stationed there to manage them. Granaries were placed along the embankments at **Huaian**, **Xuzhou**, and **Tongzhou** to facilitate the <term>Transshipment (Zhuan Shu): The process of moving grain from one boat or mode of transport to another to navigate different water levels or river systems.</term> of goods.

The region spanning from **Shaobo** and **Baima** Lakes to **Jining**, and ending at **Zhaoyang** and **Nanwang** Lakes, is constantly threatened by the specter of drought <note>original: 旱魁 (hànkuí); a mythological drought-bringing demon, here used to describe severe dry spells</note>. If the various mountain springs fail to supply enough water, the **Huitong Canal** cannot be relied upon as a permanent solution. During the Yuan Dynasty, the waters of the **Yellow River** were also borrowed to supplement the flow, but recently the danger lies in the river itself—if it does not silt up, it breaches its banks. 

Thus, in the writings of **Liu Zhuangli** <note>Liu Tianhe (1479–1546), a high official and hydraulic expert known by his pseudonym Liu Zhuangli</note>, there is a warning: "To bring in the Yellow River to save the Canal is like summoning wolf-soldiers <note>original: 狼兵 (lángbīng); fierce ethnic minority mercenaries from Guangxi used by the Ming state, often feared for their lack of discipline</note> to expel internal rebels." The fact that the Yellow River cannot consistently aid the Grand Canal is a reality of their conflicting natures—the rise of one often necessitates the decline of the other.

Others suggest that where the **Yangtze River** reaches **Jingkou**, the current is much lower than at **Danyang**. The inner river suffers from shallowness, while **Lianhu Lake** sits at a higher elevation; it would be wise to reinforce the lake’s embankments to increase its storage capacity and release the water into the canal as needed.

It is also argued that since the terrain of **Jining** is uniquely high, the scale of the **Jinkou Dam** should be increased to block the **Si River** and force its waters to flow into Jining. 

Furthermore, some propose diverting the **Qin River** from Guo Village in Wuzhi, **Huaiqing Prefecture**, all the way to **Xuzhou**, exiting at **Pei County** to augment the flow of the **Two Torrents** <note>The Xu and Lu rapids, which were notoriously difficult to navigate without high water levels</note>. 

Another proposal suggests excavating a channel seventy *li* <note>roughly 35 kilometers</note> north of Xiangfu in **Kaifeng** to connect with the **Wei River** and exit into the **Huai**. 

Finally, it is said that the canal route through the Yangtze should go from the **West River** of **Changzhou**, passing the **Xinhe** (New River) to enter the Yangtze, or perhaps following the **Mengdu Canal**.

### Discourse on the Strategic Strongholds of Guangdong

The various prefectures of Guangdong are divided into three defensive routes. The **Western Route**, comprising Gaozhou, Leizhou, and Lianzhou, is situated near <note>original: "占城" and "滿刺"</note> Champa and Melaka, as well as various other foreign territories. 

In the **Middle Route**, which includes the bays of Dongguan and surrounding areas, aquatic bandits and <term>Wokou (Japanese Pirates): Raiders of various ethnicities, though often led by Japanese or Chinese outlaws, who plagued the coastlines of East Asia during the 14th to 16th centuries.</term> appear and disappear at irregular intervals. 

The **Eastern Route**, consisting of Huizhou and Chaozhou, shares a border with Fujian Province; it is a mandatory passage for the trade ships of Zhangzhou heading toward foreign lands. Consequently, all three routes suffer equally from maritime threats. 

Under the old regulations, warships were stationed at every bay and harbor during the spring floods of each year, and were withdrawn to the <term>Naval Bases (Shuizhai): Fortified maritime garrisons where the Ming navy harbored ships and organized coastal patrols.</term> at the end of autumn. Nowadays, however, this is no longer the case. 

The incursions of the <note>original: "倭奴" (Woneu); literally "dwarf slaves," a derogatory historical term for Japanese raiders.</note> Japanese pirates are nowhere more severe than in the Eastern Route, nor is there any place more convenient for their raids. Next in severity are locations like Nantou; following those are the three prefectures of Gaozhou, Leizhou, and Lianzhou. 

Because the levels of urgency and the difficulty of the terrain vary, the distribution of soldiers and equipment must be adjusted accordingly. Under former precedents, the Middle and Eastern routes had no more than twenty warships each. Today, it is appropriate to increase these numbers everywhere, particularly in **Zhelin**. This is because Zhelin is situated a full day's journey from the main naval base, making it difficult to respond quickly to sudden alarms.

### Inspecting the Cargo

The second method is called "Inspecting the Cargo."
