# Preface to the Manual of Birds and Flowers

Among the famous masters of the Tang and Song dynasties, those who excelled at painting flowers and plants <term>huahui: a collective term for floral subjects in Chinese art</term> almost invariably also mastered the painting of birds <term>lingmao: literally "feathers and hair," the traditional category for birds and mammals, though often used specifically for birds in this context</term>. 

The *Xuanhe Catalogue of Paintings* <note>original: "Xuanhe Huapu" (宣和画譜), a foundational 12th-century catalog of the Song Emperor Huizong's imperial collection</note> records forty-six artists under the "Flowers and Birds" category. If an artist specialized specifically in "grasses and insects" <term>caochong: the sub-genre of painting focusing on small insects and meadow plants</term>, they were instead categorized under the "Vegetables and Fruits" section. 

In terms of arrangement and composition, birds are most often embellished with "woody flowers" <note>blossoms from trees or shrubs, such as plum or magnolia</note>, whereas insects are paired with "herbaceous flowers" <note>soft-stemmed garden flowers and wild grasses</note>. Because these categories should not be placed after the *Manual of Flowers* <note>original: "Ruipu" (蘂譜), referring here to the preceding floral section of the manual</note>, the herbaceous flowers and insects are presented first here.

# Preface to the Manual of Birds and Flowers

Continuing with woody plants and birds <term>lingmao: literally "feathers and hair," the traditional category for birds and mammals</term> in this sequence is not a new invention. If one observes how the ancients used the terms "flowers and trees" or "insects and birds," the order of precedence was already established in those pairings. 

The application of color <term>shese: the technical process of layering pigments in traditional painting</term> to fruits is of equal importance to that of various flowers. Red cherries and vermilion lychees were once precious tributes to the inner imperial court; green plums and crabapples <note>original: "Laiqin" (来禽), literally "attracts birds," a variety of crabapple famously mentioned in the letters of the calligrapher Wang Xizhi</note> have had their names preserved through the calligraphy rubbings of the Jin Dynasty. Thus, it is only fitting to include their likenesses in this album to rival the beauty of the floral subjects. From grasses to trees, and from flowers to fruits, there is a mutual benefit to be gained from studying them together.

As for feathered creatures, their varieties are numerous. Some dwell far off in wild nests, swimming in river reaches or sleeping on sandy banks. Others live close to humanity, darting through rafters and grand buildings, calling out for clear weather or chirping amidst the snow. 

In a small format <term>chifu: literally "a foot of silk," referring to the limited space of an album leaf or small scroll</term>, one usually depicts only a clipped branch rather than the entire tree. Placing a small bird upon such a branch is appropriate for the scale of both. However, those birds with magnificent plumage, brocade-like feathers, red necks, and ornate crests are only suitable for large-scale compositions. They should not be shrunk down, lest they lose their realism <note>original: "shizhen" (失真), meaning to lose the "truth" or essence of the subject through improper proportion</note>. How could one possibly be familiar with every single species of bird? One must naturally apply one's heart and mind to the study of their infinite variety.

## Origins and Lineage of Painting Methods <gloss>Painting Manual: Flowers</gloss>

The *Xuanhe Painting Manual* <note>The *Xuanhe Huapu*, an imperial catalog of the Song Dynasty collection commissioned by Emperor Huizong</note> argues that flowers and trees possess the essence of the Five Elements and receive the vital breath <term>qi: the circulating life force in all living things</term> of Heaven and Earth. As the forces of Yin and Yang exhale, the plants bloom in glory; as they inhale, they wither and withdraw. Thus, the flourishing beauty seen among the hundreds of grasses and trees is beyond counting. 

Their various forms and colors, though not consciously designed by the Creator, nonetheless decorate the "Great Transformation" <note>original: "Dahua" (大化), a term for the natural processes of the universe</note> and bring civilization to the world. They provide a means to observe the cosmic signs <term>xiang: meaningful patterns or images in nature</term> and harmonize the vital breath. 

Therefore, poets use these plants as vehicles for metaphors and inspiration <note>original: "Bi Xing" (比興), the traditional modes of expression in the *Classic of Poetry*</note>. Consequently, the art of painting and the art of poetry serve as inner and outer reflections of one another. Even a slender branch or a small calyx can be called beautiful among the herbaceous plants. However, for a grand and comprehensive view, the woody flowers are considered primary. Among these: the peony achieves opulence and beauty; the crabapple achieves an enchanting charm; the plum blossom achieves purity; the apricot blossom achieves abundance; the peach is lush, and the plum is plain. # Simple Exposition on Woody Flowers, Volume One

The camellia <note>original: *cha* (茶), specifically the ornamental tea-flower or camellia</note> possesses a brilliance that rivals cinnabar. The sweet osmanthus <note>original: *yuegui* (月桂), the "moon laurel"</note> produces a fragrance like "golden grains" <term>jinsu: a poetic name for osmanthus blossoms based on their tiny, golden appearance</term>. Add to these the lofty purity of the willow and the *wutong* tree, or the vigorous, aged strength of the tall pine and the ancient cypress—when these are committed to a painting, they are quite enough to inspire a refined elegance in the viewer. Such art rivals the works of Nature <term>zaohua: the creative force of the universe</term> and can even shift the human spirit.

If we examine the history of this art, the Tang Dynasty masters of flowers and plants began with the "feathers and furs" <note>a traditional category for birds and mammals</note> style established by **Xue Ji** and **Bian Luan**. Later, masters like **Liang Guang**, **Yu Xi**, **Diao Guang**, **Zhou Huang**, and the brothers **Guo Qianhui** and **Guo Qianyou** emerged in succession; all were famous for their bird-and-flower paintings.

In the Five Dynasties period, **Teng Changyou**, **Zhong Yin**, and **Huang Quan** and his sons rose to prominence one after another. **Teng Changyou** possessed a lofty heart and a unique brush-and-ink style; he did not rely on the guidance of teachers. **Zhong Yin** followed the tradition of the Guo brothers. **Huang Quan** was exceptionally skilled at gathering the strengths of various schools: for flowers, he looked to **Teng Changyou**; for birds, he followed **Diao Guang**. Even for his dragons, cranes, trees, and rocks, each had a specific classical source. His sons, **Huang Jubao** and **Huang Jucai**, were able to carry on the family methods. Because of this, their reputation was immense in their own time, and their methods have been passed down for a thousand years—this is truly no exaggeration. Indeed, the painting styles of the early Song Dynasty were based entirely on the **Huang** father and sons as the standard.

When we reach the Song Dynasty, **Xu Xi** arose and uniquely transformed the old methods. He was truly unprecedented and has remained unsurpassed. Although he lived between the eras of **Huang Quan** and **Zhao Chang**, his divine subtlety was uniquely superior. He passed his legacy to his grandsons **Xu Chongsi** and **Xu Chongju**; their family learning was inherited so well that they could truly follow in their ancestor’s footsteps. 

The color application of **Zhao Chang** was also exquisitely fine; he did not merely capture the outward likeness of a form, but could directly convey its inner spirit. Some say that **Xu Xi**, **Huang Quan**, and **Zhao Chang** followed one another in excellence. However, it has been said that **Huang Quan’s** paintings have "spirit but lack subtlety," while **Zhao Chang’s** have "subtle beauty but lack spirit." <note>This refers to the *Four Classes* of painting excellence: "Divine" (shen), "Subtle/Wonderful" (miao), "Competent" (neng), and "Untrammeled" (yi)</note> Perhaps that is the case? 

Because of this, when the artist **Yi Yuanji** first mastered the art of painting and saw the work of **Zhao Chang**, he remarked: "The world does not lack for talented people, but one must break free from old habits and surpass the ancients to truly reach the peak of excellence." **Qiu Qingyu** first studied under **Zhao Chang**, but in his later years he surpassed his teacher and directly inherited the style of **Xu Xi**. Later, **Cui Bai**, **Cui Que**, **Ai Xuan**, **Ding Kuang**, **Ge Shouchang**, and **Wu Yuanyu** were all summoned to serve in the Imperial Painting Academy, and their reputations were heavy...

# Simple Exposition on Woody Flowers, Volume One

At one time, **Ai Xuan** was also said to be second only to **Xu Xi** and **Zhao Chang**. **Ding Kuang**, however, was not quite enough to run alongside **Huang Quan** and **Xu Xi**. The formal likeness in the works of **Ge Shouchang** lacked a certain meticulous refinement. **Qi Taijian** added scholarly effort to his work and eventually caught up, advancing with great speed.

**Wu Yuanyu**, although he studied under **Cui Bai**, was able to add his own methods. Even those who were originally practitioners of the Academy Style <term>yuantie: the formal, precise style favored by the Imperial Painting Academy</term> reformed their old habits because of **Wu Yuanyu**. They became slightly more unrestrained to express their inner thoughts, washing away the common habits of the time and following the footsteps of the predecessors; this was truly due to **Wu Yuanyu's** influence.

Regarding the bird-and-flower paintings of **Liu Chang**—when **Mi Yuanzhang** <note>original: *Mi Yuanzhang* (米元章), the courtesy name of the famous calligrapher and critic Mi Fu</note> saw them, he thought they were in no way inferior to those of **Zhao Chang**. **Yue Shixuan** first studied the art of painting <term>danqing: literally "red and blue," a traditional term for painting</term> by following the methods of **Ai Xuan**. Later, he realized the constraints and limitations of **Ai Xuan's** style. He alone was able to surpass his predecessors with his brush; his flowers and birds captured their life-force <term>shengyi: the vital, living spirit of the subject</term>. Compared to him, **Ai Xuan** seemed as lifeless as a man in the underworld <note>original: *jiuquan xiaren* (九泉下人), literally "a person beneath the nine springs"</note>.

**Wang Xiao** studied under **Guo Qianhui**, but his technique perhaps did not quite reach that level. The flowers and birds created by **Tang Xiya** and his grandson **Zhongzuo** did not merely capture the physical form, but also succeeded in capturing the subjects' inherent nature. **Liu Yongnian** and **Li Zhengchen** each fully captured the various postures of their subjects in their works. **Li Zhongxuan's** flowers and birds were quite good, but they lacked an elegant charm and unrestrained grace.

Coming to the Southern Song Dynasty, though the times changed and locations shifted <note>referring to the Song court's flight south after the fall of Kaifeng</note>, the methods of painting transformed once again. **Chen Ke** looked back to the master **Xu Xi**, while **Chen Shan** looked back to **Yi Yuanji**. Therefore, their application of color was lighter and more diluted than that of **Lin Chun** or **Wu Yuanyu**.

**Lin Chun** and **Li Di** each had their own lineages passed down. **Han You** and **Zhang Zhong** followed the methods of **Lin Chun**. **He Hao's** brushwork followed **Li Di**. **Liu Xingzu** was even more dedicated to the style of **Han You**. **Zhao Boju** and **Zhao Bosu** passed down their own family traditions <note>original: *xinchuan jiafa* (薪傳家法), literally "passing the torch of the family method"</note>.

# Simple Exposition on Woody Flowers, Volume One

In spirit, they aligned with the ancients; this was truly a peak of prosperity for that era. **Li Zhu** and **Xu Rong** of the Jin dynasty, and **Qian Shunju** <note>original: *Qian Shunju* (錢舜舉), the courtesy name of the famous scholar-painter Qian Xuan</note>, **Wang Yuan**, and **Chen Zhongren** of the Yuan dynasty, were all acclaimed as Great Masters. Each had their own lineage of teachers. Those who studied **Shunju** followed the style of **Zhao Chang**. **Shen Mengxian** also followed **Shunju**, while **Wang Yuan** followed **Huang Quan**. Regarding **Chen Zhongren’s** paintings, **Zi’ang** <note>original: *Zi'ang* (子昂), the courtesy name of the polymath Zhao Mengfu</note> marveled that they were like **Huang Quan** reborn—was this not because he had truly grasped the master's "mind-method" <term>xinfa: the core spiritual principles and artistic secrets transmitted from teacher to student</term>?

**Sheng Mao** studied under **Chen Zhongshan**, and **Lin Boying** studied under **Lou Guan**. Both were able to transform their masters' methods; they were truly "indigo blue surpassing the plant from which it came" <note>a Chinese idiom meaning a student has surpassed the teacher</note>. **Chen Lin** and **Liu Guandao** adopted the methods of the ancients and created a great synthesis of styles. **Zhao Mengyu**, **Shi Song**, **Meng Yujian**, and **Wu Tinghui** were all recognized as highly skilled hands. Although **Yao Yanqing** was meticulous, he could not entirely escape the common habits of his time. **Zhao Xueyan’s** method of applying color followed strict principles, and **Wang Zhongyuan’s** use of ink was warm and moist. **Bian Lu** and **Bian Wu** were both masters of "playful ink" <note>original: *ximu* (戲墨), a spontaneous and informal style of ink painting favored by scholars</note>; all were celebrated artists of their day.

## Method of Drawing Branches

The branches and stems of woody flowers <term>muben: plants with hard, perennial stems, such as trees and shrubs</term> differ significantly from those of herbaceous plants <term>caoben: soft-stemmed plants that die back each year</term>. While herbaceous flowers should appear supple and charming, woody flowers must appear **aged and vigorous**. This is not the only distinction; there are also differences based on the four seasons. Even among spring flowers, the plum, apricot, peach, and plum-blossom <note>original: *li* (李), the Chinese plum</note> are not only different in their blooms, but their branches each possess a unique character.

The old trunks of the **plum** should appear ancient, while its tender shoots should be slender. Only then can one capture the spirit of **"iron bones and ruggedly majestic peaks."** <note>original: *tiegu lengceng* (鐵骨崚嶒), a classic phrase describing the resilient, bony strength of plum branches</note> **Peach** twigs must rise straight upward and appear thick and lush. **Apricot** branches should be rounded, smooth, and gracefully bent back. By observing these three examples, the characteristics of others can be inferred. 

As for **pines** and **cypresses**, their roots and joints must appear twisted and gnarled; for the **paulownia** and **bamboo**, the trunks and branches must appear pure and lofty. When painting a "broken branch" <term>zhezhi: a composition focusing on a single detached spray of flowers</term> arranged in space—whether upright, inverted, or horizontal—one must carefully judge the momentum <note>original: *shi* (勢), the dynamic energy or "pose" of the subject</note> so it is appropriate. The brushstroke at the end of a branch should carry the appearance of having been snapped or broken; it must **not** be cut flatly. If painting fruit, the composition should emphasize a downward-pulling momentum to truly capture its character.

## Method for Painting Leaves

It is an established rule that the leaves of herbaceous flowers are soft and tender, while the leaves of woody flowers are thick and deep. However, among woody plants, those whose leaves emerge in spring alongside the flowers—such as the peach, plum, crabapple, and apricot—should also be rendered as soft and tender, despite being woody species. Naturally, the leaves of autumn and winter should be made even thicker and deeper. 

Because herbaceous leaves are tender, they should be interspersed with "turned leaves" <note>leaves showing their undersides to suggest movement and depth</note>. As for species like the osmanthus, citrus, and camellia, which endure frost and snow without withering and remain unmoved by wind and dew, their color should be deep and thick. Yet, the principle of **light and shadow, front and back** <term>yinyang xiangbei: the structural logic of how leaves face the sun (yang) or turn away in shadow (yin)</term> naturally applies; one cannot omit the use of turned leaves. For these types, the front of the leaf should use a deep green, while the back should use a lighter green.

Whenever a leaf has been shaded with washes <term>xuanran: a technique of applying color or ink in gradients to create volume</term>, one must then **draw the veins** <term>goujin: literally "hooking the tendons," the delicate process of outlining leaf venation</term>. The thickness of the veins must correspond to the shape of the flower, and the intensity of the ink or color must match the color of the leaf. People generally know that the use of green for leaves is divided into deep and light shades, but they often do not realize that the use of red is also divided into "tender" and "failing" stages. New spring leaves often have red tips, while the old leaves of woody plants turn crimson before they fall in autumn. However, for young leaves that have not yet unfurled, the color should be carmine <term>zhi: a bright red organic pigment</term>; for withered leaves about to fall, the color should be ochre <term>zhe: an earthy, brownish-red pigment</term>. One often sees in the flower and fruit paintings of the old masters that amidst the dense green leaves, they also slightly reveal scorched edges or insect-eaten spots. These details, conversely, are what make the work superior—this is something one must understand.

### Method for Painting the Hearts and Stamens

The heart of every woody flower grows from the calyx. In species like the plum and apricot, although the calyx is not visible from the front, the central point of the flower corresponds to the calyx on the reverse; this is the "root" where the fruit will form. One should also cluster five small dots there, from which the "whiskers" <note>original: xu (鬚), referring to the filaments of the stamens</note> emerge. Yellow "buds" <note>original: rui (蕊), referring to the pollen-bearing anthers</note> grow at the tips of these whiskers. The stamens of the plum, apricot, and crabapple each have their own distinctions. The plum should be elegant and lean, while the peach and apricot should be **plump and full** <note>original: fengman (豐滿)</note>, as their blooming seasons differ. Furthermore, white plums and red plums are distinct: the white plum should be even more slender, while the red plum may be slightly fuller—yet it should not resemble the red apricot. The differences between flowers are first seen in their hearts.

### Method for Painting Roots and Bark

Woody flowers differ from herbaceous ones in that they have distinct roots and bark. The **texture strokes** <term>cunfa: a fundamental brush technique in Chinese painting used to depict the surface texture of rocks or tree bark</term> for the bark of peach and tung trees should be horizontal. The bark of old pines should resemble scales. Cypress bark should appear twisted. Plum bark should be "aged yet moist" <note>original: cangrun (蒼潤), a quality of being both weathered and full of life</note>. Apricot bark should be reddish. The bark of the **crape myrtle** <note>original: ziwei (紫薇)</note> should be smooth and slick. Pomegranate bark should appear weathered and lean. Camellia bark should be green and moist. Wintersweet bark should be aged and moist. By depicting the roots and trunks and capturing the correct bark texture, the entire essence of the woody flower is achieved.

# Secrets for Painting Flowers

The flower is born from the stem and the **calyx** <term>di (蒂): the green base or "button" that holds the petals together</term>. ### The Sequential Method of Brushwork for Painting Birds and Feathers

When painting a bird, start with a single long stroke for the upper mandible <note>original: 愕 (e), referring to the upper part of the beak</note>. Next, complete the rest of the upper mandible. Then, draw a single long stroke for the lower mandible, followed by completing the lower mandible.

When dotting the pupils <note>original: 點睛 (dian jing), the crucial final step that "brings the bird to life"</note>, you must use the corner of the mouth <note>original: 呀口 (ya kou), the gap where the mandibles meet</note> as your guide for alignment. Next, paint the head and the crown. After that, paint the mantle feathers <term>suomao (簑毛): literally "rain-cloak feathers," the long, shaggy feathers on a bird's back</term> and the wings. Then, paint the chest and the belly, continuing down to the tail. Finally, add the thighs <note>original: 腿椿 (tui zhuang), the fleshy "stump" of the leg</note> and the claws.

In essence, the bird’s form never departs from the shape of an egg. By observing this principle, you will find the techniques fully realized in the following formulas.

# Formula for Painting Roosting Birds

Everyone knows the various states of birds—flying, singing, drinking, or pecking—but few understand how they look when they roost. To place a roosting bird on a branch, you must close its eyes. In birds, the lower eyelid covers the upper; this is how they differ from four-legged animals <note>original: 禽之異乎畜 (qin zhi yi hu chu). Unlike mammals, many birds have a more mobile lower eyelid that moves upward to close the eye.</note>.

The beak is tucked into the wing, and the feet are hidden within the belly feathers. We can verify the habits of roosting birds by looking at old proverbs: "A roosting chicken must raise its spurs; a roosting duck must lower its beak." "Lowering the beak" means tucking it into the wing; "raising the spurs" means pulling up one leg. Though these proverbs speak specifically of the nature of chickens and ducks, they contain the principles for all birds. This is what a painter must know; all other types of birds can be understood by following this example.

# Formula for Distinguishing the Two Types of Beaks and Tails

When painting birds, you must distinguish between two types: mountain birds <note>referring generally to forest or land birds</note> and water birds. Mountain birds always have long tails; they fly high with light, fluttering feathers <term>xuanqing (翾輕): a term describing the light, agile flight of small birds</term>. Water birds naturally have short tails, which allow them to float and dive in the water. You must capture their specific natures before you can illustrate their forms.

A long tail is always paired with a short beak; these birds sing well and take flight easily. A short tail is paired with a long beak, which is used to search for fish and shrimp at the bottom of the water. Cranes and herons have long legs, while gulls and wild ducks have short legs. Although they all belong to the category of water birds, you must still make these distinctions.

Mountain birds live among the forest trees, and their feathers possess many colors. The Phoenix and the Golden Pheasant display a brilliant array of cinnabar red and jade green. Water birds bathe in clear waves, and their bodies are mostly clean and simple. Wild ducks and geese are mostly grey; gulls and herons are mostly white.

Only the pair of Mandarin ducks requires a distinction between male and female forms. The male possesses many colors, while the female resembles a wild heron <note>The author suggests the female's plumage is more muted or camouflaged, similar to common water birds</note>. The Kingfisher is also full of luster; its feathers are a lush, verdant green. Its emerald feathers carry tints of blue and purple, while its beak and claws are as red as cinnabar. We admire the colors of these two—the Mandarin duck and the Kingfisher—as they stand supreme among all water birds.

## Azurite
<note>original: 石青 (shiqing); a blue mineral pigment derived from the copper ore azurite</note>

When selecting Azurite, one must choose pieces shaped like "plum blossom flakes." These should be crushed and placed in a mortar to be finely ground. Use the water-levigation method <note>original: 漂 (piao); a process of stirring pigment into water and letting it settle to separate different grades of fineness and hue</note> to produce three distinct grades, then dry them in the sun.

The highest grade is light, clear, and pale in color. It is used for painting the front of green leaves to achieve a sense of depth and thickness. 

The middle grade is of ideal texture—neither too coarse nor too fine—and possesses a balanced depth of color. This is used for pure blue flower petals and for the heads and backs of birds.

The lowest grade is heavy in texture and deep in color. This is used for the wings and tails of birds, and for backing deep green leaves. 

Whenever the blue on a bird's body or a flower petal is light, use <term>Indigo</term> <note>original: 靛青 (dianqing); a vegetable dye used for shading and depth</note> for graded shading. If the blue is deep, use <term>Carmine</term> <note>original: 胭脂 (yanzhi); a red pigment often used for shading blue to create a rich, purplish depth</note> for the shading.

## Malachite
<note>original: 石綠 (shilü); a green mineral pigment derived from the copper ore malachite</note>

The method for grinding and levigating Malachite is the same as that for Azurite. It is also divided into three grades. 

The "upper" <note>In this context, the author refers to the densest grade that settles first</note> is deep in color and is only suitable for backing thick, heavy green leaves or painting green grassy slopes. 

The middle grade is slightly lighter in color. It is suitable for the green leaves of flowering plants or for the front side of leaves. One may use <term>Grass Green</term> <note>original: 草綠 (caolü); a transparent vegetable green, usually a mix of indigo and gamboge</note> to provide the structural shading, or use it for kingfishers by first applying the mineral green and then using Grass Green for the fine "silk" feather strokes. 

The lightest grade is suitable for the "reverse" (underside) of leaves. 

Whenever Malachite is used on the front of a leaf, it must be outlined and shaded with Grass Green. For deep tones, the Grass Green should lean toward blue; for light tones, the Grass Green should lean toward yellow.

When using Azurite or Malachite that has begun to dry, use <term>Cantonese Glue</term> <note>original: 廣膠水 (guangjiao shui); a high-quality animal hide glue used as a binder</note> to grind it back into a usable state. The amount of glue must be precise: if there is too much, the pigment will become sticky, sluggish, and unresponsive to the brush; if there is too little, the color will be thin and will easily flake off the surface. One must judge the proportions carefully.

## Cinnabar
<note>original: 硃砂 (zhusha)</note>

For the "great reds" in a painting, one must use **Cinnabar**, as it does not change color over time. **Silver Vermilion** <note>original: 銀硃 (yinzhu); a synthetic cinnabar</note>, however, will eventually darken or alter. Finely grind the cinnabar and add a small amount of light glue-water. Use the levigation method to let it settle, then pour off the heavy dregs at the bottom and skim away the floating yellow impurities from the top. Take the vibrant, clear red from the middle and let it sun-dry before adding glue for use. This is used for the brilliant reds of camellias and pomegranates. For the layers of flower petals, use <term>Carmine</term> <note>original: 胭脂 (yanzhi); a red dye derived from plants or insects</note> for graded shading. Because this cinnabar red is deep and heavy, it is also highly suitable for the backing technique.

## Silver Vermilion
<note>original: 銀硃 (yinzhu)</note>

If the available Cinnabar is of poor quality, it is actually not as bright as Silver Vermilion. In such cases, one may use Silver Vermilion instead. You should select high-quality "Standard Vermilion" <note>original: 標硃 (biaozhu)</note> and grind it finely. Use the levigation method to settle the surface impurities and the bottom dregs, taking only the middle portion. Add glue and it is ready for use.

## Gold Paste
<note>original: 泥金 (nijin); literally "gold mud"</note>

To prepare **Gold Paste**, take sheets of genuine gold leaf. Use a finger lightly moistened with glue-water to pick up the gold leaf and place it, sheet by sheet, into a dish. Grind it while dry. Do not use too much glue-water; if there is too much, the water will float and the gold will sink, making it impossible to grind effectively with the finger. Wait until the gold leaf is ground as fine as mud and sticks to the dish. Only then should you add boiling water to thin the mixture. Wash out the excess glue-water and dry the pigment over a gentle flame. Finally, add light glue-water to use it. Gold paste is most effective when applied over Cinnabar or <term>Azurite</term> <note>original: 石青 (shiqing)</note>, as it brings out a brilliant luster. Use it to outline and detail the feathers of mythical **Luan-Phoenixes** and **Golden Pheasants**. This will make their cinnabar-red and emerald-green plumage appear all the more radiant and magnificent.

## Realgar
<note>original: 雄黃 (xionghuang); an arsenic sulfide mineral used for warm, golden yellows</note>

Select pieces that are bright and pure. ## Applying White Powder
<note>original: 傅粉 (fufen); specifically refers to the use of white lead pigment</note>

Use "Hangzhou Return Lead" <note>original: 杭州回鉛 (Hangzhou huiqian); a high-quality white lead produced in Hangzhou</note> or "Fixed Powder." Grind it finely, then add glue and grind again. Wash it with water into a dish. Let it settle for a short moment, then pour the liquid into a different dish, discarding the heavy sediment at the bottom. Place the dish over a very gentle flame. Wait for a "black skin" or scum to float to the surface—this is the unrefined "lead nature" coming out. Use a piece of paper to skim it away. Repeat this—letting it rise and skimming it—until the blackness is entirely gone. 

Add a light glue-water and grind it further. Dry it slowly over a low flame. When it is time to paint, wash it with boiling water for use. It can be used for white flowers or mixed with various other colors. Whenever white powder is used on the front of a silk painting, it must be supported by a **backing** <note>The technique of applying color to the reverse side of the silk to enrich the tone on the front</note>.

○ **Another method: Steaming powder to remove lead impurities.**
Take a piece of firm tofu and hollow out the center. Place a lump of lead powder inside the tofu and steam it in a pot. After steaming, remove the powder and grind it for use. The "black breath" (impurities) of the lead will have been completely absorbed by the tofu.

It is better to work with light, manageable layers that can be built up. # Rouge
<gloss>Mixing the rouge</gloss>

Rouge <note>original: 胭脂 (yanzhi); a red pigment often derived from safflower or cochineal</note> must be of the highest "double-strength" quality. Soak it in boiling water, squeeze out the liquid, remove the residue, and dry it in the sun before use.

> **Layering over white.** For petals such as Hibiscus or Okra that have visible veins, one must trace over the white pigment with a chrysanthemum-red tint.
>
> **The Silk-Thread Method.** For flowers with long veins, use the "silk" technique to draw the lines extending beyond the outer frame of the petals. Then add color washes. For most flower petals, first draw a small circle in the center. From the circumference of this circle, draw out the white filaments like silk threads. Point the tips of these threads with yellow.
> 
> **The Appropriate White Method.** When painting from life <note>original: 寫生 (xiesheng); drawing from nature</note>, do not use outlines. Simply dip the brush in white and then into the color, dotting it on in varying shades of intensity. The artistic intent must exist in the mind before the brush moves; this is different from the "outline and fill" style of flowers. The branches and leaves should all be handled with a color-laden brush. If the outlines are not used, this is what is known as **Boneless Painting** <note>original: 無骨畫 (wugu hua); a technique where forms are created by ink or color washes without a preliminary pen-and-ink outline</note>. When dotting the white for flower stamens, it must be mixed with **Gamboge** <note>original: 藤黃 (tenghuang); a transparent yellow resin</note>. The yellow should not be too deep, and the glue binder should be light. When dotting the yellow stamens, they should be raised on the outside and concave in the middle; this ensures they do not appear dull or flat.
> 
> **The Back-layering Method.** When painting on silk, any flower using white powder must be backed with a layer of thick white on the reverse side of the silk. Only then will the color appear vibrant. If the front is painted with various light colors, the back should only be backed with plain white. If the color is a deep hue and still does not seem prominent enough, check it again after applying the backing powder. For a leaf where the front is light green, the back should only be backed with green. One must not use **Mineral Green** <note>original: 石綠 (shilü); a heavy malachite-based pigment</note> for this.

If you must work on a cloudy day, dry the pigment over a fire. Remove it just as it is about to dry; it must not be allowed to become completely parched and brittle.

A flower gains its beauty through the marriage of rouge and white powder. The powder provides the clean whiteness, forming the physical substance of the flower. The rouge provides the vividness, capturing the flower's spirit. Whether competing in brilliance or appearing as if "drunk or shy," that enchanting, delicate posture depends entirely upon the rouge. It is like the cheeks of a beautiful woman; the secret lies not in a deep red, but in a delicate application. One must not apply the color too heavily. Gradually add layers of dye with a light touch, and stop as soon as the effect is right.

# Lampblack

Lampblack <note>original: 煙煤 (yanmei); soot collected from oil lamps</note>. This is only used for painting the hair and fur of birds, beasts, and human figures. Invert a bowl loosely over an oil lamp. After a short while, wait for the soot to accumulate into clumps. Sweep it off, add glue, and grind it for use. Do not use too much glue; an excess will make the finish too shiny, and the **Level Ink** <note>original: 鈞墨 (junmo); a technique for balanced, non-reflective ink tones</note> will not show clearly. For insects and birds, use this for coloring the feathers of storks, rails, and thrushes, as well as the white crane.

## Indigo

**Indigo** <note>original: 靛花 (dianhua); a blue pigment derived from the indigo plant</note> might be considered the most "common" or humble of the organic colors when compared to the brilliant palette of blues, greens, golds, and vermilions. However, it is essential for mixing the various shades of green and for layering beneath **Mineral Blue** <note>original: 石青 (shiqing); azurite</note>. In the grand style of "Blue, Green, Gold, and Vermilion," it is absolutely indispensable.

Achieving a refined hue with indigo is more difficult than with other colors. While most pigments can be prepared in a single day, indigo requires several. Most pigments can be prepared in any season, but indigo—which must be mixed with glue, ground, and filtered to remove impurities—is best made during the summer. This allows it to dry in the intense sun without needing heat from a fire. If it is needed urgently, it may be boiled over a flame, but it is best if one does not let it scorch.

Many flower painters believe their success lies primarily in the application of rouge and white powder. Yet, flowers and leaves must complement one another; if the color of the leaves is lackluster, the beauty of the flower is diminished. Indigo is vital to making green, and green is vital to making the reds stand out—the two rely upon each other. The method for producing indigo is described in detail in the *First Collection of the Manual of Painting* <note>original: 畫傳初集 (Huazhuan Chuji)</note>, so it is not repeated here.

## Gamboge

The variety of **Gamboge** <note>original: 藤黃 (tenghuang); a transparent yellow resin</note> known as "Brush-tube Yellow" <note>original: 筆管黃 (biguanhuang); named for its cylindrical shape formed inside bamboo tubes</note> is the finest. It comes in two types: "tender" and "mature." The tender type is light and clear, while the mature type is heavy and opaque; one should choose between them as the composition requires.

Tender gamboge should be dissolved in water and must never be placed near a fire. If it comes near heat, it will coagulate and become gritty and difficult to use. When painting yellow flower heads, use yellow for the lighter areas. For deeper tones, use **Ochre** <note>original: 赭 (zhe)</note> or **Rouge**. When indigo and gamboge are mixed to create green, there are also three variations of the resulting color, depending on the depth and "maturity" of the pigments used.

# Preparing Ink

Ink is naturally indispensable when painting flowers. Some artists choose to paint colored flowers interspersed with **ink-washed leaves** <note>original: 墨葉 (moye)</note>. Others use no pigments at all, relying entirely on ink for both outlines and textured dots. The beauty of ink-wash lies entirely in the distinction between dark and light tones. When using ink for both, the tones for the flowers and the leaves should be distinct. If a tiny amount of <term>Gamboge</term> <note>original: 藤黃 (tenghuang); a golden-yellow resin</note> is added to the ink used for the flowers, the finished painting will show a clear separation between the blooms and the foliage, appearing as vibrant as if actual colors had been used.

# Sizing the Silk

Take <term>Raw Silk</term> <note>original: 生絹 (shengjuan); untreated silk that is too porous to hold detail without treatment</note> and adhere the top, left, and right sides to the <term>Mounting Frame</term> <note>original: 檵 (ji)</note>. For the bottom side, which is not yet adhered, use long, thin bamboo skewers to pierce through the fabric in an alternating fashion. Let it dry in the sun. Thread a fine cord through these bamboo skewers beneath the frame's crossbar. Tap the two sides of the frame outward and secure them with wooden wedges. Finally, pull the lower cord tight so that the silk is stretched perfectly flat and smooth.

First, simmer <term>Animal Glue</term> <note>original: 廣膠 (guangjiao); a high-quality binder</note> and grind <term>Potash Alum</term> <note>original: 明礬 (mingfan)</note> into a fine powder. 
*   In the **winter months**, use one tael <note>approx. 37.5 grams</note> of glue to three mace <note>approx. 11 grams</note> of alum. 
*   In the **summer months**, use a ratio of seven parts glue to three parts alum. 

Pre-soak the glue in boiling water, then bring it to a boil in a clean pot. Place the alum powder in a porcelain bowl and dissolve it with cold water. Wait until the glue has cooled slightly, then add the alum-water and stir evenly; add more boiling water as needed. Stand the framed silk against a wall. Using a wide, flat brush, apply the mixture from top to bottom. Let it dry in the sun, then repeat. Three applications are necessary for the best result. The second and third coats of glue-water should be even thinner; you may add boiling water to ensure the glue does not cool and become thick or stagnant. In the winter, the glue-water should be kept warm over a very low flame. As for the correct thickness of the sizing, you must judge it by flicking the silk with your finger: if it produces a crisp, ringing sound, it is ready.

You must apply a single, light layer of **alum water** <term>Potash Alum</term> <note>original: 礬水 (fanshui); a chemical compound used to "size" silk or paper, making it less absorbent so ink and color do not bleed uncontrollably</note> before any ink is applied to the surface.
