# The Argument of Marsilio Ficino of Florence on the Book of Mercurius Trismegistus
## To Cosimo de' Medici, Father of the Fatherland.

**At that time** when Moses was born, the astrologer Atlas flourished. He was the brother of Prometheus the natural philosopher and the maternal grandfather of the elder Mercury, whose grandson was **Mercurius Trismegistus**. Augustine writes this about him, although Cicero and Lactantius maintain there were five Mercuries in succession, and that this one was the fifth, called <unclear>Thoth</unar> by the Egyptians and **Trismegistus** (Thrice-Greatest) by the Greeks. 

They claim that he slew Argus, ruled over the Egyptians, and handed down laws and letters to them. He established the characters of their letters in the forms of animals and trees <note>original: "animalium arborumque figuris"; Ficino refers here to Egyptian hieroglyphs</note>. He was held in such great veneration by men that he was recorded among the number of the gods. Very many temples were constructed to his divine power. Out of a certain reverence, it was not permitted for the common people to pronounce his proper name rashly. The first month of the year among the Egyptians is named after him; a town was also founded by him, which even now is named in Greek <unclear>Hermopolis</unclear> <note>original: "Mercurii ciuitas"</note>, that is, the **City of Mercury**. 

They called him **Trismegistus** (Thrice-Greatest) because he was the greatest philosopher, the greatest priest, and the greatest king. For it was the custom of the Egyptians—as Plato writes—to choose priests from the ranks of philosophers, and to choose a king from the assembly of priests. Therefore, just as he had surpassed all other philosophers in keenness of mind and learning, he was then appointed a priest, where he excelled all other priests in the holiness of his life and his worship of divine things. Finally, having attained royal dignity, he eclipsed the glory of previous kings through his administration of laws and his great deeds. Thus, he was rightly called "Thrice-Greatest." 

Among philosophers, he was the first to turn from the study of physics and mathematics to the contemplation of divine things. He was the first to dispute most wisely concerning the majesty of God, the order of demons <note>In the Platonic tradition used by Ficino, "demons" refers to intermediary spirits between gods and humans, not necessarily evil entities</note>, and the transformations of souls. He was therefore called the first author of **theology**; whom Orpheus followed in the second...
