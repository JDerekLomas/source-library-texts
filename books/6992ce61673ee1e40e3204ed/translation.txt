### Provisions

Master Mao <note>referring to the author, Mao Yuanyi</note> states: When an army marches, grain must follow; this has been recorded since ancient times. However, when provisions are transported over a thousand *li* <note>a traditional Chinese unit of distance, approximately 500 meters or 0.3 miles</note>, the soldiers will still have the look of hunger on their faces. Therefore, for long-term warfare, nothing is more beneficial than the system of <term>tuntian</term> <note>state-sponsored military-agricultural colonies where soldiers farm the land they are stationed on to remain self-sufficient</note>.

When establishing these military-agricultural colonies, the study of farming matters is essential. In studying farming, one must value the use of human effort to assist the natural advantages of the land; in this way, the effort is singular but the profit is doubled. As for the daily routines of farming, one may simply consult an experienced old farmer. 

When a light army ventures far out, its momentum must inevitably rely on the transport of supplies. When a large army suddenly congregates, its momentum must likewise rely on the transport of supplies. Transport does not occur outside of two paths: water and land. Regarding water, it is said—

Fields are the source of grain, just as mines are the source of gold. During the Song Dynasty <note>960–1279 CE</note>, the profits from mining and smelting were immeasurable. ## Ancient Methods of Military Colonies
<note>original: 屯田 (tuntian). This refers to the system where soldiers were assigned plots of land to farm during peacetime or between campaigns, ensuring the army could feed itself without draining the national treasury.</note>

The *Classic of the White Planet* <note>original: *Taibai Yinjing*, a Tang-dynasty military manual written by Li Quan in 759 CE. The "White Planet" refers to Venus, the celestial omen of war.</note> says: In the "Eight Policies" of the *Great Plan* <note>A chapter of the *Classic of History* (Shujing) which outlines the fundamental principles of governance</note>, food is the highest priority. This is why when Shang Yang <note>A famous Legalist reformer (d. 338 BCE) whose harsh but effective policies transformed the State of Qin into a superpower</note> entered the state of Qin, he implemented the "Decree for Clearing the Wilds" <note>original: 墾草 (kencao). A policy to bring fallow land under cultivation to increase tax revenue and food stores</note>. When Yi Wu <note>Better known as Guan Zhong (d. 645 BCE), the philosopher-statesman who made the State of Qi the first Great Hegemon of the Spring and Autumn period</note> brought dominance to the state of Qi, he utilized the art of enriching the state through agricultural labor.

Indeed, the land is that which nourishes the people; the city is that which defends the land; and warfare is that which defends the city. When effort is devoted to farming, the people do not starve. When effort is devoted to defense, the city is not in peril. When effort is devoted to warfare, the city is not besieged. 

Within the Four Seas and beyond the Six Directions <note>The "Six Directions" refers to North, South, East, West, Above, and Below—essentially the entire known universe</note>, what is most precious? I say: The Earth is precious. Why is the Earth precious? I say: It is the root of the people. Why are the people precious? I say: They are the root of the state. 

Therefore, when raising an army for a campaign, if military prowess is exercised, the army will be victorious. When the army is held back for agriculture, if grain production is prioritized, the state will be wealthy. A strong state relies on both farming and warfare for its security; a ruler relies on farming and warfare for...

### Requirements for a Standard Military Colony
A total military colony unit comprises sixty <term>qing<note>A unit of area equal to 100 mu, roughly equivalent to 15 acres or 6 hectares</note></term> of land. The distribution of crops and seeds is as follows:

| Crop | Area | Seed Rate (per <term>mu<note>A unit of area; roughly 1/6th of an acre</note></term>) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Millet** | 40 *qing* | 1 <term>sheng<note>A unit of volume; roughly 1 liter or 2 pints</note></term> |
| **Soybeans** | 5 *qing* | 3 *sheng* |
| **Wheat** | 5 *qing* | 3 *sheng* |
| **Hemp** | 5 *qing* | 1 *sheng* |
| **Buckwheat** | 5 *qing* | 3 *sheng* |

Outside of the main colony lands, fifty *mu* are designated for vegetables. The harvest from this land is intended for the autumn banquets and the kitchen's needs. 
* Forty *mu* are for **turnips**, using five <term>he<note>A small unit of volume; 1/10th of a sheng, roughly 0.1 liters</note></term> of seed per *mu*. 
* Ten *mu* are for **radishes**, using two *sheng* of seed per *mu*.

The aforementioned seeds should be planted in accordance with the local soil and traditional farming practices of the region.

### Labor and Rations
Each colony unit is assigned sixty <term>ding<note>An adult male subject to labor service or military duty</note></term>. The rations for these men are calculated as follows:

* **Daily ration per man:** 2 *sheng* of rice.
* **Daily total for one colony:** 1 <term>shi<note>Also called a "dan," a large unit of volume equal to 100 sheng; roughly 100 liters</note></term> and 2 <term>dou<note>A unit of volume equal to 10 sheng; roughly 10 liters</note></term>.
* **Monthly total:** 36 *shi*.
* **Yearly total:** 432 *shi*.

Each colony is also provided with sixty head of oxen.

### Ancient Methods for Military Colony Provisions

For each ox, a daily ration of five <term>sheng<note>A unit of volume; approximately 1 liter</note></term> of beans is provided. This supplemental feeding begins on the first day of the tenth month and ceases on the first day of the fourth month <note>This covers the late autumn, winter, and early spring when grazing is scarce and the animals need strength for the upcoming spring plowing</note>. 

The total requirements for a standard unit are as follows:
* **Daily consumption:** Three <term>shi<note>Also known as a "picul" or "dan"; equal to 100 sheng</note></term>.
* **Monthly consumption:** Ninety *shi*.
* **Six-month total:** Five hundred and forty *shi*.

### Procedures for Harvest Assessment

When the harvest is about to begin, the grain in the military colony fields must first be bundled while still standing. To ensure accuracy in measurement, use a rope with a total length of 374 feet, 5 inches, 3 *fen*, and 3 *hao*. Take one-quarter of this length—which is 93 feet, 6 inches, 3 *fen*, and 4 *hao*—to measure the sides. Drive stakes into the four corners <note>This specific measurement defines the perimeter of one standard square mu of land</note>. Within the area marked by the rope is exactly one <term>mu</term> of land.

In the presence of the colony official, the fields are to be categorized into three grades: superior, medium, and inferior. The grain is then gathered into bundles, each measuring three feet and seven inches in circumference. By counting these bundles, the productivity of the three grades of land can be determined.

### Quality Control and Sampling

Under the supervision of the colony official, the grain samples <note>original: "苗子" (miaozi), referring here to the grain threshed from the sample area to serve as a benchmark for the total yield</note> are threshed. The volume must be measured exactly in *shi*, <term>dou<note>1/10th of a shi; approximately 10 liters</note></term>, and *sheng*. 

The administrative protocol for these samples is as follows:
1. Prepare two silk bags, each containing a portion of the grain sample.
2. One bag is presented to the official in charge of the colony; the visiting envoy shall seal it in the official's presence.
3. The other bag is presented to the visiting envoy; the colony official shall seal it in the envoy's presence.

If there is any later concern regarding loss, waste, or discrepancies in the stores, one *dou* of the grain sample shall be taken and measured against the current stock in the presence of the colony official to verify the quality and volume.

## Current System of Military-Agricultural Colonies

The *Collected Statutes of the Great Ming* states: Following the wars and famines at the founding of the dynasty, the people had no fixed dwellings, agricultural cultivation was completely abandoned, and food supplies and rations were depleted. 

Initially, the various generals were ordered to distribute troops for farming in places such as Longjiang; later, <term>Wei<note>Guards</note></term> and <term>Suo<note>Battalions</note></term> units were established throughout the realm to create the formal military colony system. 

These are overseen by the <term>Dusi<note>Regional Military Commission</note></term>. Each soldier is assigned fifty <term>mu</term> of land as one "share." In other cases, the allotment may be one hundred *mu*, seventy *mu*, thirty *mu*, or twenty *mu*, varying by region and land quality. 

The troops are generally divided with thirty percent assigned to guard the cities and seventy percent assigned to farm grain. There are also alternative distributions such as 20/80, 40/60, or 10/90 ratios <note>These ratios represent the "Defense-to-Farming" balance; in peaceful interior regions, more men farmed, while in dangerous border regions, more remained on active guard duty</note>.

### 5. Regarding the Wheels

There are seven possible placements for the wheels and three distinct styles. The seven placements are as follows: at the center of the **outer casing** <note>original: 圍 (wéi); the cylindrical housing of the screw mechanism.</note>, at the two ends of the casing, at the two ends of the central shaft, and at the two pivots.

For those wheels located on the casing, spokes are installed to clasp the circumference of the casing. For those wheels located on the shaft and the pivots, the mounting points are squared so that they may be inserted into a <term>hub<note>original: 轂 (gǔ); the central part of a wheel that rotates on or with the axle.</note></term>. This hub is likewise fitted with teeth.

Generally, all wheels are set into motion by the teeth of another wheel. The rate of speed—whether fast or slow—depends on the relative size of one wheel to the other, as well as the number of teeth each possesses. 

Therefore, if the driven wheel is compact and has few teeth, while the driving wheel is large and has many teeth, the resulting discharge of water will be rapid. Thus, it is said that the wheel is the component designed to receive and transmit rotation.

# Introduction

The common method of drawing water from a well relies primarily on **ropes and buckets** <term>綆缶 (gěngfǒu); literally "well-rope and earthenware jar"</term>. While these devices seem convenient, a farmer will bend and lift all day long and yet fail to moisten even a single acre of land.

I have heard that in the **Three Jin** <note>A historical and geographical region comprising modern-day Shanxi and parts of Henan/Hebei, known for its arid climate and reliance on well-water</note> region, the people are most diligent in drawing from wells to water their fields. In other regions, where people are accustomed to easier conditions, they see the difficulty of this labor and no longer even inquire about methods of well irrigation. Thus, when the drought of the year arrives, they stand by and watch their sprouts wither into straw. Once the famine is full-grown, they either perish from hunger or are forced to flee as refugees. It is truly a piteous sight!

Now, this device has been created. It does not employ ropes and buckets, it does not rely on a windlass, and it has no need for a shadoof. A single person operating it can do the work of several. If used for irrigating gardens, it saves approximately four-fifths of the manual labor. If those planting grain on high ground each had one well [equipped with this engine], they could save a man's field even in the event of a great drought. Even if several families shared a single well, they could remain free from starvation and displacement.

Therefore, the length of the pillar must be measured according to the depth of the water and the height of the well. 

The handle is a **tenon** <term>笋 (sǔn)</term>. It is located above the points **Fang and Xin** <note>original: "房、心"</note>; it should be carved into a square shape, corresponding to the points **Wei and Ji** <note>original: "尾、箕"</note>.

### 3. The Pot

The **pot** <term>壺 (hú); a reservoir or pressure chamber</term> is made of cast bronze. The capacity of this pot should be one and a half times the combined capacity of the **double cylinders**. Its shape is an elongated circle <note>oval or elliptical</note>.

The body of the pot is wide, while the top and bottom are tapered <term>弇 (yǎn); narrowed or constricted</term>. The degree of this tapering is calculated based on the width of the body, reduced by twenty percent <note>original: "殺其十之二"; literally "diminish it by two-tenths"</note>.

A lid is positioned at the point where the body tapers. The base of the pot follows the long axis of an ellipse <term>橢圓 (tuǒyuán)</term>. Two holes are placed along this axis; the size of these elliptical holes must exactly match the upper diameter of the water pipes.

These components are joined using melted tin. For each of the two holes in the pot, a shutter <term>舌 (shé); literally "tongue," referring to a flap valve</term> is crafted to cover <term>揜 (yǎn); to shut or conceal</term> them. The mechanism of these shutters is modeled after the vibrating reeds found inside a bamboo flute <note>original: "簫" (xiāo); a vertical end-blown flute</note>. 

Inside the pot, positioned directly between the two holes, a mounting loop <term>紐 (niǔ); an attachment point or "button"</term> is installed. The pivots <term>樞 (shū)</term> for both shutters are attached to this loop, enabling them to open and close <term>開闔 (kāihé)</term> in an alternating sequence <term>相禪 (xiāngshàn)</term>.

A circular hole is carved into the center of the lid to join with the central pipe. When fitting the lid to the pot, it is essential that there are no gaps <term>罅 (xià)</term>. Once the assembly is complete, use iron to form double rings and wrap them tightly around the unit to secure it. The joints should then be sealed <term>錮 (gù)</term> with tin; this method is chosen to facilitate future maintenance and repairs <term>繕治 (shànzhì)</term>. Thus, as the water enters the pipes, it alternates between the left and right sides to complete the cycle.
