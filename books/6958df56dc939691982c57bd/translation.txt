The Poets, and among them the ancient theologians, *Most Distinguished Cardinal*, imagine that *Mercury gave the Lyre—which he himself had invented—as a gift to Apollo; though it was not yet highly polished, it was nonetheless essential.* <note>original: "Mercurium inuentam sibi Lyram... The son of Maia, <note>Mercury</note> however, was surely not of such great means that he could adorn such a significant gift himself, or fill the hearts of mortals with such delight. It seems to me that I have acted in a similar way, relying upon your kindness...

...to be, since I have offered and dedicated these inventions of mine—perhaps not useless, though by no means polished—to you, most great **Hero** <note>original: "Heros"; a common Renaissance honorific for a noble patron of exceptional virtue</note>. For who among mortals excels you in the gifts of the mind and the body? Who imparts greater distinction to affairs? Finally, who draws men to themselves as equally as you do with the wonderful **wisdom** <note>original: "sapientia"</note> with which you are endowed? Receive therefore, I beseech you, the **first fruits** <note>original: "primitias"</note> of my talent: for I have prepared them with great labors so that I might reveal my mind to you, a thing I have always longed for since my childhood. And since such things, of whatever quality they may truly be, are owed to leading men, look favorably and kindly upon this inclination of my will toward you. Indeed, if at this time they are not of great value, then with your approval, as a **memorial** <note>original: "μνημόσυνον" (mnemosynon)</note> of my respect for you, I shall perhaps undertake greater things. In the meantime, I earnestly ask you to bring aid to these efforts of mine, and to add spurs to me as I run; for truly **beautiful things are difficult** <note>original: "τὰ καλὰ χαλεπά" (ta kala chalepa); a Greek proverb suggesting that excellence requires great effort</note>. I have certainly dared this: for you were born for all things, and you alone seem most worthy of the name of a Prince, a fact which is hidden from no one. For with the great admiration of all, you sustain many affairs and preside over them diligently, and you are most learned in **Greek and Latin letters**...

# TO THE READER.

<image-desc>A large decorative historiated initial 'P' depicting a landscape or figure, possibly a scholar or traveler in a classical setting.</image-desc>

*Ancient histories testify that the knowledge of plants <note>original: "Plantarum cognitionem"</note>, and of other natural things, was celebrated among both the ancients and the moderns, and held in the highest honor, inasmuch as it is both profitable and delightful—not only to Philosophers, but even to Kings. Nor does it seem necessary for me to bring forth the utility of knowing them in this place, since anyone who has ever made use of the industry of Physicians will have it well known. And indeed, I can be a witness and affirm that nothing can provide for the safety of the human body except the practical application of this science: by which even animals devoid of reason, taught by nature—let alone men—protect themselves from the dangers of diseases and emerge strong against them. For they themselves know what is healthful for them: we, however, unless we have been taught by someone and by the experience of others, could by no means have known these things. This, moreover, is the fault of the human race, whereby it is necessary to obtain by labor not only the supports of life, but also the remedies for health. For what would it profit to live this life if we were vexed by diseases daily affecting it? For life would be no life <note>original: "βίος ἄβιος" (bios abios), a Greek wordplay meaning a life not worth living</note>. Truly, the frequent invasion of diseases—especially Epilepsy—which required me to renounce my other studies at that time, and from which I escaped by the mercy of God the Best and Greatest and the intercession of the Blessed Virgin Mother, brought my mind to this subject. For this reason, I began to turn over the writings of Hippocrates, Galen, and others: for I wished to weigh whether what the Physicians answered should be done for the cure of the disease was also confirmed by the testimonies of the ancients; indeed, I seemed thus to satisfy both my health and my soul. So also, I wished to see whether the pharmacists <term>pharmacopolæ: literally "drug-sellers," the historical predecessors to modern pharmacists</term> observed the formulas of the Physicians, and whether both the Physicians themselves and the pharmacists understood those things which are necessary for compounding syrups and medicines—namely, Plants, Minerals <note>original: "Metallica"</note>, and the rest—and the method of composition, by reason and by ex-peri-*
