Master Fu of Qingyuan <note>Referring to Fu Guang, a Southern Song Dynasty scholar and influential student of the philosopher Zhu Xi</note> says: In the outer territories and rural fiefs, the "aid" method was used. According to the system of salary fields <term>Lutian: land granted to officials as compensation for their service</term>, a lower-ranking official begins with 100 *mu* <note>The *mu* is a traditional Chinese unit of land measurement; in this historical context, 100 *mu* was the standard allotment for a single household</note>. From middle-ranking officials upward, the amount doubles at each level; further details on this can be found in the second volume.

Citing the *Diagram of the Distribution of Salaries* <note>original: "Banlu Tu"</note> from the works of the philosopher Mencius: the sacrificial fields <term>Guitian: specific plots of land granted to officials to fund the expenses of ritual sacrifices to their ancestors</term> consist of half of the one-hundred-*mu* public field; it is simply a matter of dividing that field in half.

Therefore, this method is fixed and must not be altered. If twenty *mu* were deducted from the public field to provide for cottages and dwellings, the remaining acreage would have to be combined haphazardly. This would result in fractional remnants <note>original: "jiling," referring to odd lots or uneven pieces of land that do not fit into a standard grid</note> that would inevitably lead to troublesome and complicated reallocations.

Farmers of high, middle, and low grades are all categorized as "primary husbands" <term>Zhengfu (正夫): The male head of a household who is the primary person responsible for tax and labor duties</term>. Aside from their collective obligation to work the one-eighth share of the communal field <term>Gongtian (公田): Land cultivated jointly by eight families, the harvest of which went to the government as tax</term>, the distribution of private land is as follows:

An **upper-grade farmer** personally receives one hundred *mu* <term>Mu (畝): A traditional Chinese unit of land area; in this historical context, 100 mu was the ideal allotment for a single household</term> of private land.

A **middle-grade farmer** receives seventy-five *mu*. The remaining twenty-five *mu* of the standard allotment is granted to a "surplus husband" <term>Yufu (餘夫): Secondary male members of a household, such as younger sons, who assisted the primary husband</term>.

A **lower-grade farmer** receives fifty *mu*. The remaining fifty *mu* [of a standard hundred-*mu* plot] may either be granted to another lower-grade farmer, or divided between two surplus husbands at twenty-five *mu* each.

This logic is consistent throughout the system. The twenty-five *mu* allotted to a surplus husband represents exactly one-quarter of a standard hundred-*mu* plot.

Within the **Royal City**, state-owned residences are exempt from taxation. Regarding the produce of <term>gardens and orchards<note>original: 園圃 (yuán pǔ); these were specifically designated areas for growing vegetables and fruits near the city.</note></term>, the <term>marketplace residents<note>original: 廛 (chán); refers to the shop-dwellings or tenements where merchants lived and traded.</note></term> depend upon them. Since these marketplaces and neighborhoods are where the people make their homes, the taxes are kept low.

In the **near suburbs**, located within fifty *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters.</note> of the city, the people’s labor duties for the state are frequent and burdensome. To favor them, the tax is therefore light, taking only **one part in ten**.

In the **far suburbs**, located one hundred *li* away, the labor duties are not as frequent as those in the near suburbs; therefore, the tax is **three parts out of twenty**.

As for the outlying regions—the <term>Royal Domain<note>original: 甸 (diàn)</note></term>, the <term>outskirts<note>original: 稍 (shāo)</note></term>, the <term>districts<note>original: 縣 (xiàn)</note></term>, and the <term>administrative towns<note>original: 都 (dū)</note></term>—which lie beyond the far suburbs, the people’s labor duties are even less frequent than those in the far suburbs; therefore, the tax is **two parts out of ten**.

Only the tax on **lacquer forests** is set at **five parts out of twenty**. This is due to the concern that the people would rush toward <term>secondary occupations<note>original: 末作 (mò zuò); in the Confucian social hierarchy, agriculture was the "primary" occupation, while commerce and craftsmanship were considered "secondary" or "lesser" pursuits.</note></term> and become extravagant in their use of tools and utensils. Therefore, twenty-five percent is taken to discourage such activities.
