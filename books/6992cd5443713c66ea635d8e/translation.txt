## Commentary on Military Maxims

Master Mao says: Since ancient times, anyone discussing military strategy must begin with <term>Sun Wuzi <note>Known in the West as Sun Tzu, the legendary author of *The Art of War*</note></term>. For this reason, <term>Cao Mengde <note>The famous Han dynasty general and statesman Cao Cao (155–220 AD)</note></term> annotated the text by hand. He also compiled a "Summary of Military Experts" consisting of two hundred thousand words; this was essentially a collection of various schools of thought used to clarify Sun Tzu's principles. 

There is a work in circulation known as the <term>New Book of the Marquis of Wu <note>A work traditionally but falsely attributed to the famous strategist Zhuge Liang</note></term>, which also claims to explain Sun Tzu. However, this is a forgery and possesses no real merit. While Cao Cao’s own commentary has not been fully preserved, Sun Tzu’s original text survives. Those who are dedicated can grasp the essence of those lost ideas through Sun Tzu himself; thus, other books are not strictly necessary to pass down. 

In the <term>Pre-Qin <note>The era before the unification of China in 221 BC, considered the golden age of Chinese philosophy</note></term> era, there were six schools of military thought. Sun Tzu did not omit anything from the schools that preceded him, and those who came after him could not afford to ignore his work. This refers to the five other major schools of strategy.

<summary>In this opening preface, the author Mao Yuanyi establishes Sun Tzu's "Art of War" as the foundational text of all military knowledge, dismissing later forgeries while honoring the lost commentaries of Cao Cao.</summary>

Providing annotations and sub-commentaries for Master Sun is sufficient for our purposes. Therefore, I begin with **Master Sun Wu** <note>original: "Sun Wuzi" (孫武子), the historical Sun Tzu</note>, followed by **Master Wu** <note>original: "Wu Zi" (吴子), referring to the strategist Wu Qi</note>, as their words are more precise and core than those of other schools. 

Next comes the **Methods of the Sima** <note>original: "Sima Fa" (司馬法)</note>, then the **Six Secret Teachings** <note>original: "Tao" (韬)</note>, and the **Three Strategies** <note>original: "Lue" (略)</note>; these are included to provide a complete view of military systems and regulations. These are followed by **Master Wei Liao** <note>original: "Wei Liaozi" (尉繚子)</note>, because he captured the true spirit of employing troops, which can assist in putting the theories of the other schools into practice.

I conclude with the **Questions and Replies of Duke Wei of Li** <note>original: "Li Weigong Wenda" (李衛公問答), a dialogue between Emperor Taizong of Tang and General Li Jing</note>, Li Quan’s **Secret Classic of Venus** <note>original: "Taibai Yinjing" (太白陰經); "Taibai" refers to the planet Venus, associated with war</note>, and Xu Dong’s **Tiger Seal Manual** <note>original: "Huqian Jing" (虎鈐經)</note>. Their writings all serve to clarify the <term>Six Schools <note>The six classic military traditions of ancient China</note></term> of thought, much like the commentaries of **Jing and Jiao** <note>Jing Fang and Jiao Yanshou, who expanded on the divination systems of the I Ching</note> clarify the *Book of Changes*, or the **Three Commentaries** clarify the *Spring and Autumn Annals*. 

The *Secret Classic of Venus* originally has eight volumes, but I have recorded only two; the *Tiger Seal Manual* has twenty volumes, but I have recorded only five. This is because their remaining discussions concern technical arrangements and numbers rather than the core <term>Maxims <note>original: "Jue" (訣), referring to pithy, essential instructions or "secret formulas" for success</note></term>. By combining these nine schools, I have established the study of the **Evaluating the Essentials of Military Maxims** <note>original: "Bingjue Pingyao" (兵訣評要)</note>. As for these military maxims, one can truly master them by studying Master Sun.

When transporting provisions over a distance of a thousand <term>li</term> <note>original: "li" (里); a traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly equivalent to 500 meters or one-third of a mile.</note>, the internal and external expenses—including the costs of entertaining guests and advisors, materials such as glue and lacquer for repairs, and the maintenance of chariots and armor— <margin>In ancient times, a thousand pieces of gold</margin> will reach a daily cost of a thousand pieces of gold. Only after such wealth is secured can an army of a hundred thousand be raised. 

In the actual conduct of war, seek a swift victory. If the conflict is prolonged, your weapons will grow dull and the spirit of your men will be blunted. If you lay siege to a walled city, your strength will be exhausted. If the army is kept in the field for a long time, the resources of the state will be insufficient to sustain the burden.

When your weapons are dull, your spirit blunted, your strength exhausted, and your wealth spent, then the neighboring lords will take advantage of your state of collapse and rise against you. Even the wisest leader <note>In this context, "wise leader" refers to a general or ruler who possesses great strategic intellect.</note> will be unable to ensure a favorable outcome in the aftermath. 

Therefore, in war, we have heard of "clumsy speed" <note>This is a famous paradox in Chinese military thought: it is better to be quick and unrefined than to be "skillfully" slow and methodical.</note>, but we have never seen a case of "skillful delay" being successful. There has never been a case of a country benefiting from a prolonged war. Thus, those who do not fully understand the inherent harms of employing the military <margin>The reason for not repeating the levy and not doubling the load</margin> cannot truly understand the benefits of using it effectively. 

A general skilled in warfare does not call for a second draft of soldiers, nor do they load their supply wagons three times. They take their equipment from their own country but rely on the enemy for food. In this way, the army’s provisions can be made sufficient. 

A country is impoverished by its army when it must transport supplies over long distances. Long-distance transport makes the common people poor. Furthermore, those living near the army will find that prices rise; and when prices rise, the people’s wealth is drained away.

-># Strategy<-

After these preparations are complete, if the general still cannot master his irritation and orders his men to **swarm like ants** <note>original: "yǐ fù" (蟻附); an evocative term for soldiers scaling city walls in a frantic, disorganized mass, much like ants crawling over an object.</note>, killing one-third of his officers and soldiers while the city remains uncaptured, this is the calamity of the siege. 

Therefore, one skilled in war subdues the enemy's army without fighting; they capture the enemy's cities without attacking; they overthrow the enemy's kingdom without a lengthy operation. You must contend throughout the world with the goal of keeping everything whole <gloss>keeping the men whole</gloss> to achieve victory <gloss>competing for victory</gloss>. Thus, the army is not worn out and the profit can be complete. This is the method of **strategic attack** <note>original: "móu gōng" (謀攻); literally 'planning the attack,' referring to psychological and strategic victory over brute force.</note>.

Therefore, the rule for employing troops is:
*   If you are **ten times** the enemy's strength, surround them.
*   If **five times**, attack them.
*   If **double**, divide them.
*   If **equally matched**, you can engage them in battle.
*   If **fewer** in number, you can defend against them.
*   If **unequal** in strength <gloss>if the balance of power is not in your favor</gloss>, then avoid them.

Thus, the stubbornness of a small force makes them the prey of a larger force. <gloss>If a small force remains stubborn against a large one, it will surely be captured by the larger enemy.</gloss>
