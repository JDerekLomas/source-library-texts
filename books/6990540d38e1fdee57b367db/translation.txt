From the neuroses of childhood, we have learned that much is easily visible here to the naked eye which only reveals itself later through thorough research. A similar expectation will arise for the neurotic illnesses of earlier centuries, provided we are prepared to find them under different headings than our modern neuroses. We must not be surprised if the neuroses of these early times appear in a demonological <term>guise</term> <note>The author suggests that the "demons" of the past are the same phenomena as the "illnesses" of today, just dressed in different cultural terminology.</note>, while those of our unpsychological present appear in a hypochondriacal form, disguised as organic diseases. Several authors, foremost among them Charcot <note>Jean-Martin Charcot (1825–1893), a French neurologist who was a pioneer in the study of hysteria and a teacher of Freud.</note>, have famously recognized the manifestations of hysteria in the depictions of possession and ecstasy as preserved for us by art; it would not have been difficult to rediscover the contents of neurosis in the histories of these patients if more attention had been paid to them at the time.

The demonological theory of those dark times has proven right against all the somatic <note>Physical or bodily, as opposed to mental.</note> conceptions of the "exact" scientific period. Cases of possession correspond to our neuroses, for the explanation of which we once again call upon psychic powers. To us, the demons are evil, reprehensible wishes, the descendants of rejected, repressed instinctual impulses. We merely reject the projection into the outer world which the Middle Ages practiced with these mental entities; we believe them to have originated in the inner life of the patients, where they reside.

# I

## THE STORY OF THE PAINTER CHRISTOPH HAITZMANN

I owe an insight into such a seventeenth-century demonological neurosis to the kind interest of Hofrat Dr. R. Payer-Thurn, Director of the former Imperial and Royal Entailed Library in Vienna. In the library, Payer-Thurn had discovered a manuscript originating from the pilgrimage site of Mariazell, which reports in detail on a miraculous deliverance from a pact with the Devil through the grace of the Holy Mary. His interest was awakened by the connection of this content to the <term>Faust legend</term> <note>The German folk tale of a scholar who sells his soul to the devil for knowledge and power</note> and will lead him to a detailed presentation and adaptation of the material. However, since he found that the person whose deliverance is described suffered from convulsive seizures and visions, he turned to me for a medical assessment of the case. We have agreed to publish our works independently and separately. I express my thanks to him for his suggestion, as well as for various assistance in studying the manuscript.

This demonological case history truly provides a valuable find, which lies clearly revealed without much interpretation, just as some discovery sites yield pure metal that elsewhere must be laboriously smelted from the ore.

We will later learn, to our surprise, that in the history of the painter Christoph Haitzmann there are two written pacts <note>original: "Verschreibungen"; refers to a legalistic contract or deed of sale for one's soul</note> with the Devil: an earlier one written in black ink, and a later one written in blood. At this point, a doubt might arise in our minds regarding the credibility of the clerical reporters, warning us not to waste our labor on a product of monkish superstition. It is recounted that several named clergymen assisted the exorcised man throughout the entire ordeal and were also present in the chapel during the apparition of the Devil. If it were claimed that they, too, had seen the devilish dragon as he held out the red-inked scrap of paper to the painter (*having seen him holding out the paper to him* <note>original Latin: "Schedam sibi porrigentem conspexisset"</note>), we would be faced with several unpleasant possibilities, among which a collective hallucination would be the mildest. However, the wording of the testimony issued by Abbot Franciscus strikes down this concern. It by no means claims that the clerical assistants also beheld the Devil; rather, it states honestly and soberly that the painter suddenly tore himself away from the clergymen who were holding him, rushed into the corner of the chapel where he saw the apparition, and then returned with the scrap of paper in his hand.¹

The miracle was great, the victory of the Holy Mother over Satan was beyond doubt, but the cure, unfortunately, was not permanent. If we consider this written bond with the Devil as if it were a neurotic medical case history, our interest turns first to the question of its motivation, which is intimately connected to the occasion of its signing. Why does one sign oneself over to the Devil? Dr. Faust asks contemptuously: "What will you, poor Devil, give?" <note>A reference to Goethe's Faust, Part I, where Faust expresses skepticism about what the Devil can actually offer a human soul.</note> But he is not right; the Devil has all sorts of things to offer as payment for the immortal soul—things that men value highly: wealth, security from danger, power over people and over the forces of nature, even magical arts, and above all else: pleasure, pleasure with beautiful women. These services or obligations of the Devil are also usually explicitly mentioned in the contract with him.¹ What, then, was the motive for Christoph Haitzmann's pact?

Strangely enough, it was none of these so very natural desires. To banish any doubt about this, one only needs to look at the short remarks that the painter <note>Christoph Haitzmann, the subject of the study, was a painter by profession.</note> adds to the depictions of the Devil's appearances. So, a man signs himself over to the Devil in order to be freed from a state of emotional depression. This is certainly a compelling motive in the judgment of anyone who can empathize with the torments of such a condition, and who, moreover, knows how little the medical arts understand about alleviating this suffering. Yet, no one who has followed the story this far would be able to guess the actual wording of the bond with the Devil (or rather, the two bonds: a first written in ink, and a second, about a year later, written in blood—both allegedly still preserved in the treasury of Mariazell <note>A famous pilgrimage site in Austria</note> and recorded in the *Trophaeum* <note>The "Trophaeum Mariae-Cellense," a record of miracles and events at the shrine</note>).

These bonds provide us with two major surprises. Firstly, they do not list an obligation on the part of the Devil for which eternal salvation is pledged; instead, they list only a demand by the Devil which the painter is to fulfill. It strikes us as entirely illogical and absurd that this man stakes his soul not for something he is to *receive* from the Devil, but for something he is to *perform* for the Devil. The painter's obligation sounds even stranger.

### III

## THE DEVIL AS A FATHER-SUBSTITUTE

I fear that a sober critique will not allow us to claim that we have fully uncovered the meaning of the Pact with the Devil through that reinterpretation. It will raise two kinds of objections against it. First: that it is not necessary to view the written deed <note>original: "Verschreibung"</note> as a contract in which the obligations of both parties have found a place. Rather, it contains only the obligation of the painter; that of the Devil remained outside of its text, as if *implied* <note>original: "sousentendue"</note>. The painter, however, commits himself to two things: first, to being the Devil’s son for nine years, and second, to falling entirely into his possession after death. With this, one of the foundations of our conclusion is cleared away.

The second objection will state that it is not justified to place special weight on the expression of being the Devil’s "bond-servant son." That, it would be argued, is a common figure of speech which everyone might understand in the same way the clergymen likely understood it. These gentlemen do not translate the "sonship" promised in the deeds into their Latin, but say only that the painter *enslaved himself* <note>original: "mancipavit"</note> to the Evil One, gave himself over as property, and took it upon himself to lead a sinful life and to renounce God and the Holy Trinity. # A Demonological Neurosis in the Seventeenth Century

The merchant feels threatened, and as a side effect, this neurosis arises; from this, he also gains the advantage of being able to hide his real-life anxieties behind its symptoms. Otherwise, however, it is exceedingly impractical, as it consumes energies that would be more advantageously used for the prudent management of his dangerous situation.

In far more numerous cases, the neurosis is more autonomous and independent of the interests of self-preservation and self-assertion. In the conflict that creates the neurosis, either only libidinal <note>Refers to the energy of the "libido" or life-drive, often sexual in nature in Freudian theory.</note> interests are at stake, or libidinal ones are intimately linked with those of self-assertion. The dynamism of the neurosis is the same in all three cases. A buildup of libido <note>original: "Libidostauung"</note> that cannot be satisfied in reality finds an outlet through the repressed unconscious with the help of regression to old fixations. Insofar as the patient's ego can derive a "gain from illness" <term>Krankheitsgewinn: the internal or external advantages a patient unconsciously receives from being ill, such as sympathy or avoiding responsibility.</term> from this process, it allows the neurosis to persist, even though its economic harmfulness <note>In psychoanalysis, "economic" refers to the distribution and balance of psychic energy.</note> is beyond doubt.

Even the wretched life circumstances of our painter would not have provoked a demonological neurosis in him if an intensified longing for the father <note>original: "Vatersehnsucht"</note> had not grown out of his distress. However, after the melancholia and the Devil were dealt with, a struggle still ensued within him between the libidinal joy of life and the realization that the interest of self-preservation imperiously demanded renunciation and asceticism. It is interesting that the painter clearly senses the unity of the two parts of his history of suffering, for he attributes both the one and the other to the written pacts <note>original: "Verschreibungen"</note> he gave to the Devil. On the other hand, he does not distinguish sharply between the influences of the evil spirit and those of divine powers; he has one designation for both: "manifestations of the Devil."

## V. SHARED DAYDREAMS
->By HANNS SACHS<-

*When psychoanalysis pointed out the decisive importance of daydreams for the life path and the choice of a partner for the individual, it met—at least at this one point—with a long-standing conviction: namely, that daydreams are the general human preliminary stage from which, in gifted special cases, the ascent to the work of art, to poetry, takes place. ## Source Writings on Mental Development

# I
## Diary of a Young Girl
->(From age 11 to 14 1/2)<-
## Edited by Dr. H. Hug-Hellmuth
<note>Hermine Hug-Hellmuth was a pioneer in child psychoanalysis; the "Diary" she edited was later the subject of intense debate regarding its authenticity.</note>

> Prof. Freud in a letter to the editor: The diary is a little gem. Truly, I believe that never before has one been able to peer with such clarity and truthfulness into the mental impulses that characterize the development of a girl of our social and cultural level during the years of pre-puberty. How the feelings grow out of childish egoism until they reach social maturity; how the relationships with parents and siblings first appear and then gradually gain in seriousness and intimacy; how friendships are spun and abandoned; how tenderness fumbles for its first objects; and above all, how the mystery of sexual life first emerges unclearly, only to then take complete possession of the childish soul; how this child suffers harm under the consciousness of her secret knowledge and gradually overcomes it—all this is expressed so charmingly, naturally, and yet so seriously in these artless records that it must instill the highest interest in educators and psychologists.

> "Literarisches Echo": Female beings of the bourgeois world will feel themselves transported back, page by page, into their former selves while reading this diary; male beings, instead, will be informed of many a small detail they did not yet know.
->Lou Andreas-Salomé.<-

> "Vossische Zeitung": Imagine that Wedekind’s little Wendla <note>A character from Frank Wedekind's play "Spring Awakening," which dealt with the suppression of adolescent sexuality</note>, who perishes so tragically in "Spring Awakening," had recorded her experiences—imagine her transported into the circles of Privy Councillors and onto Viennese soil—then you have the "Diary of a Young Girl."

> "Neue Freie Presse": As in perhaps every sincere diary of an adolescent girl, the focus of interest is naturally sexuality. Sexuality, not erotica. For here, curiosity still stems from the intellectual, from the alert brain of a still undeveloped body, and the unrest wells up from the intellect, not from the still dull zones of physical feeling. Nowhere here does real satisfaction react to knowledge; on the contrary: the first accidental insight becomes a mental shock for the shy child... It is always good to understand the human condition, and for this understanding of the child's soul, this book seems to me one of the most precious that science, hand in hand with chance, has ever offered.
->Stefan Zweig.<-

->On the Censorship Ban in England<-

> "Frankfurter Zeitung": The sensation that *A Young Girl's Diary* is causing in England has resulted in a great morality campaign... Lord Alfred Douglas (the same who in his younger years was much mentioned for his relationships with Oscar Wilde, which became notorious in court) has publicly sworn a great oath to root out psychoanalysis in England. The diary of little Gretl Lainer has been chosen as the first object of his purifying rage... The London censor is surely of the opinion that it occurs exclusively in Vienna, or at most among other "Huns," that, for example, the thoughts and feelings of young girls are vividly occupied by impending physiological phenomena. The "filth" lies within the continental race.

> "The New Statesman": Gretl Lainer (the name chosen by the Psycho-Analytical Society) belongs to the Casanova type of autobiographer rather than to that of Rousseau and Marie Bashkirtseff; she is singularly little troubled with her own personality. She writes from a breathless interest in the world around rather than from any morbid taste for introspection or self-explanation.
