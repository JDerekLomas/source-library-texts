# In Memory of Hugo Schulz

The final work of Hugo Schulz—the translation of the medical prescriptions of Saint Hildegard of Bingen <note>Hildegard of Bingen (1098–1179) was a Benedictine abbess and polymath whose medical writings, *Physica* and *Causae et Curae*, blended natural science with spiritual theology.</note>—helped him endure days of heavy suffering with inner strength. It characterizes him as one of the few who, while devoted to detailed individual research, expect the ultimate solution only from great, universal connections. For this reason, he loved the history of medicine, which he mastered like almost no other. It provided his fundamental scientific works of earlier years with a longed-for and necessary philosophical <note>original: "weltanschauliche," referring to a comprehensive world-view or life-philosophy.</note> completion. For Hugo Schulz, the past and the present were always merely changing symbols of the eternal ideas of our art <note>The "art" refers to the "Ars Medica" or the art of healing.</note>. He possessed the wisdom to behold greatness and the grace to recognize it in the small.

His final thanks were given to his friend Ehrismann, into whose hands the dying man placed the proof sheets.

May this book be given as a remembrance to all those who revered and loved this truly noble man and great, kind physician.

->Sauerbruch.<- <note>Ferdinand Sauerbruch (1875–1951) was one of the most influential surgeons of the 20th century; his endorsement here lends significant weight to Schulz's legacy.</note>

In the year 1903, Paul Kaiser published the text of a manuscript kept in the Royal Library at Copenhagen under the title: "*Hildegard’s Causes and Cures*" <note>original Latin: "Hildegardis causae et curae"</note>. Paul Kaiser himself translated several sections of the handwritten text into German and published them in the "Therapeutic Monthly Journals" by O. Liebreich in 1902 under the heading: "The Writing of Abbess Hildegard on the Causes and Treatment of Diseases." Likewise, Johannes Bühler included a larger German-translated excerpt from the *Causes and Cures* in his book: "Writings of Saint Hildegard of Bingen, Leipzig 1922." However, as far as I have been able to ascertain, a complete translation of the text has not yet reached the public.

To facilitate orientation while reading the present translation, a few preliminary remarks are permitted.

The author of the *Causes and Cures* was born—as I gather from her biography by Johannes May: "Saint Hildegard of Bingen of the Order of Saint Benedict, 1098 to 1179"—in the summer of 1098 as the youngest of ten children of Hildebert, the Viscount of Bökelheim Castle on the Nahe, and his wife Mathilde. Sickly and weak from the beginning, she learned to walk only with difficulty and repeatedly suffered from nervous ailments throughout the further course of her life. At the same time, even as a child, she possessed a visionary disposition that was striking to those around her, and this disposition did not leave her in the time that followed. As early as eight years old, Hildegard was handed over to monastic discipline and schooling. She remained faithful to the monastic state and the Rule of Saint Benedict and, from the year 1147 until her death in 1179, she led the Benedictine convent founded at her instigation on the Rupertsberg near Bingen as its Abbess.

The majority of Hildegard’s many writings are theological in content. Among these, her book: "*Scivias*" (Know the Ways) <note>original Latin: "Scivias", a contraction of "Scito vias Domini"</note>, which made its way from the Rupertsberg in 1150, aroused the greatest interest on all sides and was read with zeal in German, Dutch, and French monasteries. In addition to this, however, Hildegard was also blessed at birth with the gift of observing the things and events of the outside world with open eyes and forming her own [thoughts] about them...

A significant inspiration and advancement in this field was granted to her by Bishop *Siward of Uppsala*, who was deeply interested in the natural sciences and medicine <note>original: "Arzneikunde"</note>. He visited the monastery on the *Disibodenberg*—where *Hildegard* lived before her move to *Bingen*—on the occasion of an official duty. In any case, two works are to be regarded as the result of her natural disposition and its further development through the study of writings available in her own monastic library, as well as those likely acquired through inter-library exchange. According to *Hildegard’s* own account, these were composed between 1150 and 1157: the "Physica, or Book of Simple Medicine" <note>original: "liber simplicis medicinae"</note> and the less extensive "Causes and Cures, or Book of Composite Medicine" <note>original: "liber compositae medicinae"</note>, the translation of which is presented here.

If one wishes to read an author from a long-past era as they intended to be read, and to understand them as they wished to be understood, it is a fundamental requirement—though admittedly not always easy to fulfill—that one strives to the best of one's ability to think oneself into the spirit and views of that age. As we can see from the records of *Hildegard’s* birth and death years, her life fell during the reigns of the last emperors of the Salian dynasty and the first of the Hohenstaufens. The Crusades had begun and had not remained without influence on the formation and development of the cultural and moral views of the Occident <note>The "Occident" refers to the Western world, specifically Medieval Europe.</note>. Cultural history teaches us on every page that these views differed in many respects from those of today. Standing upon that ground, living and thinking under the influence of the knowledge and ethical concepts of that time, *Hildegard* wrote her *Causes and Cures* <note>original: "causae et curae"</note>. Much that may initially seem strange to us or deviant from our habits when reading her book finds its simple explanation in the circumstances just described.

We may safely assume that personal experiences and things she witnessed herself play a role in *Hildegard’s* works. She was active as a physician <note>original: "Aerztin"</note> within the domain of her monastery and its wider surroundings, and she certainly tended and maintained her monastic pharmacy and the little herb garden <note>original: "Würzgärtlein"</note>. However, the actual foundation for her work could only be shaped by reading what had already been set down in writing by others before her. Unfortunately, *Hildegard* does not comment on this question at all. It could therefore be seen as an idle pursuit to indulge in further conjectures regarding the source material used by *Hildegard*.
