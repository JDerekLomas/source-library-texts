# INTRODUCTION.

This translation of the more important philosophical works of Leibniz <note>original: "Leibnitz"</note> provides much-needed assistance to all teachers of philosophy and its history, both in this country and in England. Until recently, no collection of the writings of this great and versatile thinker that was both complete and trustworthy had ever been made. The magnificent edition by Gerhardt has now made it possible for the translator to select, from all the recorded philosophical statements of Leibniz (including his voluminous and elaborate letters), those portions which will provide the most satisfactory survey of his system of thought. The selections in this volume appear to be well-chosen; they are sufficient to provide a reasonably comprehensive and detailed account of his system.

It is not, however, to teachers of philosophy alone that I recommend this volume. The interests and scholarship of Leibniz were unparalleled in their range and variety. He was eminent in mathematics, physical science, languages, history, theology, philosophy, and literature <note>original: "belles-lettres"</note>. Even his more specifically philosophical writings are shaped by this wide-ranging expertise. They therefore contain much that appeals to anyone inclined to approach the problems of philosophy from any point of view. Their style is free from certain characteristics that lovers of good literature often find repulsive in works of a more strictly academic, systematic, or technical nature. Indeed, the principal doctrines of Leibniz can all be discovered, at least in their early <note>original: "inchoate"</note> form, in his interesting and instructive letters to various notable people of his day.

It has not been possible for me to compare any large portion of this translation with the original. However, my confidence in Mr. Duncan’s competence and accurate scholarship is so great that I have no doubt of its excellence.

It gives me great pleasure, therefore, to welcome and to help introduce this book. It certainly fills—and, I believe, it fills well—an important gap in our philosophical literature.

->GEORGE TRUMBULL LADD.<-

Yale University, December, 1890.
