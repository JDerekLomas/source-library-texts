Did not Leland speak truly, most learned Sir, when he said that Bacon had composed a vast number of books? Yet this vast number of books, if we consider the matter a little more closely, will easily be reduced to a few. For while copies of Bacon's works were scattered here and there in different ways, it happened that the same short works were either regarded as different because they were inscribed with various titles, or certain chapters or fragments torn from them were presented as complete treatises. So that this may appear more clearly, we shall deal briefly and more explicitly with Bacon's writings, preserving the chronological order as much as we can.

Since the size of the volume is also great—for which reason it was necessary to divide it into four—and the occupations of the Apostolic See are heavy and manifold, I thought that you might see in a certain summary what is diffused more widely in the larger work. The presentation of our purpose requires that signs and particular causes be shown; but above all, universal causes are required, without which neither do signs show anything nor do particular causes demonstrate anything. For the way of knowing is natural to us from universals to particulars, as the Philosopher <note>Aristotle</note> says in the beginning of the *Physics* <note>original: "Physicorum"</note>. For when common principles are unknown, those things which are left after the common principles are also unknown. This fourth cause <note>the hiding of ignorance under a show of wisdom</note> has prevailed greatly since ancient times, just as it is now found to be shameless in theological matters as well as clearly in philosophy, through experience and examples. For Moses, most simple, received the wisdom of the law from God, against whom Pharaoh and the Egyptians, and the Hebrew people, and all nations murmured, so that the chosen people of God hardly wished to receive this wisdom; and yet the law prevailed against the adversaries, who neglected and hindered the wisdom which they had learned. Similarly, the Lord Jesus Christ, proceeding in all simplicity and without the fold of falsehood, and the most simple apostles, brought wisdom to the world; they were sufficiently contradicted by ignorance alone of such great novelty, and finally, though with the greatest difficulty, the sacred truth was received.

Then, when the holy doctors wished to give forth the floods of expositions of the divine law, and to irrigate the church with a great rush of the waters of wisdom, they were for a long time considered heretics and composers of falsehoods. For as the prologues of the Blessed Jerome to the Bible and his other works prove, he himself was called a corrupter of scripture, a forger, and a sower of heresies; in his own time he succumbed, nor could he promote his works in public. Just as the holy doctors did not have the magnificent sciences of philosophy in use, neither did those who came later—namely, Gratian <note>The 12th-century jurist who compiled the "Decretum," a foundational collection of canon law.</note>, the Master of the Sentences <note>Peter Lombard, whose "Sentences" was the standard theology textbook.</note> and of the Histories <note>Peter Comestor, author of the "Historia Scholastica."</note>, Hugh of Saint Victor, and Richard of the same: for in their times these works had not been translated, nor were they in use among the Latins. Therefore, they neglected them, nor did they know how to judge them worthy of the sacred mysteries; rather, they rejected by human sense things they did not have the use of, and spoke against them in many ways. They nevertheless took their occasion from this: that the holy doctors had previously neglected these same things. But they did not consider the reasons of the saints—namely, that they were not translated in their time, and also because the Church neglected to order them to be translated for the five reasons touched upon earlier.

The modern teachers of the common crowd, although many parts of philosophy have now been translated, still do not have the use of them; while they delight in small and cheap things, they neglect two of the better books of Logic, one of which was translated with the commentary of Al-Farabi <note>A major Islamic philosopher and scientist.</note> upon it, and the exposition of the other made by Averroes <note>Ibn Rushd, the influential Andalusian commentator on Aristotle.</note> was translated without the text of Aristotle. Far more do they neglect the rest, which possess no less dignity, such as the nine sciences of mathematics, and the six great natural sciences—which comprehend many other sciences under them—and they neglect the four most worthy parts of moral philosophy. They seek a miserable solace for their ignorance through Gratian and the other authoritative masters who had no knowledge of these parts of philosophy. For after Christ, the saints did not use the dignity of philosophy, yet not because it is contrary to sacred opinions or unworthy, since it can help usefully and magnificently toward the absolute understanding of theology, and in respect to the Church of God, the commonwealth of the faithful, and the conversion of infidels, as shall be certified in its proper time. And it is all the more wonderful that the multitude of modern students neglects these great sciences, even though they were introduced after Gratian. Some are still living who have read them thoroughly in their studies.

Therefore, this wisdom shall obtain unity from its threefold relationship. Furthermore, the way of salvation is one, although the steps are many; but wisdom is the way to salvation. For every consideration of man that is not for salvation is full of blindness, and leads to the final darkness of hell. Because of this, many famous wise men have been damned, because they did not possess true wisdom, but only that which was apparent and false; hence, "esteeming themselves wise, they became fools" according to the Scripture. Augustine, speaking of the Holy Scripture in the second book of *On Christian Doctrine*, says: "if it is true, it is found here; if it is contrary, it is condemned." And he intends that wherever a Christian finds truth, he should understand it to be the truth of his Lord. The truth of Jesus Christ is the wisdom of the Holy Scripture. Therefore, truth exists nowhere else except in that science which is contained therein. And Ambrose, in his commentary on the Epistle to the Colossians, says: "Every reason of heavenly knowledge and earthly creature is in Him, who is the head and author, so that he who knows Him should seek nothing further, because here is perfect virtue and wisdom. Since, therefore, the Holy Scripture gives us this wisdom, it is manifest that all truth is concluded here; if there is any wisdom contrary to this, it will be erroneous, nor will it have anything but the name of wisdom. ## Chapter II.

These matters become more clearly evident when considering the division of the sciences. Canon law, however, takes its name from the Sacred Scriptures and from no other source, as the name itself demonstrates; for those books of the Old Testament are called "Canonical Scriptures," as is held in the first part of the *Decretals* <note>original: "Decretorum," referring to the Decretum Gratiani, the standard collection of Church law</note> at the ninth Distinction, or they are called "Canons." For *Canon* in Greek is called *Rule* in Latin <note>original: "Canon Graece, Regula Latine dicitur"</note>. And both canon law and divine law are proven to provide a "regular" <note>meaning "governed by a rule"</note> way of living.

Furthermore, canon law is founded entirely upon the authority of Scripture and its expositors. For either the authors of Sacred Scripture, such as Augustine and others, are cited for the constitutions, or the Supreme Pontiffs <note>the Popes</note> indicate authorities and examples from the New and Old Testaments for their statutes; and therefore this law is nothing other than the explanation of the will of God in Scripture. Likewise, canon law is called "Ecclesiastical Law," by which the Church of God is governed in spiritual matters, both in its head and in its members. But Scripture speaks of nothing else but this governance. Moreover, natural law is contained in Sacred Scripture, as is clearly taught at the beginning of the *Decretum*.

Canon laws cannot be foreign to divine law; rather, they must be derived from its fountains. Common law is either divine or human: it is divine when it is brought to the world by the Spirit of God in His Scripture; it is human when it is devised by the sense of man. But it is established that the Church of God is ruled by canon law. Therefore, this same law is divine, to be drawn from the treasury of Sacred Scripture. And this is manifest to anyone considering the parts of canon law. For it either ordains the ranks of ecclesiastical offices, or determines the sacred things of God, or examines the forum of conscience, or decides ecclesiastical cases. But the roots of all these things and the upright trunk itself are found within Sacred Scripture. The branches, truly, are found with the expositors of the same, so that in the "Canon," the leaves, flowers, and health-giving fruits may be gathered. For the canonical discourses are compared to leaves of sweet adornment according to Scripture. The flowers and fruits are the golden shoots of the grain and the ripening of the grapes. And therefore, canon law and the power of Scripture are contained in one body, just as the body of a single tree is composed of roots, trunk, branches, flowers, and fruits.

Likewise, all the holy and wise men of old, in their expositions, gather the literal sense from the natures of things and their properties, so that through suitable adaptations and similarities they may draw out spiritual meanings. Augustine declares this in the second book of *On Christian Doctrine*, setting down the example of the word of the Lord saying, "Be wise as serpents, and simple as doves." <note>Matthew 10:16</note> For the Lord willed by this that, after the likeness of the serpent exposing its whole body for the defense of its head, the apostle and the apostolic man should give themselves and their possessions for Christ their head and for their faith. And because of this, every creature—either in itself or in its likeness, either in general or in particular, from the heights of the heavens to their ends—is placed in Scripture; so that just as God made both the creatures and the Scripture, so He willed to place the things themselves which were made into the Scripture for the understanding of it, both in the literal and the spiritual sense.

But the whole intention of philosophy is nothing else than to evolve the natures and properties of things; wherefore the power of all philosophy is contained in the sacred writings. This is most evident because Scripture accepts creatures far more certainly, better, and more truly than philosophical labor knows how to extract. Let this be shown for the present, among infinite examples, in the Rainbow <note>original: *Iris*</note>. The philosopher Aristotle disturbs us with his obscurities, so that we are not able to understand anything worthy of note through him. Nor is it a wonder, since Avicenna <note>The Persian polymath Ibn Sina (c. 980–1037), a central figure in medieval philosophy.</note>, the leader and prince of philosophy, confesses that he was ignorant of the nature of the Rainbow. The cause of this is that the philosophers were ignorant of the final cause <note>The "purpose" or "end" for which a thing exists, according to Aristotelian physics.</note> of the Rainbow; and when the end is unknown, those things which lead to the end are unknown; because the end imposes necessity on those things which are ordered toward the end, as Aristotle maintains in the second book of the *Physics*.

But the final cause of the Rainbow is the dissipation of watery moisture, as is clear from the book of Genesis; hence in the appearance of the Rainbow there is always a resolution of clouds into infinite droplets, so that the watery moistures are consumed as much in the air as in the sea and earth, because one part of the Rainbow falls into the spheres of water and earth. Now, the consumption of water cannot happen through the Rainbow except on account of the rays of the sun creating it. For through various reflections and refractions, infinite rays are gathered, and the gathering of rays is the cause of the resolution and consumption of waters; and therefore the Rainbow is generated by multiple reflections. For the rays cannot be gathered except through refraction and reflection, as will be shown later. From the scripture of Genesis, therefore, when it is said, "I will set my bow in the clouds of heaven, so that there may no longer be a flood upon the earth," <note>Genesis 9:13-15</note> the final cause is understood.

For even the idioms of the same language vary among different people, as is evident in the French tongue, which varies in multiple idioms among the French, the Picards, the Normans, and the Burgundians. For two things move the speculative intellect <note>The "speculative intellect" refers to the mind's capacity for pure thought and theoretical knowledge, as opposed to action.</note> or reason: namely, the dialectical method through a weak and initial habit, which is opinion, so that we may be prepared for science <note>original: "scientia"; here meaning certain, demonstrated knowledge.</note>, which is the complete and final habit in which the mind rests while contemplating truth. And this habit is not acquired through demonstration alone. But since the will, or the practical intellect, is more noble than the speculative, and since virtue combined with happiness infinitely excels naked knowledge—and is incomparably more necessary for us—it is necessary that we have arguments to exercise the practical intellect, especially since we are more infirm in this area than in speculation. For we freely taste from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil; but we find it difficult to reach for the tree of life, so that we might embrace the dignity of the virtues for the sake of future happiness. 

Therefore, it is necessary that the practical intellect has its own aids so that it may be stirred by its own proper arguments, just as the speculative is by its own. Thus, it was necessary to hand down these arguments which moral philosophy and theology use abundantly. # Second Distinction.

## In which it is shown that the things of this world require mathematics; containing three chapters.

### CHAPTER I.

*In the first, it is taught in a general sense that both celestial and terrestrial things require mathematics.*

What has already been shown regarding the sciences can now be demonstrated through the things themselves. For it is impossible for the things of this world to be known unless mathematics is known. Regarding celestial things, this is certain to everyone, because there are two great mathematical sciences concerning them: namely, speculative astrology <note>In the 13th century, "astrologia" referred to both what we now call astronomy (the study of positions) and astrology (the study of influences).</note> and practical astrology. The first speculates upon the quantities of all things in the heavens, and everything reducible to quantity, whether discrete <note>Individual units, like the number of stars.</note> or continuous quantity <note>Measurements of space and time.</note>. For it certifies the number of the heavens and the stars—whose quantity can be understood through instruments—and the shapes of them all, their magnitudes, their heights from the earth, their thickness, and the number, magnitude, and smallness <gloss>ᵃ</gloss> <note>original: "parvitatem"; some manuscripts read "rising and setting."</note> of the risings and settings of the signs and stars, as well as the movements of both the heavens and the stars, and the quantities and varieties of eclipses. Likewise, it descends to the quantity and shape of the habitable world, and all its great parts, which are called climates <note>term: "climata" — the seven latitudinal zones of the earth as defined by ancient geography.</note>, and it shows the diversity of horizons and of days and nights according to each individual climate. These things, therefore, are determined here, along with many things annexed to them.

Practical astrology, however, descends to this: that at every hour we may know the locations of the planets and stars, their aspects <note>term: "aspectus" — the angular distances between planets, believed to influence their power.</note> and <gloss>ᵇ</gloss> compositions <note>original: "compositiones"; some manuscripts read "operations."</note>, and all things that are renewed in the heavens. It also descends to those things which occur in the air, such as comets, rainbows, and other things renewed there, so that we may know their locations, heights, magnitudes, shapes, and many things which must be considered in these matters. And all these things are accomplished through instruments suitable for these purposes, and through tables, and through canons—that is, rules invented for certifying these things—so that a path may be prepared for judgments <note>Predictions or assessments of influence.</note> which can be made according to the power of philosophy. This applies not only to natural things, but to those which take an inclination from nature and freely follow the celestial disposition. This is not only for the judgment of present, past, and future things, but for performing wonderful works, so that all prosperous things of this world may be promoted and adverse things may be repressed, usefully and magnificently. Nor are these things in doubt. For the patriarchs and prophets from the beginning of the world certified these things, just as they did others. It is clear, therefore, that celestial things are known through mathematics, and that a path is prepared through it to these lower things <note>The "lower things" refer to the terrestrial world or the "sublunary" sphere</note>. Moreover, that these lower things cannot be known without mathematics is evident first by this: that we do not truly know things except through their causes—if "knowledge" is taken in its proper sense, as Aristotle says. But celestial things are the causes of lower things. Therefore, these lower things will not be known unless the celestial things are known, and those cannot be known without mathematics. Therefore, the knowledge of these lower things depends upon the same.

Secondly, we can see from the properties of things that nothing of these lower or higher things can be known without the power of mathematics. For every natural thing is produced into being through an efficient cause <note>The "efficient cause" is the agent that brings about change</note> and the matter upon which it acts, for these two concur first. For the agent, through its power <term>virtus; the inherent force or energy of an object</term>, moves and transforms matter so that a thing may be made. But the power of the efficient cause and of the matter cannot be known without the great power of mathematics, just as the produced effects themselves cannot be known. There are, therefore, these three: the efficient cause, the matter, and the effect. In the celestial bodies, there is a mutual influence of powers, such as light and others, and there is alteration in them, though not leading to corruption <note>Bacon follows the medieval view that stars are incorruptible but can still exert influence</note>. And thus it can be shown that nothing in the world can be known without the power of geometry.

We have from this argument that <gloss>c</gloss> <note>original: "similiter" (similarly); the editor notes a variant "sicut" (just as)</note> similarly other parts of mathematics are necessary: for by the same reasoning that geometry is necessary, so are the others, and without doubt far more so, because they are more noble. If, therefore, the proposition is demonstrated in geometric terms, it is not necessary in this <gloss>d</gloss> persuasion <note>The editor confirms the reading "persuasione"</note> to speak of the others.

First, therefore, I demonstrate the geometric proposition from the perspective of the efficient cause. For every efficient cause acts through its power, which it produces in the <gloss>e</gloss> subject <note>The manuscripts read "patientem" (the thing acted upon), while the printed edition uses "subjectam" (subject)</note> matter, just as the light of the sun produces its power in the air, which is the light diffused through the whole world from the solar light. And this power is called a "likeness," and an "image," and a <term>species; a technical term for the visible or spiritual force emitted by an object that allows it to be perceived or to act at a distance</term>, and by many names; and both substance and accident <note>In Aristotelian terms, a "substance" is a thing itself, while an "accident" is a quality like color or heat</note> produce this, as do both spiritual and corporal things. And substance produces it more than accident, and the spiritual more than the corporal.

And this *species* performs every operation of this world; for it acts upon the sense, upon the intellect, and upon all the matter of the world through the generation of things, because one and the same thing is produced by a natural agent in whatever it acts upon. Because it does not have deliberation, it therefore does the same thing to whatever it encounters. But if it acts upon the sense and the intellect, a *species* is produced, as everyone knows. Therefore, conversely, <gloss>f</gloss> in matter <note>The editor notes the reading "in materia"</note> a *species* is also produced.
