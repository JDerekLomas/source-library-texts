# DEDICATORY LETTER

<meta>catchword: co-Lu-</meta>

...Your Highnesses, in accordance with the respect of my most devoted soul, so that I might at least declare some attempt at gratitude for the favors bestowed upon me by some testimony. Others have been accustomed, when they dedicate their scholarly labors <note>original: "lucubrationes," literally "work done by lamplight," implying diligent study</note> to Princes and Great Men, to concern themselves primarily with weaving their genealogies, recounting their praises and their brilliant and heroic deeds in an elegant style of writing, and—as the saying goes—proclaiming them with the "fullest trumpets" <note>original: "plenissimis tubis," a rhetorical metaphor for loud, public, or ostentatious praise</note>, raising and extolling them even to the stars. I, however, knowing that Your Highnesses find anything inflated or turgid with boastful fame to be distasteful—especially since that glorious and memorable excellence of yours, combined with great-souled generosity <term>magnanima munificentia: the classical virtue of "magnanimity" (greatness of soul) expressed through lavish and noble giving</term>, has flourished through the ages of so many centuries in the most August House of Brunswick—

<summary>In this portion of the dedication, the author expresses his desire to show gratitude for the Dukes' favors and contrasts his sincere approach with other writers who use exaggerated praise and complex genealogies to flatter their patrons.</summary>

# DEDICATORY LETTER

->* 4<-

...and Lüneburg House, has shone and flourished; nay, and by Divine favor, it shall shine and flourish into eternity. For this reason, I forgo all those aforementioned praises; instead, I approach bearing a gift. It is not, to be sure, a gift of great beauty or size—though You are most worthy of the finest and greatest things—nor one gleaming with gold, silver, gems, or pearls. Such riches Your Highnesses already possess in abundance through the generous bounty and favor of the Divine <note>original: "Numinis," referring to the divine will or the power of God</note>, whereas Fortune has denied them to me and to men of my humble station. It is a gift, I say, made of paper, yet one unearthed from the true, most ancient, and venerable art of <term>Proto-Chemistry</term> <note>original: "Proto-Chimica"; the author refers to the foundational or "first" chemistry, likely what we now call alchemy or early chemical philosophy</note>. This art holds more in its hidden depths than its outward appearance promises; indeed, it is the very means by which the uncreated Spirit, the Craftsman of the World <note>original: "mundi faber," a classical way of describing God as a divine architect or smith</note>, separated the tangled elements of nature. Through this art, indeed, by...

<header>L E T T E R</header>

I persuaded myself that I could give nothing more pleasing to Your Highnesses <note>original: "Cels: V:", an abbreviation for *Celsitudinibus Vestris*</note> than this new work of this Natural Science—an art most ancient and venerable, yet marvelous and utterly essential. It is a work, I say, of the utmost curiosity <note>In this context, "curious" implies something highly detailed, expert, or requiring great care</note>, which until now no one has attempted to restore because of the envy of others. Now, although unconquered truth is commendable by its own strength alone and is sufficiently fortified by its own excellent powers—for nothing can endure that is not solidified in the very foundation of Nature and equipped with this invincible protection—nevertheless, the purity of my writing, being dedicated and consecrated to Your Highnesses and armed with such splendor, shall go forth more boldly into the light and the gaze of men with doubled strength. Receive it, therefore, with a serene countenance, Most Serene and

->Most High<-

<summary>The author argues that while the "Natural Science" he presents is powerful enough to stand on its own merits, the prestige of the Dukes' patronage will protect the work from the envy of critics. He asks the nobles to receive his restored ancient knowledge with favor.</summary>
