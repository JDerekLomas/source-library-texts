### Naval Tactical Vocabulary

*   **Sacrificial Ships** <note>original: "bichuan" (弊船). This refers to vessels that were old, damaged, or otherwise decommissioned from active service, repurposed as expendable fire ships.</note>
*   **Wooden Rafts** <note>original: "mufa" (木筏). Simple floating platforms that could be constructed quickly to augment a fire attack when ships were in short supply.</note>
*   **Straw and Kindling** <note>original: "chuxin" (芻薪). The primary combustible materials used to fill the hulls of fire ships; these were often supplemented with oils, resins, or gunpowder to ensure a fierce blaze.</note>

*   **Fire Attack** <note>original: "huogong" (火攻). A foundational pillar of Chinese military strategy, involving the deliberate use of fire to break enemy formations and destroy logistics.</note>

### The Invention of the Multi-Stage Rocket

The <term>Fire Dragon Issuing from the Water (Huolong Chushui)<note>This is one of the world's earliest known examples of a two-stage rocket. It was designed to fly just above the surface of the water to strike enemy ships.</note></term> is a sophisticated weapon of the <term>Compendium of Military Assets (Junzi Cheng)<note>The specific division of the Wubei Zhi, a massive Ming-dynasty military encyclopedia, that details the logistics and specifications of weaponry.</note></term>.

The device is constructed from a wooden tube approximately five feet long, carved and painted to resemble a <term>Dragon (Long)<note>The dragon was not merely decorative; its hollow body served as the aerodynamic housing for the second stage of the weapon.</note></term>. This "dragon" serves as the primary missile body.

#### Technical Components

*   **Propulsion System:** Four large rocket boosters are attached to the outside of the dragon's body—two near the head and two near the tail. These are synchronized to ignite simultaneously, providing the initial thrust to carry the dragon across the water.
*   **The Second Stage:** Inside the hollow belly of the dragon are hidden numerous <term>Rocket-Arrows (Jian)<note>Small incendiary arrows propelled by their own individual gunpowder charges.</note></term>. 
*   **The Ignition Mechanism:** As the four exterior boosters reach the end of their flight, they ignite a secondary fuse. This internal fuse then triggers the rocket-arrows, which are discharged from the dragon’s mouth to strike the target.

### Tactical Use in Naval Combat

This weapon is specifically designed for use at sea. When enemy ships are several <term>li</term> <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance; at the time, approximately 500 meters.</note> away, the Fire Dragon is launched from a friendly vessel. It skims the surface of the waves, propelled by its boosters. Just as it reaches the enemy, the internal "arrows" fly out in a concentrated burst, incinerating the enemy's sails and decks.

### Construction of the Fire Dragon

To construct this device, use <term>Mao Bamboo (Maozhu)<note>Likely referring to Moso bamboo (Phyllostachys edulis), known for its strength and diameter, suitable for large projectiles.</note></term> five feet in length. Remove the internal nodes and use an iron blade to scrape the outer skin thin. At the front, attach a head carved from wood in the likeness of a dragon; at the rear, attach a carved dragon’s tail. The dragon's mouth should be angled slightly upward.

#### The Internal Payload

Inside the hollow "belly" of the dragon, load several <term>Divine Engine Rocket-Arrows (Shenji Huojian)<note>These were standard Ming Dynasty incendiary rockets—arrows propelled by gunpowder tubes attached to their shafts.</note></term>. Within the dragon’s head, leave a single opening (an "eye"). Gather all the fuses from the internal rocket-arrows together into a single point.

#### The Propulsion System (The First Stage)

On both sides of the dragon’s head, attach two rocket booster tubes, each weighing one and a half <term>catties (jin)<note>Approximately 0.9 kilograms or 20 ounces.</note></term>. 

*   **Orientation:** The nozzles of these tubes should face downward and backward, while their "bottoms" (the forward-facing ends) should point upward and forward. 
*   **Binding:** Use <term>hemp fiber (mapu)</term> and <term>fish glue (yujiao)<note>A traditional high-strength adhesive made from fish swim bladders, used here to ensure the boosters do not shear off during the violent acceleration of launch.</note></term> to bind them securely to the dragon’s body.

The fuses for the internal rocket-arrows are led out through the dragon's head, split into two paths, and protected with oiled paper. These are then connected to the forward ends of the external booster tubes. 

Two identical booster tubes are attached to the sides of the dragon’s tail in the same manner. The fuses of all four external booster tubes are then braided together into a single primary ignition cord.

#### Deployment and Effect

In naval combat, this device should be positioned approximately three to four feet above the water's surface. Once the primary cord is ignited, the dragon will fly across the surface of the water for a distance of two to three <term>li (approx. 1 kilometer)<note>The text describes a remarkably long range for the period, achieved by the aerodynamic lift of the bamboo body skimming the air just above the water.</note></term>. To an observer, it appears as if a dragon of fire is charging across the river...

### The Underwater Dragon King Cannon
<note>original: 水底龍王砲 (Shuidi Longwang Pao). This device is a timed naval mine, a sophisticated piece of 17th-century maritime technology.</note>

The mine is forged from wrought iron and mounted upon a wooden board. Its true ingenuity lies in the "hidden fire" <note>the internal ignition mechanism</note>. A length of incense is bound to the device to act as a timer; when the incense burns down to the priming cord, the charge is ignited. <gloss>The timing can be precisely set for one watch or three watches.</gloss> <note>A "watch" (geng) lasted approximately two hours, allowing the mine to be timed for up to six hours.</note>

The device is encased in a cow’s stomach; if it were completely airtight, the fire would be smothered for lack of air. Therefore, a sheep’s intestine is used as a ventilation tube, <gloss>extending slightly over ten feet in length</gloss>. The end of this tube uses goose or wild duck feathers as floats; as these feathers rise and fall with the rhythm of the waves, they prevent water from pouring into the tube, which would extinguish the fire. The subtle mystery of its mechanism is found in these details.

To deploy it, one must first measure the depth of the water where the enemy ships are anchored. The mine is weighted with heavy stones and released upstream during the darkness of night to drift toward the target. When the incense reaches the ignition point, the mine detonates from the seabed, striking the ship from below. The hull is shattered to pieces, and as the water rushes in, the enemy sinks—allowing them to be captured with ease.

*   Gunpowder <note>original: 發藥 (fayao)</note> <gloss>measuring one dou or five sheng</gloss> <note>These are units of dry volume; a dou is approximately 10 liters.</note>

# The Divine Oil Jar for Flying through the Air and Gliding across Water

<note>original: 飛空滑水神油罐 (Feikong Huashui Shenyou Guan). This name describes the weapon's versatile trajectory: it could be lobbed through the air or skipped across the water's surface like a stone.</note>

This weapon is a specialized <term>incendiary jar</term> <note>original: 火罐 (huoguan); a generic term for ceramic or metal vessels packed with gunpowder and combustible oils.</note> designed for naval engagements. The vessel is typically constructed from a rounded clay or thin iron pot, chosen for its ability to shatter upon impact and spread its burning contents.

The exterior of the jar is encased in a woven rope harness, which provides a grip for the soldier to swing and hurl the device with significant centrifugal force. At the crown of the vessel, a waterproof fuse is inserted into the <term>priming hole</term> <note>original: 火口 (huokou), literally 'fire mouth'</note>. 

When deployed against an enemy fleet:
1. The fuse is lit just before the jar is thrown.
2. If aimed at the deck, the "Flying through the Air" technique is used, lobbing the jar in a high arc to crash onto the enemy's wooden superstructure.
3. If aimed at the hull, the "Gliding across Water" technique is employed. By throwing the jar at a shallow angle, it is intended to skip along the surface of the waves—much like a skipping stone—until it strikes the side of an enemy ship at the waterline, where the resulting fire is most difficult to extinguish.

The "Divine Oil" contained within is a potent mixture of sulfur, saltpeter, and resinous oils <note>The 'oil' often included petroleum products or distilled oils that would float and continue burning even if the jar hit the water before the target.</note>. This ensures that even if the jar breaks prematurely, the burning oil will spread across the water, creating a literal "sea of fire" to block or ignite the approaching enemy.

Take the whites of goose, duck, and chicken eggs—ensuring all yolks are removed—and mix them with tung oil. Fill porcelain jars with this mixture and seal their mouths tightly. Attach a thin rope to serve as a handle or sling, allowing warriors with great arm strength to wield them. When the jars are approximately twenty to thirty feet <note>original: 二三丈 (er-san zhang); a zhang is a traditional unit roughly equal to 3.3 meters or 11 feet</note> from the enemy vessel, they should be hurled so that they shatter upon impact, causing the liquid to scatter and flow in all directions.

Combined with the surging wind and waves, the deck becomes so slippery that it is impossible to stand, and the enemy's <term>equipment</term> <note>original: 噐 (qi); referring here to heavy weapons and defensive tools that require stable footing to operate</note> cannot be effectively deployed. <gloss>Furthermore, once the oil coats the ship's planks, it catches fire easily and is prone to burning.</gloss>

# Instructions for the Camp-Exploding Ground-Thunder
<note>The "Ground-Thunder" (Dilei) refers to a variety of landmines used during the Ming Dynasty. This specific entry describes a "camp-exploding" (zhaying) variant designed to be hidden along paths or at the entrances of fortifications.</note>

For **ground-thunder** <term>Dilei: literally "earth thunder," the standard Ming term for a landmine</term> devices used to disrupt enemy camps, they should be deployed in large numbers at **strategic passes and narrow defiles**. 

To construct them, take **bamboo** with a circumference of nine <note>approx. 11 inches or 28 cm</note> inches. Saw the bamboo into sections five feet in length. Hollow out the internal joints, but leave the bottom-most joint intact to act as a base. 

First, bind the bamboo tightly with **raw oxhide rope**. Next, pour **boiling oil** into the tube and let it sit for a considerable time before pouring it out. 
<note>This process of oil-soaking seasoned the bamboo, making it resistant to moisture and preventing the casing from cracking in the soil, while also toughening the fibers to increase the pressure of the eventual blast.</note>

Install a **fuse** <term>Yaoxian: literally "powder line," a length of cord impregnated with gunpowder</term> at the bottom of the tube. Use a ramrod to pack the tube with **explosive cannon-powder** until it is eight-tenths full. Then, insert **lead and iron pellets** (shrapnel) into the remaining space and seal the mouth of the tube with **wax**.

### Deployment and Ignition

To set the trap:
1. Dig a pit in the ground five feet deep. 
2. At the bottom of the pit, place a **square wooden base** to hold eight of these bamboo tubes in an upright, vertical position. 
3. Use a **wooden cover** to separate the devices from the earth above; this ensures that the weight of the soil does not crush or interfere with the **ignition lead** <term>Yaoxin: the "message" or signal part of the fuse system</term>.
4. Gather the fuses from all eight tubes into a single point.
5. Within the pit, thread these fuses into a **steel-wheel fire-starter**. 
<note>original: 鋼輪火機 (Ganglun huoji). This was a sophisticated mechanical trigger, likely a wheellock mechanism where a serrated steel wheel struck flint or pyrite to create sparks when a tripwire was pulled.</note>

Once the mechanism is triggered, the response is instantaneous, and the charges will detonate in unison.

# The Housing and Ignition Assembly

The box measures one foot five inches in length and seven inches in width <note>Traditional Chinese measurements: 1 *chi* (foot) is approximately 31 cm, and 1 *cun* (inch) is about 3 cm.</note>. A hole is bored through the center of the box's bottom to allow the drop-rope to pass through.

Two holes are carved into each end of this box. These serve as channels for the gunpowder, the fuses <note>original: 藥線 (yaoxian)</note>, and the ignition strips <note>original: 焬條 (yangtiao). These were likely chemically treated strips that helped relay the spark to the main charge.</note>.

# The Drop-Pit and Trigger Mechanism

Below the assembly, there is a pit <note>original: 潭 (tan). While usually meaning a pool or pond, in this military context it refers to a deep excavated shaft or pit.</note> more than ten feet deep. Take a board one foot in length; one end of this board is tied securely with a rope. The other end is fitted with an iron bolt. 

Behind this bolt is a ring, which is fixed with a rope that leads to the exterior. This can be operated in two ways: either a person pulls the cord manually to trigger the device, or an object is used to lure the enemy into tripping the mechanism themselves. 

When the iron bolt <note>original: 鐵軌 (tiegui). Likely referring to the locking pin or rail that holds the weight in place.</note> is pulled out, the wooden board drops away from beneath, and the "thousand-catty stone" falls through the empty space. As it drops, the steel wheels are forced to rotate rapidly. The friction creates fire upon the stone, and the gunpowder is completely ignited. 

# The Effects of the Trap

Upon ignition, the force of the blast disperses wooden shrapnel <note>original: 柝條 (tuotiao). Split pieces of wood or bamboo used as projectiles.</note>, stone shot, and caltrops <note>original: 蒺藜 (jili). Spiked metal weapons strewn on the ground to maim horses and infantry.</note>, which go flying in all directions. It is as if a whole army has suddenly appeared; the defensive line holds firm, the very earth is overturned, and the mountains shake.

# Forging the Steel Wheels

To construct the core mechanism, use three catties <note>One *jin* (catty) is approximately 600 grams.</note> of high-quality purified steel. Forge this into two wheels, shaped like cart wheels, roughly the size of a bowl's mouth. 

Use a single horizontal iron bar, seven inches long. Carve square holes into the centers of the two wheels so the iron bar can be seated and embedded within them; these must be fastened very securely. 

On the parts of the iron bar where the wooden frame rests, you must file deep notches. These should fit into the gaps of the assembly so that the mechanism does not shift or wobble. In the center of the iron bar, there must be a small pin <note>original: 釘 (ding). A nail or peg acting as an axle or anchor.</note> around which the rope can be wound to facilitate the rotation.
