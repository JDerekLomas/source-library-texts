<term>Sancai Tuhui <note>original: 三才圖會</note></term> is a monumental pictorial encyclopedia compiled by the scholar **Wang Qi** and his son **Wang Siyi** during the late Ming Dynasty (published circa 1609). 

The title refers to the <term>Three Realms <note>original: 三才, Sancai</note></term>, a philosophical concept representing the three essential components of the universe: **Heaven**, **Earth**, and **Humanity**. This work serves as a comprehensive visual record of the era's knowledge, containing thousands of illustrations and descriptions regarding astronomy, geography, biology, history, and the arts.

The Kingdom of **Goryeo** <note>original: "Gaoli guo"; though the Joseon dynasty ruled at this time, Ming records often used the older name Goryeo</note> was anciently called **Joseon** <note>Chosŏn</note>. During the **Zhou dynasty**, it was also called Joseon because **King Wu** <note>the founder of the Zhou dynasty, c. 1046 BCE</note> enfeoffed **Jizi** <note>Kija, a legendary sage-prince from China</note> as the ruler of that country.

China’s systems of **rites and music**, the *Classic of Poetry*, the *Book of Documents*, medicine, and **divination** <term>buse; specifically divination using tortoise shells and yarrow stalks</term> all flowed into this land. Their government offices, official systems, and clothing all follow the regulations established by the various dynasties of China.

Their customs value **Confucian benevolence**; the people are gentle and loathe killing, and their judicial punishments are never cruel or brutal. However, their etiquette differs from that of China. For example:
*   When meeting royal relatives or high nobility, they "pull at the throat" <note>likely a gesture involving the collar or neck while bowing</note> and kneel with their knees on the ground. 
*   When a person of lower status meets someone of high status, they crouch their bodies and lower their heads in greeting. 

This behavior is similar to how Chinese people might look away or dare not look up when encountering bandits; these are the customs of the **border peoples** <note>original: "yidi"; a term used historically for non-Han ethnic groups, here used to describe cultural differences</note>, which they practice as a matter of habit.

The land does not produce fine horses. White stone found there can be fashioned into lamps and lighting fixtures. They also produce **black hemp**, which can be woven into **summer cloth** <note>original: "xiabu"; a lightweight, breathable fabric often made from ramie or hemp</note>.

The kingdom’s territory extends two thousand *li* <note>a Chinese mile, roughly 500 meters</note> from east to west and one thousand five hundred *li* from north to south. The King resides in **Kaizhou**, which is called **Kaesong Prefecture** <note>Kaesŏng, the capital of the Goryeo dynasty</note>.

<summary>This text describes the cultural and political ties between Korea and China, emphasizing Korea's adoption of Confucian systems and Chinese administrative structures while noting unique local social customs.</summary>

### The Jurchen State
The Jurchen State <note>original: "Nüzhen Guo"</note> is situated to the northeast of the Khitan territory. It lies at the base of the Changbai Mountains <note>the "Ever-White Mountains" on the border of modern China and North Korea</note> and encompasses the headwaters of the Yalu River. This region is the ancient land of the Sushen people. 

Their founding ancestor was a man from the kingdom of Silla <note>an ancient kingdom on the Korean peninsula</note> belonging to the Wanyan clan. He fled to these northern lands and his descendants settled there for generations. Because the land produces gold, they eventually adopted "Jin" <note>original: "Jin," meaning "Gold"</note> as the name of their state. When the leader Aguda <note>Wanyan Aguda (r. 1115–1123), the founder of the Jin Dynasty</note> rose to power, he declared himself Emperor. The people of this nation all use deer skins and fish skins to craft their clothing.

### The "Wild People" and Their Customs
Within this country, there is a particular group known as the "Wild People" <term>yeren; a term used by Ming scholars to describe the northernmost Jurchen tribes who did not adopt sedentary agriculture</term>. They bind knives to their knees and calves, never removing them whether they are traveling or at rest, day or night. They hold death in low regard and value physical strength above all else; they are as fierce and dangerous as tigers or wolves. 

The men of this tribe all tattoo their faces. When other Jurchens intermarry with these "Wild People," they also follow the custom of tattooing around their mouths.

## The Kingdom of Siam

The Kingdom of Siam <note>original: "Xianluo Guo"; a name derived from the 14th-century unification of the Xian (Sukhothai) and Luohu (Lavo/Ayutthaya) kingdoms</note> is located to the south of Champa <note>a historical Hindu-Buddhist kingdom in modern-day central and southern Vietnam</note>. 

The social customs of this land place great importance on the teachings of the Buddha <note>original: "Shijiao"</note>, and as a result, many of the inhabitants are monks. Their style of dress and ornamentation is identical to that of the people of Champa. A peculiar physical trait noted by observers is that the men of this nation have hair covering their entire bodies.

## The Kingdom of Siam

The Kingdom of Siam is situated along the coast. It is their custom that men, from a young age, have their male organs <note>original: "yangwu"</note> incised to insert "eight treasures" <note>original: "qian babao"; this refers to the historical practice of inserting decorative beads or small bells—often made of gold, silver, or pearls—beneath the skin, a custom documented by several Ming dynasty travelers in Southeast Asia</note>. This is performed as a means to display wealth and prestige; if a man does not undergo this procedure, no woman’s family will accept him as a husband.

Recently, a seafaring traveler on his way to Siam stopped at an island where the mountains were entirely covered with what appeared to be black-lacquered spoons and chopsticks <note>original: "chi jin"</note>. The area was filled with massive trees. Looking up, the traveler realized that these "spoons and chopsticks" were actually the flowers and stamens of the trees. 

He gathered over a hundred pairs of these natural implements to take back with him. In practical use, they were remarkably resistant to grime; even animal fat and grease could not stain them. However, on one occasion, he happened to use one to stir his tea, and it immediately dissolved away into the liquid.

<summary>This passage describes a unique social requirement for marriage among Siamese men involving ritual ornamentation and recounts a traveler's tale regarding a miraculous tree whose "chopstick-like" flowers were used as utensils until they dissolved in tea.</summary>

The Xiongnu consist of five distinct lineages. One type has yellow hair and heads; they are said to be the offspring of mountain spirits <term>shan gui</term> and yellow wild oxen. Another type consists of those with short necks and stout, squat frames; they are believed to be the descendants of the union between large apes and wild boars. A third type has black hair and white skin; these are the descendants of the soldiers left behind by Li Jing <note>original: "Li Jing"; a celebrated general of the Tang Dynasty known for his campaigns against the Turks</note>. 

Another type is named the Turks <term>Tujue</term>; their ancestor was born from the spiritual union of the Sea Goddess Shemo Sheli and a golden-horned white deer. Yet another lineage is that of the ancestors of Taba Chihan. *The Secret History of the Yuan Dynasty* <note>a 13th-century chronicle of the Mongol Empire</note> states they were born from a blue-gray wolf and a pale doe. Twenty-five generations later, Temujin <note>the birth name of Genghis Khan</note> was born. He was known as the Chieftain of all the Great Mongols and usurped the title of Emperor.

For generations, they lived six thousand *li* <note>approximately 2,000 miles</note> northeast of the desert. Later, they resided in the Shanyin region and were known as the Tatars. Their land produces sheep and horses. They have no walled cities or permanent houses, but instead follow the availability of water and grass. Their customs prize archery and hunting; they eat the meat of sheep, horses, and wild deer, and clothe themselves in leather and hides. Temujin's fourth-generation descendant, Kublai, usurped the rule of China and declared himself Emperor <note>referring to the establishment of the Yuan Dynasty</note>.

### The Kingdom of Champa

The **Kingdom of Champa** <note>original: "Zhancheng Guo"; located in central and southern Vietnam</note> was known as **Linyi** <note>original: "Lin-yi"</note> during the Han dynasty. Its subordinate districts include **Panduranga** <note>original: "Bin-tong-long"; an ancient city-state of the Cham people</note>, **Binduoling**, **Huazhou**, **Annan**, and the **City of Sanshe**. 

The money and grain used annually within their kingdom, as well as the private servants and slaves employed there, are all provided as tribute by **Annan** <note>Annan refers to northern Vietnam, which at various times was in conflict with or subordinate to Champa</note>. For this reason, the people of Champa refer to Annan as the "Slave State." 

On the border of the kingdom stand the **Bronze Pillars of Ma Yuan** <note>General Ma Yuan of the Han dynasty supposedly erected these pillars to mark the southern limit of the Chinese Empire</note>, located to the west of the sea. To the north, the kingdom borders Annan, and to the south, it reaches **Chenla** <note>original: "Zhen-la"; an ancient name for Cambodia</note>. Ships departing from **Guangzhou** can reach the kingdom in eight days with a favorable wind. 

Many of the inhabitants share the surname **Weng**. The land produces famous varieties of incense, rhinoceros horn, and elephants. The ground is composed entirely of white sand; there is very little land suitable for cultivation. 

If a citizen is devoured by a tiger or a crocodile, a report is submitted to the King. The King then orders the **State Preceptor** to recite incantations and write talismans, which are thrown into the place where the person died. The offending tiger or crocodile will then spontaneously appear at the site. 

In cases of lawsuits involving public deception where the officials are unable to reach a verdict, the parties are ordered to cross the **Crocodile Pool**. The person who is in the wrong will be eaten by the "fish" <note>The text uses "fish" (yu) here to refer to the crocodiles in the pool</note>, while the person who is in the right will be avoided by the creatures, who do not dare to eat them.

### Panhu

<note>Panhu, the "Dragon-Dog," is a central ancestor figure in the foundational myths of the Yao, She, and Miao peoples of southern China.</note>

The legend of **Panhu** <term>Panhu; a mythical creature with the body of a dog and the characteristics of a dragon</term> dates back to the era of **Emperor Gaoxin** <note>also known as Emperor Ku, one of the legendary Five Emperors of ancient China's golden age</note>. According to the tradition, the Emperor was plagued by an invasion from **General Wu** of the Quanrong tribes. He promised that anyone who could bring him the head of the enemy general would be granted the hand of his daughter in marriage. 

**Panhu**, then a palace dog with remarkable five-colored fur, traveled to the enemy camp, killed General Wu, and returned with his head. The Emperor, though surprised, honored his vow. The princess and Panhu married and retreated to the remote valleys of the south to live in seclusion. 

The accompanying woodcut illustrates several hunters in a dynamic mountain landscape. These figures—armed with a bow and arrow, a staff, and a sword—confront the beast in a scene that reflects the martial heritage and rugged lifestyle attributed to the "Mountain People" who claim Panhu as their sacred ancestor.

**Panhu** originates from the palace of **Emperor Ku** of the **Gaoxin** clan <note>Emperor Ku (or Gaoxin) is one of the Five legendary Emperors of high antiquity, traditionally said to have reigned in the 3rd millennium BCE</note>. An elderly woman in the palace suffered from an ear ailment; when she picked at it, she removed an object shaped like a silk cocoon. She placed it inside a **gourd** <note>original: "hu"</note> shell and covered it with a **plate** <note>original: "pan"</note>. 

Before long, the object transformed into a dog with five-colored fur. Because of the circumstances of its birth, it was named **Panhu**.

At that time, there was an invasion by the **Quanrong** <term>Quanrong; literally "Dog-Barbarians," a nomadic group who were historical rivals of the early Zhou and Han peoples</term>. **Jiaozhi** <gloss>Kōchi</gloss> <note>Historical name for northern Vietnam</note>, also known as **Annam**, is inhabited by a people said to be the descendants of the mountain-dwelling wild dogs. # The Kingdom of Chenla

<note>original: "Zhenla Guo." As noted previously, this refers to the Khmer civilization of Cambodia.</note>

To reach the **Kingdom of Chenla**, one sets sail from **Guangzhou** <note>A major port city in Southern China.</note>; with a favorable northern wind, the journey takes ten days. The climate there is never cold. Regarding their customs of marriage, the **groom always moves into the bride's household**. 

When a daughter reaches the age of nine, the family invites a monk to recite sutras and perform a **Buddhist ritual** <note>original: "fan fa." Literally "Brahmanic" or "Buddhist" methods/rituals.</note>. During this rite, the monk uses his finger to ceremonially rupture the girl's hymen <note>original: "tiao sun tong shen." The mother also uses this blood to mark her own forehead, a practice they call **"attaining a lucky market"** <note>original: "li shi." Generally, girls marry once they reach the age of ten. 

If a wife has an affair with a guest, the husband is actually pleased rather than angry. He will boast to others, saying: "My wife is beautiful and clever, which is why others love her." 

As for the laws of the land: those who commit **theft** have their hands and feet amputated, or are branded with fire on their chests and backs, and marked on their foreheads. For capital offenses, the criminal is **beheaded**, or their body is impaled upon a carved wooden stake. If a local kills a **Chinese person** <term>Tang ren</term> <note>Literally "Person of Tang," a common term for Chinese people used abroad long after the Tang Dynasty ended.</note>, they must pay with their life according to the local "barbarian" laws. However, if a Chinese person kills a local, they are only required to pay a heavy fine in gold. If they lack the gold, they must sell themselves into slavery to raise the funds for their redemption.

To the north, the kingdom reaches **Champa** <note>original: "Zhan Cheng." An ancient kingdom in central and southern Vietnam.</note>. Nearby are the kingdoms of **Xipeng** <note>Possibly a transcription of a local Mon-Khmer or Thai polity; "West Peng."</note> and others. In one corner of the sky above this country, there is a slight blemish or mark. Ancient lore says this is a spot that **Nüwa** <note>The mother goddess of Chinese mythology, known for repairing the pillars of heaven and the broken sky with five-colored stones.</note> did not reach during her repairs of the heavens.

# The Kingdom of Java
<note>original: "Zhaowa Guo." Java was a major maritime power in modern-day Indonesia. During the Ming Dynasty, it was a frequent destination for Chinese trade missions.</note>

## The History of the Huihu

The **Huihu** <gloss>translated as *Yiyuan*</gloss> <note>original: 譯元 (Yìyuán). This is likely a phonetic variation or a specific transcription used in certain historical records to denote the Uyghur people</note> are the same people formerly known as the **Huihe** <note>original: 回紇 (Huíhé), an earlier phonetic rendering of "Uyghur"</note>. Their ancestors were originally part of the **Xiongnu** <note>original: 匈奴, the ancient nomadic confederation of the Eurasian Steppe</note>, consisting of fifteen distinct tribes. By the time of the Sui Dynasty <note>581–618 CE</note>, they were called the **Weihe** and were subjects of the **Tujue** <note>original: 突厥 (Tūjué), the Göktürks or "Celestial Turks"</note>. The **Tujue** relied upon the wealth and resources of these tribes to exert their dominance over the northern wilderness. 

During the Daye era <note>605–618 CE, the reign of Emperor Yang of Sui</note>, they began calling themselves the **Huihe**. Their leader at that time was named **Pusa** <note>original: 菩薩 (Púsà). While this word typically means "Bodhisattva" in a Buddhist context, here it serves as a phonetic transliteration of a specific historical chieftain's name</note>. Among all the groups living under the rule of the **Tujue**, the **Huihe** were by far the strongest. 

After **Pusa** died, their chieftain joined with various other tribes to attack the **Xueyantuo** <note>original: 薛延陀 (Xuēyántuó), the Seyanto people, another Turkic nomadic group</note>. They devastated the **Xueyantuo** and took possession of their lands, subsequently sending envoys to China to offer their formal submission and tribute.

They often compared their own ferocity and swiftness to that of a falcon <note>the character 鶻 (hú) refers to a falcon or migratory bird of prey</note>. Consequently, during the reign of **Emperor Dezong of Tang** <note>reigned 779–805 CE</note>, they formally petitioned the imperial court to change their name from **Huihe** to **Huihu**. The term <gloss>translated as *Yiyuan*</gloss> is simply a phonetic shift in the pronunciation of **Huihu**. 

Their homeland was originally situated in **Karakorum** <note>original: 哈刺和林 (Hālā Hélín), the ancient capital of the Mongol Empire and earlier Steppe powers</note>, which is known today as the **Hening Circuit** <note>original: 和寧路 (Héning Lù), an administrative division established during the Yuan Dynasty</note>.

## The Kingdom of Khotan (Continued)

In the **Kingdom of Khotan** <term>Yutian (于闐國)</term>, the women wear upper shirts and trousers secured with sashes, dressing in a manner identical to the men. When a person dies, they are cremated; the bones are then gathered and buried.

Buddhist scriptures record that the Buddha once saw a wild goose fall dead to the ground and buried it with sand. Following this example, people later constructed burial mounds consisting of several levels, which the people of the Western Regions <term>Hu (胡)</term> <note>A general term used in historical Chinese texts to refer to various non-Han peoples of Central and Northern Asia</note> call **Goose Pagodas** <term>Yanta (鴈塔)</term>. After the Buddha entered **Nirvana** <term>Niepan (涅槃)</term> <note>The final liberation from the cycle of birth and death; the passing of a Buddha</note>, his followers followed the established precedent and likewise created a burial mound of sand. His disciples lived at and guarded the entrance of the tomb mound; for this reason, they were called **Shamen** <term>Śramaṇa (沙門)</term> <note>The text provides a folk etymology here: "sha" meaning sand and "men" meaning gate/door. While the term actually derives from the Sanskrit word for an ascetic, the author explains it through the legend of guarding the sand-mound tomb.</note>. The practice of calling monks "Shamen" began from this.

In China, those who revere Buddhism all imitate these foreign customs, adding exquisite craftsmanship to build structures of seven stories. They decorate these with gold and vibrant colors in order to seek spiritual merit and blessings.

# The Kingdom of Bibiara in the Arab Lands

<note>original: 'Dashi Bibiara Guo' (大食弼琶羅國). 'Dashi' refers to the Arab world. 'Bibiara' is likely a phonetic transcription of Berbera, a historic port city on the coast of modern-day Somaliland, which was a significant node in the Indian Ocean trade networks known to the Ming dynasty.</note>

The Kingdom of **Bibiara**, located within the Arab territories, consists of four administrative districts. The land has no single sovereign king; instead, local **landed elites** <term>tuhao (土豪): local powerful families or gentry who exercised regional authority</term> take turns presiding over public affairs.

<note>The text clarifies that this "human tail" (人尾) refers to the male genitalia (陽物). This likely reflects a misunderstood or sensationalized traveler's account of local rites of passage or warrior trophies required for marriage in certain East African or Arabian coastal cultures.</note>

# The Kingdom of Dogs

# The Kingdom of Dashiguo
<note>original: 大食國; *Dàshí Guó*. This was the historical Chinese name for the Arab Empire or the Islamic Caliphate, derived from the Persian word *Tāzī*.</note>

The Kingdom of **Dashiguo** is located one thousand *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance, approximately 500 meters.</note> to the southwest in the sea, situated amidst mountain valleys. 

There is a tree here where the flowers on the branches grow in the shape of **human heads**. These heads do not understand human speech; if a person asks them a question, they only **laugh**. If they laugh too frequently, they wither and fall from the branch.
