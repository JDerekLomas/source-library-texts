### CHAPTER I

## THE NATURE OF THE AIR AND VAPORS

It is necessary, before we proceed to the illustrations of the clouds, to define the very substance of our sky. The air is not a mere vacuum, but a fluid of great subtlety, which, as the ancients said, <note>The author refers to the classical Aristotelian view of the four elements.</note> seeks its own level above the earth and water. 

<margin>The weight of the air is measured by the mercury tube.</margin>

We must consider that the atmosphere is a vast laboratory. Within it, the heat of the sun acts upon the waters of the sea to raise them in the form of invisible breath. <insert>This process is what we now call evaporation.</insert> When this breath encounters the cold of the higher regions, it thickens and becomes visible to the eye.

#### TABLE OF OBSERVATIONS
| Time of Day | State of the Sky | Temperature (Reaumur) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Morning | Clear and serene | 12 degrees |
| Noon | Scattered vapors | 18 degrees |
| Evening | Dense clouds in the West | 15 degrees |

<column-break/>

The colors of the sky are not inherent to the air itself, but are the result of the light being broken and scattered. <gloss>The scattering of light rays.</gloss> As the sun descends toward the horizon, the rays must pass through a greater thickness of the lower, more "earthy" air, which gives us the magnificent reds and purples of the sunset.

->**On the Formation of Dew**<-

I have observed that in the early hours, the grass is often wet even when no rain has fallen from the heavens. This is the "sweat of the earth," <note>original: "sudor terrae"</note> caused by the cooling of the ground which turns the nearby air into liquid drops. 

<margin>See the experiments of Mr. Boyle on the spring of the air.</margin>

It is a marvel of the Creator that such a delicate balance is maintained, for if the air were too thick, we could not breathe; if it were too thin, the waters of the world would soon vanish into the void of space. <unclear>The mathematical proportions of this balance remain a mystery.</unclear>

# ON THE COLOR OF THE ATMOSPHERE

I say that the blueness we see in the atmosphere is not its own color, but is caused by warm moisture evaporated into minute and imperceptible atoms, which catches the rays of the sun and becomes luminous against the immense darkness of the upper regions. <note>Leonardo is correctly intuiting the scattering of light, though he attributes it to "warm moisture" rather than gas molecules.</note>

<margin>The sky has no color of its own.</margin>

You can see this, as I did, by climbing <term>Monviso</term> <note>A prominent mountain in the Cottian Alps, near the border of Italy and France.</note>, where the atmosphere becomes so thin that the sky above appears almost black, while the valleys below are shrouded in a thick, white mist. 

### THE DENSITY OF THE AIR

The air is more dense the closer it is to the earth, and the thinner it is, the higher it rises. <insert>This is why the mountains appear blue in the distance.</insert> 

| OBSERVATION | EFFECT |
|-------------|--------|
| Thick air near the horizon | Appears white or yellowish |
| Thin air in the heights | Appears deep blue or black |
| Moisture in the air | Scatters the light of the sun |

->The sun does not color the sky, but reveals the particles within it.<-

<column-break/>

Furthermore, if you look at a dark object through a long stretch of illuminated air, that object will appear blue. This is why the shadows of trees appear blue in the distance, even though we know the leaves are green. <note>original: "azzurro"; Leonardo uses this term to describe the optical effect of distance and atmospheric perspective.</note>

<gloss>The shadow is the absence of light.</gloss>

<summary>Leonardo investigates the optical causes of the sky's blue color, attributing it to the illumination of moisture particles against the darkness of space, supported by his observations during mountain climbs.</summary>
<keywords>Atmospheric perspective, Monviso, light scattering, air density, optics, blue sky, shadows</keywords>

# ON THE MOVEMENT OF AIR AND THE NATURE OF CLOUDS

The air is moved by the heat of the sun, which draws up the vapors from the earth and the waters. <note>Leonardo is describing the process of evaporation and convection, which he viewed as a mechanical movement of "elements."</note> These vapors, being lighter than the surrounding air, rise until they reach a region of greater cold, where they are condensed into clouds.

<margin>The sun is the primary cause of all motion in the elements.</margin>

### OF THE DENSITY OF VAPORS

When the vapor is thick and heavy, it forms a dark cloud; when it is thin, it appears as a light mist. <insert>Observe how the wind breaks the edges of the clouds into fine threads.</insert> 

->**The Experiment of the Heated Vessel**<-

If you place a vessel of water over a fire, you will see the steam rise in a straight line until it encounters the cooler air of the room. There it begins to swirl and expand, just as the clouds do in the great expanse of the sky. <note>Leonardo frequently used domestic analogies—like boiling water—to explain planetary or atmospheric phenomena.</note>

<column-break/>

### OF THE WIND AND ITS ORIGIN

Wind is nothing other than a flow of air, caused by an inequality of heat and moisture. <gloss>Air moves from where it is compressed to where it is rare.</gloss> 

| TYPE OF CLOUD | CAUSE OF FORMATION | EFFECT ON WEATHER |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cumulus** <note>original: "nuvoli ammassati"</note> | Rising heat from the earth | Fair weather, if not too high |
| **Cirrus** <note>original: "nuvoli sfilati"</note> | High winds in the upper air | Change in the wind direction |
| **Nimbus** <note>original: "nuvoli oscuri"</note> | Great accumulation of moisture | Rain and tempest |

The air that is closest to the earth is denser than that which is above, because it supports the weight of the air that rests upon it. <note>This is a remarkably early and accurate intuition of atmospheric pressure and density gradients.</note>

<summary>Leonardo analyzes the physical causes of cloud formation and wind, using the principle of heat-driven motion and providing a classification of cloud types based on their appearance and weather effects.</summary>

# STUDIES ON THE ATMOSPHERE AND THE BRIGHTNESS OF THE AIR

The blue of the sky is not an inherent color, but is caused by heated moisture which has evaporated into minute and imperceptible particles. These particles, when struck by the rays of the sun, become luminous against the deep, intense darkness of the region of fire <note>In the medieval and Renaissance worldview, the "region of fire" was the uppermost sphere of the elements, located just below the moon, which was thought to be pitch black.</note> which forms a boundary above them.

<column-break/>

<margin>The density of the air.</margin>
This can be seen, as I myself observed, by anyone who ascends the **Monviso** <note>A prominent mountain in the Cottian Alps; Leonardo likely climbed its lower slopes or observed it closely during his travels.</note>, a peak of the Alps which separates France from Italy. At the base of this mountain spring the four rivers that water four different regions of Europe: the Po, the Ticino, the Rhône, and the Duranza. 

There, the atmosphere is so thin that it cannot support the clouds that rise from the valleys. The sky above appears a dark, almost black blue, while the sun appears much brighter than in the plains below.

### ON THE REFLECTION OF LIGHT

->The shadow of the earth<-

If the air were not filled with these tiny particles of moisture, the sky would remain as black as it appears at night. Therefore, the brightness we see during the day is merely reflected light. 

<insert>The higher one goes, the darker the sky becomes.</insert>

As the sun sets, the shadow of the earth rises through the atmosphere, and we see the blue turn to gold and then to a deep violet before the stars appear. This is because the light must pass through a greater thickness of the lower, denser air.
