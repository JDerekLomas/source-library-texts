# Preface

<image-desc>A large ornamental drop cap 'O' initiates the text, decorated in vibrant red and blue. Inside the 'O' is a miniature painting of a green leafy plant with red bell-shaped flowers, growing from a small mound of earth.</image-desc>

When I frequently turn over in my mind, with repeated consideration, the wondrous works of the Almighty and Eternal God, the Creator of all nature, I marvel at them. **How, from the beginning of the world, having created the heavens, He adorned them with the most shining stars.** To these stars He gave the power and virtue to influence all uncreated things beneath the heavens. How also He brought forth from nothing, in a most beautiful order, the four elements, which differ among themselves in a marvelous way: namely Fire, hot and dry; Air, hot and moist; Water, cold and moist; and Earth, dry and cold—bestowing upon each its own proper nature. How, moreover, the Supreme Artisan and Master, after producing herbs, stones, and all living creatures, finally made and fashioned Man, more noble than all other creatures. **And thus He disposed all things, so that everything under heaven would be arranged in a beneficial manner.** Through the mediation of the stars, they would receive their nature and permanent substance. And there would be in all things—whether herbs, stones, or even animals—a mixture of the natures of the four elements emerging from them: heat, cold, moisture, and dryness. **Also in human bodies, these exist in the best measure and temperament <note>The "temperament" refers to the balance of the four humors: blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile.</note> suitable for the life and nature of man.** **And while that temperament remains balanced, a person enjoys perfect health.** But when it is dissolved through the loss of proper governance—when heat, becoming victorious over the other natures, labors to expel cold, or when cold wishes to extinguish heat, **or if a man should be filled with disordered humors,** or if he should depart too far from his natural moisture through a deficiency—**he immediately falls into great infirmities, approaching death.** **The reason for this dissolution of the natures in which the life and health of men consist is not single, but manifold.** For sometimes it is the hidden, poisonous influence of the heavens contrary to man's nature; **at other times it is the impurity of the air, or unsuitable food, or disordered labor,** and many other things that lead a man away from perfect health. If I wished to enumerate them all, it would be easier for me (I believe) to count the sands of the sea. **Truly, since more than a thousand dangers surround man, and the accidents dissolving man's health are almost innumerable according to the various influences of the stars, there is scarcely a moment to be found in which a man is truly secure in his health or life.** **While I was silently revolving these things within me, I remembered on the other hand the wisdom of the Creator, who from the beginning provided for the human race, set amidst such great dangers, a salutary and suitable remedy**

<column-break/>

through herbs, animals, and other creatures, into which He instilled such virtue that they might lead disordered natures back to their proper temperament, harmony, and proportion. **For one herb provides heat, another induces cold, each according to the degree of its own complexion and nature. And so other creatures living in the waters or on the earth serve for the preservation of human life,** the Creator of nature so disposing it. **Thus, by the virtue of these herbs and other creatures, a man destitute of strength through a distemper of the primary qualities <note>The primary qualities are hot, cold, moist, and dry.</note> may return to full health. And because in this mortal and transitory life it is impossible for a man to possess a treasure more dear or desirable than true health of the body,** as the Wise Man says in **Ecclesiasticus 30:** <note>original: "Ecclesiastici .xxx."</note> "Health of the soul in holiness is better than all gold and silver, and a sound body than immense revenues. For there is no riches above the riches of the health of the body." It seemed to me, therefore, that it would be best to compose a book containing a multitude of figures of herbs, stones, animals, and other creatures, depicted in their certain proper colors, including also a description of their virtues. I could not have performed a more laudable work, since it brings great solace to those in good health and offers no small utility to those who are suffering. Moreover, charity urged me first and most principally to complete this task; it made me feel compassion specifically for those who lack temporal means and money for the necessity of hiring doctors and apothecaries. **For these people, aided by the teaching of this book, with very small expenses occurring, will be able to provide for themselves preservative remedies and perfected medicines.** **I was no less moved to undertake this, and indeed most especially, by a certain noble lord <note>Likely referring to Bernhard von Breydenbach or the patron of the work.</note> who, by traveling through various kingdoms and lands—namely Germany, Italy, Austria, Slavonia, Croatia, Dalmatia, Greece, Corfu, Morea, Crete, Rhodes, Cyprus, and also the Holy Land with its city Jerusalem; from there going through Little Arabia toward Mount Sinai; from Mount Sinai toward the Red Sea, Cairo, and Babylon <note>In medieval texts, "Babylon" often refers to Old Cairo (Babylon of Egypt).</note>; through Alexandria even into Egypt**—and other places, gained great experience regarding the aforementioned herbs, animals, stones, and other things necessary for the making of medicines, which were unknown due to their rarity. Describing their virtue and their likenesses under the lines of their features, form, and color, you will find them depicted in the present work in exquisite order. **Moved therefore by these two reasons, I began this laudable work, relying on the help of God, and compiled it from the most proven physicians.**

# Treatise

->of the masters: namely Avicenna, Hippocrates, Galen, Vincent, Serapion, Platearius, Pliny, Dioscorides, the Pandects <note>likely referring to the *Pandectae Medicinae* by Matthaeus Silvaticus</note>, Johannes Mesue, Palladius, Constantine, and Almansor. And many others no less experienced. It was decided that it should be called the *Hortus Sanitatis* (The Garden of Health). This entire book is divided into eight treatises, each containing a unique subject. The first treatise, therefore, concerns herbs and other things that contribute to medicinal uses. In this, you will also find suitable illustrations of the herbs. ¶ The second concerns the nature and temperaments of many animals that lead their lives on land. ¶ The third concerns birds and their natures. ¶ The fourth concerns fish and their properties and natures. ¶ The fifth concerns precious stones and their virtues. ¶ The sixth concerns the disposition of urine, its color, and its significance. ¶ The seventh contains a register for the easy discovery of illnesses and remedies. ¶ The eighth and last contains a table according to alphabetical order, demonstrating everything contained in this book. ¶ May this most pleasant, fragrant garden of ours go forth into the whole world and refresh all who contemplate it. ¶ Therefore, O Creator of the heavens and the earth, who has infused a curative power into all herbs, animals, stones, and other things contained in this work: I return immense thanks, since You have given me this treasure, hidden until now, to bring into the light. ¶ To You be honor and glory forever and ever. Amen.<-

## Chapter .I.

Arona. *Pandecta* chapter 1. *Aaron* in Greek; *Siricantica* in Arabic. In Latin, however, it is called *Barba aaron* <note>Aaron's Beard</note>, or *laurus*, or *serpentaria minor* <note>Lesser Snakeweed</note>. ¶ In the same place: Arum is an herb having leaves similar to dragontea <note>Dragon Arum</note>, but smaller and smooth, not variegated; having a stalk of two palms' length and a purple color, the seed of which is saffron-colored. It has a white root having a round bulb similar to dragontea, which can be eaten raw or cooked, because it is not very bitter. Its leaves are eaten when placed in salt. It is of a hot and dry temperament in the first degree, with the power of dissolving, loosening, and thinning. ¶ Galen, in book 2 of *On Foods*, near the end of the chapter on Arum: Indeed, the root of this plant, when eaten, is similar to that of the rape <note>turnip</note>. And he adds: And one must first pour off its water after boiling, then put it into other hot water, as was said regarding cabbage and lentils. ¶ And the same author in book 6 of *Simple Medicines*, in the chapter on Arum or *serpentaria minor*: For this proceeds from a hot earthly substance. And therefore it is of a cleansing virtue, yet not violently so like dragontea; but in heating and drying it is of the first degree in both. But its roots are more useful. ¶ Paulus <note>Paulus Aegineta</note> in the chapter on Arum: It is of a hot and dry temperament in the first degree.

## Chapter 3. <margin>wormwood</margin>

*Absinthium* <note>wormwood</note> is called *absinthion* in Latin and Greek, and *sarifon* in Arabic. *Absinthium* is a Greek name; it is most likely named because it grows in the region of Pontus <note>an ancient region on the southern coast of the Black Sea</note>. Hence it is also called *ponticum*. ¶ Pliny, in his book on simple medicines, says: There are two kinds of wormwood. One is found on the island of Pontus, or it is so named because it has a "pontic" <note>astringent or seawater-like</note> flavor; it has a green color and a bitter taste, and this kind is of lesser effect. The other is Roman wormwood: the better kind of this is that which is far removed from the smell of the sea and which grows in open lands. It has white, smooth, and flat leaves; it is gathered at the end of spring, dried in the shade, and preserved in a cloth. Wormwood has two contrary virtues: a constrictive power from its dense substance and astringency, and a laxative power from its heat and bitterness. ¶ Galen, in the letter he sent to Gluto, says: Wormwood possesses contrary virtues: one laxative and the other styptic <note>contracting or binding</note>. Because of this, if it is given during a disease that is not yet "digested" <note>matured or ready to be expelled</note>, it hardens the stubborn matter as it tries to flee, whereby it creates a sort of war. For the laxative virtue moves the matter so that it might exit through bowel movements, while the styptic virtue hardens the matter and prevents it from moving, whereby nature suffers. However, when given once the matter of the disease is digested, it becomes obedient and easy to expel in two ways: first, because its laxative power dissolves the matter; second, because its astringency helps to compress and exclude the matter of the disease which is in the cavities of the limbs. ¶ And Hippocrates, in a certain letter: Wormwood is moderately hot. Its juice, however, greatly removes choleric humors <note>yellow bile</note> if it is infused in wine; it purges through the urine. For this reason, it does not help phlegm residing in the belly, for it has a styptic virtue, and the virtue within it is bitter. ¶ Constantinus <note>Constantine the African</note>, in his *Book of Degrees*: Wormwood is hot in the first degree and dry in the second. ¶ And in the *Book of the Names of Things*: One kind of wormwood is greener and more bitter; another is whitish and exceedingly bitter. But it must have contrary qualities: namely, constrictive from the thickness of its substance, and laxative from its bitterness and heat. ¶ Avicenna: Wormwood is an herb with leaves similar to oregano. It is hot in the first degree and dry in the second. Its juice is drier, in the third degree. It is a bitter, styptic, opening, and sharp herb. ¶ Pliny, in book 27: There are several kinds of wormwood. *Santonicum*, named after a city in Gaul; *Ponticum*, from Pontus, where the livestock grow fat on it, and for that reason they are found without gall <note>bile</note>. Nor is any other kind more excellent, and it is much more bitter than the Italian variety. But the "marrow" <note>pith or essence</note> of the Pontic herb is the easiest to use and the most useful of all.

## Operations <note>Medical Uses</note>

<margin>a</margin> ¶ Almansor <note>referring to Rhazes</note>, in book 6: Wormwood strengthens the stomach, and for this reason, its flavor is transferred into wines. Taken as a drink, it wards off sea-nausea during voyages.

<margin>b</margin> ¶ Its juice preserves books and wood from worms and mice for many years.

<margin>c</margin> ¶ It is also very effective for digestion and for comforting the stomach; if cooked with oil and used to anoint human bodies, it preserves them from the bite and nuisance of fleas.

<margin>d</margin> ¶ It is an excellent remedy against the loss of speech. Therefore, wormwood is indeed a most bitter herb, but beneficial to human health.

<margin>e</margin> ¶ Avicenna: Wormwood prevents clothes from being eaten by moths and the corruption of worms. Wormwood renders books safe from mice and moths.

<margin>f</margin> ¶ Against intestinal worms: the juice of wormwood should be given with the powder of betony, or centaury, or peach-wort, or with the juice of peach kernels or their leaves.

<margin>g</margin> ¶ Johannes Mesue, in the chapter on wormwood: Wormwood draws out bile from the stomach, intestines, and liver. Its operation extends to the veins and cleanses them of choleric and watery humors. Sometimes it leads these out through the urinary tracts. Wormwood preserves against putrefaction, especially when taken as a drink of its infusion in wine, or a decoction of about 1 or 2 drams <term>drachma: a unit of weight, approx. 3.5 grams</term> every day, or its distilled water.

<margin>h</margin> ¶ Also, an oil made from it is beneficial for ear pain, ringing, and deafness. Rinsing the mouth with a decoction of it and citron peel is beneficial for foul breath.

<margin>i</margin> ¶ Also, wormwood corroborates the stomach and liver and excites a dead appetite.

<margin>j</margin> ¶ It opens obstructions and cures obstructive diseases; it kills and expels worms, especially as a plaster made in this manner: Take 2 drams of wormwood, 1 dram of euphorbium <note>a medicinal resin</note>, half a dram of burnt hartshorn <note>deer antler</note>, and as much honey as suffices.
