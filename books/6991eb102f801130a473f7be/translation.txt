**T**HE long obscurity of the Dark Ages lifted over Italy as it awakened to a national, though divided, consciousness. Already two distinct tendencies were apparent. On one hand, the practical and rational spirit was soon to be reflected outwardly in the middle-class citizen life of Florence and the Lombard cities; at Rome, this spirit had even then created the civil organization of the <term>curia</term> <note>The administrative body and court of the Pope.</note>. The <term>*novella*</term> <note>A short, realistic prose narrative, such as those found in Boccaccio's *Decameron*.</note> was its literary triumph. In art, this practical spirit expressed itself simply, directly, and with vigor. 

Opposed to this was the other great undercurrent in Italian life—mystical, religious, and speculative—which had run through the nation from the earliest times. It received fresh strength from medieval Christianity, encouraging an ecstatic mysticism that drove the populations of its mountain cities into a religious frenzy. Umbrian painting was inspired by this spirit, and the glowing words of Jacopone da Todi <note>A Franciscan friar and poet (c. 1230–1306) known for his intense religious poems or "laude."</note> expressed in poetry the same religious fervor that the life of Florence and Perugia witnessed in action.

Italy developed out of the relationship and conflict between these two forces: the rational and the mystical. Their later union in the greatest men was to—

The first offspring of this union was Leonardo. Since the decline of ancient art, no painter had been able to fully express the human form, for an imperfect mastery of technique still proved to be a barrier. Leonardo was the first to completely free his personality from this constraint and make line express thought as no one before him could do. Nor was this his only triumph, but rather the foundation on which further achievement rested. Remarkable as a thinker alone, he preferred to enlist thought in the service of art and make art the handmaid of beauty. Leonardo saw the world not as it is, but as he himself was. He viewed it through the atmosphere of beauty that filled his mind and tinted its shadows with the mystery of his nature. To all this, his birthright as a painter, a different element was added. A keen desire for knowledge, guiding his actions in life, spurred him onward. Conscious of this dominant impulse, he fancifully described himself in a Platonic allegory. He had passed beneath overhanging cliffs on his way to a great cavern. On bended knees, peering through its darkness, fear and desire had overwhelmed him—fear of the menacing darkness of the cavern, and desire

From his earliest years, the elements of greatness were present in Leonardo. However, his genius matured independently of outside influences. He took little notice of the major historical forces he encountered during his life. Following his promising childhood in the Tuscan hills, he spent his youth in Florence apprenticed to the master Verrocchio, <note>Andrea del Verrocchio (c. 1435–1488) was a leading Florentine painter and sculptor whose workshop was a training ground for many Renaissance masters.</note> alongside others whose names would later brighten the pages of Italian art history. During this time, he must have heard the fiery sermons of Savonarola, <note>Girolamo Savonarola was a Dominican friar known for his prophecies of civic glory and calls for Christian renewal; he famously led the "Bonfire of the Vanities."</note> yet, unlike Botticelli, he remained indifferent to the preacher’s pleas. He likely encountered Lorenzo the Magnificent, <note>Lorenzo de' Medici, the de facto ruler of the Florentine Republic and a famous patron of scholars and artists.</note> but there were few opportunities within the Medici family's inner circle for the young painter, who first had to seek success elsewhere. 

The splendor of Milan under *Il Moro* <note>original: "Il Moro" (The Moor), the nickname of Ludovico Sforza due to his dark complexion.</note>—then the most brilliant court in Europe—attracted him. He went there, proclaiming his abilities in a remarkable letter and boasting that he could accomplish much, though his primary desire was to create a great monument to the glory of the Sforza family. He spent many years at that court, occupied by his various ventures—painting, sculpture, engineering, and even organizing festivities—but his grandest project was destined for failure, caught up in the political downfall of Ludovico Sforza. Even to this disaster, he remained detached. Introduction

After the overthrow of the Duke of Milan, his Italian wanderings began. At one time he contemplated entering the service of an Oriental prince. Instead, he entered that of Caesar Borgia as a military engineer, and the greatest painter of the age became the inspector of a despot’s strongholds. But his restless nature did not leave him at this for long. Returning to Florence, he competed with Michelangelo; yet even the service of his native city could not retain him. His fame had attracted the attention of a new patron of the arts, the prince of the state which had conquered his first master. In this, his last venture, he forsook Italy, only to die three years later at Amboise, in the castle of the French king.

The inner nature of Leonardo remained as untouched by the men he encountered as by the events which were then stirring Europe. Alone, he influenced others, while remaining a mystery to all. The most gifted of nations failed to understand the greatest of her sons. Isabella d’Este, the first lady of her time, seeking vainly to obtain some product of his brush, was told that his life was changeful and uncertain, and that he lived for the day, intent only on his art. His own thoughts reveal him in another light. “I wish to work miracles,” he wrote. And elsewhere he exclaimed, “Thou, O God, sellest us all benefits at the cost of our toil. . . . As a day well spent makes sleep

seem pleasant, so a life well employed makes death pleasant. A life well spent is long.”

Leonardo’s views on aesthetics are all-important in his philosophy of life and art. A worker’s thoughts on his craft are always of interest. They are doubly so when there is no trace of literary self-consciousness to blemish their expression. He recorded these thoughts at the moment of their birth, for a constant habit of observation and analysis had early developed in him into a second nature. His ideas were penned in the same fragmentary way as they presented themselves to his mind, perhaps with no intention of publishing them to the world. But his ideal of art depended intimately, nonetheless, on the system he had set forth seemingly in so haphazard a manner. His method gives to his writings their only unity. It was more than a method: it was a permanent expression of his own life, which aided him to construct a philosophy of beauty characteristic of the new age.

He had searched to find a scientific basis for art, and discovered it in the imitation of nature, based on rational experience. This idea was, in part, Aristotelian, absorbed with the spirit of the time; though in the ordinary sense of the word Leonardo was no scholar, least of all a humanist. His own innovation in aesthetics was in requiring a rational and critical experience as a necessary...

However, by "imitation" in art, Leonardo did not mean a mindless reproduction of nature. When he wrote that “the painter strives and competes with nature,” he was following a more Aristotelian <note>Relating to the Greek philosopher Aristotle</note> concept. He only partially developed this idea, using "nature" in the sense used by the Stagirite <note>original: Stagirite; a common name for Aristotle, who was born in the city of Stageira</note>—as an inner force that shows itself through outward appearance. In fact, the idea of imitation as it appeared to his mind was two-fold. It was not just the external reproduction of an image, which was simple enough to achieve. The artist's true difficulty lay in reflecting inner character and personality. It was Leonardo’s firm conviction that every thought had an outward expression by which a trained observer could recognize it. He wrote that every person has as many bodily movements as they have different types of ideas. Furthermore, thought expresses itself outwardly in proportion to how much power it has over the individual and their stage of life. Painting should aim, therefore, to reproduce the inner mental state through the physical attitude assumed. This was, in other words, a natural symbolism, in which the symbol was no mere convention, but the actual outward projection of the mind's inner condition. Art here offered an equation of inward purpose and outward expression, neither being complete without the other.

Further than this, influenced by Platonic thought, Leonardo’s conception of painting was that of an intellectual state or condition, projected outwardly. The painter who practiced his art without reasoning about its nature was like a mirror unconsciously reflecting what was before it. Although painting could not be realized without a “manual act,” its true problems—problems of light, color, pose and composition, and of primary and secondary shadow—all had to be grasped by the mind without physical labor. Beyond this, the scientific foundation of art came from basing it upon an accurate knowledge of nature. Even experience was only a step toward attaining this. “There is nothing in all nature without its reason,” he wrote. “If you know the reason, you do not need the experience.”

Natural phenomenon had brought art into existence. The first picture in the world, he remarked in a clever saying <note>original: "epigram"</note>, had been "a line surrounding the shadow of a man, cast by the sun on the wall." He traced the history of painting in Italy during its period of decline <note>original: "stagnation"</note> after the decay of ancient art, when each painter copied only his predecessor. This lasted until Giotto, who was born among barren mountains and drew the movements of the goats he herded; by doing so, he advanced further than all the earlier masters. However, his successors only copied him, and painting declined again until Masaccio <note>Masaccio (1401–1428) was a foundational Florentine painter of the Renaissance, famous for his use of perspective and realism.</note> once more took nature as his guide.

A quite different and confrontational side of Leonardo’s philosophy of art—which forced him to state the broad principles of art—appears in his attacks on poetry and music as being inferior to painting. In that age of the triumph of humanism <note>The Renaissance movement that revived interest in Greek and Roman thought, often placing literature at the top of the intellectual hierarchy.</note>, literature had dominated the other arts in a manner that was often arrogant. There was another cause for his attack on poetry as well: Leonardo resented the fact that painters, who were rarely formally educated men, had not defended themselves against the insults <note>original: "slurs"</note> cast on their craft. His counter-attack may have been intended to hide his own lack of formal schooling <note>original: "scholarship"</note>, but it served another purpose as well. His conception of the universal principles of beauty was made clear by this defense. His first principle stated broadly that the most useful art was the one which could most easily be communicated to others.

Painting was accessible to everyone since its appeal was made directly to the eye. While the painter proceeded immediately to the imitation of nature, the poet's instruments were words, which varied in every land. Leonardo adopted the *Platonic view* <note>Plato argued in his "Republic" that art and poetry were deceptive imitations removed from the "ideal" truth; Leonardo uses this to critique poetry's lack of physical reality.</note> of poetry as a deceptive imitation, far removed from the truth. He called the poet a collector of other men's goods, who dressed himself in their borrowed feathers. 

Where poetry presented only a shadow to the imagination, painting offered a real image to the eye. He exclaimed that the eye—as the window of the soul through which all earthly beauty was revealed—was the noblest of all the senses. It was sight, he argued, that had discovered the science of navigation and had driven men to seek the West. Painting spoke through its finished achievements, while poetry ended with the very words with which it sang its own praises. If, then, poets called painting *dumb poetry* <note>original: "muta poesis," a traditional comparison often attributed to the Greek poet Simonides.</note>, he could retaliate by labeling poetry *blind painting*. Like his successors, *Leonardo* could not escape this logical error <note>original: "fallacy"</note>; by ignoring everything except descriptive verse, this misunderstanding was destined to burden the study of *aesthetics* <note>The branch of philosophy dealing with the nature of beauty and art.</note> until it was finally dismantled by *Lessing* <note>Gotthold Ephraim Lessing (1729–1781) was a German philosopher who, in his work "Laocoön," argued that poetry and painting have different strengths and should not be judged by the same standards.</note>.

It was the opinion of *Leonardo* that the fleeting <note>original: "temporary"</note> nature of music made it inferior to painting. Although durability was not in itself an absolute test of quality—otherwise the work of coppersmiths would be the highest art—permanence could not be entirely ignored in any final ranking of the arts. Leonardo contrasted painting with sculpture, for he had practiced both and thought himself uniquely qualified to judge their merit. He considered the former the nobler art of the two, for sculpture involved physical labor and fatigue, while by its very nature it lacked perspective and atmosphere, color, and the sense of space. Painting, on the other hand, created by an illusion, was in itself the result of deeper thought. An even broader test served to convince him of its ultimate superiority. That art was of the highest excellence, he wrote, which possessed the most elements of variety and universality. Painting contained and reproduced all forms of nature; it made its appeal through the harmonious balance of parts which gratified all the senses. By its very duality, it fulfilled the highest purpose. The painter was able to visualize the beauty that enchanted him, to bring to reality the imagination of his dreams, and to give outward expression to the ideal within.

<margin>Introduction</margin> Leonardo had found a refuge in art from the pettiness of his material environment. Like his own creations, he, too, had learned the secret of the inner life. The painter, he wrote, could create a world of his own and take refuge in this new realm. But it must not be a world of shadows only. The very mystery he felt so keenly still had to rest on a real foundation; to treat it otherwise would be to plunge into mere empty talk <note>original: "vapouring"</note>. Although he attempted to bridge the gap <note>original: "gulf"</note> which separated the real from the unreal, he refused to treat the latter as supernatural. That mystery which lesser minds found in the occult, he saw in nature all around him. He denied the existence of spirits, just as he argued against the foolishness of the false hopes <note>original: "will-o’-the-wisps"</note> of former ages—namely, alchemy and the "black arts" <note>original: "black art"; refers to magic or necromancy</note>. In a single sentence, he destroyed the pretensions of palmistry <term>palmistry: the practice of "reading" a person's character or future from the lines on their hands</term>. “You will see,” he wrote, “great armies slaughtered in an hour’s time, where in each individual the signs of the hands are different.”

His art was thus guided by realism, while its purpose was found in spirituality. The search for truth and the desire for beauty were the twin ideals he strove to attain. The intensity of this pursuit saved him from the flaw of egoism which his detachment from his surroundings would otherwise have forced upon him. For his character presented the contradiction <note>original: "anomaly"</note>, peculiar to the Renaissance, of a lofty idealism coupled in action with irre— <note>The text ends mid-word, likely "irreconcilable" or "irresistible."</note>

In his desire to surpass himself, fostered by this isolation of spirit and spurred on by the eager wish to attain universal knowledge, he has been compared to Faust; but the likeness is only half correct. He was not blind to the limitations which encompassed him, his very genius making him realize their bounds. Of the ancients he said that in attempting to define the nature of the soul, they sought the impossible. He wrote elsewhere, “It is the infinite alone that cannot be attained, for if it could it would become finite.”

In Leonardo’s personality was reflected both the strength and weakness of Renaissance Italy. So, to know him, it is necessary to understand the Italy of that age. Its brilliance, its universality, its desire for beauty, are but one side of the medal. On its reverse, Italy lacked the solid vigor of a national purpose. The discord of political disunion, reacting on art, laid bare great weakness in the want of any constructive direction toward which the strength of the Renaissance could aim. The energy was there, whether finding an outlet in statecraft or in discovery, in art or in letters. But it labored for no common end; there was internal unity of force and method, but external divergence of purpose. The tyranny of petty despots could provide no adequate ideal toward

--- Page 32 ---
which to aim. No ruler, and no city except Venice, could long symbolize the nation's patriotism. Venetian painters alone glorified the state in their work, and thus felt the living force of a national ambition which raised them above themselves. But elsewhere there was little to inspire that devotion for a common country necessary as a background to sustain the greatest work. Hence Italian art, so alive within certain limits, remained stunted beyond them. The conviction that art existed in order to express ideal beauty—that its main purpose was to please the eye and the senses regardless of the result attained—proved an inadequate compensation for all that had been withdrawn. The artistic ideal tended more and more to become a conscience and a purpose in itself, an inward impulse for action and an outward goal.

The artist's real greatness will depend at all times on his qualities as a representative. His true merit will arise from giving expression in ideal terms to his nation and to his age. Insofar as he has been able to do this and the spirit of his country is reflected in his work—insofar as he has represented what is best and most enduring within it—he will have achieved greatness. Not that this is always, or even often, a conscious expression. It is an unfair interpretation to search for deep thought in the work of either painter or poet. Neither art of-

But painter and poet, to the degree they attain greatness, express more than themselves. Ariosto, intent only to amuse, reflects with playful wit and skepticism the splendid luxury and joy of living in Renaissance court life. The care with which he chiseled each line proves that his real seriousness and conscience lay in his artistic purpose. Without Ariosto’s wit, Paolo Veronese depicted a similar side in painting, though his Venetian birthright made him celebrate the glory of the Republic. Poet and painter alike expressed far more than either could know. If such a test be applied to the artists of the Renaissance, each in turn will respond to it—just as the weakness of the later Bolognese as a school is that, beyond a certain technical merit, they meant and represented so little. But the noblest painters—Michelangelo and Raphael, Titian and Leonardo—in addition to possessing the solid grasp of technical mastery, reflected some aspect of their nation’s life and civilization. In Michelangelo was realized the grandeur of Italy struggling vainly against crushing oppression. He expressed that which was highest in it, reflecting the loftiest side of its idealism mingled with deep pessimism in his survey of life; for, wrapped in austerity, he saw mankind in heroic terms of sadness. other hand, found only beautiful sweetness everywhere. The tragedies of life failed to touch the young painter, who erased from view all struggle and sorrow, and, despite the misery that had befallen his nation, could still rejoice in the sensory beauty of the world. There was another side to the Renaissance, dependent neither on beauty nor heroic grandeur, yet sharing in both through qualities of its own. Titian, who painted the living man of action—the man of talent, capable of appreciating both ideal beauty and heroic impulse, yet guided nonetheless by practicality—reflected this more functional aspect of life. In his portraiture, he expressed the statecraft for which Italians found opportunities beyond the Alps, since it was denied to them within Italy; and Titian found even Venice too narrow for the scope of his art.

But before Titian, before Raphael, before Michelangelo, Leonardo reflected the rationalism and the mystery, the subtlety and the philosophical speculation of the age. To find in his work only the individual thought of a genius would be to mistake, perhaps, its most important aspect; for the expression of his mind, through both its brilliance and its limitations, is typical of the spirit of his time. BEGUN at Florence in the house of Piero di Braccio Martelli, on the 22nd day of March, 1508; and this is to be a collection without order, taken from many papers which I have copied here, hoping to arrange them later, each in its place, according to the various subjects treated. ### 62.

<margin>The body dies and is reborn</margin>
The body of any living thing that is fed is constantly dying and being reborn. This is because nourishment can only enter where previous nourishment has been used up; if it is used up, it no longer has life. If you do not provide the body with nourishment equal to what was there before, you will damage the health of the organism; if you deprive it of this nourishment entirely, life will be completely destroyed. 

However, if you supply it with as much as can be consumed in a day, then as much life will be restored as was consumed. It is like the light of a candle, which is sustained by the fuel provided by the liquid wax <note>original: "moisture of the candle"</note>. With rapid assistance <note>original: "succour"</note>, this light restores from below what is consumed above as it dies in dark smoke. ### 24.

There is, in addition to these, a third difficulty, and this is that a body of this kind, made of air and assumed by the spirits, is exposed to the penetrating winds which continually sunder and scatter the united portions of the air, eddying and whirling amidst the rest of the atmosphere; therefore the spirit who would pervade

this air would be dismembered or torn and broken up along with the tearing of the air of which it formed a part.

### 108.

<margin>Has the Spirit a Body?</margin>
It is impossible for a spirit, incorporated within a certain quantity of air, to move this air; and this is proved by the passage where it is said that “the spirit rarefies that portion of the air with which it is mingled.” This air, therefore, will rise high above the other air, and the air will be set in motion by its own lightness and not by the volition of the spirit; and if this air encounters a wind, the air will be moved by the wind and not by the spirit which is incorporated in it.

### 109.

<margin>Can the Spirit speak?</margin>
In order to show whether the spirit can speak or not, it is first necessary to define the voice and the manner of its origin. The following will be our definition: The voice is the movement of air in friction against a dense body, or a dense body in friction against the air (which is the same idea); and by this friction of the dense and the rare, what is rare is condensed, and resistance is caused; and again, when the rare in swift motion and the rare in slow motion come into contact, they condense one another and produce sound, and a great noise is made. The sound or murmur made by the rare moving through the rare

Therefore, we will say that the spirit cannot produce a voice unless the air is set in motion, but since there is no air within it, it cannot discharge what it does not possess; and if it wishes to move that air in which it is incorporated, it would be necessary for the spirit to multiply itself; but that which has no physical dimension cannot be multiplied. In the fourth place, it is said that no thin body can move if it does not have a stable spot from which it may take its motion, and this is especially the case when an element must move within its own element, which does not move on its own, except by uniform evaporation at the center of the thing evaporated; as occurs in the case of a sponge squeezed in the hand under water, from which the water escapes in every direction with equal motion through the spaces between the fingers of the hand that squeezes it. As to whether the spirit has an articulate voice and can be heard, and as to what hearing and sight are—the wave of the voice travels through the air just as the images of objects travel to the eye.
