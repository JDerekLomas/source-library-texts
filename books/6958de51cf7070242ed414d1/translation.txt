## TO THE COURTEOUS READER.

<image-desc>A large decorative woodcut initial 'A' within a square frame, featuring a figure kneeling or sitting amidst dense foliage and flowers.</image-desc>

*Although the ancient* Heathens <note>Non-Christians, specifically the Greeks and Romans</note> *did attribute the first invention of the knowledge of* Herbs, *and consequently of* Medicine <note>original: "Physicke"</note>, *some unto* Chiron *the* Centaur, *and others unto* Apollo *or* Aesculapius *his son; yet we that are* Christians *have learned from a better school that* God, *the Creator of* Heaven *and* Earth, *at the beginning when he created* Adam, *inspired him with the knowledge of all natural things. This knowledge successively descended to* Noah *afterward, and to his* descendants <note>original: "Posterity"</note>. *For, just as he was able to give names to all the living* creatures *according to their several natures, so no doubt he also had the knowledge of which* herbs *and* fruits *were fit either for* food <note>original: "Meate"</note> *or* medicine, *for* utility *or for* delight.

*And so that* Adam *might exercise this knowledge,* God *planted a* garden *for him to live in (wherein even in his state of innocence he was to labor and spend his time). He stocked it with the best and choicest* herbs *and* fruits *the earth could produce, so that he might have things not only for the necessity of feeding, but for pleasure also. The place or garden called* Paradise *implies as much, as do more plainly the words set down in* Genesis *chapter two, which are these:* Out of the ground the Lord God made to grow every tree pleasant to the sight and good for food. *Also, in the 24th chapter of* Numbers, *the parable of* Balaam *mentions the* Aloe *trees that* God *planted, and there are other places if there were a need to recite them.*

*But my purpose is only to show you that* Paradise *was a place (whether you will call it a* garden, *or* orchard, *or both, as it was no doubt of some large extent) wherein* Adam *was first placed to live; that* God *was the planter of it, having furnished it with trees and herbs that were as pleasant to the sight as they were good for food. Since he was to dress and keep this place, he must of necessity have known all the things that grew therein and what uses they served; otherwise, his labor and his knowledge of them would have been in vain.*

*And although* Adam *lost the place because of his transgression, yet he did not lose the natural knowledge or the use of them. Rather, just as* God *made the whole world and all the creatures within it for* Man, *so man may use all things—those of pleasure as well as those of necessity—to be helps unto him in serving his* God. *Let men therefore, according to their first purpose, use these things so that they may also remember their service to* God. *They should not (like our grandmother* Eve) *set their affections so strongly on the pleasure found in them that they deserve the loss of them in this* Paradise, *and indeed the loss of* Heaven *as well. For truly, from all sorts of* herbs *and* flowers, *we may at all times draw material not only to magnify the* Creator *who has given them such diversities of forms, scents, and colors, that the most cunning...*

The **Workman** <note>original: "Worke-man"; referring to God as the Creator</note> cannot be imitated. Such virtues and properties exist that, although we know many, many more lie hidden and unknown. Yet, they also offer many good instructions to ourselves: just as many herbs and flowers with their fragrant, sweet smells comfort and, as it were, revive the spirits and perfume a whole house, even so those men who live virtuously—laboring to do good and benefit the **Church of God** and the **Commonwealth** by their hard work or their writing—send forth a pleasing savor of sweet instructions. This is true not only during the time in which they live and are "fresh," but even when they are dry, withered, and dead, they do not cease to do as much or more for all future ages. 

Many herbs and flowers that have little beauty or scent to recommend them have much more good use and virtue; similarly, many men of excellent, rare parts and good qualities lie hidden, unknown, and unrespected until time and the use of their talents reveal their properties. Again, many flowers have a glorious show of beauty and bravery, yet are stinking in smell or otherwise of no use; so many people make a glorious ostentation and flourish in the world, when—if they do not stink horribly before God and all good men—they surely have no other virtue than their outside to commend them or leave behind them. 

Some also rise up and appear like a **Lily among Thorns**, or as a beautiful flower among many weeds or grass, either by their honorable authority or eminence of learning or riches, whereby they excel others and may thereby do good to many. The frailty of **Man's life** is also learned by how quickly they fade: before their flowering, in their prime, or soon after—being either cropped by the hand of the spectator, withered and parched by a sudden blast, or decaying by its own nature through the revolution of time. We also see that the fairest flowers or the first-ripe fruits are gathered soonest and first. 

The **mutability** <note>original: "mutabilitie"; the quality of being subject to change</note> of states and persons is also shown by this: where many goodly flowers and fruits grew this year and age, in another they are quite pulled or dug up, and either weeds and grass grow in their place, or some building is erected there, and their place is known no more. 

The **Civil respects** <note>social or cultural benefits</note> to be learned from them are many as well. The delight found in the varieties of forms, colors, and properties of herbs and flowers has always been powerful even over dull, uneducated, rustic, and savage people led only by nature's instinct; how much more powerful is it, or should it be, in the minds of generous <note>noble or high-minded</note> persons? For it may well be said, he is not humane who is not allured by this object. 

The study, knowledge, and travel required for them has been embraced by great Kings, Princes, and Potentates without disparagement to their greatness or hindrance to their more serious and weighty affairs. So, no doubt, for all who are capable of it, this study is not only pleasant but profitable—comforting the mind, spirits, and senses with a harmless delight, and enabling the judgment to provide help for many dangerous diseases. It is also an instructor in the truth of the **genuine Plants of the Ancients** <note>referring to the botanical writings of classical authors like Dioscorides or Pliny</note>, and a **corrector** of the many errors into which the world, by long continuance, has been diverted and almost fixed. It seeks to eradicate, in time and by degrees, the stubborn willfulness of many who, because they were brought up in their errors, are most unwilling to leave them without considering the good or evil, the right or wrong, they bring upon themselves. 

And for myself, I may well say that had not my own pains and studies (driven by a natural inclination) been more powerful in me than any other person's help, I had never done so much as I here publish. Some, through an evil disposition and ignorance, have so far slandered me as to say this was another man's work rather than my own, but I leave them to their folly. Nor would I have been fit or prepared for a larger work, which time may suddenly (by God's permission) bring to light, if the malevolent dispositions of degenerate spirits do not hinder its completion.

# The Epistle to the Reader.

*But persuading myself there is no shower that produces no fruit, or no word but works some effect—either of good to persuade, or of reproof to convict—I could not but declare my mind herein, let others judge or say what they please. For I have always held it an unfit thing to conceal or bury that knowledge God has given, and not to share it and further others with it as much as is convenient, yet without ostentation, which I have ever hated. Now, further to inform the courteous Reader both of the occasion that led me on to this work and the other occurrences related to it:*

*First, having perused many Herbals <note>original: "Herbals"; books containing the names and descriptions of plants with their properties</note> in Latin, I observed that most of them have either neglected or not known the many diversities of the flower plants and rare fruits known to us at this time, and (except Clusius <note>Charles de l'Écluse (1526–1609), a pioneering Flemish botanist</note>) have made mention of but a very few. In English likewise we have some extant, such as Turner <note>William Turner (c. 1508–1568), often called the "Father of English Botany"</note> and the translated Dodonaeus <note>Rembert Dodoens (1517–1585), a Flemish physician and botanist</note>, who have said little of flowers. Gerard <note>John Gerard (c. 1545–1612), author of the famous 1597 "Herball"</note>, who is the most recent, has no doubt given us the knowledge of as many as he attained unto in his time; but since his days we have had many more varieties than he or they ever heard of, as may be perceived by the store I have here produced. And none of them have particularly separated those that are beautiful flower plants—fit to stock a garden of delight and pleasure—from the wild and unfit. Instead, they have interlaced many, one among another, whereby many who have desired to have fair flowers have not known either what to choose or what to desire.*

*Various books of flowers have also been published, some in our own country and more in others, all of which are, as it were, but handfuls snatched from the plentiful Treasury of Nature; none of them being willing or able to reveal all sorts and describe them fully. But the greatest hindrance of all men's delight was that none of them had given any description of them, but the bare name only. To satisfy, therefore, the desires of those who are lovers of such delights, I took upon me this labor and expense, and have here selected and set forth a garden of all the chiefest for choice and fairest for show from among all the several tribes and kindreds of Nature's beauty. I have ranked them as near as I could, or as the work would permit, in affinity one unto another.*

*Secondly, and for the sake of those who are students of other authors, I have set down the names that have been formerly given unto them, along with some of their errors. I do not intend to encumber this work with all that might be said of them, because the deciding of the many controversies, doubts, and questions that concern them pertains more fitly to a general history. Yet I have been in some places more copious and ample than I had at first intended, the occasion drawing out my desire to inform others with what I thought was fit to be known, reserving what else might be said for another time and work; wherein (God willing) I will expand further, as the subject matter requires it of me, in what my small ability can effect.*

*Thirdly, to embellish this work, I have also set forth the figures <note>illustrations or woodcuts</note> of all such plants and flowers as are material and different one from another. I have not done as some others have—that is, providing a number of figures for one sort of plant that have nothing to distinguish them but the color—for I hold that to be superfluous and a waste.*

*Fourthly, I have also set down the virtues and properties of them in a brief manner, rather desiring to give you the knowledge of a few certain and true ones than to relate, as others have done, a needless and false multiplicity. This is so that profit as well as pleasure might be taken from them, and that nothing might be wanting to accomplish the work fully. And so much for this first part, my Garden of Pleasant and Delightful Flowers.*

*My next garden consists of herbs and roots fit to be eaten by the rich and poor as nourishment and food, as sauce or condiment, as salad or refreshing, for pleasure or profit; where I do as well play the gardener to show you (briefly, but not at length) the times...*

# The Epistle to the Reader.

I have also included the manner of sowing, setting, planting, replanting, and the like—although all these things, and many more than are actually true, are set down very extensively in the several books that others have written on this subject. I also show some of the kitchen uses (because they are kitchen herbs, etc.), although I confess I do so very sparingly. I do not intend this to be a treatise on cookery, but briefly to give a touch of that subject. 

Furthermore, I include the medicinal <note>original: "Physicall"</note> properties to show some things that others have not set forth. Yet, I do not intend to play the quack <note>original: "Empericke"; an untrained person who practices medicine based on observation alone rather than scientific theory</note> and give you recipes for medicines for all diseases, but only to show in some way the qualities of herbs to stimulate the minds of studious readers. 

And lastly, I provide an orchard of all sorts of domestic or foreign, rare and good fruits, suitable for this our land and country, which is at this time better stocked and furnished than ever in any age before. I have endeavored here, as in the other gardens, to set forth the varieties of every sort in as brief a manner as possible, without superfluous repetitions of descriptions, and only with special notes on the differences in leaves, flowers, and fruits. Some few properties are also set down—the most important rather than a complete list—as the work required. Moreover, before each of these parts, I have provided treatises on the ordering, preparing, and keeping of the several gardens and the orchard, with whatever I thought was convenient to be known for each of them.

Thus have I shown you both the occasion and the goal of this work. I have spent my time, pains, and money herein; if it is well accepted, I shall think them well employed, and I may the sooner hasten the fourth part, *A Garden of Simples* <note>A "garden of simples" refers to a garden of medicinal plants used for "simple" (single-ingredient) remedies</note>, which will remain quiet at home only until it can bring its master news of fair weather for the journey.

->Yours in what he may,<-

->**JOHN PARKINSON.**<-

## To John Parkinson, Most Diligent London Pharmacist and Most Accomplished Botanist
### From T. D. M., Much Health and Greetings.
<note>T. D. M. stands for Theodore de Mayerne, the influential physician to the Stuart kings.</note>

A panegyric poem <note>original: "Poema panegyricum" — a speech or poem of high praise</note> is what your work of tireless labor and extraordinary usefulness demands. By the right of your merit, it would have been extorted from me (my dear Parkinson) if, by the favor of the Muses and a helpful Apollo, I were permitted to dream on the two-peaked Mount Parnassus and suddenly emerge as a poet. Let other God-filled enthusiasts compose their verses in praise of your offspring <note>referring to the book</note>, which has been brought into the light under favorable omens and promises a long lineage of descendants. Poets often slip these praises into our ears or minds through fictions and the puffed-up sweetness of hyperbolic words. 

However, Truth—which loves nakedness—has declared perpetual war against any dye that overshadows rather than illustrates her native purity. She exults in simplicity, expressed through the vigorous brevity of assertions. Hear what I think of you: To me, you shall hereafter be the **British Crateuas** <note>Crateuas was a famous Greek physician and herbalist from the 1st century BCE, known for illustrating plants.</note>. Among all those known to me here, you are the most skilled, the most practiced, the most observant, and the most keen-scented botanist. It is not only fitting but even necessary that through your efforts on this fortunate Island, the study of herbs is handled, corrected, enlarged, and delivered precisely to your fellow countrymen in their native tongue. 

Well done in your diligence (excellent man)! Do not regret the labors given to such arduous late-night studies thus far, nor let it grieve you to spend more effort hereafter. "Beautiful things are difficult" <note>original: "Difficilia quae pulchra"</note>. The sweetness of well-deserved praise will soften the bitterness of your sleepless nights. A noble prize <note>original: "βραβεῖον" (brabeion)</note> awaits you as you run the Olympic race with a swift foot, eagerly rushing from the starting gates to the finish line. 

But remember: **Art is long, life is short.** <note>original: "Artem longam, Vitam esse breuem"</note> **Delay nothing.** <note>original: "Μηδὲν ἀναβάλου" (Mēden anabalou)</note> See what Hippocrates wrote to that ancient Crateuas, whose spirit (if not his soul) you have put on: *"Delay is foreign to every art, but especially to medicine; in which case, postponement brings danger to the soul."* <note>original: "Τέχνης πάσης ἀλλότριον ἀναβολὴ ἰητρικῆς δὲ καὶ πάνυ; ἐν ᾗ ψυχῆς κίνδυνος ἡ ὑπέρθεσις."</note> 

Consider Botany to be the most noble part of Medicine. You have now made offerings to Flora <note>Goddess of flowers</note> and Pomona <note>Goddess of fruit trees</note>; I hear you will soon do the same for Apollo in your **MEDICAL GARDEN** <note>original: "HORTO MEDICO" — likely referring to Parkinson's later work, the *Theatrum Botanicum*.</note>. I pray you, complete the sacred rites for the pure Vesta <note>Goddess of the hearth and home</note>, and fully open her bosom, variegated with many thousands of disease-fleeing simples <note>term: "simples" — medicinal herbs used on their own</note>, which you should show to us without a veil. Let no day pass without a line <note>original: "Nulla dies abeat fine linea" — a proverb meaning to practice one's craft every day</note>. Thus at last you will bring back the most abundant fruits of glory. I hope you may long harvest these fruits, justly earned by your sweat, in a vigorous and green old age, and enjoy them for a long time. Farewell. 

Given at London, on the Calends of October <note>October 1st</note> in the year of salvation 1629.

->Theodore de Mayerne, Knight,<-
->Chief Physician to the Courts of the Kings of Great Britain,<-
->James and Charles, Father and Son.<-

## To his friend John *Parkinson*.

Others extol your labors, *Parkinson*; give me leave now to diminish them. <note>The poet uses a rhetorical play on words, suggesting that Parkinson has brought the whole world into a small space.</note> One might believe you have traveled through the farthest Indies, since this book is nothing other than your garden. You make the Indies dwell with you; you do not seek the Indies yourself. Go now, and say that I have "diminished" your work! This book is an image; here your garden is painted. This face is worthy of the hand, and the hand worthy of the face!

I have seen a garden shining everywhere with variegated gems; Solomon was but one man, so what was the crowd? <note>A reference to the biblical King Solomon's wealth and wisdom regarding nature.</note> Just as royal palaces shine with varied splendor, and the entire halls glitter with crowds of nobles—especially when a feast day grants permission to be proud—those whom a fig tree once covered, three country estates now cover. <note>An allusion to the progress of civilization from the Garden of Eden to modern luxury.</note>

Equally, a full Court is seen in your Garden: here is a Prince, here a Duke, and here the Duke’s beautiful bride. Every day is a feast day, and they never flourish more sparingly; though this luxury is grand, it is nevertheless a daily occurrence. Behold, like an exile not unmindful of his native Paradise, he paints this for himself with natural love. He paints it to the life under the same name, and here is the sweat of the brow and the brain which he gave. Our Adam <note>Referring to Parkinson himself as a new Adam in his garden.</note> stands in the middle of this Paradise, and he himself gives each species its own name. Receive these rewards for your merits; you who give a name to the flower, may you have an eternally flowering Name for yourself.

->William Atkins.<-

## To my friend John Parkinson, Apothecary
## and Distinguished Botanist. A Poem.

<note>original: Ad Amicum Ioannem Parkinsonum Pharmacopœum & Botanicum insignem. Carmen.</note>

With how great a bond do you open the inner sanctuaries of Flora <note>The Roman goddess of flowers and spring.</note>
*And make them enjoy a freer sky?*
How ready is your will to please everyone,
*And how does anxious labor press upon you night and day?*
With how much great zeal do you seek to bring into a cultivated garden
Whatever herbs the world holds in its sphere?
Immense expense, and labor extended over many years,
*Have affected you, and no rest was granted.*
To one seeking such things, a new passion for possessing arises,
*Yet you do not seek such great gifts for yourself alone;*
You bring forth flowers described under a living image,
*Then you bring forth whatever the garden nourishes for the table;*
You bring out for us praised fruits and honors,
*You bring forth species of herbs which the writings of men celebrate nowhere else,*
Species which also possess a pleasing beauty;
*Thus, friend, you bless us with a manifold gift.*
Take this for your merits: as long as the grace of flowers blooms,
*And herbs have their powers, behold, you shall have a Name.*
May your glory bloom forever into a late age,
*A glory which shall not perish for a long time to come.*

->Thomas Johnson, member of<-
->both Societies.<- <note>Likely referring to the Society of Apothecaries and another professional or learned body.</note>
