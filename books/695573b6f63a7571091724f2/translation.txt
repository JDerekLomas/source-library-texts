This is a digital copy of a book that was preserved for generations on library shelves before it was carefully scanned by Google as part of a project to make the world’s books discoverable online.

It has survived long enough for the copyright to expire and the book to enter the public domain <note>A legal status where a work is no longer protected by intellectual property laws and is free for public use.</note>. A public domain book is one that was never subject to copyright or whose legal copyright term has expired. Whether a book is in the public domain may vary country to country. Public domain books are our gateways to the past, representing a wealth of history, culture and knowledge that’s often difficult to discover.

Marks, notations and other marginalia <note>Handwritten notes or marks made in the margins of a book by previous readers or librarians.</note> present in the original volume will appear in this file - a reminder of this book’s long journey from the publisher to a library and finally to you.

## Usage guidelines

Google is proud to partner with libraries to digitize public domain materials and make them widely accessible. Public domain books belong to the public and we are merely their custodians. Nevertheless, this work is expensive, so in order to keep providing this resource, we have taken steps to prevent abuse by commercial parties, including placing technical restrictions on automated querying <note>The use of software scripts or "bots" to automatically download or search large amounts of data.</note>.

We also ask that you:

+ *Make non-commercial use of the files* We designed Google Book Search for use by individuals, and we request that you use these files for personal, non-commercial purposes.

+ *Refrain from automated querying* Do not send automated queries of any sort to Google’s system: If you are conducting research on machine translation, optical character recognition <term>OCR: The mechanical or electronic conversion of images of typed, handwritten or printed text into machine-encoded text.</term> or other areas where access to a large amount of text is helpful, please contact us. We encourage the use of public domain materials for these purposes and may be able to help.

+ *Maintain attribution* The Google “watermark” you see on each file is essential for informing people about this project and helping them find additional materials through Google Book Search. Please do not remove it.

+ *Keep it legal* Whatever your use, remember that you are responsible for ensuring that what you are doing is legal. Do not assume that just because we believe a book is in the public domain for users in the United States, that the work is also in the public domain for users in other countries. Whether a book is still in copyright varies from country to country, and we can’t offer guidance on whether any specific use of any specific book is allowed. Please do not assume that a book’s appearance in Google Book Search means it can be used in any manner anywhere in the world. Copyright infringement liability can be quite severe.

The edition of the *Harmonic Fragments* of Aristoxenus <note>Aristoxenus of Tarentum (4th century BCE) was a Greek philosopher and the most important music theorist of antiquity.</note> which I herewith present to the public has come to fruition not without manifold difficulties, but also not without manifold assistance from various quarters. It would not befit me to speak much of the former: all those who know or have experienced for themselves what it means to treat an author—upon whom no hand has been laid for more than two hundred years—as much as possible according to the currently prevailing principles of philological science <note>The historical and linguistic study of literary texts.</note>, will estimate these difficulties for themselves and therefore exercise a fair indulgence. To give an idea of these difficulties to those who have no insight into the peculiarities of such work would be a long and perhaps futile effort. However, that is not what matters at all; what matters is how much others have contributed to overcoming those difficulties.

That I have been able to undertake and complete this work at all, I owe first of all to Professor Dr. Studemund in Marburg. He himself was considering a plan for an edition of the Greek musicians; however, when he learned that I had already been occupied for a long time with the preliminary work for such a project, he not only graciously abandoned that plan but also helped me obtain the material indispensable for an edition through collations <note>The process of comparing different manuscripts to identify variations in the text.</note> of important and new manuscripts. I am indebted to him for this with the liveliest thanks, especially as those collations were prepared with an accuracy that could not be greater, and which can only be expected from a man in whom talent, energy, and diligence are present in an equally unusual degree. The work was not restricted to the limits of mere assistance. We worked through the first half of the text together; each of us contributed what he already possessed, everything was examined once more and, naturally, subjected to many modifications. At this stage, it was no longer possible to distinguish what belonged to whom; up to that point, the work is to be regarded entirely as a joint effort. We were denied the opportunity to process the second half together in the same manner, yet even for this part, Studemund <note>Wilhelm Studemund (1843–1889), a renowned German philologist known for his work on Roman law and comedy.</note> provided me with numerous suggestions for improvement. I have not cited these as his own every time in the critical commentary, partly because I intended to take sole responsibility for adopting them, and because introducing another person as a guarantor for the argumentation itself was disruptive; but especially because it seemed more appropriate to list his suggestions in one place along with those I could not agree with. This will be done further below. The translation also owes many improvements to his hand; it is precisely here that another person feels inconsistencies so much more easily and generally sees much more objectively than the one who, struggling with the highly stubborn material and the uncommon dryness of the original's style, was sometimes simply glad to have found an adequate expression at all. From what has been said, I hope the reader of the book will gain a correct idea of the share which my friend Studemund has in it. For my part, I cannot help but publicly express my deepest thanks to him for this selfless participation and for the kind encouragement, which was very welcome, especially during the time of my strength-paralyzing illness.
