HAD I NOT OBSERVED THAT PARTIALLY BLIND <term>original: "purblind"</term> MEN HAVE spoken well of sight, and some without children <term>original: "issue"</term> have written excellently on reproduction <term>original: "generation"</term>, I—who have never been the master of any significant garden—would not have attempted this subject. But the earth is the garden of nature, and every fruitful country is a paradise. Dioscorides <note>A Greek physician and botanist (c. 40–90 AD), author of "De Materia Medica."</note> made most of his observations while marching with the Roman general Mark Antony <note>original: "Antonius"</note>; and Theophrastus <note>An ancient Greek philosopher (c. 371–287 BC) often called the "father of botany."</note> gathered his general theories mainly from the open fields. 

❦ FURTHERMORE, we are not writing a herbal <note>A book containing the names and descriptions of plants, usually with their medicinal properties.</note>, nor can this small volume deceive you, who have handled the largest of such books. You know that even three folio volumes <note>Very large books made from sheets of paper folded only once.</note> are still too small to contain the subject, and you see how many new herbals reach us from America. From persevering researchers, who have grown old studying these unique plants <term>original: "singularities"</term>, we expect such detailed descriptions; in this field, England is now so precise that it does not fall behind other countries. 

❦ WE do not claim to increase the classifications of vegetables by focusing on plants that are five-pointed <term>original: "quincuncial"</term> or net-like <term>original: "reticulate"</term> in pattern, nor do we intend to establish a new science of botany <term>original: "phytology"</term>. The field of knowledge has been so thoroughly explored <term>original: "traced"</term> that it is difficult to discover anything truly new. We write something new about old things: if truth can indeed be added to, or if those who are envious insist on something novel. For even the ancients were aware of recent anatomical discoveries, and Hippocrates <note>The famous Greek physician (c. 460–370 BC) often called the "Father of Medicine."</note> knew about the circulation of the blood. 

❦ YOU have been so far beyond commonplace <term>original: "trite"</term> learning for so long that it is difficult to find a subject suitable for you; if you have already encountered a paper on this topic, we have failed in our goal. In this era of excessive writing, minor and neglected <term>original: "barren"</term> themes are best suited for creative thought <term>original: "invention"</term>. Subjects that are discussed too often limit the imagination and tie our thoughts to the ideas of previous writers. Besides, such discussions allow for diversions <term>original: "excursions"</term> and excusably <term>original: "venially"</term> admit side-truths, even if they are somewhat distant from the main point. If we sometimes take great liberties in this regard, we are not alone, but follow the example of great authors.

We have not intimidated the average reader with any diagrams other than the pattern itself, and we have deliberately avoided using illustrations from rare or unknown plants. 

❦ YOUR discerning judgment, being so well-acquainted with this study, will expect no mathematical certainties <term>original: "truths"</term> here, as you well understand...

## I.

<vocab>I.</vocab>

The story that Vulcan <note>the Roman god of fire and metalworking</note> gave arrows to Apollo and Diana on the fourth day after their births, according to pagan theology <note>original: "Gentile Theology"</note>, may be understood as a clear insight <note>original: "no blind apprehension"</note> into the creation of the sun and moon on the fourth day. This occurred when they were first aligned <note>original: "conjoined"</note> in the same degree of the Zodiac. As for the concepts of "seeds" <note>original: "seminality"</note> and the Saturnine relationships discussed by magnetic philosophers, although they provide some light on how the world was created, they are not without their own clouds and mysteries.

But the quincunx arrangement <note>original: "Quincuncial Ordination"</note> and the network-like structure of the world was a matter of a different nature. Although we do not find its name in the first chapter of the world <note>referring to the Book of Genesis</note>, we certainly find its form in the first garden. That Cyrus the Elder <note>the founder of the Persian Empire</note>, who was raised in Assyria, was a great admirer of gardens is well known. However, it was Cyrus the Younger <note>a Persian prince of the late 5th century BC</note> who truly brought the art of gardening and the use of the quincunx to its peak. 

<margin>Xenophon in the *Oeconomicus* <note>original: "Xenophon in Oeconomico"</note></margin>

According to Xenophon <note>a Greek historian and soldier</note>, when Lysander <note>a Spartan general</note> visited Cyrus at Sardis, he was amazed not only by the beauty of the trees and the fragrance of the blossoms but also by the precise, regular order of the planting—the straight rows and the perfect angles of the quincunx.

**T**he story that Vulcan <note>the Roman god of fire and metalworking</note> gave arrows to Apollo and Diana on the fourth day after their births—according to pagan theology <note>original: "Gentile theology"</note>—may be understood as a perceptive insight into the creation of the sun and moon on the fourth day of the world. This was when the diffused light of the universe was gathered into those spheres <note>original: "orbs"</note>, which then began to shoot out their rays. There are even clearer descriptions from pagan writers regarding the creatures of the fourth day. Meanwhile, the divine philosopher <note>likely referring to Moses, the traditional author of Genesis</note> unfortunately omits the most noble part of the third day; and Ovid (whom many believe borrowed his descriptions from Moses) barely touches on the remarkable biblical account. In just three words, Ovid describes the work of the third day—the creation of plants <note>original: "vegetable creation"</note>, which was the first decorative scene of nature, the original food of animals, and the first chapter in the history of <term>physick</term> <note>medicine</note> through the preservation of health by diet.

❧ For although <term>physick</term> <note>medicine</note> may claim a high origin from God's own medical act of casting a deep sleep upon our first parent <note>Adam</note>, and <term>chirurgery</term> <note>surgery</note> may find its entire art rooted in that single passage concerning the removal of Adam’s rib, neither can compete with the antiquity of garden design and <term>herbary</term> <note>the study and cultivation of plants</note>. If Paradise was planted on the third day of creation, as the most learned theologians conclude, its "birth" was too early for <term>Horoscopie</term> <note>the practice of casting a birth chart or horoscope</note>; gardens existed before gardeners, appearing only a few hours after the earth itself was formed.

❧ Its exact location and geography are matters of deeper doubt. Yet, since it was the original garden and was almost certainly located in the East, it is more than probable that the first scientific interest and cultivation of plants flourished in those regions. And since Noah’s Ark first came to rest on the mountains of Armenia, the art of planting rose again in the East. It found its renewal not far from the place of its birth, among the plains of those same regions. If Zoroaster <note>the ancient Persian prophet</note> was actually Ham, Cush, or Mizraim <note>descendants of Noah</note>, then they were early experts in this field, leaving behind a work on agriculture, as Pliny the Elder reports.

❧ However, the account of the <term>pensil</term> <note>hanging or suspended</note> or hanging gardens of Babylon—if they were indeed built by Queen Semiramis, the third or fourth ruler after Nimrod—is of significant antiquity. These gardens were not built on the ordinary level of the ground but were raised upon pillars with passages underneath. Therefore, we cannot view them as the *first* Babylonian gardens, but rather as a more impressive advancement in the art than anything that came before. This design somewhat hints at the old opinion regarding Paradise itself, which many imagined to be elevated high above the rest of the earth.

NEBUCHADNEZZAR <note>original: "NABUCHODONOSOR"</note>, whom some consider to be the famous Syrian King mentioned by Diodorus <note>Diodorus Siculus, a Greek historian</note>, beautifully repaired that city and so magnificently built his hanging gardens that later writers gave him the honor of being their first creator. From there, overlooking Babylon and all the surrounding region, he found no limit to his ambitious gaze until, over-delighted with the splendor <note>original: "bravery"</note> of this Paradise, he fell into a melancholic transformation <note>original: "meta-morphosis"; a reference to the biblical account in Daniel 4 where the king was driven mad and lived like an animal</note>. He discovered the folly of that delight and received a fitting punishment in a very different dwelling: among the wild plants and wanderings of the fields. ❦ THE Persian nobles <note>original: "gallants"</note>, who destroyed this monarchy, maintained their own botanical excellence <note>original: "bravery"</note>. It is to them that we owe the very name of "Paradise," a word we do not find in Scripture before the time of Solomon, and which is thought to be originally Persian. The Hebrew word for that disputed garden <note>Eden</note> means nothing more than an enclosed field; interestingly, the same Hebrew root gives rise to the words for both a "garden" and a "shield" <note>original: "buckler"; both words derive from the root "gan," implying protection or enclosure</note>. ❦ CYRUS the Elder, raised in woods and mountains, followed the leanings of his upbringing when time and power allowed. He brought the treasures of the field into order and boundary, so nobly beautifying the hanging gardens of Babylon that he was also thought to be their creator. ❦ AHASUERUS (whom many believe to have been Artaxerxes Longimanus) entertained his princes and people in the country and city of flowers <note>Susa</note> and in an open garden, while Queen Vashti more modestly hosted the ladies within the palace. ❦ BUT if (as some believe) King Ahasuerus was actually Artaxerxes Mnemon—who lived a long life and reign worthy of his great memory—then our celebrated Cyrus <note>Cyrus the Younger</note> was his younger brother. This Cyrus provided the occasion for that memorable feat and almost miraculous retreat led by Xenophon <note>The "Anabasis," or the March of the Ten Thousand Greek mercenaries out of Persia</note>. He was a man of high spirit and honor, a natural king, though tragically prevented from the throne by the simple accident of being the younger son <note>original: "post-geniture"</note>. He was not only a lord of gardens but a hands-on planter of them, arranging his trees like his armies in regular, orderly rows. Thus, while the elderly Laertes <note>the father of Odysseus in Homer's Odyssey</note> is remembered for pruning hedges and clearing away thorns and briars; while King Attalus <note>Attalus III, the last king of Pergamum</note> lives on for his poisonous plantations of Aconite, Henbane, Hellebore, and other plants hardly admitted within the walls of Paradise; and while many ancient figures live on only as the names of vegetables; all of history looks upon Cyrus as the magnificent and orderly planter. ❦ ACCORDING

...to which Xenophon describes his splendid plantation at Sardis, translated by Strebæus as: "Trees placed at equal intervals, in straight rows, and everything very beautifully arranged in the form of a Quincunx <note>original Latin: *Arbores pari intervallo sitas, rectos ordines, & omnia perpulchre in Quincuncem directa.*</note>." We shall take this for granted, as it was similarly translated by the most refined Latin authors; it was not a newly invented term, but was already in use by the ancient writer Varro. This means the rows and arrangements were so neatly disposed—or five trees so set together—that a regular pattern of angles and a clear view through the trees was left on every side. It owes this name [Quincunx] not only to the group of five trees <note>The "five-point" pattern seen on the face of a die</note>, but to the figure representing that number; when this shape is doubled at the angle, it forms the letter X, which is the significant decussation <note>term: **decussation** — An intersection or crossing in the form of an X</note>, or fundamental figure. ❦ NOW, although in some ancient and modern practices the area or crossed plot might be a perfect square—similar to a Tuscan pedestal or the "five-point" <note>original: "Quinquernio or Cinque-point"</note> on a die, where the intersection of diagonal lines is regular and suitable for planting large trees (an arrangement we should certainly utilize)—we will mainly focus on the description by Curtius and Porta. In their brief account, the crossing <note>original: "decussis"</note> is made within a rectangle <note>original: "longilateral square"</note>, with opposite angles (sharp and blunt) at the intersection.
