# Jacques Marthan to Gaucher Marthan his father—a man gifted with a most complete knowledge of medicine as well as all refined literature—Greetings.

**I**f we agree with either Plutarch or Pliny, Zoroaster was the most ancient and brilliant writer on magic. I recently undertook the task of translating his little book, titled *Magic Oracles* <note>original: "μαγικὰ Λόγια" (*Magika Logia*). These are more commonly known today as the *Chaldean Oracles*, a collection of philosophical and mystical utterances from the 2nd century AD that Renaissance scholars believed were written by the ancient Persian prophet Zoroaster.</note>, which has been rescued from mold and decay. I performed this with the greatest care at the request of Conrad Neobar <note>Conrad Neobar (died 1540) was a pivotal figure in French printing, appointed by King Francis I as the first royal printer for Greek texts.</note>, a man most earnestly devoted to my studies. As I proceeded, I encountered many passages that were so corrupted—or perhaps they are intact, I wouldn't dare pronounce a verdict once I examined the work more closely—that they certainly gave me a great deal of trouble. I might even quote that saying of Plutarch, which fits this situation most aptly: **"By the gods, it seems to be hidden in riddles."** <note>original Greek: "Ὁ Μὰ τ’ τ’ τ’ θεῶν ἔοικεν αἰνίγμασι κατακεκρύφθαι." For that reason, I have rendered the poem almost entirely word-for-word. In translating the Commentary, however, it seemed it would be tedious and pedantic if I bound myself by that same scrupulousness. Whatever the result, most learned father, I dedicate this work to you so that you may understand—though I know you were already long ago persuaded of it—that I look most favorably upon our bond. May our friendship, which was first contracted by natural right <note>referring to the biological bond between father and son</note> and then greatly strengthened by your many kindnesses, be preserved at last <term>whole and sound</term> <note>original: "farta tecta," a Roman legal phrase used to describe a building in good repair; here, it metaphorically refers to the endurance of their relationship.</note> from the decay of old age itself. **Farewell.**

## Jacques Marthan sends greetings to his father, Gancher Marthan, a man endowed with the most complete knowledge of medicine as well as of all more refined literature.

**T**he little book of the most ancient Zoroaster—if we agree with either Plutarch or Pliny—who was a most illustrious writer on magic, titled the *Magical Oracles*, <note>Original Greek: *μαγικά λόγια* (magika logia). These are now commonly known as the *Chaldean Oracles*, a collection of mystical philosophical utterances from the 2nd century AD, which Renaissance scholars believed were much older.</note> was recently rescued from neglect and decay. At the request of Conrad Neobarius, <note>Conrad Neobarius (d. 1540) was the first "King's Printer" for Greek in Paris, appointed by Francis I.</note> a man most earnestly devoted to my studies, I undertook the task of translating it with the greatest possible care. However, I encountered many passages where I would not dare to pronounce, even after examining them more closely, whether they were corrupted or intact. Certainly, they were of the sort that caused me a great deal of trouble. To cite that saying of Plutarch, which can be most aptly applied here: "For the matters of the gods seem to be hidden in riddles." <note>Original Greek: "τὰ γὰρ τῶν θεῶν ἔοικεν αἰνίγμασι κατακεκρύφθαι." This reflects the cryptic and symbolic nature of ancient mystical texts.</note> For this reason, I have rendered the poem almost entirely word-for-word. Yet, in translating the Commentary, I felt it would be tedious if I had bound myself by that same scrupulousness. In any case, most learned father, I dedicate this work to you, so that you may understand—though you have long been fully persuaded of this—that my aim is most inclined toward this: that our friendship, first contracted by natural right (that is, by the tightest bond), and then greatly strengthened by your kindnesses, might finally be preserved safe and sound <note>The author uses the Latin legal idiom *sarta tecta*, literally "repaired and roofed," meaning a building in good maintenance or a relationship kept in good standing.</note> from all the effects of old age. Farewell.

...to be done as perfectly and as exquisitely as possible, and likewise by fulfilling the duty of life, the soul soon takes up its place back from here, whence it set out. 

But indeed, since many seats for the soul have been assigned and described in that place: one, by heaven, is illuminated and shining on all sides; another is poured round on all sides with darkness; and finally, there are two, mentioned above, which are intermediate—neither shining brightly nor surrounded by obscurity or darkness. 

If a soul, having glided from the first of all these seats into this mortal body, should bestow diligent and devoted effort upon it—and according to the duty it was destined to perform—the oracle declares that it may return entirely to where it set out and fly upward. Otherwise, unless it has performed its duty well, it must fly from here to a place even worse and more troublesome, its actions having been weighed in a just balance—those actions upon which it dwelt during life. 

Now, first, you are therefore reminded by the oracle to investigate the soul’s <term>conduit</term> <note>original: ὁχετοῦ (*ochetou*). In the Chaldean Oracles, this refers to the channel or "pipe" through which the divine essence flows into the human and through which it must return.</note>, that is, the path through which the soul flowed down to you from above. From there, surely, once your work has been performed for this body in the order of life, you may be restored again to that same order from which you migrated—provided that a most sacred word is joined and bound to the work. 

Understand this **sacred word** to mean that which is handed down regarding the worship usually paid to the gods; and the **work** to mean sacrifice and ceremonies. Thus, the true and authentic sense is this: besides the speech usually uttered concerning the worship consecrated to the gods or by religion, sacred ceremonies must also be adopted for this very uplifting of the soul. 

**"Not downward."** By the term "precipice," he names the fall into corruption, vice, and calamities; just as he also calls the earthly and mortal body **earth**. Otherwise, by the name of **earth**, these oracles usually mean a nature that will perish, while by **fire**, they mean a divine or celestial nature. 

But indeed, he calls the place provided with seven passages <term>Fate</term>, joined together by the **seven wanderers** <note>original: "erronibus." This refers to the seven classical planets—the Sun, Moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn—which were believed to dictate the mechanical laws of destiny.</note>. Beneath this, **Necessity**—that pressing and unchangeable force—has found its place. 

Therefore the oracle warns that you should not lean too forward **"downward,"** that is, into the dead and falling body. # ON THE SACRAMENTS IN GENERAL.

## Question I.
### *On the name and definition of a Sacrament.*

A Sacrament is properly called a <term>sign of a sacred thing</term> <note>original: *sacrae rei signum*</note>. For just as a person is recognized by their clothing, so divine and invisible things are foreshadowed through signs perceptible to the senses. Augustine says in the tenth book of *The City of God*: "A sacrament is a sacred sign."

<margin>Peter Lombard, Book 4, Distinction 1.</margin>
The Master of the Sentences <note>A title for Peter Lombard (c. 1100–1160), whose "Sentences" was the standard textbook for medieval theology students.</note> defines it in this way: *A sacrament is a visible form of invisible grace.* This definition is commonly accepted by all theologians.

->**Article I.**<-
**Whether a Sacrament belongs to the category of a sign?**

It seems not. Because a sign is that which, besides the appearance it presents to the senses, causes something else to come into one's knowledge, as <insert>the blessed</insert> Augustine says. But grace does not appear to be a thing perceptible by the senses. Therefore, etc.

<column-break/>

But against this is what is said in the Letter to the Romans: *A seal of the righteousness of faith.* <note>Romans 4:11.</note>

**I respond.** It must be said that three elements converge to create the nature of a sacrament:
1. That it be a <term>sensible</term> thing <note>Meaning it can be perceived by the physical senses, such as the touch of water or the taste of bread.</note>.
2. That it be <term>significative</term> <note>Carrying a specific symbolic meaning or message.</note>.
3. That it be <term>efficacious</term> <note>Capable of actually producing a spiritual effect rather than just representing it.</note>.

For a sacrament does not only signify grace, but it effects it within the soul <gloss>that is, it confers grace</gloss>.
