### Summary of the book of Iamblichus.

*Porphyry <note>Porphyry of Tyre (c. 234–305 AD) was a student of Plotinus and a major Neoplatonist philosopher.</note>, who among the Platonists is called "the Philosopher" because of his excellence, sent a long letter to Egypt to Anebo the priest, full of various and serious questions looking toward all parts of philosophy, especially toward God, as well as angels, spirits <term>dæmones: in Neoplatonism, these are intermediary spirits between gods and humans, not necessarily evil</term>, and souls; toward providence, toward fate, prophecies, magic, miracles, sacrifices, and vows. Iamblichus, his student, responded to Porphyry's questions. Indeed, he introduces the Egyptian priest Abammon as the one responding to Porphyry on behalf of Anebo, his own disciple, to whom Porphyry sent the letter. Therefore, of this entire inquiry and truly divine response—as if I were still too much occupied with Plotinus <note>Ficino was famously translating the massive "Enneads" of Plotinus during this period</note>—I have translated almost not the individual words, but the meanings, so that it should not be thought a wonder if the speech seems fragmented in places, especially among the principles of the argument themselves, where the loss of words is of less importance. From now on, the context of the words will also appear more continuous. I was indeed forced at times to insert into it some words for the sake of either continuity or understanding. Moreover, the cause of this swifter edition was Pierleoni <note>Piero Leoni da Spoleto (died 1492), the personal physician to Lorenzo de' Medici and a close friend of Ficino</note>, a man most desirous of all secret things. Truly, that which I admire above all is that he always seems to strive for what he already possesses. For by a certain divinity of intellect, he understands the secrets of the Philosophers, both of the Greeks and the "Barbarians" <note>In this context, "Barbarians" refers to non-Greek ancient cultures, such as the Egyptians, Chaldeans, and Persians, whom the Neoplatonists respected as sources of ancient wisdom</note>, even those not yet read.*

<summary>Marsilio Ficino introduces the "Reply of Abammon" (Iamblichus's On the Mysteries), explaining that it was written to answer Porphyry’s skeptical questions about religion and magic. Ficino also notes his own translation method—focusing on the "sense" rather than literal words—and credits his friend Piero Leoni for encouraging the project.</summary>

EGYPTIAN writers, believing that all things were discovered by Mercury <term>Mercurius: the Roman name for the Greek Hermes, here identified as Hermes Trismegistus, the legendary Egyptian sage and father of writing and philosophy</term>, dedicated their books to Mercury. Mercury presides over wisdom and eloquence. Pythagoras, Plato, Democritus, Eudoxus, and many others visited the Egyptian priests. The teachings of this book belong to the Assyrians and Egyptians, and come from the columns of Mercury <note>According to legend, the ancient wisdom of the Egyptians was inscribed on stone pillars or columns to preserve it for future generations</note>. Pythagoras and Plato learned philosophy from the columns of Mercury in Egypt. The columns of Mercury are full of teachings. Before any use of reason, there is a naturally implanted notion of the gods; indeed, there is a certain touch of divinity that is better than mere knowledge, from which is stirred the natural desire for the good, as well as reasoning and judgment. The essential knowledge of divine things is perpetual to the soul; however, this is not truly the same as the "knowledge" <note>original: cognitio; Ficino distinguishes between intellectual study and a direct mystical experience</note> by which we enjoy God. For in [ordinary] knowledge there is "otherness" <note>original: alteritas; in Neoplatonic philosophy, this refers to the separation between the person who knows and the object being known</note>, but [in this divine state there is] a certain contact...

# IAMBLICHUS

[essential knowledge is] essential and simple. For we cannot reach unity itself, unless by something most unified <note>original: vnitissimo; referring to the highest, most singular part of the human spirit</note>, and by a unity of the mind which stands above the specific properties of the soul and the intellect.

The unity of the gods itself unites souls to itself from eternity through their own unities, according to a contact so intimate and effective that it appears to be a continuity. The divine intellect gives being to the soul through its own essential act of understanding. Therefore, the "being" of the soul is a certain kind of understanding—specifically, an understanding of God, upon whom it depends.

Our true being consists in knowing God, because the principal being of the soul is its intellect, in which being and the understanding of divine things are the same thing in a perpetual act. From that principal being, however, the discursive <term>discurrentes: reasoning that moves step-by-step from one idea to another, as opposed to the instant "intellectual" grasp of truth</term> powers of the soul are derived. 

After the gods, we place demons, heroes, and pure souls: these three orders are the attendants <note>original: pedissequi; literally "foot-followers," those spirits who follow in the retinue of a specific deity</note> of the gods. We cannot reach these attendants of the gods—demons, heroes, or pure spirits—through the usual wanderings of human reason. Instead, it is necessary to rise up to an essential and eternal intelligence. 

Just as the gods are always reached by an innate notion, so too the divine powers that attend the gods are first reached only when the soul has laid aside its shifting mode of cognition. This shifting mode belongs to the rational power, which [is shaped] by intellect and intelligence...

<summary>This page explores the metaphysical bond between the human soul and the divine, arguing that the soul's truest essence is a perpetual act of understanding God. It also introduces the spiritual hierarchy of demons, heroes, and pure souls that serve as intermediaries between humanity and the gods.</summary>
