The items listed here represent two distinct categories: female adornments and the "Five Grades of Mourning" (*Wufu*).

1. **Female Adornments:** 
- <term>Bobin</term> (博鬢) are the wing-like, flared hair ornaments extending from the sides of a formal crown, typical of Ming Dynasty empresses and noblewomen. 
- The <term>Xiapei</term> (霞帔), or "Cloud Scarf," was a long, ceremonial stole weighted with a silver or gold pendant, indicating the wearer's rank.

2. **Mourning Rites:** 
The diagrams for <term>Zhanshuai</term> (Deepest Mourning), <term>Qishuai</term> (Even Mourning), <term>Dagong</term> (Greater Mourning), <term>Xiaogong</term> (Lesser Mourning), and <term>Sima</term> (Fine Hemp Mourning) illustrate the five levels of ritual dress. These garments varied in fabric coarseness and duration of wear based on the closeness of the relationship to the deceased. For instance, the <term>Zhanshuai</term> was made of the coarsest unhemmed hemp and worn for three years for a parent.

3. **Burial Preparations:**
Items like the "Eye-closing cloth" (瞑目巾), "Hand-binding cloth" (握手帛), and "Face-covering" (覆臉) were textiles used to prepare the body for the coffin, ensuring the deceased was presented with dignity according to ritual law. The <term>Fei</term> (屝) refers to coarse sandals made of grass or straw, typically worn by mourners.
</note>

The *Records of the Tang* <note>original: 唐志 *Tang Zhi*; likely referring to the "Treatise on Chariots and Clothing" in the *Old Book of Tang* or *New Book of Tang*</note> state that **Ma Zhou** <note>Ma Zhou (601–648) was a prominent chancellor of the Tang Dynasty known for his reforms in governance and ritual attire</note> took the **"Deep Robe"** <note>original: 深衣 *Shenyi*; a traditional one-piece garment where the tunic and skirt are sewn together, symbolizing moral integrity</note> made of cloth from the **Three Dynasties** <note>original: 三代 *Sandai*; the semi-mythical golden age of the Xia, Shang, and Zhou dynasties</note> and, based on its design, added a **horizontal border** <note>original: 襴 *lan*</note> and a **hem** <note>original: 裾 *ju*</note> to the bottom. For this reason, it was named the **Bordered Robe** <note>original: 襴衫 *Lanshan*</note>.

It was established as the formal attire for **high-ranking scholars**. The robes worn by **examination candidates** <note>original: 舉子 *juzi*; students who had passed local exams and were eligible for the imperial civil service examinations</note> today are precisely this style of garment.

# The Beizi <note>original: 褙子; a traditional Chinese over-robe or long jacket with a straight collar and side slits</note>

This is what is known today as a **pifeng** <term>pifeng (披風): a long, open-front cloak or mantle that became particularly popular during the Ming Dynasty</term>. The *Veritable Records* <note>original: 實録 *Shilu*; likely referring to a historical chronicle or a collection of precedents</note> state that the **Second Emperor of Qin** <note>reigned 210–207 BCE</note> issued an edict that a *beizi* should be added on top of formal **court dress** <note>original: 朝服 *chaofu*</note>.

According to its design, the sleeves are shorter than those of the inner robe, while the body of the garment is equal in length to the robe and features wide sleeves. In the **Song Dynasty** <note>960–1279 CE</note>, the length was increased to match the hem of the skirt, but the sleeves were made only slightly wider than those of the inner robe.

# Half-Sleeve Jacket <note>original: 半臂 *banbi*</note>

The *Veritable Records* <note>original: 實錄 *Shilu*; historical annals of the imperial reigns</note> state that during the **Daye era of the Sui Dynasty** <note>605–618 CE</note>, palace officials <term>neiguan (内官): eunuchs or court officials serving in the inner palace</term> frequently wore the **half-sleeve jacket**. By removing the [outer] sleeves, the long sleeves of the undergarment remained visible.

**Emperor Gaozu of the Tang Dynasty** <note>reigned 618–626 CE</note> reduced the length of the sleeves and officially called it the **half-sleeve** (*banbi*). It is what we call a **beizi** <note>original: 背子; referring here to a specific short-sleeved over-garment</note> today. In the region between the **Yangtze and Huai Rivers**, it is sometimes called a **chuozi**.

Scholars competed to wear this garment, which was first formally regulated during the Sui Dynasty. Today, its common name is the **dahuo** <term>dahuo (搭護): a cross-collared, short-sleeved robe or jacket</term>; it is also referred to as a **beixin** <term>beixin (背心): literally "back and heart," the Chinese term for a vest or waistcoat</term>.

# Unlined Robe <note>original: 衫 *shan*</note>

->Unlined Robe<-

The **Treatise on Chariots and Dress** <note>original: *Yufu Zhi* (輿服志); a standard section in Chinese dynastic histories detailing official costumes and transport</note> states that **Ma Zhou** <note>a prominent Tang Dynasty official, 601–648 CE</note> submitted a proposal arguing that in the ancient rites of the **Three Dynasties** <note>the Xia, Shang, and Zhou dynasties of antiquity</note>, there was no textual record of wearing the *shan* <term>shan (衫): a single-layered, unlined robe or long shirt, often worn for comfort or as a less formal outer layer</term>. 

In those ancient times, they had the **Deep Robe** <note>original: *shenyi* (深衣); a classical garment where the tunic and skirt were sewn together</note>. During later periods, a version with blue decorative borders on the sleeves and collar <note>original: *qinglan xiu biao zhuan* (青襴袖褾襈)</note> became the formal attire for scholars and the literati. 

Robes designed with slits at the hips were called **side-slit robes** <note>original: *que kua shan* (缺胯衫); literally "missing-hip robe," designed for mobility</note>. These were worn by commoners and are what we call **four-paneled robes** <note>original: *si ku shan* (四袴衫)</note> today. It is said that this convention essentially began with Ma Zhou.

## Jacketed Robe <note>original: 襖子 *aozi*</note>

The **Treatise on Chariots and Dress** in the **Old Book of Tang** <note>original: *Jiu Tang Shu Yufu Zhi* (舊唐書輿服志); a historical record compiled in the 10th century covering the regulations of the Tang Dynasty (618–907 CE)</note> states:

**Banquet attire** <note>original: *yanfu* (燕服); informal clothing worn for leisure or private parties</note> was, in ancient times, considered **private dress** <note>original: *xiefu* (褻服); literally "untidy" or "under-dress" not suitable for formal ritual</note>. It is also referred to as **everyday dress** <note>original: *changfu* (常服)</note>. 

In the **Jiangnan** region <note>the cultural heartland south of the Yangtze River</note>, the people wore headscarves, coarse woolens, skirts, and short tunics. However, the **Northern Dynasties** <note>386–581 CE; a period when Northern China was governed by dynasties of diverse ethnic origins, notably the Xianbei</note> mixed these styles with the clothing systems of the **frontier tribes and military** <note>original: *rongyi* (戎夷); a term for non-Han peoples to the north and west</note>.

By the time of the **Northern Qi** <note>550–577 CE</note>, there were tall hats, short boots, joined trousers, and the **jacketed robe** <term>aozi (襖子): a lined or padded upper garment, often shorter than a full robe, favored for warmth and mobility</term>. Colors such as vermilion, purple, black, and yellow were worn according to individual preference. 

Except for the **Grand Assembly of the New Year** <note>original: *yuanzheng dahui* (元正大會); the most formal annual court event</note>, these garments were used universally for all occasions. This was likely done for the sake of **convenience**. 

Therefore, the jacketed robe used in our current era began with the Northern Qi.

# Inner Sleeping Robe <note>original: 裻 *du*; often refers to a garment with a prominent back seam or a specific type of inner robe used for sleeping</note>

The **Classic of Poetry** <note>original: *Shijing* (詩經); the oldest collection of Chinese poetry, dating from the 11th to 7th centuries BCE</note> says, "Embracing the quilt and the inner robe." 

The **Analects** <note>original: *Lunyu* (論語); the foundational text of Confucianism</note> states, "One must have a sleeping robe, the length of which is one and a half times the body's length." This pertains to the customs of the **Shang and Zhou dynasties** <note>c. 1600–256 BCE</note>.

The **Miscellaneous Records of the Western Capital** <note>original: *Xijing Zaji* (西京雜記); a collection of historical anecdotes regarding the Han dynasty court at Chang'an</note> says that when **Zhao Feiyan** <note>the Empress of Emperor Cheng of Han, famous for her slender figure and dancing skill</note> was elevated to Empress, her younger sister, the **Lady of Bright Deportment** <note>original: *Zhaoyi* (昭儀); a title for a high-ranking palace consort, in this case referring to Zhao Hede</note>, presented her with thirty-five inner robes, including the "Mandarin Duck Inner Robe." Thus, the specific name *du* began to be used during the **Han dynasty** <note>206 BCE – 220 CE</note>.

# Mattress <note>original: 褥 *ru*</note>

# Crowns
<note>original: 冠 *Guan*</note>

The *Records of Rituals* <note>original: 一儀實錄 *Yiyi Shilu*</note> states: Since the time the Yellow Emperor <note>A legendary sovereign and cultural hero of Chinese mythology</note> created crowns and ceremonial caps, women's head ornaments lacked established patterns or decorative styles. Until the Zhou dynasty, these were nothing more than head-wraps made of wide bamboo sheaths. It was only during the Han dynasty that women of the palace who received imperial favor were first granted "Green Tortoiseshell Hibiscus Crowns." Thus, such specialized items began with the Han.

# Hair Buns
<note>original: 髻 *Ji*</note>

The *Records* state: The women of the Suiren clan <note>The mythical discoverers of fire</note> were the first to bind their hair into buns. Eventually, the ornaments used by queens evolved into "accessories and braids" <note>original: 副編 *Fu bian*; complex hairpieces and supplemental braids used in high ceremonial dress</note>. The scholar Zheng Xuan noted that in the capital region, these were known as "false buns" <note>early forms of wigs or hairpieces used to add volume and height</note>.

# Hairpins
<note>original: 釵 *Chai*; specifically refers to two-pronged hairpins</note>

The *Records* state: The Suiren clan first created the hair bun. The daughter of the goddess Nüwa used thorn branches and bamboo to make single-pronged pins <note>original: 笄 *ji*</note> to pierce and secure the hair. By the era of Emperor Yao, these were made of copper and inserted horizontally. Emperor Shun favored ivory and tortoiseshell. Guo Xian’s *Record of Penetrating the Mysteries* <note>original: 洞冥記 *Dongming Ji*, a 1st-century collection of Han dynasty legends</note> states: In the first year of the Yuanding era (116 BCE), a divine woman visited Emperor Wu of Han and left behind a jade hairpin. Because of this, the palace ladies began to craft them for themselves.

# Bracelets and Finger Rings
<note>original: 釧 指鐶 *Chuan Zhihuan*</note>

During the Later Han dynasty, Sun Cheng and nineteen others were recognized for their meritorious service in establishing Emperor Shun <note>reigned 125–144 AD</note> on the throne. As a reward, each was granted gold bracelets and finger rings. This serves as evidence that the design and use of bracelets were already established during the Han period.

The *Essential Meanings of the Five Classics* <note>original: 五經要義 *Wujing Yaoyi*</note> states: In ancient times, when the imperial consorts and concubines attended to the sovereign, those who were ready for duty would come forward wearing a silver ring. Those entering wore the ring on the right hand, while those withdrawing wore it on the left. This was originally a regulation from the Three Dynasties <note>The Xia, Shang, and Zhou dynasties, considered the golden age of Chinese ritual</note>, and it is the ancestor of what we today call the "abstinence ring" <note>original: 戒指 *Jiezhi*; the modern Chinese word for 'finger ring,' which literally translates to 'abstinence ring' or 'ring of restraint'</note>.

## Cloud Stole
<note>original: 霞帔 *Xiápèi*</note>

Regarding the stole <term>original: 帔 *Pèi*; a long, narrow decorative garment worn over the shoulders, trailing down the front</term>, the *Veritable Records* <note>original: 實錄 *Shilu*; likely referring to a specific historical chronicle</note> state that this garment did not exist during the Three Dynasties <note>the Xia, Shang, and Zhou periods, traditionally considered the foundation of Chinese civilization</note>. 

It is said that the Qin dynasty <note>221–206 BC</note> introduced "draped silk" <term>original: 披帛 *Pībó*</term>, which was made of a fine, sturdy silk fabric. By the Han dynasty, these were crafted from lightweight gauze. During the Yongjia era of the Jin dynasty <note>307–313 AD</note>, the "platform-style stole" was established. Thus, the concept of draped silk originated with the Qin, while the formal stole began with the Jin.

Tang dynasty <note>618–907 AD</note> regulations permitted women below the rank of the Three Consorts <note>the highest-ranking imperial wives below the Empress</note> to wear it generally. Unmarried women of the scholar and commoner classes would wear "draped silk" while staying in the family home; however, once they were married, they would wear the formal "stole" <note>original: 帔子 *Pèizi*</note>. This practice served as a way to distinguish their social status and whether they were "at home" or "wedded." 

By the Song dynasty <note>960–1279 AD</note>, there were three distinct grades of stoles. The "Cloud Stole" <note>literally "Rosy Cloud Stole," named for its beautiful colors resembling the sky at dawn or sunset</note> was a mark of high prestige; a woman could not wear it unless it was bestowed upon her as a special imperial honor.

<note>The accompanying illustration shows a "Xiápèi," a long, V-shaped ceremonial stole that would be draped over the neck and shoulders. It is decorated with intricate patterns of clouds and auspicious birds, ending in a pointed or weighted tip often adorned with a decorative pendant.</note>

# First Degree of Mourning: Unhemmed Robes <term>Zhancui; the most severe level of ritual mourning, worn for three years for a father or by a ruler's subjects</term>

According to *Master Wen’s Family Rituals* <note>original: 文公家禮 Wéngōng Jiālǐ; a foundational text by the Neo-Confucian scholar Zhu Xi, posthumously titled Master Wen</note>, the term "unhemmed" <note>original: 斬 zhǎn</note> means the edges are not stitched or finished. Both the upper robe and the lower skirt are made from the coarsest raw hemp cloth. The side seams and the bottom edges are all left unhemmed. 

The robe's length reaches past the waist, sufficient to cover the upper edge of the skirt. The seams of the robe face outward <note>seams are usually hidden; facing them outward symbolizes the wearer's total disregard for appearance during deep grief</note>. On the back, there is a "load-bearing board" <term>fùbǎn; a rectangular patch of cloth representing the heavy burden of sorrow carried by the bereaved</term>. On the front, directly over the heart, is the "mourning patch" <term>cuī; a small piece of cloth symbolizing the grief centered in the chest</term>. On the left and right are "split collars" <term>pìlǐng</term>. Under both armpits are side-flaps <term>rèn</term> that hang downward in the shape of a swallow’s tail to cover the side gaps of the skirt. 

The lower skirt <term>shāng</term> consists of three panels in the front and four panels in the back. The seams of the skirt face inward. The front and back sections are not connected to one another. Each panel is made with three pleats <term>pì; a specific ritual fold</term>. To "pleat" refers to folding the two sides of the fabric so they meet, leaving the center hollow.

## Construction of the Crown <term>Guānzhì</term>

The construction of the crown uses fabric that is slightly finer than that used for the robe and skirt. It uses a frame made of paper or cardboard for support. It is three inches wide and long enough to span across the top of the head. It is fashioned with three pleats. 

A single hemp rope is used to secure it: starting from the forehead, it is bound toward the back of the head, where it crosses and returns to the front at each ear. Here it is tied to form the headband <term>wǔ</term>. The remaining ends of the rope hang down as chin-straps <term>yīng</term>, which are tied beneath the jaw <term>hán</term>.

### Accessories of the First Degree of Mourning

The <term>Headband (Shǒuzhì); a thick ring of unrefined hemp worn around the head</term> is made from hemp with seeds <note>original: 有子麻 yǒuzǐmá; male hemp, chosen for its coarseness to reflect the severity of grief</note>. Its circumference is nine inches. The root-end of the hemp should be positioned on the left side; from the forehead, wrap it toward the right. Additionally, use a cord to serve as the chin-straps.

The <term>Waistband (Yāozhì); a thick hemp rope worn around the waist over the robe</term> has a circumference of a little over seven inches. The two ends are crossed and tied together. At each end, leave the root-portions of the hemp hanging down loosely for a length of three feet. On both sides of the knot, attach small cords to secure the band in place.

The <term>Twisted Sash (Jiǎodài)</term> is made from a single strand of hemp rope with seeds. Its thickness is a bit more than half that of the waistband. Fold it in the middle to create two "limbs" of about one foot each, then join them together. The overall thickness should then match that of the head and waist bands. To wear it, wrap it around the waist starting from the left, passing around the back to the front. Take the right end and thread it through the loop created by the two limbs, then tuck it back in on the right side, positioned underneath the waistband.

The <term>Mourning Staff (Zhàng)</term>:
*   For a father, use bamboo. The root-end must be at the bottom <note>Bamboo is round and remains green through the winter, symbolizing the enduring nature of the father's authority and the "Heavenly" principle</note>.
*   For a mother, use paulownia wood. It is carved round at the top and square at the bottom <note>The square base symbolizes the Earth, traditionally associated with the mother, while the round top symbolizes Heaven</note>.

The <term>Sandals (Jù)</term> are made of sedge <note>original: 藨 biāo</note>. Specifically, use cogon grass to weave the sandals; they are similar in style to modern cattail leaf shoes.

# Trimmed Mourning (Qi Shuai)
<note>original: 齊衰 (Qíshuāi); the second of the five degrees of mourning in Confucian ritual. The name "Qi" means to trim or hem, referring to the fact that the edges of the hemp garment are neatly stitched, unlike the highest degree (Zhan Shuai) where the edges are left raw and ragged to show extreme grief.</note>

"Trimmed" refers to hemming the edges. For this grade, second-grade raw hemp cloth is used <note>original: 枲 (xǐ); specifically the fibers from male hemp plants, which were considered slightly less coarse than the female hemp used for the highest mourning grade</note>. The sides and the bottom edges are hemmed; all other aspects are the same as the Unhemmed Mourning grade.

The **regulation cap** is made using cloth for the headband and the tassels; all other aspects are the same as the Unhemmed Mourning.

The **head cord** is made of hemp that has no seeds. Its circumference is approximately seven inches. The root end of the cord is placed on the right side, and the tip is tied beneath the root. The cloth tassels are the same as those used in Unhemmed Mourning.

The **waist cord** is over five inches in circumference. Its construction is the same as the Unhemmed Mourning.

The **twisted belt** for Trimmed Mourning and the grades below it is made of cloth. The right end is folded back by more than one foot. The **carved staff** is made of paulownia wood; the top is shaped round and the bottom is shaped square <note>In Chinese cosmology, the circle represents Heaven and the square represents Earth. The paulownia staff is used when mourning a mother, contrasting with the bamboo staff used for a father.</note>.

**Coarse sandals** are a type of rough footwear. They are made from sparsely woven grass.

# Eye-Covering Cloth and Hand-Grasping Cloths

### Eye-Covering Cloth
<term>míngmù jīn: a square of fabric placed over the face of the deceased to cover the eyes.</term>

Use processed silk <note>original: "shújuàn" (熟絹), silk that has been boiled to remove sericin, making it soft and white.</note> cut into a square measuring one foot and two inches. It should be double-layered and stitched together, filled with silk floss <term>mián: traditionally silk wadding used for padding</term> inside. The four corners are equipped with straps, which are tied and knotted behind the head.

### Hand-Grasping Cloths
<term>wòshǒu bó: narrow strips of padded fabric used to wrap the hands of the deceased.</term>

Use one width of processed silk. Each piece is one foot and two inches long and five inches wide. The interior is filled with silk floss. These are used to wrap the hands, and both ends of each cloth are equipped with straps for tying.

<note>These items, the *míngmù* and *wòshǒu*, were essential components of the "minor dressing" (xiǎoliàn) of the body. Their purpose was both practical—maintaining the dignity of the deceased by keeping the eyes closed and the hands positioned—and symbolic, marking the transition from the world of the living to the ritual state of the dead.</note>

### The Bamboo Hat

The bamboo hat <note>original: "lì" (笠)</note> is a piece of headgear. In ancient times, the bark of the *tai* plant <note>original: "tái" (臺), a type of sedge grass</note> was used to make these hats. This is what the *Classic of Poetry* <note>The Shijing, or Book of Songs, the oldest collection of Chinese poetry</note> refers to when it mentions "sedge hats and black cloth caps." 

Today, these hats are made by weaving a bamboo frame into a shell and wrapping it with bamboo sheaths or broad leaves. They come in various sizes, but all feature a raised, conical top and a circular rim. They are designed to provide shelter from the rain and shade from the sun, serving as the natural companion to the grass raincoat.

### The Grass Raincoat

The grass raincoat <note>original: "suō" (蓑)</note> is a garment for the rain. The poem "No Sheep" in the *Classic of Poetry* says, "Who brings the raincoat? Who brings the hat?" 

Mao's Commentary <note>A foundational Han-dynasty commentary on the Classic of Poetry</note> states: "The raincoat is used to prepare for rain; the hat is used to resist the summer heat." 

The *Tangyun* <note>A Tang Dynasty rhyme dictionary</note> notes that *suo* is the name of a grass that can be used to make raincoats. It is also known as a *sui* <gloss>pronounced as "sui"</gloss> or a *zhen* <gloss>pronounced as "zhen"</gloss>. 

The *Shuowen Jiezi* <note>An early 2nd-century dictionary</note> says that in the ancient Qin region, this garment was called a *bi* <gloss>pronounced as "bi" or "pi"</gloss>. 

The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese encyclopedia/dictionary</note> identifies the "Bi-hou" as the *suo*. Because these raincoats are constructed from *sha* <note>sedge</note> grass, the name shares the same sound as the word for that grass. 

It is also called a *xue*. The *Register of Agricultural Tools* lists "raincoats, *xue*-capes, baskets, and hats" together. Today, all such items are generally referred to as *suo*. Among all types of rain gear, this is considered the most lightweight and convenient.

# The Back-Shell

![Woodcut illustration of a 'Fu Qiao', a protective woven bamboo back-shell used by farmers for shade and rain protection.](illustration)

->**The Back-Shell**<-
<gloss>Also known as a "Partridge" or a "Thin Shoulder Wrap"</gloss>

The back-shell <note>original: "fù ké" (覆殼)</note> is woven from bamboo strips into a shape resembling a turtle's shell. It is then wrapped with bamboo sheaths <note>the protective outer skin of bamboo shoots</note> and worn over a person's back. 

Cords are tied beneath the shoulders to secure it. During the work of weeding and hoeing, it is used to ward off the fierce sun, while also serving as a garment for the rain. There is a curved opening at the bottom to allow for ventilation, and it is also designed to shed rainwater effectively. 

In the height of the summer heat, when a farmer has this tool, he can avoid the suffering of intense exposure to the elements. It is truly a case of "a gourd being worth a thousand pieces of gold" <note>An idiom referring to a simple, humble object that proves to be of immense value in a moment of crisis or extreme need</note>.

# The Ventilating Hairpin

->**The Ventilating Hairpin**<-
<gloss>Also known as a "Breath Tube"</gloss>

The ventilating hairpin <note>original: "tōng zān" (通簪)</note> is a hollow pin used to pierce through the hair. It is also called an "air cylinder" <note>original: "qì tǒng" (氣筒)</note>. It is crafted from the tapered tip of a deer antler <gloss>if deer antler is unavailable, bamboo or wood may be used as a substitute; a large feather quill is also acceptable</gloss>. 

It is approximately three inches long <note>roughly 10 centimeters; the "cun" or Chinese inch varied but was generally longer than a standard imperial inch</note>. Several small holes are pierced horizontally around its circumference so that they all connect through the hollow center; for this reason, it is called the "Ventilating" Hairpin. 

When male and female farmers work under the scorching summer sun, bending their waists and bowing their heads, their internal heat <note>original: "qi" (氣)</note> rises and sweat pours out. Their hair buns <note>original: "fà jì" (髮髻)</note> become steamed and stifling. By inserting one or two of these pins to allow the air to circulate, they will naturally feel refreshed and comfortable. 

Though this object is humble and small, its effectiveness in benefiting people is truly admirable.

# Straw Sandals (Fei)

Straw sandals <note>original: "fēi" (扉)</note> are shoes made of grass. The *Zuo Zhuan* <note>The *Commentary of Zuo*, a foundational historical chronicle of ancient China</note> states: "Provide their provisions and their straw sandals." The *Shuowen Jiezi* <note>An influential 2nd-century dictionary</note> says: "*Fei* are grass shoes." The sub-commentaries of Kong Yingda <note>A prominent Tang Dynasty scholar</note>
explain that both *fei* and *ju* <term>jù (屨): a general term for woven shoes</term> are items worn on the feet; the different names merely distinguish between the "good" (fine) and the "bad" (coarse). The *Commentary on Mourning Garments* <note>Part of the *Rites of Zhou* or *Book of Rites*</note> states: "Coarse footwear refers to sandals made of rough sedge grass <note>original: "kuǎi" (蒯)</note>."
Thus, these sandals are made using grass. Wang Erzhang <note>A scholar cited to clarify terminology</note> remarks that "lu" <term>lǚ (履): a broad term for footwear or the act of treading</term> is a general way of referring to all shoes. Here, the terms
*fei* and *ju* are compared and contrasted so that the reader may clearly understand the distinction between them.
