## EPISTLE

<meta>catchword: Domi- (likely 'Dominis', referring to the 'Lords' or 'Counts' in the dedication)</meta>

*...I should think that knowledge is to be drawn from books—a blind conviction that many have abused, neglecting the voice of their teachers to the greatest detriment of their studies. Unless this error is corrected, it threatens the immediate ruin and destruction of the liberal arts and disciplines <term>humanioribus artibus & disciplinis</term> <note>The 'more humane' arts, referring to the humanities and classical education.</note>, and will bring back the barbarism which our ancestors, with the greatest effort, barely managed to repel from the schools. For these two things, which have the greatest affinity and kinship with each other, are not to be separated. There are many things which are not set forth by teachers; those must be drawn from books. Yet many things set forth in books are more difficult and escape the sharpest mind; here, teachers must be consulted to explain the 'knots' of difficulty. I believe it is clear to no one that whoever separates these two from each other will suffer the greatest loss of time and studies. To make this opinion of mine publicly known, beyond those scholastic labors which I sustain by teaching both publicly and privately, it seemed right to make public this Perspective of Roger Bacon <note>original: "Rogerii Bacconis Perspectivam." Roger Bacon (c. 1214–1292) was a pioneer of the scientific method; 'Perspective' was the medieval term for the science of optics.</note>, transcribed by me from the oldest parchment manuscripts in the public library of Oxford University <term>Oxoniensis Academiae</term>. In it, Roger—a most eminent man and easily the leading philosopher of that century, whose knowledge I admire among other Scholastic Doctors <term>Scholasticos Doctores</term> <note>The 'Scholastics' were the medieval academics who combined philosophy with Christian theology.</note>—has gathered into a summary what was treated at length by other experts in optics <term>Opticis</term>.*

G." - likely an abbreviation for 'Vestris Generositatibus', a formal honorific meaning 'Your Graces' or 'Your Nobilities'</note>, so that some testimony and monument of my humble respect toward you might stand. Since the most illustrious and powerful Prince and...*

# On Bacon Revived.

Just as the Sun, rising when it has scattered the shadows of the night, 
    spreads its golden radiance everywhere through the land;
Just as also, when she has first filled her horns <note>a poetic reference to the crescent moon becoming full</note>, beautiful Cynthia <note>a name for the Moon goddess</note>
    illuminates the **world** with new rays:
So do the divine writings of Bacon shine, and now,
    revived, they see a day hitherto unknown.
And those things which had been immersed in perpetual darkness
    possess a light greater than the Evening Star <note>original: *Hesperio sidere*, referring to the planet Venus</note>.
Therefore the honor of Bacon and his buried name rises 
    again, and flourishes in late posterity.
Truly this is the goal: virtue lives on after death;
    the Muse <note>the personification of poetic and intellectual inspiration</note> forbids a man worthy of praise to perish.
Go, see the light, foster-child of a famous father;
    go, happy one, fly through the learned mouths of men.
Go, book, and protect your father’s fame;
    immortal glory, life, and perennial honor shall follow.

**Ludovicus Combach.**

*Do not believe that medicines are written for weary eyes,*
    *you who see the title of this book is "On Vision."*
*Bacon did not wish to describe here the diseases of the eye,*
    *which he handed down with great skill in other books.*
*But rather, what the nature, the manner, and the cause of seeing might be;*
    *how vision differs, and how far it can penetrate.*
*All that the ancients or the moderns wrote,*
    *he embraced with wonderful skill.*
*Whatever <term>Alhazen</term> <note>Ibn al-Haytham (c. 965–1040), the influential Arab "father of optics"</note> holds at length, and <term>Vitello</term> <note>Witelo (c. 1230–1280), a Polish friar and physicist whose work on optics was a standard text</note> wrote,*
    *this one man teaches with transparent brevity.*
*Therefore, you who seek to reach the goal briefly,*
    *read this; it can stand alone in place of many.*

**M. Christophorus Combach.**

->ROGER BACON<-

...parts of <term>optics</term><note>original: "perspectivę"; in the Middle Ages, this term referred to the science of light, vision, and mirrors, rather than artistic perspective.</note>, such as the book on sight, the book on mirrors, and others besides these. <term>Alhazen</term><note>Ibn al-Haytham (c. 965–1040), a pioneering Arab mathematician and physicist whose work on optics deeply influenced European scholars.</note>, however, treated the subject with excessive verbosity, both in his content and his method. <term>Ptolemy</term><note>Claudius Ptolemy (c. 100–170 AD), the Greco-Egyptian astronomer and mathematician.</note>, on the other hand, proceeds with sufficient moderation. Now, therefore, at your insistence, I wish to gather together certain essential kernels into a compendium drawn from all these authors.

But one must always have at hand my treatise on the <term>generation of species</term><note>Bacon’s theory of "species" describes how physical forces—like light, heat, or sound—multiply and radiate through space to affect other objects.</note>, and their multiplication, action, and corruption, without which nothing worthy of note can be understood regarding optics. Moreover, this science is far more beautiful and useful than others, and therefore more delightful, since our primary delight is in sight. And light and color possess a special beauty beyond other things presented to our senses; and it is not only that their beauty is recognized, but a greater utility and necessity arise from them. For Aristotle says in the first book of the *Metaphysics* that sight alone reveals to us the differences between things; for through it we seek out certain experiences of all things that are in the heavens and on the earth. 

For those things in the heavens are considered through visual instruments, as Ptolemy and other astronomers teach. And similarly for those things generated in the air, such as comets and rainbows and the like. For their height above the horizon, their magnitude, shape, number, and all that is within them, are verified through methods of seeing with instruments. Those things which are here on earth we experience through sight, because a blind person can experience nothing of this world that is worthy of note. Hearing causes us to believe, because we believe our teachers, but we cannot experience what we learn except through sight. But if we were to rely on taste, touch, and smell, then we would be putting on a "beast-like wisdom." For brute animals are concerned with things that can be tasted and touched, and they exercise smell for the sake of taste and touch; but the things verified by these senses are base and few, and common to both us and the beasts, and therefore they do not rise to the dignity of human wisdom.
