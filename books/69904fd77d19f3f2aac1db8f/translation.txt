ALTHOUGH my duty and authority as a translator have drawn my own "Marsilian Plotinus" <note>original: "Marsilium Plotinum"; Ficino refers to his life's work translating and interpreting the philosopher Plotinus</note> (so to speak) away through the habit of daily association, and have demanded longer explanations for his arguments; nevertheless, when yesterday I brought a certain Latin explanation to that very most holy likeness of God <note>The Sun</note> which the Platonic parent <note>Plato</note> participates in according to the highest degree: for our Dionysius the Areopagite <note>A 5th-century Christian theologian whom Ficino believed was a disciple of St. Paul; he blended Christian thought with Neoplatonism</note>, in the manner of the Platonists, attempts an edition of punctuation <note>likely referring to the clarity of divine arrangement</note> in minds liberated by the most blessed illustration of the Sun. Since God and the rare light shine red with many names, I have now judged this matter and this clarity worthy (for you wish from this work what is especially convenient in a brief summary: this glowing Phoebus <note>A name for Apollo, the sun god</note> as it has been set down). I lead this sum greatly toward Rhodes <note>A metaphor for a place of great light or perhaps a specific destination</note>: to send it to the parent of the Medici; since it will be the one thing to which I have dedicated this new interpretation of Plato, lest any force should stand in the way. For light for light, and again that highest mind of Plato’s work prepares for the King of Lights; which merit has inspired your mind toward Marsilio. And so, having been kindled by this ardor of light, may you revere it and embrace it with your whole loving mind.

# THE PREFACE OF MARSILIO FICINO TO THE BOOK "ON THE SUN" ADDRESSED TO THE MAGNANIMOUS PIERO DE' MEDICI

I am pursuing daily, Magnanimous Piero, the new translation of Plato <note>Ficino refers to his monumental project of translating Plato's complete works from Greek into Latin</note> begun long ago under your family's auspices. As you are well aware, I am explaining it with frequent divisions of the chapters and with arguments as lengthy as the subject matter itself demands. 

Thus, when I recently arrived at that Platonic mystery where he most skillfully compares the Sun to God himself, I decided to explain such a great matter at somewhat greater length. I did this especially because our Dionysius the Areopagite <note>A 5th-century Christian theologian whom Ficino believed was a direct disciple of St. Paul; he blended Christian thought with Neoplatonism</note>—the foremost of the Platonists, whose translation I currently have in my hands—gladly embraces a similar comparison of the Sun to God. 

Therefore, while I was working by the light of this Sun, as if by a lamp, through many nights of study, I thought to select this most choice matter from the larger work and commend it in its own short volume. I decided to send this mystery concerning the Sun—a gift of Phoebus <note>original: "Phębeū munus"; Phoebus is an epithet for Apollo, the god of the sun and music</note>, as it were—to you above all others. For you are a foster son of Phoebus, the leader of the Muses, and a patron of the Muses yourself. Indeed, this entire new translation of Plato is dedicated to you alone. 

I send this so that, in the meantime, by this light—which is like a moon—you might predict what the entire Platonic work will be like, just as the moon reflects the Sun. And if you have ever loved my Plato (or rather, yours for a long time now), may you, ignited by this light, love him more ardently hereafter, and embrace that which you love with your whole mind.

# Words to the reader. That this book is allegorical and mystical rather than dogmatic. Chapter One.

It is a Pythagorean precept, noble-minded Peter, and a truly divine one, that one must not speak of divine matters or mysteries without light. In these words (as I judge), that wise man signifies not only that nothing should be dared in divine matters except insofar as the light of God itself has revealed it to minds inspired from above; but he also seems to warn that we should not set out to perceive or declare the hidden light of divine things without first preparing this manifest light. Therefore, for the present, we shall approach the former from the latter, not so much by formal reasoning as by certain comparisons drawn from light to the best of our ability.

But you, meanwhile, most careful reader—and, I hope, most indulgent toward us—remembering that we are operating under the Sun of Apollo <note>The god of light, music, and prophecy, used here to represent poetic inspiration</note> and in a poetic manner, do not hereafter demand from us more severe or (as the Greeks say) dogmatic <note>original: "dogmatica"; referring to fixed, literal doctrines</note> treatments. Indeed, with Phoebus <note>Another name for Apollo</note> as my guarantor—for these are his gifts—I have promised only an allegorical and mystical <note>term: anagogical (anagogicam) — a method of spiritual interpretation that leads the mind upward to divine realities</note> exercise of the wits. The Muses never dispute with Apollo; instead, they sing. Even Mercury himself, the first inventor of the art of disputation, though he treats grave matters with Saturn or Jupiter, nevertheless plays when he is with Apollo. He indeed jests not only aptly, but divinely. As for us, would that we at least do so not ineptly.

Let us begin our play. But now, as we are about to perform these preludes of ours—whatever this light may turn out to be—let us come forth into the light, with the Supreme Good, that is, the Most High God, happily inspiring us.

## Chapter II. How the light of the Sun is similar to the Good itself, namely God.

Nothing reflects the nature of the Good more than light. First, indeed, light appears as the purest and most eminent thing in the realm of the senses. Second, it spreads most easily, most widely, and in an instant. Third, it meets and penetrates all things without harm; it is most gentle and soothing. Fourth, it carries with it a nourishing heat, fostering, generating, and moving all things. Fifth, while it is present and within all things, it is defiled by none and mixed with none. Similarly, the Good itself towers over the entire order of things. It expands most widely. It soothes and entices all things. It compels nothing. It has Love as a companion everywhere, like a heat. By this Love, individual things are everywhere enticed, and they freely embrace the Good. Being most present in the innermost parts of things everywhere, it has no <note>The author means the Good remains transcendent and untainted by the material world.</note> commerce with them. Finally, just as the Good itself is inestimable and ineffable, so almost is light. For no philosopher has yet defined this; so that while nothing is clearer than light anywhere, nothing on the other hand seems more obscure—just as the Good is the best known of all things and equally the most unknown. ### The Sun: Illuminator, Lord, and Governor of the Heavens. Chapter III.

The Sun, as the manifest lord of the heavens, rules and governs absolutely all celestial bodies. I shall for the present pass over its immense magnitude—which is thought to contain the Earth one hundred and sixty-six times <note>original: "centies sexagiesque et sexties"</note>. In the first place, it implants light into all the stars: whether they possess a small native light of their own (as some suspect), or none at all (as most believe). Furthermore, that specific sign among the twelve is called "living" (as Aly <note>likely Albohali or Al-Sufi, medieval Arabic astronomers</note> and Abraham <note>likely Abraham Ibn Ezra, a prominent Jewish scholar and astrologer</note> say), and it appears so by its effect, because the Sun gives it life at that time. Indeed, the Sun fills two signs on either side with such great power that both spaces are called by the Arabs the "Guides of the Sun" <note>original: "ductoria Solis"</note>, that is, the "Royal Throne" <note>original: "regium solium"</note>. While the planets are within this space—provided they avoid being scorched <note>a technical term for a planet being too close to the sun, known as "combust"</note>—they prevail with marvelous power. This is especially true if, while positioned in this interval, the superior planets rise before the Sun, and the inferior planets rise after the Sun. Again, that sign in which the Sun reigns—that is, Aries—becomes the head of the signs; it signifies the head in any living being. Also, that sign which is the Sun’s domicile—that is, Leo—is the heart of the signs, and it holds the place of the heart in any living being. Now, truly, when the Sun reaches Leo, a plague, as if of fire <note>original: "Pyronicum," referring to a feverish or burning pestilence</note>, truly...

## Chapter 4: The conditions of the planets in relation to the Sun.

Likewise, certain fixed and defined spaces in the heavens are seen everywhere to be determined by the Sun itself. When the planets have first traversed these, they change their motion and condition. For **Saturn**, **Jupiter**, and **Mars**, upon reaching a **trine** aspect <note>original: "conspectum trinum"; an astrological aspect where planets are 120 degrees apart</note> in the third part of the sky from the Sun, immediately change their motion and move either forward or backward. If they are **oriental** <note>rising before the Sun</note> from the Sun, they move backward <term>retrograde: the apparent backward motion of a planet as seen from Earth</term>; if they are **occidental** <note>setting after the Sun</note>, they move forward. **Venus** and **Mercury**, however...

Certain greater but fixed distances are traversed from the Sun and toward the Sun. Indeed, **Venus** is said to depart no further than 49 degrees, while **Mercury** departs 28 degrees. The **Moon** changes its shape and nature in every aspect <note>The "aspect" refers to the angular relationship between two celestial bodies</note> of the Sun. And like a second Sun, passing through four ages, it represents the four seasons of the year. Whenever it is joined with the Sun, the nature of the coming month is declared by that very degree of conjunction and the configuration of the sky. Furthermore, all the planets, as soon as they touch the **heart of the Sun** <note>original: "cor Solis" — a technical term for a planet being in the exact same degree as the Sun, also known as Cazimi</note>, dominate all others at that time, however brief it may be. Otherwise, when approaching the Sun, they are meanwhile vacant from their usual office. Indeed, when meeting with the Sun, **Saturn** is judged to set aside its former rigidity, and **Mars** likewise its accustomed ferocity.

The superior planets <note>The planets further from the Sun than Earth: Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn</note> ascend as the Sun approaches them. However, as it departs, they conversely descend. When joined to the Sun, they are at the highest point of their **epicycle** <term>epicycle: a small circle the center of which moves around the circumference of a larger one, used to explain planetary orbits</term>; when opposite, they are at the lowest. In **quadrature** <note>An angle of 90 degrees between two celestial bodies</note>, they are at a middle altitude. The Moon, in both cases, exists at its peak in the deferent <note>The main circular path</note>. In quadrature it descends. **Venus** and **Mercury**, if they are joined to the Sun while moving forward, are at their highest; if moving backward <note>retrograde motion</note>, they are at their lowest. Nor is it permitted for the planets to complete the circuit of their epicycle before they have revisited the Sun, as if their lord, in **conjunction**. In these things we have said, the superior planets—when they change their step into the **trine** aspect <note>An angle of 120 degrees</note> in the sight of the Sun—truly seem to revere the royal gaze of the Sun.

### The Power of the Sun in Generations and Seasons.
### In Birth and in All Things. Chapter V.

Finally, in the birth of every human being, the position of the **Moon** itself declares the lord of the birth and the moment of conception. The conjunction or opposition <note>Conjunction occurs when the Sun and Moon are together (New Moon); opposition occurs when they are on opposite sides of the Earth (Full Moon)</note> of the Moon with the Sun occurring before a person's birth reveals the truth and the fortune of that birth. Furthermore, the **Part of Fortune** designates the space first observed between the Sun and the Moon, then projected from the degree of the Ascendant. Accordingly, **Astronomers** find and measure the movements of the planets through the already discovered movement of the Sun. The Sun, by its own motion, distinguishes nights and days, hours, months, and years. Likewise, by its light and heat, it generates, quickens, moves, regenerates all things, cheers them, and warms them; and those things which had been hidden, at its first arrival...

### Praises of the Ancients for the Sun: and how the powers of the heavens are all in the Sun and from the Sun. Chapter VI.

For these reasons, Orpheus called Apollo the life-giving eye of heaven. And these things which I shall say are gathered summarily from the Orphic hymns: The Sun is the eternal eye seeing all things. The pre-eminent celestial light, tempering the heavenly and the worldly. Leading, or rather drawing, the harmonious course of the world. The Lord of the world. Immortal Jupiter, the eye of the world running its circuit, holding the seal and shaping all worldly things. The Moon is heavy with stars. The Moon is the queen of the stars. # A Defense of his book concerning the Sun and Light.

## Marsilio Ficino of Florence sends greetings to Filippo Valori, the Florentine ambassador to the Pope.

I have been holding back the book concerning the Sun, which was intended for the most illustrious Piero de' Medici <note>Piero the Unfortunate (1472–1503), son of Lorenzo the Magnificent</note>, at my home for quite some time now. Indeed, contrary to the Gospel precept, I was almost impiously hiding a lamp—or rather a Sun—under a bushel basket. But that man, Piero Nero <note>original: "Petrus Nerus"; a close associate of Ficino and a member of the Platonic Academy</note>, whom I have frequently called "the most brilliant" in my letters, judged that it would be a pious act for this light to shine forth to as many people as possible (as God commands). And so, I most willingly obeyed this most brilliant man, to whom the care of light and brilliance especially pertains. Thus, beyond the mystery commended to the Great-Souled Piero de' Medici, I have permitted the book to be openly set in type by the printers through the efforts of Piero Nero.

Behold then, my Valori—you who are dearer to me than light itself—this Sun of mine, however small it may be, is now rising in public. But perhaps it is soon to be obscured by rising mists, or will even prove troublesome to certain owls and bats <note>Ficino uses "owls and bats" as a metaphor for critics or those who lack the spiritual "sight" to understand Neoplatonic philosophy</note>. The winds will either scatter these mists beforehand, or the Sun itself, when it has ascended higher, will thin them out and dissolve them. As for the bats who grow dim-sighted and go blind in the light: advise them—if only they are capable of taking advice—not to blame the light, which is healthy for everyone else. Instead, let them flee this splendor in their usual fashion and, as is fitting for those unworthy of the light, let them remain constantly in hiding.
