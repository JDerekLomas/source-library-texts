After the mournful catastrophe of the universal flood and of *Noah’s* Ark <note>original: "Arcæ Noëmicæ"</note>; after the tragic destruction of the human race; after the most sorrowful appearance of the world and of all perishing things: I now bring forth onto the stage the *Tower of Babel*, representing a new state of the later age and the presentation of an unusual scene. A *Tower*, I say, constructed by the primeval tyrant of the world, *Nimrod* <note>original: "Nembrod"</note>, the son of *Ham* and *Cush* <note>original: "Chami Chusique"</note>, in the plain of *Shinar* <note>original: "Sennaar"</note> while the assembly of the entire human race was still lingering in that same place. A *Tower* celebrated—not to say infamous—throughout the perennial course of the ages for this reason alone: that the rash daring of human impiety attempted, with foolish presumption, to exalt a structure even to the furthest borders of the heavens. A structure, I say, which was guilty of treason against divine majesty <note>original: "læsæ majestatis divinæ"</note>; for while it arrogantly raised itself to a height unsuited to human affairs, it was cast down by the lightning of celestial vengeance and laid aside its pride. And so, as a clear proof of its own subversion, it testified to the supreme dominion of divine power and the truth of the Deity who is to be feared everywhere...

Beholding this impiety, and struck by an intimate grief of heart, *Noah* <note>original: Noë</note>, that restorer of the human race, soon separated his sons *Shem* and *Japheth* from the impious lineage of *Ham* <note>original: Chami</note> by divine command. From this we may be taught that there is no battering ram more violent for overturning the state of empires and kingdoms than impiety; and no protection more robust for preserving and supporting them than piety and religion. Therefore, I have seen fit to present an image of both these primeval empires—as a kind of prototype for all others—within the outlines of this volume I have written. I do this so that the kings of the earth and the rulers of the world may contemplate, as if in a mirror with an attentive examination of the mind, what befits the scepter and the purple and what does not; what is in harmony with right reason and what is discordant; and finally, what is composed and conformed to the standard of the Divine Moderator, and what is deformed.

<margin>Tacitus, Book I</margin> *Tiberius* used to say <note>original: "dicebat Tiberius"</note> that a mind of such great mass was not only capable of the task, but greater than it. How exactly and prudently you have conformed the administration of the empire committed to you according to the precise rule <term>amussim: a carpenter's rule or level used to ensure perfect alignment</term> of this *Noachic* Archetype <note>referring to the Ark of Noah as a model of divinely-guided governance and preservation</note>, and with what great faith and wisdom you have steered this ship amidst so many waves and storms, the world knows and admires. For what person—even one nourished in the densest hiding places of wild beasts or the innermost enclosures of inaccessible mountains—could now remain ignorant of either your virtue or the nefarious counsels of others? These were dangers unheard of in any age, by which your sacrosanct head—and with you, the very life of the entire *Austrian* House <note>The House of Habsburg</note>—was attacked through the highest wickedness. What place could be so cut off from our world by the intervals of lands and seas that the fame of your name and the sweetest breeze of your piety has not reached it? What corner of the world is so alienated from all humanity, what nation so uncultivated, that it did not shudder at the machination of those most foul monsters? By these, your safety—nay, ours, and that of all good men, for whom you are the life and salvation—was endangered.

O, the infamy of these times and morals! O, the tartarean <note>hellish</note> ferment of our most polluted age! O, the most stinking cancers of an ulcerated world, portents of a failing earth, plagues of the human race, infernal vipers—I say, the detestable Catilines <note>referring to Lucius Sergius Catilina, the archetypal Roman conspirator</note> of the empire! These men, whether drunk on the philter of blind ambition or driven mad by the fury of a haughty mind, sought to build their own *Tower of Babel*—that is, a tower of confusion—in the airy spaces of a most vain imagination. They did so by mixing the highest with the lowest and the sacred with the profane through truculent villainy. They sought to erect thrones for themselves without a foundation, while trampling those truly erected by God. They believed they could achieve this by no better—nay, by a worse—plan than if they were to utterly overturn, through the hidden techniques of their machinations, that sacred fabric of the empire destined for you by divine providence, which has been supported <note>original: "succollatam," literally carried on the neck or shoulders</note> with the greatest majesty through the course of so many centuries by the *Austrian* Atlases <note>The Habsburg rulers, compared to the Titan Atlas who held up the heavens</note>, intending to fashion a new empire for themselves from its ruins. They seemed like new *Caligulas* resurrected from the lower world; just as he wished the Roman people had but a single neck so that he might strike down the head of the world with a single blow to more abundantly satisfy his most bloody thirst, so these men, of far more foul wickedness and madness...

### CHAPTER I.

->Concerning the departure of Noah and his sons from the ark.<-

<margin>Chapter I. The first men did not immediately descend into the plains after leaving the ark.</margin> If we weigh the actions of the first men after the flood with an equal balance of the mind, right reason surely seems to dictate that the sons of Noah—after their departure from the ark, and after the sacrifice performed to God for the benefits received, and after the command and blessing bestowed upon them regarding the multiplication of the human race through the words (*Increase and multiply and fill the earth* <note>Genesis 9:1</note>)—did not immediately descend to the lower depths of the mountains. Rather, it is not far from the truth that they remained in the mountain plateaus for several years. For it is known from Geography that on most of the higher mountains, fields spread far and wide are always found, just as experience teaches us from the chains of the Alps, the Apennines, and other mountains to be seen throughout all Europe; these are found to be wonderfully suited and convenient for nourishing sheep, cattle, and herds with most fertile pastures. We believe, therefore, that Noah lived with his sons in similar fields.

<margin>Noah did not immediately descend into the level of the plains after exiting the ark, but remained in the mountains.</margin> For since in these types of higher plateaus which sat upon the mountain ridges, the veins of springs would be opened more quickly and seasonably for the necessary use of water (which was not the case in the lowest plains) due to the downward flow of muddy matter, and the fields would be rendered fit and suitable for agriculture by the flow of moisture, certainly *Noah* <note>original: "Noëmus"</note> could not have chosen a better or more convenient station, given his supreme skill regarding the knowledge of natural things. Although the sacred text says nothing of these matters, reason nevertheless seems to dictate, as I said above, that it must have happened in this manner.

For Noah, illuminated by divine light, knew the state of the reborn world; he knew very well the massive metamorphosis <note>A complete transformation of the earth's surface caused by the receding waters.</note> of the globe after the flood. He knew, finally, how long and for how many years they would have to linger in the mountains until the lower places below the mountains were fit for human habitation. And in this way it happened that he, in the mountains as if in a safer station, took the beginning of propagating the human race, until he was forced to betake himself to the lower plain by the multitude of sons rising up to an immense number.

<margin>Chapter II.</margin> Since nothing is more suited to the multiplication of mankind than an abundance of nourishment, without which they could not endure life, Noah—compelled by necessity—first of all taught his sons the method of cultivating the fields. He did this through the knowledge divinely infused into him (as we have previously shown regarding the construction of the Ark). Just as he had acted as a provident steward <note>Latin: *oeconomus*, a household manager or provider</note> before the Flood in gathering the necessary things for the use of the animals and the maintenance of the Ark, so too after the Flood, he spared no labor in cultivating his descendants and instructing them in the use of necessary things. As they were to fill the entire face of the orb through their migrations, they would propagate to posterity the methods and principles they had received as a wealthy inheritance—a successive tradition of arts flowing from Noah.

<margin>The necessity of the blacksmith's art and others.</margin> Furthermore, since they required other arts to subdue the earth for the exercise of agriculture, he trained his sons primarily in the Blacksmith’s art <note>Latin: *Ferraria arte*</note>. This was knowledge he had either drawn from the divine or had learned before the Flood from Tubal-cain, the master smith in every kind of iron and brass (as is held in *Genesis Chapter IV*). He fashioned tools not from native mountain mines—which at that time could not yet be worked—but from the iron implements he had brought with him into the Ark for this very purpose. These would have included plowshares, sickles, saws, grappling hooks, axes, knives, and the like. We also judge that he handed down the art of pottery, or the plastic art, by which jars, pots, bowls, and similar items for domestic use were fashioned from clay. <margin>The necessity of the builder's art.</margin> To protect themselves against storms and other injuries of the air, he prescribed the rules for building huts, cottages, and houses, just as he had seen and built before the Flood. <margin>The necessity of agriculture.</margin> Moreover, he brought into the Ark every kind of legume, as well as the seeds of wheat, emmer, and winter-wheat for farming, so that after the Flood, by the occasion of present necessity, he could live by immediately fertilizing the fields with seed. For how could that most provident steward have had seeds ready at hand if he had not carried them into the Ark with him? The entire earth was covered in filth and mud, and for several years could have produced none of these things. I wish the same to be understood regarding the planting of fruit-bearing trees and vineyards. For no sensible person could conceive how they could have lived for even the space of a single year without fruits, without bread, without meat, eggs, and the other supports for sustaining human life. Noah had foreseen that all these things would be necessary after the Flood; he knew that because of the grim and mournful face of the earth, all these things would be lacking. Hence, perhaps at the dictation of God himself, for the necessary requirements of a world being born anew, he carried with him into the Ark the seeds of all things, agricultural instruments, the shoots of fruit-bearing trees, and clusters of vines, so that he might use them for the benefit of men and animals without further delay.

<margin>The care and diligence of the wives.</margin> And while Noah taught these things to his sons, the care of matters proper to the female sex was left (if it is permitted to conjecture) to the wives. For these he prescribed the rules for sowing flax for the supply of clothing, soaking it in water, combing it into tow, and then spinning and weaving; he also gave instruction in the art of wool-working. Furthermore, he assigned them to the raising of hens and chicks for the procurement of eggs, and finally to every refinement of domestic service. In this way, these first colonies of mortals after the Flood most industriously put into execution what they had learned from Noah; and they, in turn, taught their descendants the various modes of living entrusted to them, as we shall demonstrate in the following pages.

Chapter V. In what manner the doubt I have proposed may be resolved in like fashion,

<margin>Whether there were different dialects in that original language.</margin>
I respond: since daily experience teaches us that no language is pronounced with such perfection by all the people of any nation that it does not deviate at least somewhat from the usage of the purer language which flourishes in the courts of Princes; and this is clearly evident in the *Italian*, *German*, *French*, *Spanish*, *Arabic* tongues and the like. It is believed that in *Italy*, the *Tuscan* language is the purest of all, which nevertheless in *Milan*, *Genoa*, *Bologna*, *Venice*, and among the *Samnites*, *Apulians*, and *Calabrians* differs greatly from its primeval purity; and yet it is one and the same language, namely that used in *Italy*, which all the natives speak and in which they understand one another mutually. I wish this to be understood likewise concerning the other languages of any nation, so that there is scarcely a language to be found in the world that does not suffer its own corruptions, whether this happens from the association of neighboring regions and peoples, or from the character of particular provinces and the nature of the place, or from the invention of new words. This is so true that I would not say it occurs only in separate provinces, but not even in great cities are different modes of speaking lacking, as experience has taught us here in *Rome*.

<margin>What the primeval language after the flood was.</margin>
It is asked, therefore, how the sacred text—*The earth was of one lip, and of the same speech* <note>Genesis 11:1; original: "Terra itaque erat labii unius, eorundemque sermonum"</note>—is to be understood? I respond, that there was indeed a language common to all, but the modes of pronouncing, which we call dialects, were different. For just as they were divided into three tribes according to the generations of the three sons of *Noah*, so also we believe that the use of the purer language remained only among the leaders of the tribes—*Shem*, *Ham*, *Japheth* and their sons and grandsons—while the popular and common language, arising from the various mixing of men (as we know happens in all other languages), declined somewhat from the use of the purer tongue, yet not so much that they did not mutually understand one another. And in this way the sacred text can easily be explained, as is clear from what was said just before; but which language that was specifically, we shall set forth in the *third book*, God willing.

<margin>The first men did not move toward the land of Shinar immediately after the flood.</margin> It is recorded in *Genesis* chapter 8 <note>The text says VII, but the exit from the Ark is in chapter VIII</note> that the ark came to rest on Mount *Ararat*. They were then compelled to descend into the flatter places of the sub-mountainous regions, to found a larger colony there as if in more fertile fields. Nor should anyone persuade himself that the peoples began their migration from that same place immediately; rather, it was after very many years, which is clearly evident from the context of the history itself, since between the building of the *Tower* and the flood—as we shall prove shortly by chorographic calculation—a span of 275 years is defined. It is clear, therefore, that they spent these years partly in the habitation of the mountains, partly in the flatter plains, and partly in the delay made on the journey to the plain of *Shinar*. For let us suppose they remained 20 years in the mountains, 75 in the flatter places, and spent five years on the journey toward *Shinar* (which space, according to geographical accounts, is a journey of two months); this would result in one hundred years, a number which is far from the years of the construction of the *Tower*.

I shall now show how they can be said to have come from the region of the *Ararat* plain, from the *East*, into the plain of *Shinar*. It is certain that they found vast plains below Mount *Ararat*, which they had entered coming out of the mountain valleys—plains perhaps once most fertile, today found partly fertile and partly sterile. Since these extend into the southern region far and wide, almost as far as the *Persian Sea*, under a long series of mountains, who does not see that these plains and regions are properly "eastern" with respect to the land of *Shinar*? <margin>The curiosity of the argument.</margin>
*These things, I say, impelled me, as far as it was permitted and as much as the capacity of my narrow mind would allow, to bring this argument into the light with the most detailed exposition, both for the knowledge of the truth of the most hidden things and for the delight of more curious minds. And our Tower would have to be that many miles high to touch the heaven of the Moon. Having thus explored the height of the Tower, let us now see how great its mass would be in solidity <note>volume</note>. First, according to the opinion of certain Arabic authors, let us assume the Tower was conical (though others feel it was square), and that at its diameter (or one side) it occupied a space of eight miles—that is, eight thousand paces. Its square will be 64 square miles. Now, since according to the ratio of Archimedes, the square of any diameter relates to the area of the same circle as 14 does to 11, by multiplying 11 by 64 and 

<margin>Section I.</margin> dividing the product by 14, the area of the circle will be 50 (or more accurately 50 2/7) square miles, or 50,000 paces. These again, according to the rules of Archimedes regarding the dimension of the cone and cylinder, multiplied by a third part of the height of the Tower (namely 59,557), will give the solidity of the whole Tower as 2,977,850 cubic miles.

Furthermore, since one mile consists of 5,000 geometric feet (which are equivalent to 1,000 paces), according to the rules of squaring and cubing, one square mile will be 25,000,000 feet, and a cubic mile will contain 125,000,000,000 cubic feet. Let us now suppose that the side of one fired brick corresponds to a geometric foot, and that six such square stones constitute a cube. Certainly, the number of said stones will be 125,000,000,000 (that is, the number of cubic feet), which, when multiplied by the total solidity of the Tower, will give the multitude of cubic bricks required to build this immense mass: 374,731,250,000,000,000. Since, however, the whole Tower would be divided into various chambers and dwellings, let us subtract a thousandth part from the total solidity, and this number will remain: 374,354,625,000,000,000. This is the true solidity of the Tower, a number so immense that it can hardly be conceived by the mind; hence, from the calculation of this Tower, innumerable absurdities follow.

### CHAPTER III.

#### ->Concerning the Tower in the city of Babylon built by Ninus and Semiramis nearly one hundred years after the destruction of the Tower of Nimrod.<-

<margin>The form of the building.</margin>

Just as the tides of ambition and the arrogance of the human mind know no limit, so too did Ninus and his wife Semiramis follow the example of Nimrod <note>original: "Nembrod"</note>. To display a specimen of primeval magnificence and to acquire perpetual glory and immortality for their names, they built another tower. This was not, as many falsely believe, upon the remains of the abandoned Tower of Nimrod, but approximately one hundred years after the division of tongues and after the death of Belus. It was built in the city of Babylon—which she likewise built with admirable and prodigious magnificence as a new city after the old one was destroyed, as we shall describe in the following pages. This tower was not of the same vastness as that which Nimrod had constructed, but was founded on a narrower base, rising to a very great height and conspicuous for its grand ornaments, as is evident in the illustration. It was composed of eight towers, the first of which (marked A B) contained a full stadium in circuit—that is, 125 paces or 625 feet. Its diameter was 39½ paces, which is nearly two hundred feet, and the height of the first tower was the same. By means of a helix or spiral <note>original: "cochleam," literally a snail-shell shape</note>, by which the entire mass was entwined with wondrous and rare skill, the ascent was carried up with such a gentle slope that pack animals and men could conveniently reach its summit with no difficulty. Furthermore, seats were arranged here and there with truly elegant workmanship, so that those exhausted by the ascent might find both peaceful rest and a most pleasant prospect over the entire plain of Babylon and the surrounding regions, lying open to the great pleasure of the spectators. The interior recesses of the chambers were designated for archives, or for the grain supply, and other uses and public conveniences. The remaining seven towers were similar to the first in symmetry, but according to the laws of optics, they decreased in bulk in a rated proportion as they rose in height. For if each individual tower had been equal to the first, that entire mass of towers would easily have raised itself to a height of eight stadia, or 1000 paces (one mile). No foundation could have been equal to supporting such an immense mass, nor could such a lofty and slender height have withstood the rushing blasts of the winds, supported by so meager a fulcrum <term>hypomochlio: a technical term for a pivot point or supporting base</term>. Thus, it seemed required by a certain law of proportion that the towers should diminish; yet even in this manner it could hardly stand, since according to the proportions it displayed, it could have been cast down in part by a more vehement blast of wind, as experience teaches. But let us now bring forward the words of *Herodotus*, with which he skillfully describes this tower:

<margin>The incredible greatness of spirit in Semiramis.</margin>
So great was the strength of spirit in Semiramis, and so great the fire of her ambitious heart, that she seemed unable to rest until she could at least equal, if not surpass, the glory of her husband Ninus <note>Ninus was the mythical founder of Nineveh and husband of Semiramis</note> through the memory of incredible deeds. She was first the one responsible for the surrender of the city of *Bactria*, and consequently the entire kingdom of the *Bactrians* (concerning which, see Diodorus, Book 1, Chapter 3). *Ninus*, admiring her spirit—which was beyond all that is expected of the female sex—took her as his wife. Under this Emperor of the early world, Ninus, the Empress Semiramis, putting on a masculine and warlike spirit within a female body, performed deeds that not only surpassed the female condition, but were so arduous and sublime that they were truly "marvelous and beyond belief" <note>original Greek: παράδοξα καὶ ἄπιστα (paradoxa kai apista)</note>. Indeed, if the truthful accounts of writers are to be believed, she seems to have left the fame of all Gentile women and brave heroines many leagues <note>original: "parasangs," a Persian unit of distance</note> behind her. But since we have already dealt in the preceding pages with the eight-tiered Tower <note>The Tower of Babel</note> and the city of Babylon erected by her, it now remains for us to describe her Hanging Gardens with equal detail.

<margin>The Hanging Gardens.</margin>
Thus says Diodorus in Book III, Chapter 4: *There was also a hanging garden near the citadel, made not by Semiramis, but by a certain Syrian King at a later time to please her.* However, whatever Diodorus may write here, almost all ancient writers assert that it was stated incorrectly, since it should be believed that this was a unique work of Semiramis. Since that Syrian King, whatever his name may have been, pursued her with such singular favor, he also gave care to the building of hanging gardens; here, adapting himself to the mind of Semiramis (which he knew to be swollen with a love for unheard-of and unusual things), he seems to have persuaded her to build gardens planted with every kind of flower, plant, and tree above the roofs of her palace for her own delight. Diodorus describes the nature of such a structure in these words: *Each side of this Garden,* he says, *extends for four plethra <note>An ancient unit of area; Kircher uses "jugeribus" in Latin</note>. The approach was like that of a mountain, with buildings constructed one above the other, so that the view from it lay open far and wide. Vaults were placed on the ground to support the weight of the entire Garden; then others were built one upon another, always increasing in size. For the upper ones, which contained the surface of the Garden walls, rose to a height of 50 cubits; their width was 12 feet. A large part of the wall was built at great expense, with a thickness of 22 feet. The floor was composed in this manner: stone beams were placed, 16 feet long and 6 feet wide; over these, for a pavement, were spread reeds bound together with asphalt; above these, baked bricks in a double row set in plaster; thirdly, lead sheets, so that no moisture might flow down to the vaults; above these were the receptacles through which the moisture would drain. Deep soil was heaped upon this pavement, enough for the roots of large trees, creating the Garden. In it, tall trees of every kind provided a pleasant sight; the vaults also provided light to one another in such a way that royal chambers were held within them; furthermore, a secretly constructed aqueduct watered the Garden.* Thus far Diodorus.
