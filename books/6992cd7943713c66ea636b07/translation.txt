<note>Guanyin (the Bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara) is the embodiment of infinite compassion. This woodcut depicts a specific popular manifestation known as the "Fish-basket Guanyin" <note>original: "Lan'er Guanyin"</note>. In this form, she is shown as a simple young woman carrying a basket—a symbol of her willingness to appear in humble forms to rescue sentient beings from the "ocean of suffering."</note>

# **Portrait by Wu Daozi** <term>Wu Daozi Huaxiang</term>

<note>Wu Daozi (active c. 710–760) is one of the most celebrated figures in Chinese art history, often called the "Sage of Painting" <note>original: "Huasheng"</note>. ### Page 4

The Great Being Guanyin <term>Guanyin Dashi</term> <note>The Bodhisattva of Compassion, Avalokiteshvara, who is depicted in various forms across Asia.</note> was never heard to be referred to as a woman in ancient times. Upon examining historical records such as *The Forest of Gems in the Garden of the Dharma* <note>original: "法苑珠林"; a massive Buddhist encyclopedia compiled in 668 CE</note>, *Verified Omens* <note>original: "宣驗"; Xuan Yan ji</note>, and *Signs from the Unseen World* <note>original: "冥祥"; Ming Xiang ji</note>, we find that from the early dynasties down through the Liang <note>502–557 CE</note>, the manifestations of Guanyin were portrayed as either a Bodhisattva, a monk <term>shamen</term>, or a Daoist practitioner. There is not a single instance of the deity appearing as a woman.

The error of the female form began with the people of the Song Dynasty <note>960–1279 CE</note>, and monks of the Yuan Dynasty <note>1271–1368 CE</note> subsequently claimed the deity was the daughter of King Miaozhuang <note>original: "妙莊王"; King Miaozhuang is the father of Princess Miaoshan in the popular Chinese legend of Guanyin's origin</note>. This is quite laughable.

However, Changfu <note>Likely a reference to a commentator or scholar</note> says: While the story of King Miaozhuang is indeed absurd, the claim that the female appearance began only in the Song or Yuan dynasties is also incorrect. For instance, Shiyuan Chu’s *Records of the East Forest on Mount Lu* contains descriptions of "crowns of flowers and a hundred jewels, with an appearance swaying with grace." The monk Jiaoran’s *Praise of Guanyin* includes phrases such as: "Compassion acts as rain, fragrant kindness acts as wind; it sprinkles upon fragrant lapels and drifts over light pendants."

Do these descriptions not clearly refer to a woman's attire and appearance? Furthermore, the portrait by Wu Daozi <note>the "Sage of Painting" from the Tang Dynasty</note> still exists today as a stone carving in Chuzhou. It depicts dangling jewelry and bracelets, bearing no resemblance at all to the typical appearance of a monk or a traditional Bodhisattva. Thus, we know the "womanly" interpretation of Guanyin did not originate in the Song or Yuan dynasties <note>but was already present in the Tang Dynasty artistic tradition</note>.

***

->**The Perfected Warrior**<-
<note>original: "眞武" (Zhenwu). Also known as the "Perfected Martial Emperor," he is one of the most significant deities in the Daoist pantheon, particularly revered during the Ming Dynasty.</note>

The Perfected Warrior was the Crown Prince of the Kingdom of Pure Joy <term>Jingle Guo</term>. Born with divine spiritual qualities, he encountered the Primordial Lady of the Purple Void <note>original: "紫虚元君" (Zixu Yuanjun); a high-ranking Daoist goddess associated with the stars and the transmission of sacred texts</note>, who bestowed the Way <term>Dao</term> upon him. He further encountered a celestial deity who granted him a precious sword. He then entered Wudang Mountain <note>A mountain range in Hubei province, renowned as a sacred site for Daoism and martial arts</note> to practice self-cultivation for forty-two years. Upon the completion of his labors, he ascended to heaven in broad daylight.

By order of the Supreme Emperor <note>referring to the Jade Emperor, the ruler of Heaven</note>, he was sent to guard the North. With disheveled hair and bare feet, he treads upon the true essence of the Water and Fire hexagrams <note>original: "離坎" (Li and Kan). In the *I Ching*, these represent Fire and Water; in Daoist alchemy, balancing these opposites is key to immortality</note>. He established the dark heraldic banner and set up his flag to govern the position of the "Dark Warrior" <term>Xuanwu</term> <note>The "Dark Warrior" is the ancient directional deity of the North, represented by a turtle entwined with a snake</note>. His divine majesty is awe-inspiring and has been manifest through successive generations. His original title was "Xuanwu," but during the Song Dynasty, the name was changed to "Zhenwu" to observe a naming taboo <term>bihui</term> <note>A Chinese custom prohibiting the use of the personal names of emperors or ancestors; the character "Xuan" was part of a Song emperor's ancestral name</note>.

To the north of Wudang Mountain lies the "Needle-Grinding Stream." It is said that while the Perfected Warrior was practicing his cultivation, he spent a long time without yet attaining a union with the Profound Origin. In a moment of urgency, wanting to leave the mountain, he suddenly encountered an old woman holding an iron pestle, grinding it upon a stone. When he asked what she was doing, the woman replied, "I am making a needle." He asked, "Is that not too difficult?" The woman said, "With enough effort, success comes naturally." The Perfected Warrior reached a great enlightenment, immediately returned to his grotto, and refined his practice.

In that region, there are "Nuo Plums" <term>Nuomei</term>. Legend tells that the Perfected Warrior once cut a branch from a plum tree and grafted it onto a Nuo tree <note>A type of evergreen tree</note>, vowing: "If I achieve the Way, let flowers bloom and fruit set." Later, it happened exactly as he had said. To this day, the tree still stands.

# Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms
## Volume on People, Part II

->**The Divine Lord**<-
<note>original: "帝君" (Dijun). Specifically referring to Wenchang Dijun, the God of Culture and Literature. He is a primary deity in the Daoist pantheon responsible for the fates of scholars and students.</note>

The Divine Lord was originally the essence of the Zhang and Yi constellations <note>The 24th and 27th of the Twenty-Eight Mansions in Chinese astronomy; the Zhang constellation is traditionally associated with the "Spreading" of culture or nets</note> in the heavens. During the *Yisi* year of the reign of King Wu of Zhou, he was born into the Zhang family in the region between Wu and Kuai. He served the Zhou dynasty with the utmost loyalty. After his passing, his spirit wandered to the land of Shu <note>modern-day Sichuan province</note>. By the command of the Supreme Sovereign <term>Shangdi</term>, he carried out his divine transformations at the gates of Shu, frequently residing in the Spirit-Response Cave of Jianxin. Because many of his miraculous manifestations occurred there, a temple was first established in that location. As the land belonged to Zitong County, the people subsequently referred to him as "Zitong."

In later times, he manifested various incarnations to perform deeds that benefited the common people. The Supreme Sovereign then appointed him to oversee the office of Wenchang <note>Literally "Literary Flourishing," a star cluster near the Big Dipper</note>, placing him in charge of matters regarding the civil service examinations. During the time of Liu Yue, there was a Master Kuang named Xu practicing self-cultivation at Southern Cliff Mountain <note>original: 南崿山 (Nan'e Shan)</note>. At that time, a young man frequently came to visit him. His speech and arguments were extraordinary and grand, which the Master found remarkable.

The Master asked him, "I have observed your refined demeanor for many days now. I venture to ask of your home region and your name?"

The youth replied, "My surname is Liu and my name is Yue. I reside to the left of this mountain. Below the mountain, there is a stone approximately twenty feet high; if you knock upon it, I shall immediately invite you in."

The Master followed his instructions and went to visit. When he knocked on the stone, it suddenly opened of its own accord, revealing double doors and a vast cavern within. A young maidservant with her hair in tufts welcomed the Master, followed by two attendants in green robes carrying crimson ceremonial banners <note>original: 絳節 (jiangjie); tallies or banners used by immortals as symbols of authority</note> who led the way.

He saw pavilions and terraces of varying heights, shimmering in gold and emerald. There were rare birds, strange beasts, and extraordinary flora. A <term>True Man <note>original: 眞人 (Zhenren); a Daoist term for a perfected being or immortal who has achieved the highest state of spiritual transformation</note></term>, wearing a jade crown and a red sash with a sword hanging from his belt, came out to greet him. 

The Master felt a desire to remain and live there. However, the True Man said, "Your <term>hidden merit <note>original: 陰功 (yingong); the Daoist and Buddhist concept of good deeds performed in secret, which are essential for spiritual advancement or immortality</note></term> is not yet complete. We shall meet again in the future; it will not be too late to follow me then."

The Master was given three cups of Jade Wine to drink and a single sip of the <term>Soup for Extending Life and Protecting Destiny <note>original: 延齢保命湯 (Yanling Baoming Tang)</note></term>, after which he departed. When the Master looked back at the stone he had knocked upon, it appeared exactly as it had before. He tried knocking once more, but there was no response.

As for the **Old Man of Mount Tai** <note>original: 太山老父 (Taishan Laofu)</note>, no one knows his surname or given name. During the eastern imperial inspection tour of Emperor Wu of the Han Dynasty, the Emperor saw an old man hoeing by the side of the road. Marvelling at this, the Emperor questioned him. The old man replied:

"When your servant was eighty-five years old, I was frail, aging, and near death. My hair was white and my teeth had fallen out. Then, a **Daoist master** <note>original: 道士 (daoshi)</note> taught me the practice of **abstaining from grains** <note>original: 絕穀 (juegu); a Daoist dietary practice for longevity</note>, consuming **atractylodes** <note>original: 朮 (zhu); a medicinal root used in traditional herbology</note>, and drinking only water. He also taught me how to construct a **Divine Pillow** <note>original: 神枕 (shenzhen)</note>. 

Inside this pillow are thirty-two medicinal substances: twenty-four of these substances symbolize the **twenty-four solar terms** <note>original: 二十四氣 (ershisi qi); the divisions of the traditional Chinese agricultural calendar</note>, and the other eight substances correspond to the **eight winds** <note>original: 八風 (bafeng); directional winds associated with cosmic harmony</note>. Your servant followed these instructions and practiced **energy guidance** <note>original: 導行 (daoxing); physical and breathing exercises similar to Qigong</note>. Consequently, I was transformed from old to young; my white hair turned black again, and new teeth grew where others had fallen. Now I can travel three hundred *li* in a single day. Your servant is currently one hundred and eighty years old."

Emperor Wu cherished this formula and bestowed gold and silk upon the man. Later, the old man entered deep into **Mount Tai** <note>original: 太山 (Taishan)</note>. Every five or ten years, he would return to his home village, but after three hundred years had passed, he never returned again.

## Master White Stone <note>original: 白石生 (Baishi Sheng)</note>

**Master White Stone** was a disciple of **Pengzu** <note>a legendary figure in Chinese mythology renowned for his extreme longevity, said to have lived over 800 years</note>. Even after reaching the age of two thousand years, he still possessed the youthful appearance of a man in his thirties. 

He derived his name from his residence on **Mount Baishi** <note>literally "White Stone Mountain"</note>, where he lived in a cave. His primary method of cultivation involved **boiling white stones** <note>original: 煮石 (zhushi); a miraculous alchemical practice where stones are softened into a form of edible paste or "gruel" for sustenance</note> to serve as his daily food. 

Unlike many other **immortals** <note>original: 仙人 (xianren); a person who has attained transcendence and eternal life through Daoist practice</note> who aspired to ascend to the heavens and serve in the celestial bureaucracy, Master White Stone had no desire for such honors. He famously claimed that the joys of the human world were quite sufficient, and he saw no reason to exchange the freedom of the mountains for the duties of a heavenly official. 

In depictions, he is often shown in a state of quiet contemplation near his mountain home, surrounded by the very stones that granted him his name and his long life.

## Master White Stone <note>original: 白石生 (Baishi Sheng)</note>

**Master White Stone** was a disciple of the **Elder of the Middle Yellow** <note>original: 中黃丈人 (Zhonghuang Zhangren); a high-ranking deity in the Daoist pantheon associated with the center/earth element</note>. By the time of **Pengzu** <note>the Chinese "Methuselah," said to have lived over 800 years</note>, Master White Stone was already more than two thousand years old. 

He did not desire **celestial ascension** <note>original: 飛昇 (feisheng); the process of rising to the heavens to join the divine bureaucracy</note>; instead, he valued **longevity** <note>original: 長生 (changsheng)</note> for its own sake. He regarded **Golden Liquid** <note>original: 金液 (jinye); a precious alchemical elixir made from refined minerals</note> as the supreme medicine, but because his family was poor, he could not afford to obtain it. To remedy this, he raised pigs and herded sheep for over ten years until he had amassed a fortune of ten thousand pieces of gold. Only then was he able to purchase the ingredients for the medicine and consume it.

He used to boil **white stones** to serve as his daily food. Because he resided on **White Stone Mountain**, he was eventually given the name Master White Stone. At times he would eat dried meat, and at other times he practiced **grain avoidance** <note>original: 辟穀 (bigu); a Daoist dietary discipline of abstaining from the "five grains" to achieve physical and spiritual purity</note>. 

He was capable of traveling three or four hundred *li* <note>approximately 100 to 130 miles</note> in a single day. His complexion and appearance remained like that of a man in his thirties. When someone once asked him why he did not care to ascend to the heavens, he replied:
