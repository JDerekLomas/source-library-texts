## The Eastern African Continent

The Eastern African Continent borders the Indian Ocean <note>original: 因里阿海 (Yinliao hai), a phonetic rendering of "India Sea."</note>. From the coastline in the east to the western interior, its width varies, approximately five to six hundred *li* <term>li: a traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters or 1/3 of a mile</term>. From Samana <note>original: 舍摩那司 (Shemonasi), likely referring to Sofala in modern Mozambique</note> in the south to the coast of Agas <note>original: 阿嘎 (A-ga), likely referring to Cape Guardafui</note> in the north, the coastline stretches for approximately three thousand *li*. The land is broad in the north and narrow in the south. In the interior lie the great Lupata Mountains <note>original: 路巴達 (Lubada). These are the Lupata Mountains in modern Mozambique.</note>; their peaks and valleys rise and fall, stretching along the coast. There are two major rivers: the Rufigi River <note>original: 路菲尼河 (Lufenhe)</note>, which flows into the sea from Mozambique <note>original: 摩新彌葵 (Moxinmikui)</note>, and the Zambezi River <note>original: 偃彌西河 (Yanmixihe)</note>.

# Lesser Western Ocean: East Africa, Part 2

### Inhambane
Inhambane <note>original: 英漢門 (Yinghanmen)</note> is bounded by the sea to the east, Sofala to the north, and the territory of Shemonasi <note>original: 舍摩那司 (Shemonasi); likely referring to the regional tribes or the Limpopo area</note> to the southwest. The climate and soil are poor and unhealthy. The indigenous population <note>original: 土番 (tuban)</note> numbers three thousand; they are known for being warlike. There is a Portuguese <note>original: 葡萄亞 (Putuoya)</note> fortress garrisoned by one hundred and fifty soldiers. The region produces dyes, ivory, and yellow wax <note>beeswax</note>, with an annual trade value of no less than 100,000 pounds <note>original: 棒 (bang); a 19th-century phonetic rendering of the British "pound"</note>. Furthermore, ships transport "mouths" <note>a historical term for enslaved persons</note> to be sold in Mozambique <note>original: 莫新彌葵 (Moxinmikui)</note>. There are more than ten such vessels, each carrying four to five hundred people.

### Sofala
Sofala <note>original: 蘇華臘 (Suhuala)</note> is bounded by the sea to the east, Manica <note>original: 麻尼加 (Manijia)</note> to the west, Inhambane to the south, and Mocaranga <note>original: 摩嘉蘭 (Mojialan)</note> to the north. The coastal wilderness borders the sea, but there are few beaches or shoals. Due to the daily rising tides, it is impossible to reach the shore without a small boat. The indigenous people are of the Kaffraria variety <note>original: 加付臘黎阿 (Jiafulalia); a historical term for the Bantu-speaking peoples of Southern Africa</note>; they often carry sharp blades and do not accept colonial restraint. When the Portuguese first arrived, this was a major market town; they constructed a fortress to defend against the indigenous people. The local products—gold and ivory—were all originally transported via the Zambezi River <note>original: 染彌西河 (Ranmixi He)</note> for trade. Now, however, the transport route has been shifted to Quelimane <note>original: 歸爾厘馬 (Guierlima)</note>, which is under the jurisdiction of Mozambique.

### Manica
Manica <note>original: 嘛尼加 (Manijia)</note> is bounded by Sofala to the east, by wilderness to the west, by the territory of Shemonasi <note>likely the Sabia River region</note> to the south, and by the wilderness of Mocaranga to the north. The gold is found mixed within mountain rocks; although they exert the greatest effort to extract it, the yield is not abundant. The forts <note>original: 礮臺 (paotai); literally "cannon platforms"</note> are not particularly strong; while they are sufficient to ward off pirates, they would find it difficult to resist a major enemy force.
