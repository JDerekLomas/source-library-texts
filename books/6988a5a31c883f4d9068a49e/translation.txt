### To the Very Reverend and Most Distinguished Lord Doctor,

A constant awareness of the promise by which I recently bound myself compelled me to satisfy your most delightful letter by responding as quickly as possible. I acknowledge my fault in this matter, and I am not entirely certain whether I can use this meager excuse: that because of the daily occupations of my studies, it has not been possible until now to fulfill this duty toward You. Yet in excusing myself, I now accuse myself more heavily; for I shall never regret spending even a great part of my study time in writing letters to You. It is all the more proper for me to do this so that I do not seem to want to frustrate Your expectation, nor to be seen wishing to be freed from the requirement of rendering to You an account of my progress and leisure, as commanded by my *Most Illustrious Father* <note>original: "Illustriss. Genitoris"; the Prince is referring to Duke August the Younger of Braunschweig-Wolfenbüttel</note>.

For why should I fear to write to You—a beginner <term>tyro: a novice or recruit</term> to such a practiced Master—lest perhaps you think that I, constrained until now by fear and shame, have decided that delays must be woven? While I respond diligently and seriously to your frequent letters, I might seem to some to be seeking the praises with which your kindness has so far overwhelmed me, and to think myself worthy of them. Although I ought not to suspect this of you; for you know that just as flattery is far from you, so ambition is far from me. You surely intend this: *you praise in order to teach*, and while you proclaim great things about me, you openly advise me on what I must do now, and even more in the future. With this profession of my modesty, I temper the excess of your letters—an excess not of love, but of honor. Nor will there be need hereafter for time spent either in excusing myself or in returning like for like.

As for what you add here from the Gospel, *He must increase, but I must decrease* <note>original: "Me crescere, Te minui oportere"; a reference to John 3:30. Andreae had humbly compared himself to John the Baptist and the Prince to Christ, suggesting his own time was passing as the Prince’s began.</note>, I am bound to reply from another perspective: that you are great among those born of women <note>original: "inter mulierum filios"; an allusion to Matthew 11:11, where Jesus praises John the Baptist</note>, indeed the *Greatest* of the scholars of our age, and it is of the highest importance to the Church and the Republic of Letters <term>Republic of Letters: The "Respublica literaria," the international community of intellectuals and scholars who shared ideas during the 17th century</term> that you feel perhaps some old age of the *body*, but none of the *mind*. 

For your merits are certain and present, while those things you proclaim about me are uncertain, although I trust that your prayers will obtain this from God: that I may be able to satisfy, to some extent, the expectations of my Lord Father, of yourself, and finally of the public. Fare well with your family, and continue your work.

Wolfenbüttel,
August 13, 
Year 1644.

->Yours,<-

->RUDOLF AUGUST<-

### LETTER XLII.

->**Most Illustrious and Most High Prince,**<-
->**Most Clement Lord,**<-

I am greatly indebted to Your Highness for interpreting my affection for You so kindly. For you may easily understand how far I am from flattery by the tribute I wrote for your Father <note>Johann Valentin Andreae had written a biography/eulogy for Duke August of Brunswick-Wolfenbüttel, Rudolf August's father</note>, whom I praised far below his actual merits. I did this so I would not seem to be praising him to his face, acting more as a Historian than an Orator, presenting a bare image of Virtue. I wish for you to feel the same regarding my services toward You: specifically, that I am not so much congratulating You on the immense benefit of your proper education and upbringing (than which nothing greater could happen at your age), as I am approving of your obedience and docility. Through these qualities, you bring delight to your Father (may he live as long as possible!) and raise the hopes of all your people for the most praiseworthy progress and growth of your House. Truly, I praise in you this "pliable wax" <note>original: "ductilem ceram"; a common 17th-century metaphor suggesting a young person's mind is soft and ready to be molded by virtuous teaching</note>, upon which your Father’s character is happily impressed, and I follow you with my pious prayers. The greatest of these prayers is for the **LONG LIFE OF YOUR FATHER**, which may perfect, solidify, and strengthen all our expectations regarding You—the one thing upon which your own safety and the public welfare depend.

For you are not unaware of the saying of a certain Wise man: that those who do not act badly are happy, and those who act well are happier, but those for whom it is a necessity that they *cannot* act badly are the happiest of all. It is as if the condition of Youth, when joined with great fortune, can scarcely keep a straight course without the "bit and spurs" <note>original: "frænum & calcaria"; a classical metaphor for the discipline and encouragement needed to keep a soul on the path of virtue</note>, and the very necessity of acting well might serve as a total rule of life. While such a rule may seem a bit harsh to others, I understand that for you it is very easy: namely, that you compose your whole self according to the sight and nod of your illustrious Father and the warnings of your Teachers. For this reason, I congratulate You from my heart and prophesy all the best for you.

### LETTER XLII.

->Most Illustrious and Serene Prince,<-
->Most Gracious Lord,<-

*I owe much to Your Highness for interpreting my affection toward you so kindly. You can easily understand how far I am from flattery by the eulogy I wrote for your Lord Father* <note>Duke August the Younger (1579–1666), Rudolf August’s father and a major patron of Andreae.</note>, *whom I praised far below his actual merits, so that I might not seem to be speaking merely to please him, but rather playing the part of a historian rather than an orator by presenting the plain image of his Virtue. I wish for you to feel the same regarding my services to you: that I am not so much congratulating you on the immense benefit of your proper education and training (of which no greater thing could happen to you at this age), but rather testing your obedience and teachability, by which you bring delight to your Father (may he live as long as possible!) and raise all your people into the hope of the most praiseworthy progress and the growth of your House. Indeed, I praise in you this ductile wax* <note>A common 17th-century metaphor for a young person's mind, suggesting it is soft and ready to be molded by education.</note>, *upon which your Father’s character is successfully impressed, and I follow you with my pious prayers. The greatest of these is for the LONG LIFE OF YOUR FATHER* <note>original: "LONGÆVITATE PATRIS"</note>, *which may complete, solidify, and strengthen all our expectations of you; upon this one thing your well-being and that of the public depends. For you are not unaware of the saying of a certain wise man: that those who do not act badly are happy, those who act well are happier, but those for whom it is a necessity that they cannot act badly are the happiest of all—as if the condition of youth in great fortune could hardly keep a straight course without bit and spur, and the necessity of acting well itself could serve as every rule. While this might seem somewhat harsh to others, I understand it is very easy for you; namely, that you compose yourself entirely according to the sight and will of your renowned Father and the advice of your teachers. On this account, I congratulate you from my heart and augur all the best.*
