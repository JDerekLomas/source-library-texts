# Diagram of the Rival States of the Later Five Dynasties
<note>The "Five Dynasties" refers to the five short-lived regimes that controlled the heartland of Northern China in rapid succession after the fall of the Tang dynasty. The "Rival States" are the various kingdoms that ruled the south and west during this same period of fragmentation.</note>

**King of Qi**
<gloss>Li Maozhen occupied Fengxiang <note>in modern Shaanxi</note>. Emperor Zhuangzong of the Later Tang changed his title to King of Qin. By the reign of Emperor Mingzong, the state was abolished.</gloss>

**King of Wu**
<gloss>During the reign of Emperor Zhaozong of Tang, Yang Xingmi served as the Military Governor of Huainan. His son, Yang Wo, succeeded him. At the beginning of the Liang <note>Later Liang</note> dynasty, Zhang Hao murdered him and installed Yang Longyan. Later, Xu Zhigao usurped the throne and changed the state's name to Southern Tang.</gloss>

**Northern Han**
<gloss>Founded by Liu Chong, the younger brother of Liu Zhiyuan <note>founder of the Later Han</note>. It was eventually destroyed by the Song dynasty.</gloss>

<column-break/>

**King of Chu**
<gloss>Ma Yin occupied Tanzhou <note>modern Changsha</note>. The state was later destroyed by the Southern Tang.</gloss>

**King of Yan**
<gloss>Liu Shouguang occupied Youzhou <note>modern Beijing</note> and declared the Great Yan dynasty. He was killed by the Later Jin.</gloss>

**Wuyue**
<gloss>Qian Liu occupied Hangzhou. Eventually, the state surrendered its territory and returned to the sovereignty of the Song dynasty.</gloss>

<column-break/>

->**Liang —— Tang —— Jin —— Han —— Zhou**<-
<note>These are the central Five Dynasties of the North: Later Liang, Later Tang, Later Jin, Later Han, and Later Zhou. They are presented here as the central axis of political legitimacy around which the rival kingdoms are organized.</note>

<column-break/>

**King of Min**
<gloss>Wang Shenzhi occupied Fuzhou. Later, Wang Yanzheng resided in Jianzhou and titled the state Yin. During the Jin period, it was destroyed by the Southern Tang.</gloss>

**Southern Tang**
<gloss>Xu Zhigao usurped the state of Wu and established the Kingdom of Qi at Jinling <note>modern Nanjing</note>. Later he declared himself Emperor, renamed the state Tang, reclaimed his original surname of Li, and changed his personal name to Li Sheng.</gloss>

**Later Shu**
<gloss>Founded by Meng Zhixiang. It was later destroyed by the Song dynasty.</gloss>

<column-break/>

**King of Shu**
<gloss>Wang Jian was enfeoffed as the King of Shu by Emperor Zhaozong of Tang. Later, Emperor Zhuangzong <note>of Later Tang</note> destroyed this state. Meng Zhixiang subsequently re-occupied the territory <note>founding the Later Shu mentioned above</note> until the final ruler surrendered.</gloss>

**Southern Han**
<gloss>During the time of Emperor Zhuangzong of Later Tang, Liu Gong <note>also known as Liu Yan</note> was first enfeoffed as the King of Nanping, then titled himself the King of Nanyue, and finally changed the state name to Han while occupying Guangzhou.</gloss>

**Nanping**
<gloss>Ruled by the Gao family. The founder was originally named Gao Jichang, but he changed his name to Gao Jixing to avoid a Tang dynasty naming taboo <note>the practice of changing names to avoid using the characters found in an emperor's name</note>. The state was abolished during the Song dynasty.</gloss>

# Portrait of Emperor Taizu of the Later Liang
<note>Emperor Taizu (reigned 907–912 CE), born Zhu Wen, was the founder of the Later Liang dynasty. His rise to power marked the definitive end of the Tang Dynasty and the beginning of the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period, a time of significant political fragmentation in China.</note>

**Iconography and Style**
The illustration is a traditional woodcut portrait, a style commonly used in Ming dynasty encyclopedias to provide readers with a visual reference for historical figures. The Emperor is depicted in formal attire, characterized by:

*   **The Headgear:** He wears a traditional scholar-official's cap <term>putou; a black cloth hat originally worn by commoners that evolved into formal court headwear with stiffened "wings"</term>.
*   **The Robes:** His clothing consists of wide-sleeved imperial robes, signifying his status as the Son of Heaven, despite his origins as a rebel soldier.
*   **The Depiction:** Following the conventions of ancestral portraiture, the features are stylized to convey dignity and authority rather than providing a strict photographic likeness.

<note>Historical records often describe Zhu Wen as a brutal and pragmatic leader. However, in this collection, he is presented with the standard iconography of a legitimate sovereign, reflecting the book's purpose as a comprehensive record of recognized dynastic transitions.</note>

# Portrait of Emperor Zhuangzong of the Later Tang
<note>Emperor Zhuangzong (reigned 923–926 CE), born Li Cunxu, was the founder of the Later Tang dynasty. Of Shatuo Turkic descent, his family was granted the imperial surname "Li" by the Tang court for their military service. He was a brilliant commander who overthrew the Later Liang, though his reign was short-lived and marked by a deep obsession with Chinese opera.</note>

<note>original: "後唐莊宗" (Hou Tang Zhuangzong); "三才圖會" (Sancai Tuhui); "人物" (Renwu/Human Affairs)</note>

**Visual Representation**
The woodblock illustration depicts the Emperor in a pose of formal authority, consistent with the iconographic standards for the "Human Affairs" section of this encyclopedia.

*   **The Headgear:** The Emperor wears a traditional formal cap <term>putou; a black cloth hat that evolved from a simple head-wrap into a stiffened, winged symbol of official status</term>. 
*   **The Imperial Robe:** He is shown in flowing, wide-sleeved robes. The simplicity of the line work emphasizes the dignity of his position as the restorer—albeit briefly—of the Tang dynastic name.
*   **Physical Features:** He is rendered with a full beard and mustache, which were traditional markers of maturity and masculine authority in imperial portraiture.

**Historical Context**
This portrait serves as a visual record of a pivotal figure who attempted to reclaim the legacy of the Great Tang Dynasty. Despite his military genius, historical records often critique him for allowing actors and eunuchs to influence his court. This image, however, omits such personal failings, presenting him instead as a legitimate "Son of Heaven" in the lineage of Chinese rulers.

<note>The Sancai Tuhui was a massive Ming Dynasty encyclopedia intended to compile all known knowledge. Figures like Emperor Zhuangzong were included to provide a complete visual history of China’s political transitions.</note>

## Portrait of Emperor Gaozu of the Later Jin
<note>Emperor Gaozu (reigned 936–942 CE), born Shi Jingtang, was the founder of the Later Jin dynasty. Like the rulers of the preceding Later Tang, he was of Shatuo Turkic descent. He is a deeply controversial figure in Chinese historiography because he ceded the strategically vital Sixteen Prefectures to the Khitan-led Liao dynasty in exchange for military support, and famously referred to himself as the "son-emperor" in diplomatic correspondence with the Khitan ruler, who was younger than him.</note>

<note>original: "後晉高祖像" (Hou Jin Gaozu Xiang); "石敬瑭" (Shi Jingtang)</note>

**Visual Representation**
The woodblock print follows the established iconographic style of the *Sancai Tuhui*, focusing on the status and formal identity of the sovereign.

*   **The Headgear:** The Emperor is shown wearing a formal <term>putou; a black cloth cap with stiffened wings or "feet" that became the standard headwear for the scholar-official class and royalty from the Tang through the Ming dynasties</term>.
*   **The Imperial Robe:** He is dressed in a round-collared ceremonial robe. The clean, heavy lines of the woodcut emphasize the bulk and presence of the figure, denoting his imperial station.
*   **Physical Features:** In keeping with the conventions of the time, he is depicted with a full beard and mustache, symbols of patriarchal authority and wisdom.

**Historical Context**
This portrait documents the transition from the Later Tang to the Later Jin. While Shi Jingtang's decision to cede northern territories left a lasting scar on Chinese defense—leaving the heartland vulnerable to northern invasions for the next four hundred years—his inclusion in this volume demonstrates the Ming compilers' desire to present a complete, unbroken record of those who held the "Mandate of Heaven" during the chaotic Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period.

# Portrait of Emperor Gaozu of the Later Han

<note>original: "後漢高祖" (Hou Han Gaozu). This refers to Liu Zhiyuan (reigned 947–948 CE), the founder of the Later Han dynasty. Like his predecessors in the Later Tang and Later Jin, he was of Shatuo Turkic descent. His reign was extremely brief, lasting only about a year before his death.</note>

<note>original: "三才圖會" (Sancai Tuhui). This is the title of the source material, a massive encyclopedia compiled by Wang Qi and his son Wang Siyi during the Ming Dynasty, intended to catalog all knowledge across the "Three Realms" of Heaven, Earth, and Humanity.</note>

**Visual Description**
The portrait depicts the Emperor in a formal, frontal pose characteristic of imperial woodcut hagiography. 

*   **The Headgear:** He wears a <term>putou; a traditional black gauze cap with stiffened, wing-like extensions</term>. By the time of the Five Dynasties and into the Ming when this book was published, this headwear was the definitive symbol of officialdom and imperial authority.
*   **The Robe:** He is shown in a dark, round-collared robe. The simplicity of the garment in the illustration emphasizes the dignity of the ruler's carriage rather than intricate textile patterns.
*   **Facial Features:** He is rendered with a prominent mustache and a long, flowing goatee, following the aesthetic standards for a "venerable leader" in Chinese portraiture.

**Historical Significance**
Liu Zhiyuan was a high-ranking military official under the Later Jin. When the Khitan Liao Empire invaded and destroyed the Later Jin in 947 CE, the Khitan Emperor initially declared himself the ruler of China. However, due to local resistance and supply issues, the Khitans withdrew north. Liu Zhiyuan seized this power vacuum in <term>Taiyuan; a strategic city in modern-day Shanxi province</term> to proclaim the founding of the Later Han.

<margin>Founder of the Later Han</margin>
<gloss>Liu Zhiyuan, posthumously known as Emperor Gaozu</gloss>

While his dynasty was the shortest of the Five Dynasties—lasting only four years total before being overthrown by the Later Zhou—Liu Zhiyuan is remembered for providing a brief moment of stability and Chinese-style imperial continuity in the wake of the Khitan withdrawal.

## Portrait of Emperor Gaozu of the Later Zhou

<note>original: "後周高祖" (Hou Zhou Gaozu). This refers to Guo Wei (reigned 951–954 CE), the founding emperor of the Later Zhou dynasty. Unlike the previous three dynasties of the "Five Dynasties" period, which were ruled by Shatuo Turkic elites, Guo Wei was Han Chinese. His reign is celebrated by historians for its focus on civil governance and administrative reform.</note>

<note>original: "郭威" (Guo Wei). The personal name of the Emperor. He rose from the ranks of the military to become a powerful general under the Later Han before founding his own dynasty.</note>

**Visual Description**
This woodcut portrait follows the established iconographic tradition for founding emperors within the *Sancai Tuhui* <note>The "Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms," a Ming Dynasty encyclopedia</note>.

*   **The Headgear:** The Emperor is shown wearing a <term>putou; a traditional headpiece made of black gauze</term>. It features the distinctive stiffened, wing-like flaps extending horizontally, which served as a symbol of high-ranking status and imperial dignity.
*   **Facial Features:** He is depicted with a prominent, full beard and a steady, authoritative gaze. In traditional Chinese portraiture, these features were often stylized to suggest a "dragon-like" countenance, signifying a mandate to rule.
*   **The Robe:** He wears a dark, round-collared robe. The clean, bold lines of the woodblock print emphasize his physical presence and the stability he brought to the central plains of China after decades of upheaval.

# The Last Emperor
<gloss>His name was Yanxi, and he was the grandson of Emperor Daozong. He gave himself the title **Emperor Tianzuo** <note>original: 天祚; "Heaven-Blessed"</note>. Prince Chun declared himself the **Tianxi Emperor** <note>original: 天錫; "Heaven-Bestowed"</note> and changed the era name to *Jianfu*. He reigned for only three years before passing away. The officials Xiao Gan and **Yelü Linya** <note>Also known as Yelü Dashi, the future founder of the Western Liao or Qara Khitai</note> then established Chun's wife, Lady Xiao, as the Empress Dowager and changed the era name to *Dexing*. When the Jin soldiers broke through the defenses of Yan, Yelü Linya and Lady Xiao fled to Jiashan. The legacy of the Xiao lineage ended, and the *Dexing* era was abolished. As the Last Emperor fled toward the Western Xia, he was captured by the Jin people before he could arrive. He was granted the humiliating title of "Marquis of the Seashore." At that time, the Empress Dowager Wei <note>of the Jin</note> ordered ten thousand horses to trample his corpse. He reigned for twenty-four years and lived to the age of fifty. During the *Dinghai* year <note>1107 CE</note>, he changed the era name three times: *Qiantong* <note>Heavenly Governance</note>, *Tianqing* <note>Heavenly Celebration</note>, and *Baoda* <note>Preserving Greatness</note>.</gloss>

# Genealogical Chart of the Jin Dynasty
<note>The Jin Dynasty (1115–1234) was established by the Jurchen people of Northeast Asia. The name "Jin" means "Gold," taken from the Ashi River in their homeland. This chart traces the succession of their emperors from the founding conqueror to the final ruler who oversaw the dynasty's fall to the Mongols.</note>

**Taizu, Aguda**
<note>reigned 1115–1123. The founding emperor and unifier of the Jurchen tribes. He defied the Liao Dynasty and laid the foundations for a continental empire.</note>

**Taizong, Wuqimai**
<note>reigned 1123–1135. The younger brother of Aguda. His armies captured the Northern Song capital of Kaifeng and expanded Jin territory deep into central China.</note>

**Xizong, Danlama** — **The Prince of Hailing, Liang**
<note>Xizong (reigned 1135–1149) was the grandson of the founder. He was eventually assassinated by his cousin, the Prince of Hailing (reigned 1149–1161). The Prince of Hailing is famously known for moving the capital to Yanjing (modern-day Beijing) but was eventually murdered during a failed campaign against the Southern Song.</note>

**Shizong, Yong** — **Crown Prince Yungong** — **Zhangzong, Jing**
<note>Shizong (reigned 1161–1189) is often called the "Lesser Yao and Shun" for his peaceful and prosperous reign. His son, Crown Prince Yungong, predeceased him, leading to the succession of his grandson, Zhangzong (reigned 1189–1208), whose reign marked the cultural peak of the dynasty.</note>

<gloss>Yunji</gloss>
<note>original: 允濟. This refers to Wanyan Yunji, also known as Prince Shao of Wei. He took the throne after Zhangzong's death but was deposed and killed after failing to defend against Genghis Khan's initial invasions.</note>

**Marquis of Donghai County** — **Xuanzong, Xun** — **Emperor Ai, Shouxu**
<note>The "Marquis of Donghai County" was the title held by Xuanzong (reigned 1213–1224) before he was placed on the throne. He moved the capital to Kaifeng to escape the Mongols. The lineage ends with Emperor Ai (reigned 1224–1234); his posthumous name *Ai* means "Lamentable," reflecting his tragic role as the sovereign during the final destruction of the Jin state by the Mongol-Song alliance.</note>

The Jin Dynasty was established by the Jurchen nation of the Wanyan clan. They originally resided in Shangshuo <note>Located in modern-day Harbin, Heilongjiang</note>, moved their capital to Yan <note>Modern Beijing</note>, and later moved to Bian <note>Modern Kaifeng</note>. The dynasty began when the Founding Emperor, Taizu (Wanyan Aguda), declared himself emperor in the *Wuxu* year <note>1118 AD</note>. It ended with Emperor Aizong in the *Jiawu* year <note>1234 AD</note>. In total, there were nine lords reigning over a period of one hundred and seventeen years. The Song and Yuan <note>the Mongol Empire</note> joined forces to destroy them.

# Taizu
<gloss>Aguda, who later changed his name to Min. He destroyed the Liao Dynasty and declared himself Emperor. He reigned for nine years and lived to the age of fifty-one. He changed his era name twice: Shouguo (2 years) and Tianfu (6 years). The sixth year of Tianfu was also the first year of Taizong's Tianhui era.</gloss>

# Taizong
<gloss>Named Wuqimai, later changed to Sheng. He was the younger brother of Taizu, the eighth in line to the throne. He captured and held in captivity the two emperors of the Song <note>Emperors Huizong and Qinzong, following the fall of Kaifeng in 1127</note>, subjecting them to humiliation. He reigned for twelve years and lived to the age of sixty. He used one era name: Tianhui (12 years).</gloss>

# Xizong
<gloss>Named Helia, later changed to Dan. He was the eldest grandson of Taizu. He was murdered by Wanyan Liang. He reigned for fourteen years and lived to the age of thirty-one. He changed his era name three times: Tianhui (3 years), Tianjuan (3 years), and Huangtong (8 years).</gloss>

# The Prince of Hailing
<gloss>Named Liang; his posthumous name was Yang. He was a grandson of Taizu. He murdered Xizong and usurped the throne to establish himself. He moved the capital to Yanjing and then again to Bianliang. During a southern campaign against the Song, he reached Yangzhou, where he was killed by his own officers and soldiers. He reigned for thirteen years and lived to the age of forty. He changed his era name three times: Tiande (4 years), Zhenyuan (3 years), and Zhenglong (5 years).</gloss>

# Shizong
<gloss>Named Yong. He was a grandson of Taizong <note>Historical records usually identify Shizong as the grandson of Taizu; the text here attributes his lineage to Taizong</note>. During his reign, the North and South were respectfully at peace, and the borders remained tranquil. He reigned for twenty-nine years and lived to the age of sixty-seven. He used one era name: Dading (29 years).</gloss>

# Zhangzong
<gloss>Named Jing. He was the grandson of Shizong. He reigned for nineteen years and lived to the age of forty-one. He changed his era name three times: Mingchang (5 years), Cheng’an (6 years), and Taihe (8 years).</gloss>

# The Yuan Dynasty

According to the *Secret History of the Yuan Dynasty* <note>original: *Yuanzhao Mishi*, the oldest surviving literary work in the Mongolian language</note>, the ancestors of the Yuan were a blue-grey wolf born of Heaven and a pale-white doe who mated. Together they crossed the Tenggis <note>likely Lake Baikal</note> and came to the headwaters of the Onon River at Mount Burkhan Khaldun. There they gave birth to a son named Batachikhan. Batachikhan fathered Tamacha, and by the twelfth generation, Bodonchar was born. In the thirteenth generation, Temujin was born; he took **Borjigin** as his clan name and became the **Grand Ancestor** <note>Taizu</note> of the Yuan Dynasty.

Initially, the Grand Ancestor Temujin took the throne at the Onon River in the year of *Bingyin* <note>1206</note>. He resided in the desert regions as the lord of the northern tribes. For four generations spanning fifty-seven years, the Mongols ruled from the steppes. Then, in the year of *Guihai* <note>1263</note>, the **Illustrious Ancestor** <note>Shizu, also known as Kublai Khan</note> moved the capital to Yanjing <note>modern-day Beijing</note>. Thirteen years later, in the year of *Bingzi* <note>1276</note>, he unified the realms. The dynasty ended with **Emperor Shun** <note>Toghon Temür</note> in the year of *Wushen* <note>1368</note>. Ten emperors ruled from within China for a period of ninety-three years. Counting from the beginning, there were fourteen emperors in total, lasting one hundred and sixty-three years until Emperor Shun returned to the desert and the state fell.
