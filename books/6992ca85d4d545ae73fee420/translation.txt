# Preface to the Corrected Yellow Emperor’s Inner Canon: Grand Basis

Since the <term>Ancestor Dragon<note>original: 祖龍 (*Zulong*). A historical epithet for Qin Shi Huang (r. 221–210 BCE), the first emperor of a unified China, known for the "burning of books and burying of scholars."</note></term> burned the books, the ancient records of the <term>Three Sovereigns<note>original: 三皇 (*Sanhuang*). Mythical sage-kings—typically Fuxi, Shennong, and the Yellow Emperor—credited with founding Chinese civilization and medicine.</note></term> were all committed to the flames. The *Book of Changes* of Fuxi survived by chance because it was used for divination, while the *Yellow Emperor’s Inner Canon* was spared because it concerned medicine and pharmacology. 

Therefore, the "Treatise on Arts and Literature" in the *History of Han* listed the "Medical Canons" as the very first category of records. These are the same <term>Basic Questions<note>original: 素問 (*Suwen*). This half of the *Inner Canon* focuses on the philosophical and theoretical foundations of medicine, such as Yin-Yang and the Five Elements.</note></term> and <term>Spiritual Pivot<note>original: 靈樞 (*Lingshu*). This half focuses more on the physical body, particularly the meridian system and the practice of acupuncture.</note></term> that have been passed down to the present day. 

The *Basic Questions* discusses **principles and vital energy**, while the *Spiritual Pivot* discusses **physical form and substance**. These two books were originally intended to complement one another and are truly the ancestors of all medical science. 

Commentators often claim that Chinese medicine focuses exclusively on **principles and vital energy**. For instance, the theory that "extreme cold transforms into heat, and extreme heat transforms into cold" is a most profound principle. Consequently, when treating internal conditions such as <term>Cold Damage<note>original: 傷寒 (*Shanghan*). A broad category of febrile diseases caused by external pathogens.</note></term> or <term>Warm Heat<note>original: 溫熱 (*Wenre*). A category of infectious or seasonal diseases.</note></term> illnesses, the medicine is often so effective that the disease is removed as soon as the remedy is administered. The source of this success actually stems from the *Basic Questions*. 

Conversely, Western medicine focuses exclusively on **physical form and substance**. For example, they use tubes for listening to the lungs <note>a reference to the stethoscope</note>, instruments for measuring the throat, and mirrors for examining the eyes <note>likely an ophthalmoscope</note>, as well as techniques like anatomy and "needle-work" <note>likely referring to surgery or hypodermic injections</note>. These arts all seek ever-greater precision. Thus, in treating external conditions such as falls, injuries, or various ulcers and abscesses, they often achieve immediate results. ### Introductory Remarks on the *Inner Classic Grand Basis*

In the first year of the **Yongxi** era of the Song Dynasty (984 CE), the *Record of Seeking Books in Japan* <note>original: "Yang-shi Riben Fangshu Zhi." This refers to bibliographic records noting the survival of ancient Chinese texts in Japanese libraries.</note> stated that Japan preserved many ancient books that were no longer accessible to medical practitioners in China. This was certainly no empty claim. From the Jin and Yuan dynasties (12th–14th centuries) onward, medical books only occasionally cited this work to verify the meaning of specific characters, but it was rarely utilized in full. This was not because the physicians of those later eras had nothing to offer, but because this book—the ***Grand Basis***—had already been lost in China during that period, leaving them with no means to study or verify it.

The commentary on the ***Basic Questions*** <term>Suwen: the primary theoretical text of the Yellow Emperor's Inner Classic</term> by **Quan Yuanqi** <note>a 6th-century physician whose early commentary is a vital historical reference</note> has been lost for a long time. When **Lin Yi** and his colleagues produced the *New Corrections* edition, they frequently cited Quan’s work to rectify errors in **Wang Bing’s** <note>an 8th-century official whose edited version of the Neijing became the standard, though he was often criticized for reordering the text</note> commentary of the *Basic Questions*. 

The passages they cited from Quan’s version often match the text found in the ***Grand Basis*** <term>Taisuo: a reorganized version of the Inner Classic by Yang Shangshan</term>. This proves that the text compiled in the *Grand Basis* is an ancient version from before the Tang Dynasty. Consequently, it contains many instances that can correct errors in the current editions of the ***Spiritual Pivot*** <term>Lingshu: the second half of the Inner Classic, focusing on acupuncture and anatomy</term> and the ***Basic Questions***. In this volume, wherever the *New Corrections* cited Quan to correct Wang, we have recorded the details under the **Critical Commentary** <term>Ping'an: interpretive notes and evaluations by the editor</term> sections. This allows us to preserve the authenticity of the original ancient text and rectify the mistakes made by Master Wang.

Ancient texts contain many "phonetic loans" <term>jiajie: a practice where a character is used for its sound to represent a different word with a similar pronunciation</term>. Since this book is based on a Tang Dynasty scroll manuscript, it contains characters such as "scrofula" written with a variant radical, or "inclined" written as "fontanelle," "appearance" written as "child," and "sharp" also written as "child" <note>original: 瘰 written as 瘰, 頗 written as 囟, 貌 written as 兒, 銳 written as 兒. ### Editorial Principles and Citations

The table of contents has been arranged at the beginning of the volume to facilitate easy reference and research.

The original manuscript of this book contains many "vulgar characters" <term>suzi: non-standard, simplified, or regional character variants used in handwritten manuscripts rather than formal printing</term>. For example, "to emit" <note>original: fa</note> was written as 𤼵, "to block" <note>original: e</note> was written as 𨵿, and "scorched" <note>original: jiao</note> was written as 瞧. These types of characters have all been uniformly corrected to their standard forms.

In the "New Corrections" <note>scholarly revisions commissioned during the Song Dynasty</note> of the *Basic Questions* <term>Suwen: one of the two foundational texts of the Yellow Emperor’s Inner Classic</term>, the *Grand Basis* is cited in more than 160 entries. Among these, over 100 entries are already present in this version of the text, while over 50 entries are missing from it. Furthermore, in the editions of the *A-B Classic of Acupuncture*, the *Pulse Classic*, and the *Arcane Essentials from the Imperial Library* <note>original: Jiayi Jing, Maijing, and Waitai Miyao</note>—all of which were edited by Lin Yi and his colleagues—the *Grand Basis* is cited a total of thirty-some times. In the Japanese work *Heart of Medical Prescriptions* <note>original: Yixinfang, the oldest surviving Japanese medical text</note>, it is cited in over 20 entries. 

Upon examining the present book, I found that some of these cited sections are preserved while others are lost. For the preserved sections, wherever the wording of the classic text or Yang’s Commentary <note>referring to the 7th-century scholar Yang Shangshan</note> differs from the characters used in our manuscript, I have clearly noted the differences in the commentary beneath the text. As for the lost sections, I have compiled these "fragments of lost text" separately and appended them to the end of the work. Each entry is carefully labeled to show which book it was sourced from. This ensures that even these "shattered jade disks and broken tablets" <note>original: lingbi duangui; a metaphor for precious but fragmentary remnants of ancient treasures</note> are preserved, as they are exceptionally valuable to our understanding.

Having established that all diseases arise from disruptions in Qi, the text now details the specific physiological mechanisms by which different emotions—anger, joy, sorrow, and fear—damage the body's internal systems and energetic flow.</meta>

<page-num>Nine</page-num>
<header>Shao Ya 2</header>

<note>original: "傷二魄" (Damage to the two souls). The original manuscript's character for "soul" (Po) was written as "lump" or "block," but was corrected during proofreading. According to the classics, the Lungs store the Qi, and the Qi houses the "Po" <note>the corporeal soul associated with physical life</note>. Therefore, "damaging the Po" is consistent with the Lungs being affected.</note>

# Qi Bo said: Anger causes the Qi to reverse its flow; if severe, it leads to

# The vomiting of blood and the upward flow of food.
<gloss>Because anger pulls the Qi upward, the flow is reversed. If the anger is extreme, this reversal causes the vomiting of blood <note>original: "歐血" (Hematemesis)</note> and forces food to move upward instead of down. Level Note: Regarding "the upward flow of food," the *Suwen* <note>Basic Questions</note> version reads "diarrhea with undigested food," but the *New Correction* notes that the *Jiayi Jing* and *Taisu* <note>Great Innocence</note> versions both read "food moves upward," which matches this manuscript.</gloss>

# Joy causes the Qi to become harmonious and the will to be fulfilled; the Nutritive and Defensive Qi flow smoothly, and therefore

# The Qi relaxes.
<gloss>When one is joyful, the Qi is balanced and the mind's intent reaches its destination. The Nutritive and Defensive Qi <term>Ying-Wei: the internal nourishing energy and the external protective energy of the body</term> move without obstruction; thus, the Qi "relaxes" into a state of health. Level Note: In the versions found in the *Suwen* and *Jiayi Jing*, the word "flow" is not present.</gloss>

# Sorrow causes the system of the heart to tighten and the lungs to spread;

# The lobes lift up, the two burners become blocked, the Nutritive and Defensive Qi do not disperse, and heat gathers within; thus, the Qi dissipates.
<gloss>The vessels of the lungs enter the heart system. Since the lungs govern sorrow, the Qi of sorrow gathers there, causing the lung lobes to lift and the heart system to tighten. This blocks the upper and middle burners <term>San Jiao/Triple Burner: the "passageways" for fluids and energy in the torso</term>. Because the Nutritive and Defensive Qi gather in the heart and lungs and cannot disperse, the Spirit has no place to rest, leading to internal heat that "consumes" or dissipates the Vital Qi. Level Note: The scholar Quan Yuanqi noted that sorrow damages the heart; when the heart system tightens, it agitates the lungs, causing the lung lobes to lift and the meridians to reverse flow.</gloss>

# Fear causes the Essence to retreat; once it retreats, the Upper

# Burner closes; once closed, the Qi returns; once it returns, the Lower Burner bloats; thus, the Qi does not circulate.
<gloss>Although the "Gate of Life" stores Essence <term>Jing: the fundamental physical essence of the body</term>, it is broadly called the Kidney. The Kidney meridian rises through the liver and diaphragm into the lungs and heart. When a person is terrified, their Essence shrinks back. This causes the Upper Burner—located at the opening of the stomach—to close. When the passage is blocked, the Qi cannot ascend and is forced back down into the Lower Burner, causing abdominal distension and preventing the Qi from circulating. Level Note: The *Jiayi Jing* records "Essence retreats" as the primary effect of fear.</gloss>

<page-num>Twelve</page-num>

The Divine Master's section also notes that the *New Correction* of the *Basic Questions* says "Qi does not flow" should <gloss>read "Qi flows downward"; however, considering the original commentary, it should still read "Qi does not flow."</gloss>

## Heat causes the interstices of the skin to open and the Nutritive and Defensive energies to flow; thus, there is heavy perspiration
## And leakage.
<gloss>When the Qi cannot flow properly or because of heat, the skin's interstices <term>Couli: the textures and pores of the skin and muscles</term> open, allowing the Nutritive and Defensive energies <term>Ying-Wei: the internal nourishing energy and the external protective energy of the body</term> to move outward, resulting in a great leakage of sweat. Level Note: In the *Basic Questions* and the *A-B Classic of Acupuncture*, "heat" (熱) is written as "burning heat" (炅). In the *Basic Questions*, the phrase "thus there is heavy perspiration and leakage" is written as "perspiration leaks out greatly, and thus the Qi leaks."</gloss>

## Cold causes the interstices of the skin
## To close; the Qi does not flow, and thus the Qi retracts and gathers.
<gloss>Because the Nutritive and Defensive energies cannot pass through, encountering cold causes the skin's interstices to become blocked. Consequently, the Qi gathers and becomes a source of illness. Level Note: The *A-B Classic* lacks these three lines. In the *Basic Questions*, this section appears before the lines on "Burning Heat." The word "gathers" (聚) is written as "is" or "finished" (矣) in the *Basic Questions*.</gloss>

## Worry causes the Heart to have nothing to lean on, the Spirit to have nowhere to return, and contemplation to have no fixed point; therefore,
## The Qi is thrown into chaos.
<gloss>The activity of the Heart-Spirit is affected by human worry. When the will is fixed on a distressing matter, although there is mental activity, it has no external object to engage with, hence it is said to have "nothing to lean on." Wherever the Qi resides, the Spirit must return to it; now, being bound by worry, the Qi gathers and does not flow, so the Spirit has no place to return. Contemplation is also a function of the Spirit. Worry prevents one from anticipating things clearly because the Qi has no master to guard it, resulting in chaotic Qi. Level Note: In the *Basic Questions* and *A-B Classic*, "worry" (憂) is written as "fright" (驚). The *New Correction* notes that in the *Great Innocence*, "fright" is replaced by "worry." "Lean on" (寄) is written as "depend on" (倚) in other versions. "Mental activity" (心情) is written as "mental essence" (心精) in other editions.</gloss>

## Labor leads to
## Panting and gasping with perspiration; Qi escapes from both inside and outside, and thus the Qi is consumed.
<gloss>When a person uses their strength to the point of exhaustion, the Qi gathers as panting and gasping, and perspiration emerges from the skin. Both internal and external Qi are weakened and consumed. Level Note: Regarding "panting and gasping," the original *Basic Questions* version reads "panting, and both the internal viscera and bowels <note>the Zang and Fu organs</note> perspire." Since sweat is a form of Qi, sweat equals the exhaustion of Qi. The Zhao edition reads "panting and," while the *A-B Classic* is identical. For "inside and outside," the *Basic Questions* reads "outside and inside."</gloss>

## Thinking causes the body and Heart to have a focus, and the Spirit
## To have a place to stop; the Qi lingers and does not flow, and thus the Qi knots.
<gloss>Focusing thoughts on a single matter causes the Heart-Qi to dwell on one thing; therefore, the Spirit is occupied by a central object. The Heart-Spirit pulls the Qi together, causing it to "knot" and become an illness. Level Note: The word "body" (身) is absent in the *Basic Questions* and *A-B Classic*. In the *A-B Classic*, "focus" (存) is written as "damage" (傷). In the *Basic Questions*, "stop" (止) is written as "return to center" (歸正). The *New Correction* notes that the *A-B Classic* uses "stop" for those two characters. "Qi lingers" (氣留) is written as "Qi flows" (氣流) in the *A-B Classic*.</gloss>

# Regulating Diet
<gloss>The editor notes: This section, from the beginning of the chapter, can be found in the *Divine Pivot* (Lingshu), Volume 8, Chapter 56, "The Five Flavors." It also appears in the *A-B Classic of Acupuncture and Moxibustion*, Volume 6, Chapter 9, though the ordering of the text differs slightly. ## The Five Qi
<gloss>Chapter 13: Declaration of the Five Qi</gloss>

**The Yellow Emperor said: "I wish to hear about the 'Grain Qi' <note>The vital energy derived from food and drink</note> and its Five Flavors. How do they enter the five internal organs and differentiate themselves?"** <gloss>The essence and fluids of grain energy consist of five types of flavors. How does each enter the five internal organs and distinguish itself?</gloss> **Bogao <note>A physician-minister to the Yellow Emperor</note> replied: "The stomach is the sea of the five Zang-organs <note>The five solid internal organs: Liver, Heart, Spleen, Lung, and Kidney</note> and the six Fu-organs <note>The six hollow organs: Stomach, Small Intestine, Large Intestine, Gallbladder, Bladder, and the Triple Burner</note>. Both water and grain enter the stomach; thus, the five Zang and six Fu organs all receive sustenance from the stomach."** <gloss>The stomach receives water and grain and transforms them to nourish the five Zang and six Fu organs. Because all these organs receive this energy, the text says they "receive sustenance." Editor's note: In the *A-B Classic*, the response is attributed to Qibo instead of Bogao. That version lacks the words "water and grain," and after "sustenance," the *Divine Pivot* includes the word "Qi."</gloss> **"Each of the five flavors goes to the organ it favors: the sour flavor of grain goes first to the Liver; the bitter flavor goes first to the Heart; the sweet flavor goes first to the Spleen; the pungent flavor goes first to the Lung; and the salty flavor goes first to the Kidney."** <gloss>The flavors "going to what they favor" means that when fluids transform into the five flavors, their five medicinal natures differ. Because these natures correspond to the <term>Wuxing: the Five Phases (Wood, Fire, Earth, Metal, Water)</term>, each flavor naturally seeks out and "favors" the organ that shares its elemental nature. Editor's note: From "the sour flavor" down to "the Kidney," the phrasing in the *A-B Classic* is slightly different but follows a similar logic.</gloss> **"Once the Grain Qi and the fluids have moved, and the Nutritive and Defensive energies <term>Ying-Wei: the two primary forms of Qi that circulate in the body to provide nourishment and protection</term> are in great circulation, the dregs are then transformed and passed down in order."**

<summary>This page explains the "sea of grain" (the stomach) as the central hub for nutrition, detailing how the five flavors of food specifically target and nourish the five internal organs. It highlights the transition from the consumption of food to the circulation of vital energy (Ying and Wei) and the elimination of waste.</summary>
