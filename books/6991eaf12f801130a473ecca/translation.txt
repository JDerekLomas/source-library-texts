# LEONARDO DA VINCI

## ANATOMICAL STUDIES

### THE THORAX AND THE ABDOMEN

<column-break/>

->**PLATE I**<-

**The Mechanism of the Heart and Lungs**

The heart is a muscle of great strength, much more powerful than the other muscles. <note>Leonardo was among the first to correctly identify the heart as a muscle rather than a heat-generating organ.</note> I ask whether the heart, at its death, changes its capacity or not; and if it is found to be of greater capacity or less than when it was alive.

<margin>The movement of the lungs</margin>

The lungs are always full of air, even when they have expelled it. <note>This refers to "residual volume," the air that remains in the lungs after a forceful exhale to prevent the collapse of the air sacs.</note> When the lung is expanded, it touches the ribs; and when it is contracted, it is separated from them.

<insert>Note: examine the valves of the heart in a dead ox.</insert>

The spirit <note>original: "spirito" - in this context, Leonardo refers to the vital force or pressurized air believed to move through the body.</note> passes from the heart to the lungs through the artery-like vein <note>original: "vena arteriale" - the pulmonary artery.</note>, and it is not possible for it to return, because the valves are so arranged that they close when the spirit tries to go back.

<column-break/>

->**OBSERVATIONS ON THE DIAPHRAGM**<-

The diaphragm is a thick, muscular membrane that separates the vital organs <note>The heart and lungs</note> from the natural organs <note>The digestive system</note>. 

| Organ | Function | Position |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Heart | Source of Heat | Above Diaphragm |
| Lungs | Cooling of Heart | Above Diaphragm |
| Stomach | Digestion | Below Diaphragm |

When the diaphragm contracts, it flattens and allows the lungs to fill with air. <gloss>This is the cause of breathing.</gloss> 

<unclear>The nerves of the neck</unclear> descend to the diaphragm and control its movement. If these nerves are severed, the breathing stops immediately and the man dies. <note>Leonardo is likely referring to the phrenic nerve.</note>

### Sheet No. 1

->**ANATOMICAL STUDIES OF THE TRUNK**<-

The following observations pertain to the structure of the ribs and the movement of the diaphragm. <note>Leonardo was one of the first to accurately describe the mechanical action of the diaphragm during respiration.</note>

<margin>The heart is a tool of the soul, moved by the heat of the blood.</margin>

When the lung expands, the ribs must necessarily rise to create space for the air. <insert>This occurs through the action of the external intercostal muscles.</insert> Observe how the spine serves as the foundation for all these bones, like the mast of a ship. <note>Leonardo frequently used architectural and mechanical metaphors to explain the human body.</note>

<column-break/>

The vessels of the liver are distributed like the roots of a tree. <gloss>The portal vein</gloss> carries the nourishment from the stomach to be refined. <note>original: "vena porta"</note>

| PART OF THE BODY | MECHANICAL FUNCTION |
| :--- | :--- |
| Ribs | Protection and expansion |
| Diaphragm | The primary driver of breath |
| Spine | The central support |

<margin>Note the curvature of the lower back.</margin>

<unclear>... of motion ...</unclear>

Because a weight does not move except along the line which is most perpendicular to the center of the world <note>Leonardo refers to the "center of the world" as the center of gravity or the center of the Earth</note>, therefore a weight will move so much more swiftly along a given line the further that line is from the said perpendicular. | Motion made along lines which do not pass through the center of this world cannot be of the same velocity nor of the same force, as they lack one of the two principal conditions: namely, velocity and perpendicularity between the line of motion and the line of the horizon. | The closer the line of motion of a weight is to the perpendicular line of the center of the world, the swifter that line of motion will be; | and that velocity will be so much more powerful in its impact; | and that velocity will occupy so much less time. And if this percussion <note>impact or strike</note> is made in the air, that velocity will be maintained longer because the air offers no resistance unless it is compressed by said velocity.

---

<margin>+</margin>
If air, water, and earth have the same velocity and impact against the same object, that impact will be so much more powerful in one than the other as the material is denser | than the other; | therefore, earth will be more powerful than water, and water more than air. | But if the velocity of the wind that strikes a sail is greater than the velocity of the water that strikes the oars of the same vessel, | the force of the air will be greater than that of the water in proportion to how much its velocity exceeds the density of the water, making it greater than what the velocity of the water cannot maintain; and we see this in ships that travel with swift winds against a swift current of water.

---

<margin>B.</margin>
The motion of a stone falling through the air is so much swifter as it is closer to the end of its motion. | This does not happen because the air does not resist, but because the weight acquires one degree of velocity for every space of time; | and this happens because the air does not wish to condense in front of the stone; | and the air not being condensed, it does not resist the motion of that stone.

<margin>+</margin>
The power of the mover is so much greater as that power reaches the end of its motion in less time; | therefore, if that power is less by the same amount, the velocity will be so much less as that power reaches the end of its motion in more time.

<margin>The air follows the heavy body into the water and then returns to the surface.</margin>

Furthermore, the air that is trapped beneath the heavy body at the moment of impact is forced into the water, forming a bubble. This air, being lighter than the water, seeks to return to its own element <note>Leonardo refers to the Aristotelian concept of "natural place," where elements like air naturally rise above denser elements like water</note>. In doing so, it creates a reflux or a counter-motion that disrupts the initial waves caused by the strike. 

<column-break/>

If the heavy body penetrates deeply, the cavity it leaves behind is instantly filled by the surrounding water. This sudden rush of water toward the center causes a collision of currents, which then results in a vertical spout or column of water rising back into the air. 

<gloss>The height of the spout is proportional to the speed of the falling body.</gloss>

Thus, we see that the movement of the water is composed of three distinct actions: the initial displacement by the body, the rising of the trapped air, and the collision of the water filling the vacuum. Each of these actions produces its own set of circular waves <note>original: "tremolamenti"</note> on the surface, which intersect and interfere with one another until the equilibrium is restored.

## Why the lungs
Why the lungs expand and contract and do not move by themselves.
If it were not for the diaphragm <note>Leonardo uses the term 'diaframa'</note>, which in its extension draws air through the windpipe <note>original: "aspre arteria" – a term used historically for the trachea</note> and with that inflates the lungs, and thus keeps them inflated until the diaphragm itself withdraws and relaxes the parts of the abdomen; and with that withdrawal, it presses the lungs and drives out the air. Then the lung remains empty of wind, and the lung, by its own weight, falls and contracts by itself, and not by any force that presses it, except for its own weight—just as the bladder of fish does, which inflates with wind and, by its weight, falling and emptying itself, then sinks to the bottom.

The gallbladder is placed in such a position so that the liver presses upon it with its weight during every movement of the body.

## Why the valve of the ventricle
Why the valve of the heart's ventricle is made of three pieces.
The valve of the heart's ventricle is of three pieces because it has to close an orifice that is triangular; and if it were of two pieces, it could not close such an orifice well, because two pieces never join well at their corners as the three pieces do. And this you will see by experience: by making a circular orifice and then pressing it in two opposite places, you will see that it becomes oblong and can never be closed at its ends except with great difficulty. For this reason, Nature made such an orifice so that it opens in a triangle and closes in a triangle, because three arches join better than two. And Nature did this because the life of the animal consists in the movement of the blood, so that it should not perish.

### [On the mechanics of the heart and the flow of blood]

The heart, as a primary instrument of life, performs its office through the tension and relaxation of its parts. When the heart contracts, it expels the blood into the great vessels, and at that moment, the membranes <note>valves</note> are driven upward by the force of the blood. These membranes are so arranged that they meet at their edges, forming a seal that prevents any reflux <note>backflow</note> of the blood into the chamber from which it was just expelled. 

<margin>The heart is the source of heat and life.</margin>

The strength of these membranes is not uniform, for they are reinforced by cords <note>chordae tendineae</note> which anchor them to the muscular walls. Without these cords, the force of the blood would flip the membranes backward, like a sail caught in a gale, and the heart would fail in its purpose.

---

### On the nature of the vessels

The artery <note>likely the aorta</note> is composed of a thicker and more robust skin than the vein, because it must endure the sudden and violent pulse of the blood as it is driven out by the heart's contraction. The vein, however, being the vessel of return, does not suffer such violence and is therefore thinner and more flexible.

As previously noted, the heart does not merely open and close. It possesses a twisting motion <note>torsion</note> similar to the wringing of a cloth. This twisting serves to squeeze the blood out from the very depths of the ventricle, ensuring that the motion is efficient and that no blood remains stagnant.

### On the bones of the ribs

The bones of the ribs must be long according to their connection with the <term>vertebrae</term> <note>original: "spondili"</note> of the spine, because if they were all of one piece, they would not be able to bend, nor widen, nor lengthen among themselves without breaking. When the chest expands, the ribs are raised upward and the vertebrae extend; and when the chest contracts, the ribs lower and the vertebrae bend back. ### Conclusion: No difference between earth and water in the impacts
made by the **lightning bolt** <note>original: "saetta del tuono"; literally "arrow of thunder," a common Renaissance term for a lightning strike.</note> through the air, because it is seen through the [effects]. *   Double in weight
*   Doubled in its nature
*   Doubled in diameter
*   Doubled in resistance

And where there was one unit of weight, motion, density, and resistance, it shall be doubled in each—in weight, density, motion, and resistance. Consequently, the resistance will be doubled because the power will also be doubled, and thus the effect will also be doubled. Wherefore, as a consequence, the space will have become doubled. And if the space is doubled, the resistance of density has not [simply] changed, because the space is doubled and thus its power is also doubled, and its resistance is doubled, and the effect is doubled; wherefore, as a consequence, the space has become doubled.

# On the Distribution of the Lower Vessels

The great trunk of the body, having descended through the chest and abdomen, reaches its final division at the level of the small of the back. Here, the vessel splits like a fork in a road, sending life-giving blood to each of the lower limbs. These branches, which we call the <term>iliac arteries</term> <note>From the Latin 'ilia', referring to the flanks or groin area</note>, are the primary conduits for the legs.

<margin>The pulse may be felt here in thin subjects</margin>

It is of the highest importance for the surgeon to recognize the depth at which these vessels lie. They are protected by the heavy bones of the pelvis and the thick muscles of the loin, yet they remain vulnerable to internal ruptures or the hardening of the vessel walls. 

### Observations on the Veins

Parallel to these arteries lie the returning vessels, or veins, which carry the blood back toward the heart. 

| Vessel Name | Primary Function | Connection |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Common Iliac** | Main branch from the trunk | Splits into internal and external |
| **Internal Iliac** | Supplies the pelvic organs | Deeply seated within the cavity |
| **External Iliac** | Becomes the femoral artery | Passes under the ligament to the thigh |

-> **Clinical Applications** <-

When the flow is obstructed in these lower regions, the limb may grow cold and pale, a condition the ancients called <term>ischemia</term> <note>original: "ισχαιμία" — a restriction in blood supply to tissues</note>. <insert>Modern practice suggests elevation of the limb in cases of swelling.</insert> 

<gloss>Note the proximity to the nerves</gloss>

We must also consider the <term>anastomoses</term> <note>The natural connection or "bridge" between two blood vessels</note>, which act as secondary paths. If the main road is blocked, nature often finds a smaller path to ensure the survival of the flesh. As the great physician Galen once wrote, <note>original: "Natura nihil frustra facit"</note> "Nature does nothing in vain," and nowhere is this more evident than in the redundant pathways of the blood.
