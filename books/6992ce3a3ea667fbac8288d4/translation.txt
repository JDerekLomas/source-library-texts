### Water, Part 1

Master Mao <note>The author, Mao Yuanyi, refers to himself here in the third person as a mark of philosophical authority.</note> says: There are two ways of dealing with water. The other is to engage in combat *upon* the water, using swift skiffs and heavy warships.

Those who wish to utilize water must first understand water conservancy <term>shuli (水利): the science of managing water resources, including irrigation and the control of flow</term>; for this reason, I have placed it at the head of this section. Because its methods are precise and subtle, it follows next.

When fighting on the water, all parties are equally subject to the power of winds and waves. There are favorable conditions and unfavorable ones. 2. **Reversing**
3. **Sabotaging**
4. **Severing**

By exhausting the resource the enemy depends on, their position becomes untenable. This is what is meant by "Sabotaging."

## The Water Level

The Water Level <term>水平 (shuiping): A traditional Chinese surveying instrument, essentially a long spirit level used to ensure that canals and embankments were perfectly horizontal over long distances.</term> consists of a wooden trough two feet four inches in length. Three basins are carved into it—one at each end and one in the center. Each basin is one inch eight *fen* <note>The "fen" is a traditional unit of length, approximately 1/10th of a Chinese inch (cun).</note> wide, one inch three *fen* long, and one inch two *fen* deep. The distance between these basins is one foot five inches. Between the basins are water-connecting channels, each two *fen* wide and one inch three *fen* deep. 

A piece of floating wood is placed into each of the three basins; these floats are slightly smaller than the basins themselves and are three *fen* thick. On top of these floats, upright "teeth" <note>These are sight vanes. By looking across the tops of these three floating "teeth," a surveyor can determine a perfectly level line regardless of the ground's unevenness.</note> are established, standing eight *fen* high, one inch seven *fen* wide, and one *fen* thick. The trough is kept stationary and does not rotate to serve as a fixed reference...

The legs of the device are adjusted so that the height is level with the observer’s eye. Pour water into the basins; the floating wood pieces in the three basins will rise simultaneously. Squinting with one eye <note>original: 眇目 (miao mu). This refers to the technique of closing one eye to focus the line of sight across the markers, much like using a modern telescopic sight.</note>, look across them. If the three teeth <note>The "teeth" are the protruding tops of the wooden floats mentioned on the previous page.</note> are perfectly aligned, then it serves as a standard for the world.

Whether at a distance of ten paces, one *li* <note>A traditional Chinese mile, approximately 500 meters.</note>, or even several dozen *li*, as far as the eye can reach, one places the **Sighting Board** and the **Graduated Rod**. A white rope is also used to calculate the distances. In this way, the height in *zhang*, *chi*, *cun*, and *fen* <note>Traditional units: 1 zhang ≈ 10 feet; 1 chi ≈ 1 foot; 1 cun ≈ 1 inch; 1 fen ≈ 1/10th inch.</note> can be known. This system is called the **Water Level**.

**The Sighting Board** <term>照版 (zhaoban): A surveying target or signal board used to provide a clear visual point of reference over long distances.</term> is shaped like a square fan. It is four feet long: the lower two feet are painted black and the upper two feet are white. It is three feet wide, with a handle one foot long for holding.

**The Graduated Rod** <term>度竿 (dugan): A measuring staff, similar to a modern surveyor's leveling rod, used to record vertical elevation.</term> is twenty feet long. It is engraved with two hundred inches and two thousand *fen*. Within each inch, smaller markings denote the *fen*. According to the direction and the distance or height, the rod is used in reflection with the Sighting Board. Squinting with one eye, the surveyor looks at the teeth of the three floating wooden markers and the Sighting Board. One uses the measurements on the Graduated Rod to determine the height, proceeding step by step along the route.

# Warships

## Patrol Boats
<note>original: 遊艇 (Youting). Literally "roaming boats," these were light, fast vessels used for reconnaissance, scouting enemy movements, and carrying messages between the main fleet elements.</note>

## Covered Strikers
<term>Mengchong: An ancient class of assault ship characterized by a narrow hull and a deck covered in raw oxhide. This protection shielded the rowers and soldiers from arrows and fire, allowing the vessel to "strike" or ram into the enemy line at high speeds.</term>

## Tower Ships
<note>original: 樓船 (Louchuan). The "capital ships" of the ancient Chinese navy, these multi-decked floating fortresses featured several stories of battlements, allowing archers and trebuchet operators to fire down upon smaller vessels.</note>

## Fast Skiffs
<note>original: 走舸 (Zouge). Small, extremely rapid rowing boats. They were manned by elite sailors and used for surprise boarding actions or quick retreats, serving as the "cavalry" of the water.</note>

## Combat Vessels
<note>original: 鬪艦 (Doujian). The standard heavy-duty warships used for direct engagement. They typically featured crenelated walls (parapets) to protect soldiers during ship-to-ship combat.</note>

## Sea Falcons
<term>Haihu: Literally "Sea Falcon." These ships were designed with a low center of gravity and high prows to withstand heavy waves. They were known for their stability in the open ocean, much like a falcon remains steady in the wind.</term>

## Guangdong-style Ships
<note>original: 廣東船 (Guangdong chuan). Referring to vessels built using the craftsmanship of the southern coast. These were often recognized for their superior "V-shaped" hulls, which allowed for better sailing in deep-water maritime environments compared to flat-bottomed river boats.</note>

## Pointed-Head Ships of Xinhui County
<note>original: 新會縣尖頭船 (Xinhui xian jiantou chuan). A specific regional design from Xinhui (in modern Guangdong province). These vessels featured a distinctive sharp, pointed bow designed to cut through water and maneuver through coastal shallows or river mouths with high efficiency.</note>

## Dongguan County "Big-head" Boat
<note>original: 大頭船 (Datouchuan). A distinctive vessel from Dongguan in the Pearl River Delta, named for its broad, flat, and high prow which allowed it to navigate both river mouths and coastal waters effectively.</note>

## Great Fuzhou Ship
<note>original: 大福船 (Dafuchuan). The powerhouse of the Ming navy. Originating from Fujian, these ships were massive, featuring deep V-shaped hulls and high bulwarks that allowed them to literally look down upon and crush smaller enemy vessels during naval engagements.</note>

## Grass-skimming Boat
<note>original: 草撇船 (Caopiechuan). A lighter, more maneuverable version of the Fuzhou ship, designed to "skim" across the water. It was primarily used for chasing pirates who fled into shallow inlets where larger ships could not safely follow.</note>

## Haicang Ship
<note>original: 海滄船 (Haicangchuan). A versatile mid-sized warship. While smaller than the Great Fuzhou Ship, it was faster and required fewer crew members, making it the workhorse for standard coastal patrols and escort duties.</note>

## Wave-cleaving Boat
<note>original: 開浪船 (Kailangchuan). Engineered for speed, this vessel featured a sharp, narrow prow designed to "cut" or "open" the waves, minimizing water resistance during high-speed pursuit operations.</note>

## High-tiller Boat
<note>original: 高把稍船 (Gaobashaochuan). A vessel characterized by its unusually long or elevated steering oar (tiller). This design gave the helmsman a better vantage point and more leverage to navigate through treacherous reefs and narrow, winding channels.</note>

## Balahu Boat
<note>original: 叭喇唬 (Balahu). An agile, shallow-draft boat used for sudden attacks and fire-ship tactics. The name is likely a phonetic transliteration of a local maritime term; these boats were small enough to be carried on the decks of larger ships and deployed for close-range combat.</note>

## Chongcao Ship
<note>original: 艟艚 (Chongcao). A traditional heavy-hulled vessel used for transport and general combat duties, known for its durability and its ability to carry a significant number of soldiers and supplies.</note>

## Cangshan Boat
<note>original: 蒼山船 (Cangshanchuan). Literally "Green Mountain Boat." These were the smallest of the standard naval combat vessels, often used for boarding actions and delivering elite "shock troops" to the enemy's decks.</note>

# Treatise on Armament Technology, Volume 116: Military Equipment — Exposition on Naval Warfare

## Eight-Oared Boat
<note>original: 八槳船 (Bajiachuan). A light, nimble vessel propelled by eight oars. These were often used as auxiliary craft for larger ships, serving as scouts or for rapid communication between the fleet.</note>

## Eagle Boat
<note>original: 鷹船 (Yingchuan). Named for its speed and predatory nature, the Eagle Boat featured a narrow, light frame. It was designed to swoop in for quick strikes or to chase down fleeing pirate vessels that were too fast for heavier warships.</note>

## Fishing Boat
<note>original: 漁船 (Yuchuan). While originally civilian vessels, fishing boats were often requisitioned by the Ming military. Their unassuming appearance allowed them to conduct reconnaissance or transport supplies and soldiers without drawing immediate attention from enemy forces.</note>

## Net-Shuttle Boat
<note>original: 網梭船 (Wangshouchuan). An extremely small and fast boat, shaped like a weaver’s shuttle. It was utilized for "hit and run" tactics, darting through the waves to deliver fire-arrows or explosives before retreating.</note>

## Two-Headed Boat
<note>original: 兩頭船 (Liangtouchuan). This ingenious vessel was designed with a bow at both ends, allowing it to reverse direction instantly without the need to turn the ship around—a vital advantage in narrow, treacherous river channels or during a chaotic retreat.</note>

## Sand Ship
<note>original: 沙船 (Shachuan). A classic flat-bottomed vessel characteristic of the northern Chinese coast. Its design allowed it to sail over sandbanks and shallow waters where V-hulled ships would run aground, making it the primary transport and warship for the Jiangsu and Zhejiang regions.</note>

## Centipede Ship
<note>original: 蜈蚣船 (Wugongchuan). A long, multi-oared galley named for its resemblance to a centipede. This design was heavily influenced by Portuguese "fusta" galleys; the many oars provided high speed and independence from wind conditions, making it a formidable pursuit vessel.</note>

## Bird-Beak Ship
<note>original: 鳥嘴船 (Niaozuichuan). Named for its sharp, protruding prow that resembled a bird's beak. This design helped the vessel cut through the water more efficiently and was often equipped with specialized cannons or "bird-beak" muskets (matchlocks) for long-range engagements.</note>

## Mandarin Duck Oar Boat
<note>original: 鴛鴦槳船 (Yuanyang Jiangchuan). A vessel that utilized oars paired like "Mandarin ducks"—which symbolize inseparable couples in Chinese culture. This system optimized the rhythm and power of the rowers, allowing for sustained speed during long-distance patrols.</note>

### The Sea Falcon Ship <note>original: 海鶻 (Hǎihǔ). A heavy warship named for its resemblance to a predatory sea bird, specifically designed for stability in the turbulent waters of the open ocean.</note>

The **Sea Falcon** is a vessel characterized by a **prow that is narrow and sharp**, while the **stern is broad and expansive**. This hydrodynamic shape allows the ship to cut through water with precision while maintaining a sturdy base.

On both the port and starboard sides of the hull, there are **floating boards** <term>浮板 (fúbǎn): lateral outrigger-like structures or thickened hull sections designed to provide buoyancy and balance.</term> that are shaped like the **wings of a falcon**. ## The Paddle-wheel Boat
<term>Paddle-wheel Boat (Chelun ge; 車輪舸); a warship propelled by human-powered paddle wheels rather than traditional oars or sails alone, allowing for high speed and maneuverability.</term>

The paddle-wheel boat measures forty-two feet in length and thirteen feet in width <note>original: 4 zhang 2 chi long, 1 zhang 3 chi wide</note>. On the outside of the hull, hollow side-frames are constructed, each one foot wide. Within these empty spaces, four wheels are installed. The blades of these wheels extend into the water to a depth of approximately one foot. Men are stationed inside to turn the mechanism, causing the vessel to move as if it were flying.

The layout of the ship is as follows:
*   **The Bow:** A flat-headed front section, eight feet long.
*   **The Main Cabin:** The central section, twenty-seven feet long.
*   **The Stern:** A rear section seven feet long, which serves as the **helm tower** <note>the raised structure used for steering and observation</note>.

Running through the center of the cabin from the front to the back is a single large ridge beam. **Cover boards** are installed to hang down from both sides of this beam. Each board is five feet long and two feet wide. They are fitted with rotating axles at the bottom, functioning much like hanging windows.

When approaching the enemy, the crew first stays hidden inside and discharges **Divine Sand**, **Divine Arrows**, and **Divine Fire** <note>specialized incendiary and blinding weapons; "Divine Sand" often referred to caustic powders or fine shot</note> through the openings. Because the boards are closed, the enemy cannot see our troops.

As the enemy's momentum begins to weaken, our soldiers simultaneously flip the boards upward and stand along both sides. In this position, the boards function as **side-shields** <note>Pangpai; tall shields used for cover</note>. Both the boards and the main cabin are wrapped in layers of **raw oxhide** <note>used as a form of "organic armor" to protect the wood from fire and projectiles</note>. From their positions inside, the men throw **fire-balls**, launch **dart-spears**, and utilize **hook-prongs** and other equipment. By these means, the enemy ship will inevitably be burned and destroyed.

## The Red Dragon Boat
<term>Red Dragon Boat (Chilongzhou; 赤龍舟); a swift and maneuverable Ming Dynasty warship characterized by its draconic iconography, used for both intimidation and as a primary assault vessel.</term>

The Red Dragon Boat is a formidable vessel of the fleet, combining symbolic majesty with practical naval design.

*   **Iconography:** As shown in the illustration, the boat is distinguished by a large, ornate dragon head at the prow and a jagged, upturned tail at the stern. This design gives the vessel its name and serves to identify it as a specialized combat craft within the naval hierarchy.
*   **Structure and Rigging:** The ship features a single mast supporting a rectangular mat-sail <note>original: 席帆 (xifan); sails made of woven bamboo or reed mats were common in Chinese naval history for their durability and ease of repair</note>. A crew member is stationed on the deck to handle the rigging, allowing the boat to harness the wind effectively.
*   **Navigability:** The hull is designed to navigate through rhythmic ocean waves with high agility. It is suited for both coastal defense and deep-water engagements, where its speed allows it to act as a vanguard or a pursuit craft.

<note>The Red Dragon Boat represents the Ming Dynasty's approach to psychological warfare—using fierce aesthetics to demoralize the enemy—while maintaining the technical standards required for effective maritime combat.</note>

The vessel's shape resembles a dragon and is divided into three distinct horizontal layers. The interior is used to store various <term>machinery and armaments (qixie; 噐械)</term> as well as <term>incendiary weapons (huoju; 火具); these include early gunpowder tools such as fire-arrows or primitive grenades</term>.

The prow of the ship is fashioned into the likeness of a dragon’s head with an open mouth. Inside this head, there is enough space to accommodate one soldier, who acts as a lookout to observe the movements of the enemy. 

The "back" or upper cover of the boat is reinforced with bamboo strips and "caltrop" nails <note>original: 菱角釘 (lingjiaoding); these were four-pronged iron spikes designed to prevent enemy boarding parties from gaining a foothold on the deck</note>. This plating must be applied densely and evenly to ensure the surface is solid and impenetrable; this serves as the primary protective roof of the vessel. At the "chest" (the forward upper section) of the boat, a small door is cut, protected by a shutter made of iron plate.

The middle layer of the boat is situated in the center of the hull. Here, a "well" <note>original: 井 (jing); likely referring to a vertical hatch or central corridor</note> is opened to allow the crew to move between levels and to provide a vantage point for discharging firearms. On both sides of this middle section, one soldier is stationed to man the oars. Additionally, two sturdy wooden frames are erected to prop up the ship’s upper cover, which allows the crew more space to operate the incendiary weapons effectively.

At the very bottom of the vessel is the <term>keel (longgu; 龍骨); literally translated as "dragon bone," this is the central structural beam of the ship's hull</term>. The hull is hollowed out to accommodate a mechanical system <note>original: 機括 (jikuo)</note> utilizing iron weights as ballast. This design ensures that even amidst fierce winds and surging waves, the boat remains stable and cannot be easily capsized or sunk.

The head of this boat is exceptionally sturdy. In front of the single mast and sail, a window is cut where one soldier is stationed to hold the <term>rudder (duo; 舵)</term> and navigate the waterways. The remaining crew consists of two men in charge of the incendiary weapons and two men who work in shifts to man the oars. 

If several hundred of these vessels were constructed, they would appear as a fleet of magnificent "Red Dragons" <note>original: 赤龍 (Chilong)</note> roaming across the rivers and seas.
