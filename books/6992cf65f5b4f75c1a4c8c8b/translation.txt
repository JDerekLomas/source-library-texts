# Southwestern Seas: The Kingdom of Western India, Part I

<note>While the text uses the term "Western India" (西印度), the geography and names clearly describe Persia (modern-day Iran). Early Chinese geographers often grouped these regions based on their position relative to the Indus River.</note>

The kingdom was eventually destroyed by the **Arabs** <note>original: 阿丹 (*Adan*); likely referring to the Islamic Caliphate</note>, and they changed their customs to follow the **Islamic religion** <note>original: 回教 (*Huijiao*)</note> of the Arabs. They led armies on eastern expeditions, expanding their territory even further. Suddenly, they were again invaded by the **Turks** <note>original: 都魯機 (*Duluji*); the Ottoman Empire</note> and the **Tartars** <note>original: 韃韃里 (*Dadali*)</note>, and the country once again became a battlefield.

Until the year 1586 <gloss>the 14th year of the Wanli reign of the Ming Dynasty</gloss>, a man named **Abbas** <note>Shah Abbas I, "the Great" (r. 1587–1629)</note> gathered his tribal forces and drove out all the foreigners. He restored the old capital and established a government, which lasted for over two hundred years. 

Later, the **Afghans** <note>original: 阿富晏士 (*Afuyanshi*)</note> rose in rebellion. Everywhere they went, they burned and slaughtered, leaving the people in utter misery. Fourteen years later, a ruler known as **Kouli Khan** <note>original: 高里坎 (*Gaolikan*); referring to Nader Shah, often called Thamas Kuli Khan in Western sources</note> raised an army, attacked the Afghans, and set himself up as King. However, he merely replaced violence with violence, and when he died, he left no heir. 

The various tribal leaders fought one another for the throne until a great general seized power, calling himself King **Karim Khan** <note>original: 加林坎 (*Jialinkan*); Karim Khan Zand, founder of the Zand Dynasty</note>. He remained on the throne for sixteen years. Upon his death, the country fell into chaos once again. This was the year 1779 of the Christian era <gloss>the 44th year of the Qianlong reign of the Qing Dynasty</gloss>. 

Then, an eunuch named **Agha Mohammad** <note>original: 阿牙磨哈墨 (*Ayamuhamo*); Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar, who founded the Qajar Dynasty</note> used his military forces to settle the unrest in the year 1796 <gloss>the 1st year of the Jiaqing reign of the Qing Dynasty</gloss>.

### The Rise of the Arabs and the Conquest of the West

As the legends say, his words were cryptic and subversive. <note>This likely refers to the teachings of the Prophet Muhammad, which early Chinese historians often viewed through a lens of "mysterious" or "deceptive" rhetoric because they differed so greatly from traditional Chinese or Buddhist thought.</note> He gathered a following of outcasts and fugitives at the **Henghe River** <note>original: 恒曷水 (*Henghe Shui*); likely referring to the borders of the Sassanid Persian Empire or the Oxus River region</note>, where they raided merchant caravans. Securing the western frontiers, he declared himself king. He relocated a **Black Stone** <note>original: 黑石 (*Heishi*); a clear reference to the Black Stone of the Kaaba in Mecca</note> and treated it as a sacred treasure. When the Persians sent an army to suppress him, the Persian forces were utterly defeated and forced to retreat.

From this victory, the Arabs grew in power until they finally destroyed the **Persian Empire** and defeated **Byzantium** <note>original: 拂菻 (*Fulin*); the Eastern Roman Empire</note>. It was only then that they came to possess granaries filled with millet and wheat. They turned their sights southward, invading the **Brahmin lands** <note>original: 婆羅門 (*Poluomen*); Northern India</note> and annexing various kingdoms.

Their elite troops eventually numbered four hundred thousand. ### The Land of Berbera

Within the southwestern sea lies the tribe of **Berbera** <note>original: 撥拔力 (*Bobali*); referring to the Somali coast of East Africa</note>. They are an independent people who serve no master. Their land produces none of the five grains; instead, they eat meat. They have a custom of piercing the veins of their cattle to mix the blood with milk for drinking. They wear no woven clothes, covering their bodies with sheepskins. 

The women of Berbera are noted for being both wise and beautiful. The land is rich in ivory and **ambergris** <note>original: 阿末香 (*Amoxiang*); a precious substance found in the sea, used for perfume and medicine</note>. When Persian merchants wish to trade in their markets, they must form caravans of several thousand men. They must perform a ritual of "biting and cutting" <note>an ancient blood oath</note> to seal their vows before trade can begin. 

The Berbera army utilizes weapons made of ivory and horn, along with bows, arrows, and spears. They once boasted two hundred thousand soldiers, but they were eventually defeated by the Arabs. 

In the second year of the **Yonghui reign** <note>651 AD, during the reign of Emperor Gaozong of Tang</note>, the King of the Arabs sent his first official envoy to the Tang court to present tribute.

# New Tang History: Persia

The ***New Tang History*** <note>original: 新唐書 (Xin Tang Shu); the official history of the Tang Dynasty compiled in the 11th century</note> states that **Persia** <note>original: 波斯 (Bosi)</note> lies to the west of the **Dahe River** <term>Dahe Shui: a name used in ancient Chinese texts for the Amu Darya or Oxus River</term>. To the northwest, it extends over 4,000 *li* to reach the borders of **Byzantium** <term>Fu-lin: the medieval Chinese name for the Byzantine Empire</term>.

The population numbers in the hundreds of thousands. Their ancestors were the former kings of Persia, who were a separate branch of the **Greater Yuezhi** <note>original: 大月氏 (Da Yuezhi); an ancient nomadic people of Central Asia</note>. The king took the name of the region as his surname and title. He governs from three primary cities and oversees more than ten other major cities. 

In their customs, they honor the right side and humble the left. They offer sacrifices to Heaven, Earth, the Sun, the Moon, Water, and Fire. For evening sacrifices, they use **musk** <note>original: 麝 (she)</note> mixed with **refined oil** <note>original: 蘇 (su); likely referring to ghee or clarified butter</note> to rub onto their foreheads, noses, and ears. During worship, they must cross their legs.

Commonly, the people go barefoot. The men shave their hair and wear robes with uncut collars. They use blue and white fabrics for their headscarves and capes, which are bordered with brocade. The women braid their hair and wear it hanging behind. In warfare, they ride **elephants**; a single elephant carries a hundred soldiers. If they lose a battle, the soldiers are all put to death <note>indicating a strict martial law where failure was a capital offense</note>.

In the judgment of crimes, no written documents are used; instead, cases are decided in open court. To determine guilt, a judge sears the accused's tongue with a glowing hot iron. If the wound heals white, the person is considered righteous; if it turns black, they are judged at fault. Punishments include **shaving the head** <term>kun: an ancient degrading punishment</term>, wearing an iron collar, or **cutting off the nose** <term>yi: a traditional form of mutilation as punishment</term>. For minor crimes, they may be tattooed or forced to wear a wooden block around their neck for a specified period.

They wear embroidered fine silks and ramie fabrics, as well as boots and shoes. The women wear long robes and pearl-adorned crowns with tassels. Their ears are decorated with gold and jeweled rings, their wrists with gold and precious bracelets, and they even wear rings upon their feet. Their silk kerchiefs and vessels of gold and silver are crafted with such skill that they surpass the beauty of pure gold.

Their currency is called the **pululi** <note>original: 哺嚕黎 (bululi); likely referring to a silver or gold coin of the region</note>, which weighs one **mace** <note>original: 錢 (qian); a traditional Chinese unit of weight, approximately 3.7 grams</note> and has patterns inscribed on its face. Their red copper coins are called **pulusi** <note>original: 哺嚕厮 (bulusi); derived from the Arabic *fulus*, a common term for copper currency</note>, which are used for daily trade in the markets. 

The climate is mild. Their calendar has no leap months; instead, they determine the length of the month by the sighting of the moon. When the new moon is first seen at night, the following day marks the beginning of a new month. There are those skilled in **astronomy and divination** <note>original: 推步 (tuibu); the traditional science of calculating celestial movements and the calendar</note> who can predict exactly which day spring begins and the flowers will bloom, or which day autumn begins and the foliage will wither. Their predictions for solar and lunar eclipses, wind, rain, and the tides are all remarkably accurate. 

The houses of the people are built with stacked stone walls, topped with roofs of brick or earth, reaching heights of fourteen or fifteen feet. The marketplaces, with their stalls of prepared foods, fine silks, and books, are much like those in China. Their prepared grains are often made with **butter-sugar** <note>original: 酥糖 (sutang); likely referring to halwa or similar rich, honeyed confections</note> and honey, making the flavor exceptionally delicious. The King holds China in high esteem. Upon hearing that the merchant fleet has arrived, he personally leads his officials to welcome them. After the imperial edict is proclaimed within the country, he issues a general decree to his subordinates to bring out all their treasures for trade. 

In the nineteenth year of the **Yongle** era <note>1421 CE, during the sixth voyage of Admiral Zheng He</note>, a Chinese man named Zhou frequently purchased cat’s eye gems weighing about two *qian* <term>original: 錢; a traditional unit of weight, roughly 3.7 grams</term>.
