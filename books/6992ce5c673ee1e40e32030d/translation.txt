# The Taowu

The <term>Taowu</term> <note>original: 檮杌; one of the "Four Perils" (Four Evil Creatures) of ancient Chinese mythology, often representing stubbornness and ignorance</note> is a beast of supreme evil that delights in combat. It will not retreat even when facing death. It is a creature found within the <term>Western Wastes</term> <note>A mythical region at the edge of the world often mentioned in the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*</note>.

In appearance, it resembles a tiger, but its fur is over three feet long. It possesses a human face and the claws of a tiger. Its tusks measure eighteen feet in length <note>The original text uses the measurements one *zhang* and eight *chi*. In legendary descriptions, these exaggerated sizes are intended to convey a sense of overwhelming terror</note>. 

When it captures humans, it devours them. In combat with other beasts, it will never withdraw or yield; it fights until the very end, stopping only upon its death. It is also known by the name <term>Daoshou</term> <note>original: 倒壽; literally "Inverted Longevity" or "Life-Inverter"</note>.

<note>The illustration shows the Taowu as a hybrid creature. By combining the human face (intelligence/sentience) with the tiger’s body (predatory power) and an unbreakable will to fight, the Ming dynasty authors characterized it as the embodiment of "obstinate' or "perverse" evil—intelligence used for purely destructive ends.</note>

<summary>This page describes the Taowu, one of the Four Perils of Chinese mythology, detailing its tiger-like body, human face, and its reputation for relentless, suicidal aggression.</summary>
<keywords>Taowu, Four Perils, Chinese mythology, Western Wastes, mythical beasts, Sancai Tuhui, Daoshou</keywords>

# The Yan

In the "Assembly of Kings" chapter of the <term>Book of Zhou</term> <note>original: 周書王會篇 (Zhoushu Wanghui pian); a text describing foreign envoys bringing tribute to the Zhou court</note>, it is recorded that the <term>Northern Tang-Rong</term> <note>original: 北唐戎; a northern nomadic group</note> presented the **Yan** as tribute. It is said that the **Yan** resembles a <term>Yuguan</term> <note>original: 隃冠; likely referring to a type of ceremonial cap or headgear, though the exact nature of this comparison is debated</note>. It is not precisely known what a "Yan-cap" is.

However, in the <term>Classic of Mountains and Seas</term> <note>original: 山海經 (Shanhaijing); an ancient Chinese compendium of geography, flora, and fauna</note>, it is noted that on Mount <term>Xianpeng</term> <note>original: 縣堋之山; a mythical or distant peak located in the northern regions</note>, the **Yan** beasts are very numerous.

The **Yan** resembles a donkey but possesses cloven hooves. Its horns are like those of an <term>antelope</term> <note>original: 羚羊 (lingyang)</note>. It is also known by the name **Yan-donkey** <note>original: 𨸏驢 (Yanlü)</note>. Commentators suggest that it is a variety of "mountain donkey" and consider it to be a species of mountain goat. It grows to the size of a deer, and its hide is durable enough to be used for making boots.

<note>The illustration depicts the Yan as a sturdy, caprid-like animal. By citing both the *Book of Zhou* (a political record) and the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* (a geographical mythography), the author bridges the gap between legendary creatures and the practical realities of nomadic tribute and leatherworking.</note>

<summary>This page describes the Yan, a donkey-like animal with antelope horns found on Mount Xianpeng, noting its history as a tribute gift and the utility of its hide.</summary>
<keywords>Yan, Yan-donkey, Book of Zhou, Classic of Mountains and Seas, Mount Xianpeng, tribute, mountain goat, wildlife</keywords>

# The Wolf

The wolf is a greedy and fierce beast. When objects are gathered in a disorganized or messy manner, it is called <term>lang-ji</term> <note>original: 狼籍; literally 'wolf-mats.' Historically, this referred to the way wolves would trample grass into messy beds or leave the remains of their prey scattered about</note>. It is also described by the phrase <term>li-mi lang-li</term> <note>original: 粒米狼戾; literally 'grains of rice scattered in a wolfish mess,' a classical idiom used to describe a scene of wasteful abundance or general disorder</note>.

**Wolf**, **Wolfish Disarray**, **Scattered Abundance**

<note>The accompanying woodcut illustration shows a wolf in a classical landscape, positioned near a pine tree on a rocky outcrop. In traditional Chinese natural histories like the *Sancai Tuhui*, the wolf is often depicted not just as a biological entity, but as a moral symbol of greed and destructive disorder. The text focuses heavily on how the animal's perceived behavior has influenced the Chinese language through idioms of messiness and waste.</note>

## The Black Fox

In the Northern Mountains, there is a creature called the **Black Fox**, which is a <term>divine beast</term> <note>original: 神獸 (shenshou); a mythical animal possessing spiritual power, often appearing as a favorable sign from the heavens.</note>. When a sovereign ruler is able to bring about a state of <term>Great Peace</term> <note>original: 太平 (taiping); a classical Chinese concept of an era of perfect social harmony and prosperity.</note>, then this beast will reveal itself. It also appears when the <term>Four Barbarians</term> <note>original: 四夷 (siyi); a historical term referring to the diverse tribes and kingdoms situated in the four cardinal directions around the Central Plains of China.</note> come to offer tribute. During the reign of **King Cheng of Zhou** <note>reigned c. 1042–1021 BCE; a monarch of the Western Zhou dynasty famous for a period of stability and the Duke of Zhou's regency.</note>, one was known to have appeared.

<note>The woodcut illustration depicts the Black Fox with a thick, upright bushy tail, walking through a landscape of stylized clouds or hills. In the context of this Ming-dynasty encyclopedia, animals are often categorized by their moral or cosmological significance. The Black Fox serves as a "monarch's omen," legitimizing a ruler's virtue through its rare appearance.</note>

# The Lushu

On **Mount Niuyang** <note>original: 杻陽山 (Niuyang shan); described in the "Classic of the Southern Mountains" as a mountain rich in minerals, with gold and silver on its southern slopes and white jade on its northern slopes.</note>, there is a beast whose form resembles a **horse** with a **white head**, **tiger-like stripes**, and a **red tail**. Its voice is like **singing** <note>original: 謡 (yao); specifically refers to a folk song or a chant performed without instrumental accompaniment.</note>. It is named the **Lushu** <note>original: 鹿蜀 (lushu); a mythical chimera. Some modern scholars compare its description—a striped, horse-like creature—to the appearance of an okapi or zebra, though it remains a creature of legend.</note>. If a person wears its skin <note>original: 寢其皮 (qin qi pi); literally "sleeps on its skin," implying the hide is used as bedding or a garment to harness its spiritual properties.</note>, it is **propitious for their descendants** <note>original: 宜子孫 (yi zi sun); a traditional formulaic blessing meaning the object grants fertility or ensures the success and proliferation of one’s children and grandchildren.</note>.

### The Bai Ze

On **Mount Dongwang** <note>original: 東望山 (Dongwang shan); literally "East-Looking Mountain."</note>, there is a **marsh beast** <note>original: 澤獸 (zeshou); a creature inhabiting wetlands or marshes.</note>.

One of its names is the **Bai Ze** <note>original: 白澤; literally "White Marsh." It is one of the most significant auspicious beasts in Chinese mythology, symbolizing wisdom and protection against malevolent spirits.</note>.

It is capable of **human speech**. When a **ruler possesses virtue** <note>original: 王者有德 (wang zhe you de); a recurring theme in Chinese political philosophy where miraculous creatures appear only during the reign of a moral sovereign.</note> that shines brightly into the **dark and distant reaches**, then it will **appear**.

Long ago, when the **Yellow Emperor** <note>original: 黃帝 (Huang Di); the legendary ancestor of the Chinese people and a foundational culture hero.</note> was on a **tour of inspection** <note>original: 巡狩 (xunshou); a formal progress through the realm by an ancient monarch to inspect the lands and spirits.</note> and reached the **Eastern Sea**, this beast **spoke to him**, providing guidance to **eliminate harms for the era** <note>original: 為時除害 (wei shi chu hai); historical accounts suggest the Bai Ze described the forms and names of 11,520 types of supernatural beings, teaching the Emperor how to defend the people against their hauntings.</note>.

<note>The accompanying woodcut illustration depicts the Bai Ze as a majestic quadruped. In late imperial iconography, it is often shown with a leonine body, a thick mane, and sometimes extra eyes or horns, signifying its ability to "see" and identify all the hidden demons of the world.</note>

## The Mo

<note>original: 貘 (mò). In modern Chinese, this refers to the tapir. However, in traditional Chinese lore and this Ming Dynasty encyclopedia, it is described as a mythological chimera with the ability to consume metal and protect humans from unseen harms.</note>

In the **valleys of the southern mountains**, there is a beast named the **Mo**. It has the **trunk of an elephant**, the **eyes of a rhinoceros**, the **tail of a cow**, and the **paws of a tiger**. 

Its body is **yellow and black** in color. If a person **sleeps upon its skin**, it can **ward off pestilence** <note>original: 辟瘟 (pìwēn); a reference to traditional beliefs that certain hides could block contagious diseases or "miasmatic" vapors.</note>. Drawing its **form can ward off evil** <note>original: 辟邪 (pìxié); a common practice where illustrations of powerful beasts were posted on gates or walls to frighten away malevolent spirits.</note>. 

It **licks and eats copper and iron**, and does not eat other things. <note>This peculiar diet of metal is a hallmark of the Mo in ancient literature, often leading to it being associated with the panda in some historical interpretations, though its appearance here is strictly chimeric.</note>

<note>The accompanying woodcut illustration depicts the Mo with a distinctive trunk and spotted hide, sitting in a posture reminiscent of a protective guardian lion.</note>

# The Jiao

On **Jade Mountain** <note>original: 玉山 (Yùshān); a mythical mountain often mentioned in ancient Chinese geography as the residence of the Queen Mother of the West.</note>, there is a beast named the **Jiao** <note>original: 狡 (jiǎo). While this character later came to mean "crafty" or "cunning," in this context it refers to a specific auspicious creature described in the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*.</note>. 

It is shaped like a **dog** but has **leopard-like markings**. It possesses the **horns of an ox**. Its **voice is booming** and it has a **huge mouth** and a **black body**. 

When it is sighted, there will be a **great harvest throughout the world** <note>original: 大穰 (dà rǎng); an ancient term for an exceptionally bountiful crop.</note>. 

**Master Han** <note>original: 韓子 (Hán Zǐ). Likely referring to the philosopher Han Fei, or a commentator on ancient texts who noted the creature's prophetic nature.</note> says that its appearance is the **omen of a bumper year**.

<note>The accompanying woodcut illustration depicts the Jiao in a dynamic walking pose. Despite its fearsome leopard spots and horns, its association with the "great harvest" makes it a welcome sight for farmers and rulers alike, symbolizing a time of plenty and the absence of famine.</note>

## The Qiongqi

On **Gui Mountain** <note>original: 邽山 (Guī Shān); a mountain described in the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* as being rich in copper and jade.</note>, there is a beast shaped like an **ox**. It has a **hedgehog’s tail** and its body is covered in **hedgehog-like bristles**. Its **voice** sounds like the **howling of a dog**.

When it encounters a **fight**, it **assists the one who is in the wrong** <note>original: 不直者 (bù zhí zhě); referring to the party who is unjust, dishonest, or the aggressor.</note>. Its name is the **Qiongqi** <note>original: 窮奇 (Qióngqí). One of the "Four Fiends" (Sì Xiōng) of Chinese mythology, it represents the subversion of virtue.</note>. It is also **capable of eating humans**.

<note>The accompanying woodcut illustrates the Qiongqi's strange, hybrid anatomy. Unlike the helpful Jiao on the previous page, the Qiongqi represents a reversal of the natural moral order. Ancient legends suggest that if it hears of a man who is loyal and upright, it will bite off his nose; if it hears of a man who is wicked, it will hunt a beast to present to him as a gift.</note>

**Vocabulary:** Qiongqi (The Perverse Fiend), Gui Mountain, Hedgehog tail, Howling dog, Classic of Mountains and Seas.

# The Meng Huai

On **Qiaoming Mountain** <note>original: 譙明之山 (Qiáomíng Zhān); a mythical peak from the *Classic of Mountains and Seas*, said to be where the Qiaoming river originates.</note>, there is a beast. It is **shaped like a civet** <note>original: 貍 (lí); often refers to a leopard-cat or a wild feline.</note> with **red bristles** <gloss>Lu</gloss> <note>original: 魯 (Lǔ). This gloss likely indicates a textual variant or a phonetic similarity to another creature or region.</note> and it resembles a **pig**. <term>Qiangliang</term> — A deity originating from the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* (Shanhaijing), specifically the "Classic of the Great Wilderness: The North." It is often interpreted as a nature spirit or a god of the northern regions, embodying the fearsome power of the tiger.

<term>Holding a Snake (Xianshe)</term> — In early Chinese mythology, the act of holding or "taming" snakes is a common attribute of high-ranking deities. It signifies their authority over the primal and often dangerous forces of the natural world.

<term>Great Wilderness (Dahuang)</term> — A term used to describe the prehistoric, unmapped lands surrounding ancient China. These areas were believed to be inhabited by gods, strange tribes, and hybrid beasts that did not follow the standard laws of nature.

<summary>This page describes Qiangliang, a hybrid deity from the northern Great Wilderness characterized by a tiger's head and a human body. It serves as a classic example of the Ming dynasty's efforts to categorize and illustrate the ancient, supernatural inhabitants of the world's edges.</summary>
