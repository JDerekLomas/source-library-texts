While a **purpose** stands before our soul as a complete whole, the **accomplishment** is usually only achieved piece by piece; thus, we resign ourselves to providing materials for a history, rather than the history itself. ### 119.

Hypochondriacs <note>In the 19th century, "hypochondria" referred to a specific nervous disorder affecting the digestive system and spirits, often linked to melancholy.</note> frequently see black figures resembling threads, hairs, spiders, flies, or wasps. These phenomena also appear at the onset of "black cataract" <note>original: "schwarzen Staar"; a historical term for amaurosis or blindness where the eye looks clear but cannot see, often related to optic nerve issues.</note>. Some see semi-transparent small tubes, like the wings of insects, or water bubbles of various sizes; these sink down when the eye is raised, sometimes hanging together exactly like frog spawn, and are noticed sometimes as complete spheres, sometimes as lenses.

### 120.

Just as light can arise there without external light, so too do these images arise without external images. They are partly temporary, and partly last for a lifetime. Sometimes a color also appears in this context: for hypochondriacs frequently see narrow yellow-red bands in the eye, often more intensely and more frequently in the morning, or on an empty stomach.

### 121.

That the impression of any image remains in the eye for some time is known to us as a physiological phenomenon (23); the excessively long duration of such an impression, however, can be regarded as morbid <note>meaning "pathological" or indicative of disease.</note>.

### 122.

The weaker the eye is, the longer the image remains within it. The retina <term>Retina: the light-sensitive inner lining of the back of the eye</term> does not recover as quickly, and one can view the effect as a kind of paralysis <term>Paralyse: a loss of function or sensation; here referring to the "stunning" of the optic nerve</term> (28).

->123.<-

Regarding dazzling images, it is not surprising. If one looks into the sun, one can carry the image around for several days. Boyle <note>Robert Boyle (1627–1691), an Anglo-Irish natural philosopher known for his work on chemistry and physics.</note> recounts a case lasting ten years.

->124.<-

The same occurs, relatively speaking, with images that are not dazzling. Büsch <note>Johann Georg Büsch (1728–1800), a German mathematician and educator who wrote on the senses.</note> says of himself that a copperplate engraving remained in his eye, perfect in all its parts, for seventeen minutes.

->125.<-

Several persons, who were inclined toward spasms and <term>plethora</term> <note>An excess of blood in the body, believed in the 18th century to affect sensory perception.</note>, retained the image of a bright red <term>calico</term> <note>A plain-woven cotton cloth, often printed with bright patterns.</note> with white shells in their eyes for many minutes and saw it hovering like a veil before everything. It only disappeared after long rubbing of the eye.

->126.<-

Scherfer <note>Karl Scherffer (1716–1783), a Jesuit mathematician and physicist who studied the theory of colors.</note> remarks that the purple color of a fading, strong light impression can last for several hours.

->127.<-

Just as we can produce a light phenomenon on the <term>retina</term> <note>The light-sensitive inner surface of the eye.</note> by applying pressure to the eyeball, a red color arises from weak pressure, and a fading light is produced, as it were.
