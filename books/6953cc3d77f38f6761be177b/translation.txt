# INTRODUCTION.

---

The present volume continues the publication of the dated documents from the archives of Zenon; the 158 texts which are here printed cover the period between the beginning of year 30 and the end of year 35 (256 to 250 B.C.). About forty of the more interesting have been already published in our *Annals*, and many of the others are for the present merely fragments, though I have little doubt that some of them will be completed before very long by the discovery of the missing parts. But besides these, the reader will find here a large quantity of quite new material arranged in the order in which it once passed through Zenon's hands. During the period in question, Zenon was permanently stationed at Philadelphia <note>A Greek settlement in the Faiyum region of Egypt, named after Queen Arsinoe Philadelphos</note> and the position which he occupied there as Apollonios' man of business brought him a multitude of correspondents from all classes of the population. Above all, we have a long series of letters from Apollonios himself; and though these are mainly instructions about the management of the estate, the actual words of a man who for many years played a leading part in public life, revealing as they do something of his real character, cannot fail to be interesting.

In arranging the material, I have gone on the assumption that the dates refer to the <term>regnal year</term> <note>A system of dating based on the years of a monarch's reign, in this case Ptolemy II Philadelphos</note>, unless we find in the text a definite indication to the contrary. It will be observed that the accounts nos. 59206, 59292, 59293 and 59296 are clearly dated by the <term>financial year</term> <note>The fiscal calendar used by the Ptolemaic administration for tax and agricultural purposes</note> which began on the 1^st^ of Mecheir <note>The sixth month of the Egyptian calendar</note>, and we must admit the possibility that the scribes who were accustomed to reckon by the financial year in their accounts may have fallen into the habit of using it for other documents as well. I think indeed that this is not only possible, but probable. If for instance we compare *P. S. I.* <note>Pubblicazioni della Società Italiana, a standard series for papyri</note> 379 and 381, we cannot but conclude that the scribe who wrote the latter text has in fact dated it by the financial year. But fortunately it is not until we reach the reign of Euergetes <note>Ptolemy III Euergetes, successor to Philadelphos</note> that this uncertainty becomes seriously troublesome. For during the later part of the reign of Philadelphos, the financial year coincided with the regnal except for a short space of from one to three months. # ZENON PAPYRI.

**59140. Letter from Alexandros to Zenon.** — 0.23 m x 0.08 m. — Date: 28^th^ May, 256 B.C.

In a letter of which the meaning is clearer than the grammar, Alexandros informs Zenon that he has inquired about the charges which Kritias has been making against Charidemos and has found them to be false. He speaks of Kritias denouncing *the soldiers from your side* <note>original: "τῶν παρὰ σοῦ στρατιωτῶν"</note>, the allusion being to Charidemos; but this phrase does not mean that Zenon held a military command, though he may have had some authority over the <term>cleruchs</term> <note>Soldiers who were granted plots of land in Egypt by the Ptolemaic government in exchange for military service.</note> in his district. Alexandros may perhaps be the <term>chief of police</term> <note>original: "ἀρχιφυλακίτης" (archiphylakites), a local official responsible for security.</note> mentioned in an unpublished papyrus in Michigan.

-> Alexandros to Zenon, <-

-> sent for Charidemos, <-

### 59142. Letter from Apollonios to Zenon. — 0 m. 14 x 0 m. 32. — Date: 1^st^ July, 256 B.C. (plate I).

Edg.*, 107) and the girls were employed in a wool factory belonging to him in Memphis (see *P. S. I.*, 854).

### 59143. Letter from Nikanor to Zenon. — (a) 0 m. 065 x 0 m. 075; (b) 0 m. 07 x 0 m. 145. — Date: about 13^th^ July, 256 B.C.

> Commerce with Asia, "Ishmaelites from Gilead with their camels bearing spicery and balm and myrrh," had long ago made the camel known to the Egyptians, but it does not seem to have been introduced into Egypt until Ptolemaic times. At the date of the present letter the camel was certainly being used to some extent in the interior of Egypt. See also no. 59207, 7 and *B. G. U.*, 1351, 1353.

**59145. Petition to Zenon from Sphragis.** — 0 m. 33 x 0 m. 095. — Date of reception: 2nd August(?), 256 B.C. (pl. II).

Sphragis, either the <term>maidservant</term> <note>original: "paidiske"</note> bought by Zenon three years ago in Transjordania (see no. 59003) or more probably another woman of the same name, had been robbed on her way to Sophthis, a village in the Memphite <term>nome</term> <note>An administrative district in Ancient Egypt</note>, of a few articles which she was carrying, two garments, some wool and a little copper money. She asks Zenon to write to Leontiskos, the chief policeman in that district, to give her back the things stolen, which were now reported to have been found.
