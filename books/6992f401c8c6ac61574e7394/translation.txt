# A Plough’s Depth of Apricot-Blossom Rain

<note>The phrase "A plough's depth of apricot-blossom rain" (original: 一犁杏雨) is a poetic expression for the arrival of spring. It refers to a rainfall that is exactly sufficient to soak the earth to the depth of a plough-blade, occurring precisely when the apricot trees are in bloom—the traditional signal for the agricultural season to begin.</note>

<term>Kangxi (康熙)</term> — The era name of the Qing dynasty's fourth emperor, meaning "Peaceful Prosperity."

<term>Apricot-Blossom Rain (杏雨)</term> — A literary term for the gentle, steady rains of early spring.
<term>Plough Depth (一犁)</term> — A measure of rainfall indicating the soil is moist enough for cultivation.

# Imperial Preface to the Pictures of Tilling and Weaving

I, the Emperor, have diligently and cautiously studied the principles of governance day and night. I have come to realize that for the sustenance of the people, food and clothing are of the utmost importance. This is the reason why works such as the "Odes of Bin" and "Against Luxurious Ease" <note>These are classic texts from the *Classic of Poetry* and *Classic of History* that emphasize the hardships of farming and the dangers of laziness.</note>, as well as the Tang dynasty "Rhapsody on Sowing, Reaping, and Sericulture" <note>original: *Tianbao Jia Se Can Sang Fu*</note>, have been passed down to this day. Those who sought to strengthen the state through ancient codes and records studied these matters deeply. The governance of a nation must not be handled with negligence. Reflecting on the eras following the Three Dynasties <note>The Xia, Shang, and Zhou dynasties, considered the golden age of Chinese civilization.</note>, we can observe the traces of their history because the words of the edicts from the Western Capital <note>Refers to the Western Han Dynasty (206 BCE – 9 CE) headquartered in Chang'an.</note> were preserved. An ancient proverb says: "If one

<column-break/>

man does not plow, someone may suffer from hunger; if one woman does not weave, someone may suffer from cold." This is the fundamental origin of food and clothing. It is also said: "When the elderly are provided with silk and the working people do not freeze, both the young and the old will reach a state of harmony." In ancient times, people abandoned secondary pursuits <note>Refers to commerce or luxury trades.</note> to focus on the fundamental work of the land. To uphold this, whenever I go on inspection tours to observe local customs, I inquire about the people’s contentment in their labor across both northern and southern territories. I gather information on the nature of the soil, the suitability of various seeds, and the differences in the timing of the seasons. I have brilliantly recorded these matters in the <insert>Hall of Linguistic Origins</insert> <note>original: *Yuyuantang*, a hall in the Forbidden City or the Summer Palace used for study.</note>, inquiring into the essence of ancient strategies and remaining constantly attentive to the proper timing of the agricultural cycle.

<term>Odes of Bin (豳風)</term> — A section of the *Classic of Poetry* (Shijing) that describes the agricultural life and seasonal hardships of commoners in the ancient state of Bin.
<term>Against Luxurious Ease (無逸)</term> — A famous chapter from the *Classic of History* (Shujing) where the Duke of Zhou warns the King against falling into a life of leisure and forgetting the toil of the peasants.
<term>Sowing and Reaping (稼穡)</term> — A classical term (*Jia Se*) for the entirety of agricultural labor, specifically the planting and harvesting of grain.
<term>Sericulture and Mulberry (蠶桑)</term> — The process of raising silkworms (*Can Sang*) and growing mulberry trees to feed them, representing the production of clothing.
<term>Fundamental and Secondary (本/末)</term> — In Confucian economics, agriculture is the "fundamental" (*ben*) basis of the state, while commerce and crafts are "secondary" or "peripheral" (*mo*).

<summary>In this preface, the Kangxi Emperor establishes the "Pictures of Tilling and Weaving" as a tool for governance, arguing that a ruler's primary duty is to ensure the people have food and clothing. He links his own imperial inspections of farmland to the ancient traditions of legendary Chinese kings.</summary>

## Tilling Illustration 5: The Stone Roller

The old farmer labors at the harvest, his preparations thorough; from morning to night he guides the plow, refusing to rest. Now he hitches the black ox <term>original: *wujian*, a strong black buffalo or ox</term> to pull the stone roller <term>liuzhou: a heavy cylindrical stone tool used to level the soil and break up remaining clods</term>, ensuring the spring waters fill the leveled fields.
<note>By the Kangxi Emperor (r. 1661–1722). This poem highlights the transition from the rough labor of plowing to the refinement of leveling the soil.</note>

---

Like a wheel turning on a machine, the stone rolls along. Moving east and west, why such haste? In leveling the field, one dares not complain of toil. The spring ridges wind like ribbons, and the fertile soil is as rich as ointment <note>original: *gao*, meaning fat or grease, used here to describe the nutrient-rich, dark mud of a healthy paddy</note>. The creatures of the water provide a meal; tipping the wine jar, one enjoys the taste of crab claws.
<note>By the Yongzheng Emperor (r. 1722–1735). Yongzheng’s verse often balances the hardship of work with the rustic rewards of the rural lifestyle, such as fresh seafood and wine.</note>

---

Carrying the rain and guiding the plow, the task is completed in a single evening; laboring across the acres, who would dare ask for rest? Back and forth the stone roller turns like a weaver’s shuttle, as the rich soil is spread evenly across the old fields.
<note>By the Qianlong Emperor (r. 1735–1796). The "shuttle" metaphor emphasizes the rhythmic, repetitive movement of the roller across the landscape.</note>

**Translator’s Introduction to the Work:**

This title slip introduces one of the most significant artistic and political projects of the early **Qing Dynasty**. Commissioned by the **Kangxi Emperor** in 1696, these "Pictures of Tilling" were intended to honor the hard work of the peasantry and remind the imperial court of the agrarian foundations of the empire. 

The artist, **Jiao Bingzhen**, was a high-ranking official and astronomer who was famously influenced by European Jesuit missionaries at court. This work is celebrated for its unique style, which blends traditional Chinese ink techniques with Western-style **linear perspective**, making the fields and landscapes appear to recede into the distance—a revolutionary look for 17th-century Chinese art.

<term>Imperial Edition (Yuzhi): Indicates a work commissioned, written, or personally overseen by the Emperor himself, signifying its high status and authority.</term>

<term>Subject/Official (Chen): A humble term used by court officials when addressing the Emperor or presenting work to the throne.</term>
