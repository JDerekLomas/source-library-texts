## TO THE MOST REVEREND LORD
# NARCISSUS MARSH
### Bishop of Ferns

->EDWARD BERNARD sends greetings.<-

Indeed, venerable sir, I have preferred to relate the passing of our dear Guise <note>original: "GUISII"; referring to William Guise (1653–1683), a brilliant Oxford specialist in Oriental languages who died at the age of 30.</note> to you while in mourning. For you, above all others, know how to value that loss; indeed, insofar as your heavy responsibility for the churches permits, you strive to restore that same level of scholarship to this age through your own similar studies. We are therefore deprived—even as heaven is enriched—of a man who was flourishing and most distinguished in his youth, his lineage, his means, his piety, his wisdom, his learning, and in every single gift of intellect. 

From the historians, whom our friend followed through every age of human affairs, he gained wisdom and the light of moral conduct; but the strength and perfection of his reason—for such is its certainty—he acquired from the mathematical sciences, most learned Prelate, following your own example. Furthermore, how much he excelled in philosophy, two of the famous Colleges of this place <note>Oxford University, where Bernard and Guise were based.</note> are always ready to testify. And how much such a great talent was able to achieve in Theology in a short time, the whole of Oxford <note>original: "Oxonia"</note> felt and marveled at when he spoke once and then again from the pulpit of divine eloquence. 

Yet even these were not enough for such a rare nature. Almost the dialects of all nations, which you yourself have mastered or I would wish to learn—whether they be soft or harsh, from nearby or remote in the furthest reaches of Asia—he made familiar to himself <note>The text ends here with the catchword "sibi" (to himself), transitioning to the next page.</note> as if they were the gateways to a rare and profound knowledge...

For this reason, he diligently consulted the Arabic philologists and especially their poets—masters of an ancient eloquence untainted by any enemy.

Then a most vast field of study opened to our friend—one no less fruitful than all Greek literature (who would believe it?) but even more extensive.
