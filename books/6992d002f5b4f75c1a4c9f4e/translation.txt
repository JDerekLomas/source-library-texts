# Illustrated Gazetteer of the Maritime Kingdoms <note>original: 海國圖志 (Haiguo Tuzhi)</note>

<note>Compiled by the scholar-official Wei Yuan (1794–1857) and first published in 1842, this work is one of the most influential books in modern Chinese history. It was written in response to China's defeat in the First Opium War, aiming to provide a detailed account of Western geography, technology, and political systems. Wei Yuan’s goal was to encourage China to "learn the superior techniques of the foreigners in order to control them."</note>

### Miscellaneous Accounts of Western Instruments and Crafts

The *Record of Island Leisure* <note>original: 海島逸志 (Haidao Yizhi), a travelogue by the 18th-century Chinese traveler Wang Dahai describing his experiences in Southeast Asia</note> states: When Western ships sail, they do not rely solely on the compass <note>original: 指南車 (zhinanche), literally "south-pointing carriage," here used generally for the mariner's compass</note>. They use a "heaven-measuring ruler" <note>This refers to the sextant or octant, celestial navigation instruments used to determine latitude</note> to measure; by this, they know exactly how many *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters or 1/3 of a mile</note> the ship has traveled. They are also able to consult their charts to identify the locations of sandbanks, reefs, and muddy shoals in the sea with absolute precision. The instrument’s shape is somewhat like a paper folding fan, capable of opening and closing. It has one horizontal ruler and one diagonal ruler. The rulers are marked with divisions and inches, all written in Western characters. Every measurement must be taken precisely at the hour of noon, when the sun is at its zenith. The horizontal part is used to establish a level plane, while the diagonal part is used to observe the distance of the journey and the depth of the sea. However, even if one perfectly understands the measurements of the ruler, it is of no use without sea charts; therefore, the sea chart is the essential foundation <term>ti|The "substance" or "body" in Chinese philosophy, referring here to the fundamental data of the map</term>, and the heaven-measuring ruler is its application.

### The Heaven-Observing Tube
<term>chātiāntǒng|An early Chinese term for the barometer. Wei Yuan is describing how atmospheric pressure affects the mercury column to predict weather.</term>

The **Heaven-Observing Tube** consists of two styles of glass tubes, shaped like the handle of a writing brush and measuring over one foot in length. The interior is filled with mercury <note>original: 水銀 (shuiyin), "water-silver"</note> and the device is placed within a protective case. On the side, Western characters are inscribed. The mercury is able to rise and fall automatically. Generally, when the weather is clear and bright, the mercury sinks; when the weather is overcast and gloomy, the mercury rises. As it fluctuates in height, one observes the characters on the side to monitor the weather and the darkness of the clouds; it has never failed to be an accurate measurement.

### The Time-Setting Clock

The **Time-Setting Clock** divides a single day into twelve double-hours, which are further split into two cycles for day and night. Midnight <note>original: 子正 (zizheng), the midpoint of the Zi hour (11 PM–1 AM)</note> is twelve o'clock; the start of the following hour <note>original: 丑初 (chuchu), the beginning of the Chou hour (1 AM–3 AM)</note> is one o'clock. Noon <note>original: 午正 (wuzheng)</note> is again twelve o'clock, and the start of the afternoon <note>original: 未初 (weichu)</note> is once more one o'clock. Combined, a single day consists of ninety-six quarters <term>ke|A traditional Chinese time unit. By the 19th century, Chinese scholars harmonized the traditional 96-quarter day with the Western 24-hour system, making each "ke" exactly 15 minutes.</term>, with each quarter lasting fifteen minutes; this is a standardized system. These clocks vary in size: the smallest are barely an inch wide, while the largest reach several feet in height. There are also models with more than ten small bells linked in a series, producing a rhythmic chiming sound like the "eight sounds" <note>The "eight sounds" (bayin) refers to the ancient Chinese classification of musical instruments based on material: metal, stone, silk, bamboo, gourd, clay, leather, and wood</note>, which is quite melodious...

### Western Vessels and Shipbuilding

In the harbor of **Batavia** <note>original: 葛雷巴 (Gěléibā). The administrative center of the Dutch East Indies, modern-day Jakarta.</note>, there is an island known as **Deck Island** <note>original: 甲板嶼 (Jiǎbǎnyǔ). Historically identifying Onrust Island, which the Dutch used as a major naval repair and construction yard.</note>. It is so named because it is the location where the **Dutch** <note>original: 和蘭 (Hélán).</note> build their **decked ships** <term>jiǎbǎnchuán|A term used in the 19th century to describe Western square-rigged ships or galleons, distinguished by their multiple decks and heavy armament.</term>. These ships have a fixed lifespan; after twenty-five years, they must be dismantled. Those hull planks that remain usable are salvaged for other purposes, while those that are useless are burned to recover the iron nails.

The planks of these ships are over a foot thick. The horizontal crossbeams that provide structural support are reinforced with iron plates clamped on both sides. Furthermore, the external hull planks are covered with sheets of copper and lead, laid in continuous layers to coat the entire surface <note>This refers to the practice of "copper-bottoming," which protected the wooden hulls from the Teredo worm and seaweed growth, allowing for greater speed and longevity.</note>.

The masts are constructed in three connected sections, and the sails are made of cloth. A ship carries a total of forty-eight sails, ranging in size. The rigging, knots, and stays on the sides are all forged from steel and iron; thus, they are incredibly sturdy and secure, preventing any mishaps <note>original: 悞事 (wùshì). Accidents or errors in operation.</note>.

The hull of the ship is shaped like a **battlement** <note>original: 女牆 (nǚqiáng). A defensive parapet on a city wall.</note>, where dozens of **cannons** are positioned. Large ships carry two tiers of cannons, while smaller ones carry a single tier. Every sailor is assigned a specific duty; even in the deep of night or during violent rain and fierce storms, they do not dare to be even slightly negligent. The maritime law is severe—serious offenses are punishable by immediate execution. The Captain is the absolute authority on the vessel. Because of this high level of organization and firepower, the pirates of the high seas do not dare to approach these decked ships.

Adam Schall von Bell <note>original: "Tang Ruowang" (湯若望, 1591–1666), a German Jesuit missionary who served as an astronomer in the Qing court</note>, in his treatise on telescopes, explained the use of one concave lens and one convex lens. Nearsightedness is the counterpart to the vision of old age <note>presbyopia; referred to in the text as "old-age flowers"</note>. Consequently, the principle of the telescope was realized through an understanding of the eyeball itself. In nearsighted eyes, the cornea is overly **convex** <note>curved outward</note>. Therefore, a **concave** <note>curved inward</note> lens is used to "kill" <note>offset or neutralize</note> that excess convexity, allowing the nearsighted person to see into the distance. In the eyes of the elderly, the cornea becomes nearly flat. Therefore, a **convex** lens is used to increase its convexity, allowing the presbyopic person to examine fine details. It is simply the principle of *correcting deficiencies and remedying defects* <term>bu pian jiu bi (補偏救弊): a traditional medical and philosophical concept of restoring health or function by counteracting an imbalance</term>.

The **concave** lens is called the "Great Light-Brightener." <gloss>The terms "Great Light-Brightener" are applied to both the concave and convex lenses; this is based on the original *Treatise on Telescopes*.</gloss> The strength of the **convex** lens lies in its ability to enlarge the images of objects. However, when viewing an object through a **concave** lens, it appears smaller. Conversely, when using a **convex** lens to look at distant things, the view is blurry. <gloss>Viewing near objects with a convex lens is extremely clear; its weakness is in distance viewing.</gloss> This is the principle of the microscope: it resolves blurriness and enlarges the image to reveal what is small. This, too, is the art of *correcting deficiencies and remedying defects*.

Generally, objects at a distance cannot be seen because their image is too small and their color is too faint. In a telescope, the **convex** lens serves as the outer lens; it enlarges the image but cannot avoid blurriness. The **concave** lens serves as the inner lens <note>the eyepiece</note>; it increases the clarity and brightness of the light but cannot avoid making the image appear even smaller. When the two are combined, they work in harmony and assist one another. This is how the telescope is constructed.

Yet, there are naturally cases where the lenses do not align perfectly for the viewer. What then? For a nearsighted user, if the tube is slightly retracted, the **concave** lens exerts its strength. For those with the vision of old age...

<margin>Method of Making Western Telescopes</margin>

Regarding the principles of these instruments, the proportional combination of the power of **convex** and **concave** lenses depends entirely on their depth. Since the specific degree of depth cannot be measured directly, one instead measures the length of the light collection <note>This refers to determining the focal length by observing where the lens focuses light</note>. 

The method is to take a **convex** lens and face it toward the sun, using a board covered with white paper to catch the light. Moving the lens gradually from near to far, the sunlight striking the board changes from large to small, and the light becomes increasingly intense. Beyond this point, it becomes large and faint again. The measurement at the smallest and most intense point is the "deep power" of that lens. <gloss>The shorter the distance, the deeper the power; the longer the distance, the shallower.</gloss> 

Professional lens-makers call this "how many inches of light." This is the same method used for a "fire glass" <term>fire glass (火鏡): a burning lens used to focus sunlight to ignite tinder</term> to start a fire. Because the light passes through directly and converges into a small point, we name this the "Direct Convergence Limit" <note>The author's term for the focal point or focal length of a lens</note>.

Regarding **convex** lenses: 
* One that is convex on a single side is called **single-convex** <note>plano-convex</note>. 
* One that is convex on both sides with exactly equal depth is called **double-convex** <note>equi-convex</note>. 
* Those with unequal depths are called **asymmetric-convex**. 

Although their uses are the same, the lengths of their power limits differ. Only the Direct Convergence Limit remains the same regardless of which face is directed toward the sun. If the board is placed above the lens so as not to block the sun slightly...

If you tilt the lens, there is a reflection of the sunlight that projects onto the board. Here, too, one can find a point that is extremely small and intense. For a **double-convex** lens, regardless of which of the two sides faces the sun, this measurement will be equal. For an **asymmetric-convex** lens, the measurements will necessarily be unequal. 

As for the **single-convex** <note>plano-convex</note> lens, in theory, one side should have a limit and the other should not, yet in practice, one is long and the other is short, typically in a ratio of one to three. * If the flat side faces the sun and the **Indirect Convergence Limit** is 2, the method to find the **Direct Convergence Limit** is to multiply by six, which gives 12. 
* If the **Direct Convergence Limit** is 30, the method to find the **Indirect Convergence Limit** is to divide by six, which gives 5.

Those who study geography also observe that the four smaller circles <note>The Arctic Circle, the Tropic of Cancer, the Tropic of Capricorn, and the Antarctic Circle.</note> necessarily divide the Earth horizontally into five segments. Each location within these segments—whether it lies in the south, the north, or the middle—must inevitably possess one of the three climatic characteristics: **frigid, temperate, or torrid**. These distinctions make each region unique; therefore, we name each segment accordingly.

Let us first discuss the **Torrid Zone** <note>original: "Redao" (熱道), literally "The Hot Path," referring to the Tropics.</note>. This zone lies between the two southern and northern boundaries <note>The Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn.</note> and has a width of forty-six degrees and fifty-six minutes. The ancients believed that because this segment of land sits directly beneath the vertical rays of the sun, the ground was so intensely hot that it consisted entirely of barren sands. For this reason, they named it the "Torrid Zone." 

It was only when later generations conducted detailed investigations that they realized the ancient claims were erroneous. In fact, this region of the earth experiences the sun, moon, frost, dew, wind, and rain, all of which descend in their proper seasons. Not only is it a place where humans can live in peace and comfort, but its biological production is even more abundant than elsewhere. Every year...

The third is the **Frigid Zone** <term>handao</term> <note>Literally "The Cold Path."</note>, of which there are also two. One lies within the **Arctic Circle** <term>beiji yuanxian</term>, and the other lies within the **Antarctic Circle** <term>nanji yuanxian</term>. Each has a width of twenty-three degrees and twenty-eight minutes. They are named the "Frigid Zones" because, in these sections, for more than half of every year, the sun’s light is not seen. Even when they do receive the sun's illumination, its light is cast only at a sharp, oblique angle. Furthermore, whenever the sun is below the horizon, the cold is felt intensely, and there is no place that is not covered in ice. 

Although these lands are not as pleasant or as rich in natural products and suitable for human habitation as the **Torrid** <note>The Tropics</note> and **Temperate** zones, nonetheless, these regions still possess inhabitants and livestock. For instance, in northeastern **Russia** <note>original: "Eluosi" (峨羅斯)</note> and the various countries of **Europe** <note>original: "Ouluoba" (歐羅巴)</note> near the **Arctic Ocean** <note>original: "Beiyang" (北洋), literally "Northern Ocean."</note>, these areas are by no means entirely devoid of people and living things.
