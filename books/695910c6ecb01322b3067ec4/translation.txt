-># Preface<-

This work presents to the inhabitants of this great world, both present and future, new methods for health, for the good condition of the body and its senses, for the protection of life, and—if it is permitted to say so—for the postponement of those fatal and decreed times <note>The author refers to the "appointed hour" of death</note>. These methods are not fleeting or liable to be lost or forgotten, but are bound fast in the eternal marks of letters. In ancient ages, it was an exceptional part of royal learning to inquire into nature and to unearth the riches and resources of the sky, earth, and seas by scrutiny. By combining the studies of Apollo <note>The god of healing and truth</note> and Mars <note>The god of war</note>, rulers made nature a greater possession of their kingdom. Thus, even in the midst of battles and arms, Caesar <note>Julius Caesar</note> found time for the regions of heaven and the gods above. We know from books of more than historical truth that the kings Solomon and Zoroaster were not only skilled in the properties of nature and the causes of things, but interpreted them piously and wisely. Pliny is our authority that Alexander the Great, inflamed with the desire to know the natures of animals, delegated that commentary to Aristotle, and ordered several thousand men—who made their living by hunting, fowling, and fishing—to obey him. 

The subject itself forbids me from passing over an example of such grace in your most illustrious House of Nassau, for I know, Most High Prince, that it is a mark of your piety and great soul that you gladly hear the praises of your kinsmen. For the most excellent Count John Maurice <note>original: "I. Mauritius"; referring to Johan Maurits of Nassau-Siegen, 1604–1679</note>, having subdued many provinces in the West Indies <note>The Dutch colonies in Brazil</note> and fought through land and sea battles, and having defended and increased sincere religion, devoted what spare time he allowed himself for the recreation of body and mind amidst such great labors and the care of the state to a single distraction: the cultivation of the intellect. He even practiced the counting-board and that "learned dust" <note>A reference to the sand-trays used by ancient mathematicians for geometry</note> in a manner rivalling the subtlety of Archimedes. Furthermore, he so promoted these efforts of ours regarding Natural History <term>Natural History: the systematic study of organisms and the natural world</term> that he not only learned the innate power of many things himself, but opened the way for them to be produced for the public benefit. # Preface

I must confess that this fortunate opportunity fell to me while I was performing honored services for the most magnificent Company <note>The Dutch West India Company</note> and the Most Illustrious Count of Nassau <note>Johan Maurits of Nassau-Siegen, Governor of Dutch Brazil</note>. Through this foreign journey, I desired to serve both my fatherland and myself, and to transmit something of value to posterity. For this reason, I entrusted one part of the **Natural History** to my household companion, Georg Marcgraf <note>original: "G. Marcgravio"; a German astronomer and naturalist</note>, while I collected and applied the other part myself, through tireless observation as far as my official duties permitted. I examined the inner parts of the earth, the animals, and even the humans themselves; I sketched the likenesses of the species from life; I described their strengths and medicinal powers in a simple style; and I demonstrated how greatly the science of Medicine might be increased by these advancements.

Let those listen who—whether through great stubbornness or ignorance, it is uncertain—are sworn only to the observations of the ancients. Anxiously following in those same footsteps, they believe the art of healing is perfected in all its parts. They consider it a violation of their principles to probe the deeper secrets of nature or to add to existing discoveries; thus they indulge their own laziness and allow themselves to be lulled into servitude or sleep by the industry of those who came before.

Indeed, just as it is a mark of grateful piety to apply and venerate the institutions of the ancients, so too must it be considered envy and detraction to forbid us the right to extend the boundaries and limits of learning. For nature is not (to claim the words of Pliny as my own) so weary and exhausted that she can no longer bring forth anything praiseworthy. Nor did those ancients write with the intent of deterring us from similar endeavors, but rather so that we might have an example of labor excellently applied.

If those most pure priests of nature, Hippocrates and Aristotle, could rise from the underworld, they would disown these men as degenerate disciples. Upon seeing this theater of the New World, they would, I believe, bear witness to the ignorance of so many things with no fewer tears than that famous man of Abdera <note>Democritus, the "Laughing Philosopher," known for mocking human folly</note> showed with his laughter at the turns of fortune. But the reality of the matter refutes them sufficiently.

You, however, Most High and Unconquered Prince, receive with a serene countenance this gift of America, which commends itself to you and adopts you as its patron during the most joyful beginnings of your command and sovereignty. It is a gift neither cheap nor to be valued more for its appearance than for its practical use...
