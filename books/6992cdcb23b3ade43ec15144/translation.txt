# Muskmelon
<gloss>original: 甜瓜 (*Tiangua*). Corrected and integrated into this division during the Jiayou era of the Song Dynasty (1056–1063); previously categorized under the entry for "Melon Pedicle" in the classic texts.</gloss>

### Explanation of Names
**Sweet Melon** <gloss>From the Materia Medica</gloss> **Fruit Melon**
<gloss>Shizhen says: The seal script character for "melon" (瓜) is a pictograph representing the shape of a melon hanging amidst its curling vines and tendrils. Because the flavor of the muskmelon is sweeter than all other melons, it alone earned the name "Sweet" or "Sugary Melon." It was previously categorized in the Vegetable Division, but this was an error. According to Wang Zhen <note>Wang Zhen (1271–1333) was a Yuan Dynasty official and agronomist famous for his "Book of Agriculture" (*Nong Shu*).</note>: Melons vary in kind, but have two primary uses. Those served as fruit are "Fruit Melons," such as the muskmelon and the watermelon; those served as vegetables are "Vegetable Melons," such as the cucumber <note>original: 胡瓜 (*Hugua*), literally "Barbarian Melon," referring to its introduction from the West.</note> and the pickling melon <note>original: 越瓜 (*Yuegua*).</note>. Generally, produce that grows on trees is called "fruit," while produce that grows on the ground is called "vegetable." Large melons are called *gua*, while small, emerging melons are called *die* <note>The terms *gua* and *die* appear in the "Classic of Poetry" (*Shijing*) to describe the growth and proliferation of a family line.</note>.</gloss>

### Collected Explanations

<gloss>The *Bielu* <note>original: 別錄 (*Miscellaneous Records*), an early pharmaceutical text compiled by Tao Hongjing.</note> states: Melon stalks are found in the marshes of Mount Songgao <note>Mount Song in Henan Province, one of the Five Sacred Mountains of China.</note>. They should be harvested on the seventh day of the seventh lunar month and dried in the shade.</gloss>

<gloss>Su Song <note>A Song Dynasty statesman and scientist (1020–1101) known for his work on herbalism and horology.</note> says: The "Melon Pedicle" is simply the stalk of the sweet melon. It is found everywhere, planted in gardens and fields. There are two varieties, green and white, though the seeds of both are yellow. For medicinal use, the stalks from the early-ripening green melons are considered the best quality.</gloss>

<gloss>Li Shizhen says: Sweet melons are planted in great abundance throughout the northern territories and the Central Plains. They are sown in the second or third months. The plants grow as spreading vines with leaves several inches across. In the fifth or sixth months, yellow flowers bloom, and by the sixth or seventh months, the melons are ripe. Their varieties are extremely numerous. In shape, they may be round, long, pointed, or flat; some are as large as a foot in diameter, while others are small enough to be held in a single pinch. Some have ridges, while others are smooth. Their colors range from blue-green and green to yellow-spotted or white-streaked. The inner flesh may be white or red, and the seeds may be yellow, crimson, white, or black.</gloss>

<gloss>According to Wang Zhen’s *Book of Agriculture*: The varieties of melons are too many to list individually. Some are named for their shapes, such as "Dragon Liver," "Tiger Paw," "Rabbit Head," "Raccoon Head," "Sheep Marrow," and "Honey Tube." Others are named for their colors, such as the "Black Melon," "White Round," "Yellow-Flesh," "White-Flesh," "Small Green," and "Large Speckle." However, regardless of the name, their flavor never strays from being sweet and fragrant. The *Guangzhi* <note>original: 廣志 (*Comprehensive Records*), a geography and natural history text from the Jin Dynasty.</note> claims that only melons from Liaodong, Dunhuang, and Lujiang are superior. However, one cannot easily rank the "Large Melons" of Guazhou, the "Imperial Melons" of Yangcheng, the "Warm Melons" of Western Shu, or the "Cold Melons" of Yongjia as better or worse. The sweet melons of Gansu have skin and flesh that are both as sweet as malt syrup or honey; even after the skin is dried in the sun, it remains delicious. There is also a variety in Zhejiang...</gloss>

"Shade melons" are planted in shaded areas. When they ripen, they are golden yellow in color, and their skin is relatively thick. They can be stored until the following spring and still taste as fresh as if they were new. These are the results of skillful cultivation techniques and are not necessarily restricted by the quality of the soil. The seeds of the sweet melon can be dried until they crack; the kernels inside can then be removed and eaten as a fruit. All melons are extremely sensitive to the scent of **musk** <term>Musk (a pungent secretion from the musk deer used in perfumes and medicine)</term>; if they are exposed to it, the vine may fail to produce even a single fruit.

# Melon

**Pulp: Flavor and Nature**
It is sweet, cold, and "slippery" <note>In traditional medicine, "slippery" (hua) refers to foods that have a lubricating or laxative effect.</note>. It is considered slightly toxic. Da Ming <note>Chen Zangqi, an 8th-century pharmacologist.</note> says it is non-toxic. Sun Simiao <note>A famous Tang Dynasty physician often called the "King of Medicine."</note> says that eating too much causes **jaundice** <note>original: 黃疸 (*huangdan*)</note> and leaves a person weak and emaciated. There are many contraindications to consider.

**Medicinal Effects and Cautions**
If people who are recovering from an illness eat too much, it may cause nausea and vomiting. For those suffering from **Leg Qi** <term>Leg Qi (a condition often associated with beriberi, characterized by swelling, numbness, and weakness in the legs)</term>, eating melons will ensure the ailment is never cured. Chen Shizhen <note>A Tang Dynasty scholar and dietary expert.</note> says: Eating too much causes dampness and itching in the groin, leads to the formation of sores, triggers chronic "cold masses" <term>Cold masses (lumps or stagnation in the abdomen believed to be caused by the accumulation of "cold" energy)</term>, and can cause abdominal pain.

"Deficiency heat" <note>A state of feverishness or agitation caused by a lack of cooling fluids in the body.</note> from melons causes belching, weak breath, and weakness in the hands and feet. Eating a small amount is acceptable. The *Longyu Hetu* <note>An ancient apocryphal text of mystical and naturalist lore.</note> states: Any melon with "two noses" <note>Referring to two protrusions at the base.</note> or "two belts" <note>Referring to double stems or distinct patterns.</note> can be fatal. In the fifth lunar month, any melon that sinks in water will cause a "cold illness" if eaten, which will not be cured for the rest of one's life. Melons that have been touched by frost in the ninth month will cause chills and fever during the winter. Eating melons alongside oil-fried cakes will cause illness. If one suffers from bloating after eating too much melon, eating **salt flowers** <note>High-quality salt crystals.</note> will cause the melon to dissolve.

Tao Hongjing <note>A famous 5th-century Daoist and alchemist who organized the early Materia Medica.</note> says: If you eat too much melon, simply stepping into water will cause the melon to be cleared and dissolved through the urine. Li Shizhen says: Zhang Hua's *Record of Diverse Matters* <note>original: 博物志 (*Bowu Zhi*); a 3rd-century collection of wonders and natural history.</note> claims that if a person soaks in cold water up to their knees, they can eat dozens of melons at once. If they soak up to their neck, they can eat even more. The water itself then takes on the scent of melons. The fact that water dissolves melon is simply part of the plant's nature. # Melon Pedicel

<margin>Melon Pedicel</margin>
<margin>Compendium of Materia Medica</margin>

Its nature is cold, and it should be used to induce vomiting. In Greater Yang <term>Taiyang: The first and most superficial stage of an external illness</term> diseases where there is a blockage in the diaphragm, body heat, and headache, but the pulse is faint and weak—this indicates an injury caused by cold water during the summer months where the fluid is traveling through the skin. It is appropriate to induce vomiting. In Lesser Yang <term>Shaoyang: A stage of illness characterized by alternating chills and fever</term> disease, if there is a headache, alternating chills and fever, and the pulse is tight but not large, this indicates phlegm sitting above the diaphragm. It is appropriate to induce vomiting. 

When the chest feels stuffed and solid, with a localized, gloomy pain, and the patient is unable to eat and desires someone to press on the area—yet they produce turbid saliva and suffer from diarrhea ten or more times a day, and the pulse at the wrist <term>Cunkou: The position on the wrist used for pulse diagnosis</term> is slightly wiry—one should induce vomiting. For "chest anguish" <term>Aonong: A specific TCM symptom describing a distressing, suffocating sensation in the chest</term>, irritability, and insomnia where sweating or purging treatments have not yet been used (known as "excess irritability"), one should induce vomiting. When stagnant food remains in the upper stomach <term>Shangwan: The upper part of the stomach cavity</term>, one should induce vomiting. In all these cases, Melon Pedicel Powder is the primary treatment. 

However, those who have lost significant blood or are in a state of extreme deficiency <term>Xu: A state of bodily weakness or lack of vital energy</term> must not be given Melon Pedicel Powder. 

**Cheng Wu-yi says:** For what is high, transcend it; for what is in the upper <note>The original woodblock text reads "three" (三), likely a transcription error for "upper" (上)</note> region, surge it. Therefore, "transcending" <term>Yue: To lift and expel pathogens upward</term> is achieved through the bitterness of Melon Pedicel and Fermented Soybeans, while "surging" <term>Yong: To induce rapid vomiting</term> is achieved through the acidity of Small Red Beans. The combination of sour and bitter flavors causes surging and draining, which belongs to the *Yin* <term>Yin: The cooling, downward, or receptive principle</term> quality. 

**Wu says:** The *Classic of Difficult Issues* <term>Nanjing: An ancient medical text explaining complex concepts of the Yellow Emperor’s Inner Canon</term> states: "If there is a pulse in the upper region but no pulse in the lower region, the person should vomit; if they do not, they will die." This refers to internal injury from food and drink clogging the chest. The injury from food suppresses the "sprouting" or life-generating energy <term>Shengfa zhi Qi: The rising energy associated with the Liver and Spring</term> of the Great Yin and Wood phases, causing it to be trapped below. One should use Melon Pedicel Powder to vomit. This is what the *Basic Questions* <term>Suwen: The primary foundational text of Chinese medicine</term> means by "When the root is stagnant, express it." By removing physical obstructions from the Upper Burner <term>Shangjiao: The upper division of the body, including the heart and lungs</term>, the "root" (the body's core energy) can breathe freely; Heaven and Earth then intersect, and all things flow. However, if the pulse at the foot <term>Chi: The pulse position associated with the Kidneys and the lower body</term> is severed, do not use this, for fear of damaging the True Vitality <term>Zhenyuan: The original life essence one is born with</term> and preventing the Stomach Qi from recovering. 

**Zong Shuang <note>original: "宗爽", a taboo-avoidance substitution for Zongshi (宗奭), the name of the scholar Kou Zongshi</note> says:** This substance induces the vomiting of saliva profusely, but it does not harm the person. It is far superior to mineral emetics like copper green or sal ammoniac. 

**Zhen Heng <note>The courtesy name of the famous physician Zhu Danxi</note> says:** The nature of the Melon Pedicel is aggressive and can easily damage the Stomach Qi. For those with weak stomachs, one should use other medicinal herbs as substitutes. One should exercise extreme caution and avoid its use after a long illness or after childbirth.

# Collected Explanations
<note>A standard section in Chinese medical texts where the author compiles opinions from various historical botanical and medical scholars.</note>

**Tao Hongjing states:** Sugarcane produced in Jiangdong <note>The region south of the lower reaches of the Yangtze River.</note> is considered the best. Luling <note>Modern Ji'an, Jiangxi province.</note> also produces good varieties. One type from Guangzhou is the "Reed Cane" (*Dizhe*) <note>original: 荻蔗</note>; its joints are sparse and thin, but it is also edible.

**Su Song states:** It is now grown throughout Jiangxi, Zhejiang, Fujian, Guangdong, Hunan, and Sichuan. Large specimens can reach over ten feet in height. Its leaves resemble those of a reed. There are two primary types: The **Reed Cane** (*Dizhe*) has thin, short stalks with sparse joints; it is suitable for eating raw or for boiling into a thin syrup. The **Bamboo Cane** (*Zhuzhe*) <note>original: 竹蔗</note> has thick, long stalks and can be pressed for its juice to create granulated sugar. Much of this is produced in the prefectures of Quanzhou, Fuzhou, Jizhou, and Guangzhou. Granulated sugar mixed with cow's milk makes **"Milk Sugar"** (*Ruzhe*) <note>original: 乳餹</note>, which is uniquely produced in Sichuan. The sugarcane that southerners transport to the northern regions is mostly the Reed Cane variety, as Bamboo Cane is less common there.

**Meng Shen states:** Sugarcane that is reddish in color is called **"Kunlun Cane"** (*Kunlunzhe*) <note>original: 崑崙蔗; "Kunlun" often referred to dark-skinned peoples or distant southern lands in this period.</note>. The white variety is called Reed Cane. Bamboo Cane from Sichuan and Lingnan <note>The regions of Guangdong and Guangxi.</note> is superior. Although Jiangdong has it, it is inferior to the Sichuan variety. The Milk Sugar produced in Kuaiji <note>Modern Shaoxing, Zhejiang.</note> is nearly superior even to that of Sichuan.

**Li Shizhen states:** Sugarcane is grown in garden beds in clusters and relies heavily on the fertility of the soil. The stalk resembles bamboo but is solid on the inside. Large ones are several inches in circumference and six or seven feet long. The joints near the root are dense and gradually become sparser toward the top. It sprouts leaves like those of a reed but larger, reaching three or four feet in length and spreading out in all directions. The stalks are harvested in the eighth or ninth month and can be stored through the spring to be served as a fruit. 

According to Wang Zhuo’s **Classic of Sugar Frost** <note>original: 糖霜譜; *Tangshuang Pu*, an 1154 AD treatise on sugar production.</note>, there are four colors of cane. One is called "Du Cane" (*Duzhe*), which is the Bamboo Cane; it is green and tender with a thin skin, and its flavor is exceptionally pure.

# Volume 33

The Tang dynasty poet Wang Wei <note>original: 王維, a famous poet and polymath (701–761)</note> once wrote in a poem about cherries: "After eating your fill, there is no need to worry about internal heat, as the high officials provide sugar cane juice, for its nature is cold." This is indeed correct. However, Meng Shuo <note>A Tang dynasty physician specializing in dietetics</note> claimed that eating sugar cane alongside wine and food would trigger chronic ailments. Does he not know that sugar cane possesses the power to relieve alcohol poisoning and clear heat?

Daming <note>also known as Rihua Zi, author of a 10th-century materia medica</note> further stated that "sand sugar" <note>brown or granulated sugar</note> can detoxify alcohol. What he did not realize is that once sugar has been boiled and refined, its nature changes; it then assists the alcohol in generating heat. This is quite different from the nature of the raw, fresh juice. 

According to the *Guest Talk of Mr. Mian* <note>original: 澠氏客話, a collection of miscellaneous notes</note>: Licorice becomes "hot" <note>meaning it gains a warming property in TCM</note> when exposed to fire, while sesame oil becomes "cold." Sugar cane becomes "warm" when boiled into syrup, yet water becomes "cold" once it has been boiled into soup and allowed to settle. From these examples, one can understand the fascinating differences in the inherent natures of things. 

Furthermore, unofficial histories <note>original: 野史</note> record that Lu Jiang once suffered from a combination of malaria and consumption. He suddenly dreamed of a woman dressed in white who told him, "Eating sugar cane will cure you." At dawn, he bought several stalks of cane and ate them; by the next day, his illness was gone. This serves as proof of the cane's ability to assist the Spleen and "harmonize the center" <note>to balance the digestive system</note>.

It is commonly called the "water caltrop" <note>lingjiao</note>. In ancient times, authors often failed to distinguish between the different varieties. However, Wang Anpin’s *Records of Wuling* notes that those with three or four horns are called *ji*, while those with only two horns are called *ling*. In the *Zuo Zhuan* <note>a narrative history of ancient China from the 4th century BCE</note>, it is mentioned that the official Qu Dao had a great fondness for eating *ji*—this is the very plant. The *Erya* <note>the oldest surviving Chinese dictionary</note> calls it *ling* or *juemou* <note>pronounced "mei"</note>. Furthermore, Xu Shen’s *Shuowen* <note>an early 2nd-century dictionary of Chinese characters</note> states that in the State of Chu it was called *ji*, while in the State of Qin it was called *xie*. 

Note: I find that Master Yang’s *Records of the Red Lead* <note>Danqian Lu, a work by the Ming scholar Yang Shen</note> mistakenly identified *ji* as the foxnut <note>Euryale ferox</note>. He cited the *Li Sao* <note>"Encountering Sorrow," a classic poem by Qu Yuan</note>, which mentions "weaving caltrops and lotus to make a garment," arguing that caltrop leaves are too small and fragile to be woven into clothes; however, these conclusions are all errors. According to the *Erya*, *xie* is actually a name for the sicklepod <note>Cassia obtusifolia, a land plant</note>, not the water caltrop. Additionally, the *Pi Ya* <note>an 11th-century dictionary of flora and fauna</note> states that "ji-he" refers to the stalks of the lotus flower that rise out of the water, not the foxnut. These are instances where different plants share the same name. Since both Xu Shen and Yang Shen failed to investigate this thoroughly, I have corrected the record here.

## Euryale Seed <note>original: 芡子, *Qianzi*. The Gorgon Nut.</note>
<gloss>Standard pronunciation</gloss> **Yellow-water Flow**

> **Tao Hongjing states:** This is what we now call the Euryale seed. The flower atop the stem resembles a chicken’s comb, which is why it is called "Chicken-head."
>
> **Su Song states:** Its seed pod is shaped like the head of a chicken or a wild goose, which accounts for its various names.
>
> **Li Shizhen states:** The Euryale <gloss>Qian</gloss> can provide relief during times of frugality <gloss>jian</gloss> or shortage <gloss>qian</gloss> <note>This is a linguistic play on words: the name of the plant *Qian* (芡) sounds like the word for "shortage" (歉)</note>, hence it is called "Chicken-pouch" <gloss>Jiyong</gloss>. The name "Mao" appears in the *Zhuangzi* in the "No Ghost" chapter. The name "Ling" <note>not to be confused with the Water Caltrop</note> appears in the "Five Elements" chapter of the *Guanzi*. Yang Xiong’s *Regional Descriptions* <note>original: 方言, *Fangyan*</note> says: In the Southern Chu region it is called "Chicken-head"; in the You and Yan regions <note>modern-day Beijing/Hebei</note> it is called "Wild-goose Head"; and in the Xu, Qing, Huai, and Si regions, it is called "Euryale Seed" <gloss>Qianzi</gloss>. Its stem is called *ling* or *fu*. Zheng Qiao, in his *Comprehensive Treatises*, identified the "Hooked Euryale" <gloss>Gouqian</gloss> as the same as this plant, but he was mistaken.

# Collected Explanations

> **The Miscellaneous Records state:** The fruit of the Chicken-head grows in the marshes of Thunder Lake <note>original: 雷池, *Leichi*, located in modern Anhui province</note>. It is harvested in the eighth lunar month.
>
> **Han Baosheng states:** The sprouts grow in the water. The leaves are as large as lotus leaves, but they are wrinkled and covered in prickles. The flower and its seed pod are as large as a fist and shaped like a chicken's head. The fruit is like a pomegranate; its skin is dark green-black, and the flesh inside is white, like non-glutinous rice.
>
> **Su Song states:** It is found everywhere, growing in ponds and marshes. The leaves are commonly called "Chicken-head Plates." The fruit forms beneath the flower. Its tender stems are called *ling*, which is why it is also named *ling-ying*. People harvest the kernels to use as a vegetable.
>
> **Kou Zongshi states:** It is found throughout the empire. People living near the water harvest the seeds, remove the husks, and pound the kernels into a flour. This is then steamed and refined to make cakes, which can be used to... <note>the text cuts off, likely continuing onto the next page regarding dietary uses</note>
