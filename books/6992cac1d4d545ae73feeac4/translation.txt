Places on Earth are named according to their corresponding locations in the heavens. For instance, the two points at the top and bottom of the map that correspond to the North and South Poles of the heavens are likewise called the North and South Poles. The horizontal line that divides the north and south into two halves and corresponds to the celestial equator is also called the Equator. All other lines are named according to this same principle.

### Lines of Longitude and Latitude

Longitude lines <term>經線 (*jingxian*) — literally "warp threads" in a fabric, used here to describe the vertical lines on a map.</term> are based upon the Equator. The Equator is divided into 360 degrees, and from each degree, an elliptical arc is drawn. These arcs meet at the North Pole above and the South Pole below to represent the Earth's 360 degrees of longitude. These lines serve as the meridians <term>子午線 (*ziwuxian*) — literally "Rat-Horse lines," referring to the North-South axis in the traditional Chinese zodiacal compass system.</term> for every location.

Latitude lines <term>緯線 (*weixian*) — literally "woof threads" in a fabric, describing the horizontal lines on a map.</term> are based upon the meridians. The meridian is divided into 360 degrees, and a circle is drawn through each degree. Only the Equator is a "Great Circle"; as one moves further from the Equator, the circles gradually become smaller until they converge into a single point at the North and South Poles. These represent the ninety parallels <term>距等圈 (*judeng quan*) — literally "equal-distance circles," referring to the parallel circles of latitude.</term> to the north and south of the Equator.

# Correcting Midnight and Noon

The time difference of any given place is always determined by its longitude <term>經度 (*jingdu*) — the coordinate measuring east-west distance from a prime meridian.</term>. The sun travels one full circle around the Earth every day, which constitutes twenty-four hours. As the sun travels fifteen degrees every hour, it moves one degree every four minutes. Therefore, if one knows the longitude of two different locations, one can deduce the time difference between them. For a location to the East, the time difference is added; for a location to the West, the time difference is subtracted.

For example, if we take the Capital <note>referring to Beijing</note> as our primary reference: the longitude of the Capital is 134 degrees, 2 minutes, and 30 seconds. The longitude of the capital city of the Kingdom of Joseon <note>modern-day Seoul, South Korea</note> is 145 degrees. Its distance is 10 degrees, 57 minutes, and 30 seconds East of the Capital. However, the deferent circles of the **five planets**—Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn—take the Sun as their center, and each of these deferents also possesses an equant circle.

The third system is that of **Nicolaus Reimers Ursus** <term>瑪爾象 (*Ma’erxiang*)</term> <gloss>refer to Diagram 18</gloss>. He argues that the Earth is the center of the universe and does not move from its location, but it rotates once every day upon the North and South Poles. Around the Earth, the **Moon**, the **Sun**, and the **fixed stars** revolve, while the orbits of Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn revolve around the Sun. While the systems of these two scholars have their merits, neither is as precise as that of **Copernicus** <term>歌白尼 (*Geba’ni*)</term>.

# IV. Copernicus <gloss>Refer to Diagram 5</gloss>

The **Sun** is placed at the center of the universe. The closest to the Sun is **Mercury**, followed by **Venus**, then the **Earth**, then **Mars**, then **Jupiter**, and finally **Saturn**. # Supplementary Illustrations to the *Illustrated Explanation of the Earth*

To the right is the <term>Complete Map of the World</term> <note>original: 坤輿全圖 (Kūnyú quántú). A traditional term for a world map, literally "Map of the Earth’s Chariot."</note>, which has been supplementally drawn based on the "Terrestrial Map" from the <term>*Illustrated Gazetteer of the Western Regions*</term> <note>original: 西域圖志 (Xīyù túzhì). This refers to the *Qinding Huangyu Xiyu Tuzhi*, an 18th-century imperial gazetteer of the Qing Empire’s newly incorporated western territories.</note>. That gazetteer uses the two poles as the circular boundaries; the names of the provinces, as well as <term>Xinjiang</term> and the <term>Muslim regions</term> <note>original: 回部 (Huíbù). A historical Qing term for the Turkic-speaking Muslim areas of southern Xinjiang.</note>, are all recorded in detail. This information aligns perfectly with what <term>Youren</term> <note>original: 友仁 (Yǒurén). This is the courtesy name of Giulio Aleni (1582–1649), a Jesuit missionary and scholar known as the "Confucius from the West," who authored influential Chinese-language geographies.</note> stated. I have now selected the place names mentioned by Youren and labeled them; however, because the page is too narrow, I could not record everything in detail. "Asia" is written here as *Yaxiya* <note>original: 亞西亞</note> rather than *Yaxiya* <note>original: 亞細亞</note>, "Persia" as *Boxier* <note>original: 伯西爾</note> rather than *Boxier* <note>original: 伯西兒</note>, and "Peru" as *Bailu* <note>original: 百露</note> rather than *Bolu* <note>original: 伯露</note>. The characters differ but the sounds are similar; these are simply variations in translation. However, certain places mentioned by Youren—such as the Tong River, the <term>Nilu River</term> <note>original: 泥碌河 (Nílù hé). A phonetic rendering of the Nile River.</note>, the <term>Yin River</term> <note>original: 銀河 (Yínhé). Likely the Rio de la Plata, or "River of Silver."</note>, and the <term>Country of Jiduo</term> <note>original: 吉多 (Jíduō). Likely referring to Quito.</note>—are not found in the "Terrestrial Map." Because there is no way to know their exact locations, I have temporarily omitted them for now, pending further investigation.
