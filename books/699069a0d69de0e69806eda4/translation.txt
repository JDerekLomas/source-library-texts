## 2. The Process of Amalgamation.

...one only needed to attach the already existing traits of the pagan evil being to the Christian Devil, whereby the preliminary sketch of the devilish figure received its detailed execution. J. Grimm <note>Jacob Grimm (1785–1863) was a pioneering German scholar of folklore and linguistics; he argued that many Christian traditions have deep roots in older Germanic mythology.</note> did not only make this observation, but in his masterly way proved regarding the Devil that he is Jewish, pagan, and Christian all at once. He grows like an avalanche, which gathers more and more material along the path over which it rolls, only to reach a terrifying size.

A blending of pagan customs with Christian ideas, or pagan conceptions with Christian institutions, lies in the very nature of the developmental process. It cannot be expected that the newly converted immediately penetrated into the essential core of Christian truth, nor from the converters that they should be satisfied with the external confession—baptism and the veneration of the cross; indeed, it is doubtful that the majority of the "apostles to the pagans" were capable of spiritually enlightening their <term>neophytes</term> <note>neophytes: individuals who have recently converted to a new religion or belief system.</note>. From the instructions of Gregory I <note>Pope Gregory the Great (c. 540–604 AD), known for his efforts in organizing the mission to convert Anglo-Saxon England.</note> for his missionary Augustine <note>Augustine of Canterbury, the monk sent by Gregory to England in 597 AD.</note>, it is clear that the early church teachers fundamentally followed a <term>theory of accommodation</term> <note>theory of accommodation: a missionary strategy where Christian practices were adapted to fit existing local cultures and traditions to make the new faith more acceptable.</note>, which necessarily resulted in a process of amalgamation of pagan elements with Christian ones in general, and thus also in relation to the concept of the Devil. In the decree of 601, Gregory the Great exhorted Augustine: not to destroy the pagan temples, but to transform them into Christian ones, and to let the pagans keep their customary feasts, but to use them for the celebration of church dedications and feasts of martyrs.¹

> "Because they (the newly converted Anglo-Saxons) are accustomed to slaughtering many oxen and horses at the festivals of the devils (meaning the old pagan gods), it is absolutely necessary that these celebrations be allowed to continue and be given a different underlying reason. Thus, on the days of church dedication and on the memorial days of the holy martyrs, whose relics are kept in those churches built on the sites of pagan sacrificial groves, a similar celebration should be held; one should mark out a festive area with green branches and organize a church banquet. Yet..."

### Second Section: Development of the Concept of the Devil.

"...one should no longer bring animal sacrifices in honor of Satan, but rather slaughter animals for the praise of God and for the sake of nourishment, and thank the Giver of all good things for the gift."¹ <note>original: "soll man nicht fürder zu Ehren des Satans Thieropfer bringen... und dem Geber alles Guten für die Gabe danken."</note>

The early Church fathers, who fundamentally applied a strategy of leniency during conversion, viewed pagan customs merely as a false path from which they had to divert their new converts. Regarding the practice of oracles, which was deeply rooted among the pagans, it appeared to them as a transition into "Christian tracks" if Christians consulted the Old and New Testaments (<term>sortes sanctorum</term> <note>sortes sanctorum: "Lots of the Saints," a practice of opening the Bible at random to find guidance or predict the future, similar to the Roman "Sortes Virgilianae."</note>) and interpreted a randomly opened biblical passage as an oracular decree for their affairs. It becomes quite clear from Augustine <note>Augustine of Hippo (354–430 AD), a foundational Christian theologian.</note> why the older fathers tolerated this pagan use of the holy books, as he writes in his letter to Januarius:
> "Those indeed who read lots from the pages of the Gospels—and while it is to be hoped that they would rather do this than run to consecrate themselves to demons—nevertheless, this custom also displeases me: wanting to turn divine oracles, which speak of another life, toward secular business and the vanity of this life." <note>original Latin: "Hi vero qui de paginis evangelicis sortes legunt... ad vitae hujus vanitatem propter aliam vitam loquentia oracula divina velle convertere."</note>

Gregory of Tours <note>A Gallo-Roman historian and Bishop (c. 538–594 AD).</note> permitted this kind of prophecy, which he himself did not abhor, to the Christians of his diocese; it was also in full swing among the clergy,² and just how popular it was in general is evident from the fact that it persisted until the 9th century—and secretly among the common people for much longer—despite the protests of the Councils (some of which³ imposed excommunication for it) and despite the laws enacted against it by the Carolingians.⁴ <note>The Carolingians were the Frankish noble family that included Charlemagne.</note>

Even more profound is the instruction for preaching at the first encounter with the pagans, contained in a letter from Bishop Daniel to Boniface.⁵ <note>Saint Boniface (c. 675–754 AD) was the "Apostle to the Germans"; Daniel was the Bishop of Winchester who advised him on missionary tactics.</note> It is circumspect and benevolent, recommending gentleness and moderation, and forbidding provocative mockery so as not to embitter the pagans, but rather to lead them gradually into the bosom of the Christian Church. ### Second Section: Development of the Concept of the Devil.

...[making an image] of Jupiter into one of Saint Peter, while Anna Perenna <note>An ancient Roman goddess of the circle of the year; her festival was held in March.</note> became Saint Anna Petronella, who is still venerated in the Roman Campagna today, and so forth. ¹ Just as many Christian churches in Rome were built from the materials of pagan temples, elements from one sphere of faith were similarly applied to another. The wax figures used in "hellish apparatus" <note>This refers to the tools of sorcery and witchcraft.</note>—which were crafted, pierced, burned, or melted by the servants of the Christian Devil to harm the people they represented—were taken over from pagan sacrificial practices, where the custom had arisen of shaping animals out of dough or wax to offer as sacrifices. ²

Paganism, therefore, was not and could not be eradicated, despite the zeal that later rose against pagan customs in sermons and church council decrees, and despite the *List of Pagan Customs* <note>original: "Indiculus paganiarum," an 8th-century Latin document listing forbidden non-Christian superstitions and sacrifices.</note>, formulas of renunciation, ecclesiastical measures, and state ordinances. Despite all this, pagan ideas were not weeded out from the circle of belief of the converts; they hid under Christian forms, merged with Christian views, and this amalgam filled the believers' horizon. A striking example of this blending of the pagan and the Christian is that of the Danish King *Sweyn Forkbeard* ³ who, during a sea voyage to England, made a triple vow: to the pagan <term>Bragafull</term> <note>A ceremonial "chieftain’s cup" or ritual toast made during Norse feasts where solemn vows were sworn.</note>, to Christ, and to Michael all at once. Another example is that of an Irishman named *Ketil*, who usually called upon Christ, but turned to *Thor* in important matters.
