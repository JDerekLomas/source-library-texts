# Preface to the Southeast Oceans

Wei Yuan states: Among the records of maritime nations, none are more muddled than the "Treatise on Foreign Countries" in the *History of Ming* <note>original: "明史外國傳" (Mingshi Waiguo Zhuan). This was the official history of the preceding Ming dynasty, compiled by the Qing government.</note>. That treatise was finalized during the Qianlong era <note>The reign of the Qianlong Emperor, 1735–1796.</note>, and was based only upon the *Draft History of Ming* and Wang Qi’s *Supplement to the Comprehensive Examination of Literature*. It suffers from three major flaws.

The first flaw is the failure to distinguish between the <term>Western Oceans</term> <note>original: "西洋" (Xiyang). In this context, it refers to the Indian Ocean and the European powers reached via that route.</note> and the <term>Southern Oceans</term> <note>original: "南洋" (Nanyang). Refers to Southeast Asia.</note>. Places like Calicut <note>original: "古里" (Guli), a major port on the southwest coast of India.</note> and the Coromandel Coast <note>original: "瑣里" (Suoli), referring to the Chola Kingdom or the Coromandel region of southeastern India.</note> are all countries near the Southern Oceans, yet they are grouped in the same volume as the Netherlands and the <term>Franks</term> <note>original: "佛郎機" (Folangji). A term derived from "Franks," used by the Chinese to refer first to the Portuguese and later the Spanish.</note>. Italy is located at the furthest western reaches of the Great Ocean, yet it is grouped in the same volume as Cochin <note>original: "柯枝" (Kezhi), in modern Kerala, India.</note> and Bengal. To claim that the "Franks" are near Malacca is like saying the ancient states of Qin and Yue <note>Two states from ancient China located at opposite ends of the known world; an idiom for things that are vastly far apart.</note> share the same mat. This is the first flaw.

The second flaw is the failure to distinguish between island nations and coastal nations. To claim that Brunei <note>original: "淳泥" (Chunni).</note> is the same as Patani <note>original: "大泥" (Dani), a kingdom on the Malay Peninsula.</note> is to move an island onto the coast. To claim that Aceh <note>original: "亞齊" (Yaqi), on the tip of Sumatra.</note> is the same as the <term>Tazik</term> <note>original: "大食" (Dashi). A traditional Chinese term for the Arabs.</note> or Persia is to move the coast onto an island. To claim that Johor is the "Land of the Buddha" <note>Wei Yuan is criticizing the confusion between Johor and India, the birthplace of Buddhism.</note>, or that the "East and West Zhu Islands" are the same as India <note>original: "天竺" (Tianzhu).</note>, or that Panduranga <note>original: "賓童龍" (Bintonglong), a kingdom of the Champa people in modern Vietnam.</note> is Shravasti <note>An ancient Indian city associated with the life of the Buddha.</note>, is to move the "Western Heaven" <note>A common Chinese name for India, particularly in a Buddhist context.</note> into the Eastern Seas. As for Borneo, Java, and the regions below...

He argues that previous scholars incorrectly grouped distant nations together and confused islands with mainland territories.</summary>

With the Ming dynasty, everything changed. Has the destiny of the vast oceans changed along with the map, shifting from the West to the East? In previous eras—regardless of whether it was a time of Great Unification or the periods of fragmented regional regimes like the Eastern Jin, Southern Tang, Southern Song, Qi, or Liang—the history books never ceased to record distant islands sailing across the seas to bring treasures and tribute. They offered tributes of grass-woven garments and animal skins. 

Why is it that today, none of these nations appear at the Imperial Gatherings? <note>original: "王會" (Wanghui). A classical reference to the "Gathering of Princes," referring to the formal assembly of vassal states paying tribute to the Chinese Emperor.</note> It is because the ships of the **Red Barbarians** <note>original: "紅夷" (Hongyi). A historical Chinese term originally referring specifically to the Dutch, but later used more broadly for Europeans with powerful naval artillery.</note> have sailed eastward. Wherever they find a shore, they fight for it; wherever they find an island, they occupy it. They establish forts and trading ports, and set up military defenses. All the vital strategic passages of the **Southern Oceans** <note>The Nanyang, referring to the South China Sea and the surrounding Southeast Asian archipelago.</note> have now entirely become the major hubs of the **Western powers**. 

When the geography and the times change, historical conventions must also change with the world. To record the Southern Oceans is, in reality, a means to record the Western powers. Therefore, I now use the five nations of **Luzon** <note>referring to the Spanish-occupied Philippines</note>, **Holland**, **Portugal** <note>original: "佛郎機" (Fulangji), a transcription of "Frank" used historically for the Portuguese</note>, **England**, and **Prussia** <note>original: "布路亞" (Buluoya)</note> to organize the framework of the Southern Oceans. 

As for the four nations of **Vietnam**, **Siam**, **Burma**, and **Japan**—although they have not yet been swallowed up by the "Western pirates"—I have included them in these chapters because their affairs involve **Maritime Defense**. In the region of **Hue Harbor**, there were originally three countries: one called **Quang Nam** <note>Also known as Cochinchina, the southern region of Vietnam.</note>, one called **Cambodia** <note>Transliterated as *Ganbodie*.</note>, and one with a different name. **Tonkin** <note>The northern region of Vietnam.</note> also includes two small states that are dependencies of Cambodia: **Laos** and **Champa** <note>Transliterated as *Laoshi* and *Qiongba*.</note>. <gloss>Cambodia</gloss>

The territory borders Quang Nam and is likely the **Panduranga** <note>An ancient kingdom of the Cham people, transliterated as *Bintonglong*.</note> mentioned in the *History of the Ming Dynasty*, bordering the ancient Yu with seven hundred and seventy-four cities. These are essentially the small nations along the coast east of Quang Nam.

In the year 1774 <gloss>the 39th year of the Qianlong reign</gloss>, because the King of Quang Nam, Quang Trung <note>Likely referring to the displacement of the Nguyen Lords; however, historical records often distinguish between the Tay Son leader Quang Trung and his rival Nguyen Phuc Anh.</note>, was young and met with disaster, he fled to a sea island with his remaining officials. There he met the French teacher **Pigneau de Behaine** <note>Transliterated as *Atelan*, referring to the Bishop of Adran.</note>. They shared a bond of friendship, and motivated by righteous indignation, they went to France to request military assistance. # Historical Evolution of the Partitioned Kingdoms of Vietnam, Part I

Their customs involve digging homes into the earth to face the sun; their dwellings have no fixed location. # Book of Liang

The kingdom of **Linyi** <note>the early precursor to the Champa kingdom</note> was originally **Xianglin County** <note>"Elephant Forest" County</note> in the **Rinan Commandery** of the **Han Dynasty**. ### Succession and Conflict

In the second year of the Yongchu era of Emperor Gaozu <note>421 CE, during the reign of the founding emperor of the Liu Song dynasty</note>, as recorded in the *Book of Song* <term>Song Shu; the official history of the Liu Song dynasty</term>, King Fan Yangmai of Linyi <note>Champa</note> sent an envoy to present tribute. In response, the imperial court officially confirmed his title and rank. 

In the seventh year of the Yuanjia era <note>430 CE</note>, his son, also named Yangmai, sent an envoy to present a personal petition. He claimed there was discord between his kingdom and the province of Jiaozhou <note>northern Vietnam</note> and begged for imperial leniency and forgiveness. However, in the eighth year <note>431 CE</note>, he reversed his stance and dispatched a fleet of over a hundred "tower ships" <note>large, multi-decked warships</note> to raid Jiude and enter the mouth of the Sihui River. 

Ruan Mizhi, the Governor of Jiaozhou, dispatched Captain Xiang Daosheng with three thousand men to suppress the raid. They attacked the fortress of Qusu City but were unable to capture it and eventually withdrew. Linyi then intended to launch a full-scale invasion of Jiaozhou and requested military assistance from the King of Funan <note>an ancient kingdom centered in the Mekong Delta</note>. However, the King of Funan declined, citing the great distance between them. 

In the tenth year <note>433 CE</note>, Yangmai sent another envoy to present local tribute and a formal memorial requesting that he be allowed to govern the territory of Jiaozhou. The imperial court responded by granting him the title of General of the Soaring Dragon.

### The Campaign of the Yuanjia Era

During the early Yuanjia era of Emperor Taizu <note>Emperor Wen of Song</note>, Linyi continued to violently raid the commanderies of Rinan and Jiude. In the twenty-third year <note>446 CE</note>, the Emperor ordered Tan Hezhi, the Governor of Jiaozhou and General of the Soaring Dragon, to lead a punitive expedition against them. Zong Que, a General of the Grand Marshal’s Office, was placed under Tan Hezhi's command. 

Tan Hezhi sent his adjutant, Xiao Jingxian, to lead the vanguard, with Zong Que serving as his second-in-command. Upon hearing that an army had been dispatched, Yangmai sent a memorial expressing his desire to return the captured citizens of Rinan and to offer national treasures as tribute. 

In the second month, the imperial army reached the garrison at Zhuwu. Although Yangmai spoke of submission, his defensive precautions became even more stringent. Seeing through this, Jingxian and the others advanced toward Qusu City. Yangmai sent both naval and infantry forces to provide immediate relief to the city, but Jingxian defeated these reinforcements. 

The imperial forces launched an all-out assault on the city, and in the fifth month, they successfully captured it. They beheaded the enemy commander and seized a quantity of gold, silver, and various treasures that was impossible to count. Riding the momentum of their victory, they pursued the enemy to the capital of Linyi itself. King Yangmai and his son fled for their lives. The rare and exotic treasures captured by the army were of such quality they had no names; they also melted down "gold people" <note>statues of deities made of precious metal</note>, obtaining several hundred thousand pounds of gold.

### The Conquest of Linyi

Tan Hezhi <note>A general of the Liu Song dynasty</note> dispatched a subsidiary force to resist the invaders, but they were defeated by the rebels. The imperial soldiers were unable to withstand the charge. The men said: "We have heard that the majesty of the lion subdues all hundred beasts." ### Records from the Book of Southern Qi

**The Southern Barbarians: The Kingdom of Linyi**

The Kingdom of Linyi <term>Linyi; an early Chinese name for the Champa kingdom in modern central Vietnam</term> is located three thousand *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters</note> south across the sea from Jiaozhou. To its north, it connects to Jiude. During the Qin dynasty, it was the site of the former Linyi County. At the end of the Han dynasty, its ruler declared himself King. 

In the fifth year of the Taikang era of the Jin dynasty <note>284 CE</note>, the kingdom first sent tribute to the imperial court. During the Jianxing era <note>313–317 CE</note>, Fan Zhi, a leader of the Rinan barbarians, had a servant named Wen. Wen had worked as a merchant and witnessed the systems and institutions of the "Upper Kingdom" <note>China</note>. He taught the King of Linyi, Fan Yi, how to construct walled cities, moats, towers, and palaces. 

The King wears a "celestial crown" <note>likely a mitre or tiara influenced by Hindu-Buddhist styles</note> resembling a Buddha’s crown. His body is draped with fragrant tassels and necklaces. The people are fierce and hardy, well-acquainted with their mountains and rivers, and skilled in combat. They blow conch shells to serve as horns. The people go mostly naked. The climate is warm throughout the four seasons, with neither frost nor snow. They value women and hold men in low esteem. Dark skin is considered a mark of beauty; this is true of all the various southern kingdoms. 

At Qu Su City, when an eight-foot gnomon <note>A vertical pillar used to measure the sun's shadow for geographic and calendrical calculations</note> is erected, the sun's shadow at noon measures eight inches to the south. From Linyi, traveling south-southwest for over three thousand *li*, one reaches the kingdom of Funan <note>An ancient Indianized kingdom centered in the Mekong Delta</note>.

# Illustrated Treatise on the Maritime Kingdoms, Volume 16

->By Wei Yuan<-

Since the eras of the kings Fan, Hu, Feng, and Shi <note>Successive rulers or clans of the Linyi/Champa region</note>, and since the era of the Qin dynasty descendants who migrated there, the people have been colored by barbarian transformation. The ancient customs of Rinan <term>Rinan; an ancient Chinese commandery in central Vietnam, literally meaning "South of the Sun"</term> have changed and vanished entirely.

The people dwell in nests in the trees or stay in mountain lodgings. Their settlements border the mountains, surrounded by brambles, reeds, and thickets. The forests rise to brush the clouds, and a deep, dark mist hangs perpetually; it is not a place where civilized men would feel at ease. In the city of Qu Su, they established an eight-foot gnomon <note>A vertical pillar used to measure the sun's shadow for astronomical calculations</note>. The shadow of the sun measured eight inches to the south. Because the shadow falls to the south of the gnomon, the commandery was named "South of the Sun." The Northern Star <note>Polaris</note> appears low, almost dissolving into the horizon. They say that because the sun is to the north, they "open their northern doors to face the sun." This is the general nature of the place.

During the Han dynasty, Zhang Zhong of Rinan was recommended for office and entered the capital, Luoyang. During the New Year's Day assembly, Emperor Ming asked him: "Is it true that in Rinan, people face north to look at the sun?" 

Zhang Zhong replied: "In the empire today, there are commanderies named Yunzhong <note>'Within the Clouds'</note> and Jincheng <note>'Golden City'</note>, yet they do not necessarily have clouds or gold in reality. The sun likewise rises in the east. As for the climate, it is warm and humid. When the sun is directly overhead, the officials and commoners build their dwellings according to their own convenience, facing east, west, south, or north. There is no fixed orientation."

The nature of the people is fierce and brave in battle. They are comfortable in the mountains and skilled on the water, but they are not practiced in fighting on flat ground. As the ancients said, the Five Ridges <note>The mountain ranges separating Central China from the Southern coast</note> are what Heaven used to separate the internal from the external. How much more so for this land, which lies at the end of a long journey across the sea? Looking back at the Nine Ridges, it is even more remote. It is no longer just a matter of a long and difficult road; it is truly a dark and mysterious realm of the wilderness.

In the second year of the Jianyuan era <note>344 CE</note>, King Fan Wen of Linyi <term>Linyi; an early kingdom of the Cham people in central Vietnam</term> attacked the commanderies of Rinan, Jiuzhen, and Jiude. The people fled, and for a thousand *li*, no cooking smoke was seen. He then moved the capital of the King of Linyi. 

This city lies two thousand five hundred *li* southwest of Guangzhou. At the southwest corner of the city, high mountains and long ridges stretch toward the heavens. To the north of the White Ridge, it connects to a ravine and a great headwater. The Huai River <note>Original: "淮水"; likely referring to a local river in central Vietnam such as the Perfume River, rather than the major Huai River in central China</note> emerges from the Nana Da boundary. It passes through three layers of long islands and hidden mountains to the west, and passes East Heng Mountain. To the south of that ridge, the headwaters of the Small Huai River emerge from the Songgen boundary. It flows through the upper mountains and hidden valleys, winding around the southern bends and passing eastward to join the main Huai flow, eventually pouring into Dian Prefecture. 

The western edge of the city borders the mountains, while the northeast side faces the water. The waters of the Chongzheng and Liupu rivers encircle the city walls. To the southeast, outside the moat, the perimeter is eight *li*. To the west, the ridges are high and long. The city is narrow from north to south; the northern edge and southern end curve inward, with a total circumference of eight *li*.
