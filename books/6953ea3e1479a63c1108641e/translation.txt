### Rules of Use

We are proud of our partnership with libraries, through which we digitize public domain books and make them accessible to everyone. These books belong to all of humanity, and we are merely their custodians. Nevertheless, digitizing books and supporting this project is costly; therefore, to continue providing this resource, we have taken certain measures to prevent the commercial use of these books. One of these is technical restrictions on automated queries.

We also ask you:

*   **Do not use the files for commercial purposes.** We developed the Google Book Search program for all users; therefore, please use these files only for personal, non-commercial purposes.
*   **Do not send automated queries.** Do not send automated queries of any kind to the Google system. If you require access to large volumes of text for research in the fields of machine translation, optical character recognition <note>OCR</note>, or other similar purposes, please contact us. For these purposes, we strongly recommend using exclusively public domain materials.
*   **Do not remove Google logos and other attributes from the files.** The images in each file are marked with Google logos to tell readers about our project and help them find additional materials. Do not remove them.
*   **Comply with the laws of your country and others.** Ultimately, you bear full responsibility for your actions—so please ensure that you are not violating the relevant laws of your country or other countries. Keep in mind that even if a book is no longer under copyright protection in the USA, it does not necessarily mean it can be distributed in other countries. Unfortunately, intellectual property legislation is very diverse, and there is no universal way to determine how a book may be used in a specific country. Do not assume that if a book appears in Google Book Search, it can be used anywhere or in any way. The penalties for copyright infringement can be very serious.

# TO THE ILLUSTRIOUS JUNIO
## BOBALI, SON OF ANDREW,
## OF RAGUSA
### GIAMBATTISTA DELLA PORTA OF NAPLES
### SENDS FONDEST GREETINGS.

<image-desc>At the top of the page, there is a decorative horizontal border composed of three rows of repeating geometric and floral motifs. The main text body begins with a large square decorative woodcut initial 'A' featuring foliate scrollwork.</image-desc>

**A**s for the other daughter who remained at home, Illustrious Junio, I could scarcely restrain her once she was stirred up and rushing toward you. The immense praises which her sister <note>The "sister" refers to the earlier, shorter version of this work; the "other daughter" is this expanded edition.</note> brought back concerning her husband filled her soul with such noble envy that, having broken every bond of restraint and modesty, she cannot control herself. She cries out that she desires no other man but you; if she were denied to you, she would rather grow old in her own home than risk the danger of belonging to others. Indeed, if they are compared to one another, she does not seem less worthy than her sister. If the former searches out causes, this one produces the effects; if the former contemplates, this one—being active enough—proposes that those things which fell under contemplation should come into the light of men. And if the former is proud of her discovery, this one is no less ambitious for her portentous operations. In these, not only is the mind nourished by understanding, but all the senses of the body are also enticed and refreshed by the results. This Magic, therefore, excels all other sciences (I except divine Philosophy, however) so that the other arts and faculties seem to serve her as if she were a queen. Cicero writes: *No one among the Persians was accustomed to obtain the kingdom who had not first mastered Magic.* <note>original: "Neminem apud Persas regno potire solitum, qui prius Magiam non percepisset."</note> And Plato in the *Alcibiades* says: *The sons of Persian Kings are imbued with it, so that...*

<summary>Giambattista della Porta dedicates his expanded work on Natural Magic to the Ragusan nobleman Junio Bobali, personifying the book as a "daughter" eager to meet her patron. He argues that practical magic is superior to mere contemplation because it produces tangible effects and was historically required for Persian kings.</summary>

# DEDICATORY EPISTLE
->TO THE READER<-

*...a model of the worldly Republic, and may learn to administer and distribute their own Republic accordingly. Why should I mention those most famous throughout almost the entire world—Pythagoras, Empedocles, Apollonius of Tyana, and Plato—who burned with such a desire for this [art] that they traveled across the globe to learn it, undertaking what were more like exiles than mere voyages? Having returned, they preached it and held it among their secret mysteries. Why mention the Magi Kings <note>The Three Wise Men or Magi of the Nativity</note> coming to adore Christ, and many others renowned in this art, with whose names the monuments of the ancients are filled? But who will justly weigh my labors, vigils, and expenses, which I have spent in adorning this daughter of mine <note>A metaphor for the book itself</note>, so that she might one day seem worthy of you; lest, in the emulation of noble works, she should waste away from envy of her sister? The sum of it is, since she is not inferior to her sister, she disdains to marry an inferior man. For you, born of the same blood, adorned with the same gifts of Nature and Fortune, and of the same character of mind and excellence of speech. If in one there is the magnificence of Alexander <note>Alexander the Great</note>, in the other is that of Caesar. If in one is the strength of the Fabii, in the other is that of the Scipiones <note>Famous families of Roman generals and statesmen</note>: one is not so much wise as the other is prudent, and finally, to put it in a word, your integrity of character, incredible humanity, and your other virtues so strive among themselves: that while one strives to surpass the other, in this noble contest of distinguished virtues, each one surpassing itself crowns you with all their pomp and triumph. I myself, who am compelled to confess these things, and since nothing is more desired in my prayers, I obey not unwillingly. I send her to you. But in such great love, do not forget me. Farewell, and give greetings in my name to your brothers, Marino and Michael.*

<summary>The author justifies the prestige of his work by citing ancient philosophers and the Magi, ultimately presenting the book as a "daughter" worthy of the dedicatee's high noble character. He concludes with personal greetings to the dedicatee and his brothers.</summary>
