# Disputation
## ON EMPHYTEUTIC LAW.
### First Thesis.

**E**mphyteusis <term>emphyteusis: a type of long-term or perpetual lease of land where the tenant is required to improve the property</term>, according to the reasoning of its name and its etymology, we believe is derived from the Greek <note>original: ἀπὸ τοῦ ἐμφυτεύειν (*apo tou emphyteuein*)</note>, which signifies to engraft or to plant, and by metaphor, to improve; because through emphyteusis—that is, through grafting and planting—fields are made better.

Wesembeck in *Paratitla* <note>a summary of the Digest of Roman Law</note> on the title "if a field is sought as a tribute-paying land," note 2. Alvaro Valasco in *Thesaurus on Emphyteutic Law*, question 2, note 4. Sichardus on the Code title "on emphyteutic law," note 4. He who receives an emphyteusis is called an *emphyteuta* or an *emphyteuticarius*. The established law is called emphyteutic or emphyteuticarian law. See Code, laws 1 and 2, "on emphyteutic law"; see also Donellus, book 9 of *Commentaries on Civil Law*, chapter 13.

<margin>2</margin>
Emphyteusis is therefore (a) a contract, (b) of the law of nations <term>law of nations: *ius gentium*, a legal system traditionally considered common to all peoples</term>, (c) named, (d) of good faith <term>good faith: *bonae fidei*, a contract where the parties are judged by standards of fairness rather than just the strict letter of the law</term>, (e) established by consent alone, (f) by which an owner agrees with someone to grant his estate in perpetuity, or for a time; in exchange for an annual payment (g) to be provided in recognition of ownership (h), for the purpose of cultivation (i) and enjoyment.

<gloss>a) According to the definition of the most distinguished Lord Teacher, Dr. Harpprecht, in his *Commentary on the Institutes*, title on leasing, § 3, note 4. Schardius in *Legal Lexicon* under the word *emphyteusis*.</gloss> <gloss>(b) Code, laws 1 and 2, "on emphyteutic law," and by the argument of the law *Labeo* in the Digest "on the significance of words."</gloss> <gloss>(c) Argued from the Digest, law 5 and law 9, "on justice and law," with Aristotle as a witness in book 2 of the *Economics*. # *On the Law of Emphyteusis.*

Regarding the Church of Constantinople, religious places, etc., Conrad Ritters states in *Commentary on the Novels, part 1, chapter 8, number 56*, that this limitation has no relevance to our modern customs. (b) For instance, a lease for 10, 20, 30, or more years, *Law the last, Digest, if a taxpayer's field is sought; and Cujas in his commentary on the Pandects <note>original: "π." - The Digest or Pandects of Roman law</note>, Novel 120, chapter 5.* Or it can be granted for the life of the person granting it, or for two or three generations by agreement. Nor does it cease to be a true emphyteusis <note>A long-term or perpetual lease of land for a fixed rent</note>, even if it is established in these ways. For although the natural conditions of the contract may be modified, the legal scholars commonly teach that the force and name of the contract itself are not removed. *Valasco, Practical Tract, question 1, number 12.*

<margin>9</margin> When established in perpetuity <note>forever</note>, if it is civil <note>governed by secular law</note>, it passes to any heirs whatsoever; but if it is Ecclesiastical, it passes only to descendants. This also holds true in an emphyteusis contracted for a specific time, though this does not exceed the predefined time limit.

*Argument from Novel 55, chapter 2; Novel 120, chapter 6; see Clarus, on the section "Emphyteusis," question 28; Borcholten on Fiefs, chapter 3, number 14; Obrecht, Disputation 36, thesis 90 and following.* This is the commonly held rule. Furthermore, an Ecclesiastical emphyteusis can be contracted with the proper solemnity <note>legal formalities</note> so that it may pass even to outside heirs. For it is a matter of established law that church property, provided the required formalities are observed, can be fully alienated <note>transferred or sold</note>, *Clementines, 1 & 2, regarding things of the Church not to be alienated.* Why then should it not be possible for <gloss>this</gloss> lease to be granted in emphyteusis? This is an argument from the greater to the lesser, since the ownership of the property remains with the Church. *See Dr. Bocer, said Disputation, thesis 34 and following; Dr. Harpprecht, said section 3, number 160; Clarus, said place, question 6, number 4.* He calls this the common opinion, and *Trentacinquius, in the preface of the cited place, number 4*, explains it elegantly.

<margin>10</margin> Furthermore, an emphyteusis is established not only by a will [a] and by agreement [b], but also by prescription [c], as if from the unspoken consent of the owner and the possessor. For example, if someone has possessed a certain field as an emphyteutic property for a legal period of 30 or 40 years (namely, by paying the <term>Canon<note>The fixed annual rent paid by an emphyteutic tenant to the landowner.</note></term> to the owner every year). [c]
