# Peony

The *Flower Manual* by Jia Dan <note>Jia Dan (730–805) was a renowned geographer and scholar of the Tang Dynasty</note> states that the people of the Tang called the peony the **Woody Herbaceous Peony** <term>Mushaoyao (木芍藥): a name used to distinguish the woody tree peony from its soft-stemmed herbaceous relatives</term>. Master Ou’s <note>Referring to Ouyang Xiu (1007–1072), the celebrated Song Dynasty statesman and author of the first monograph on peonies</note> *Peony Treatise* lists over ninety varieties; furthermore, nineteen varieties were once presented as tribute from Yi Prefecture <note>Modern-day Yi County, Hebei</note>. In the present day, the names and categories of this flower are even more numerous.

Master Su <note>Referring to the famous poet and polymath Su Dongpo (1037–1101)</note> says: "The method for viewing peonies is that it must be done before noon. Once noon has passed, the blossoms become limp and lose their vitality." The propagation of this flower through division and grafting should be performed in the ninth month of autumn, while transplanting should be done during the flowering season. <note>While modern gardening usually avoids transplanting during bloom, traditional Chinese manuals often recorded specific windows of time believed to ensure the plant's aesthetic success or vital energy.</note>

Shu Yuanyu <note>A Tang Dynasty official known for his literary skill</note> composed the *Rhapsody on the Peony*, and Qiu Xuan authored the *Record of the Honor and Disgrace of the Peony* <note>A work that metaphorically graded peony varieties as if they were court officials</note>.

# Herbaceous Peony

The herbaceous peony <note>original: 芍藥, *Shaoyao* (Paeonia lactiflora)</note> grows in the river valleys and hills of the Central Peak <note>Mount Song, one of the Five Sacred Mountains of China, located in Henan Province</note>. Today, it can be found everywhere, though the specimens from the Huainan region <note>The area between the Huai and Yangtze Rivers</note> are considered superior.

In spring, the plant produces red buds that grow in clusters. Each stem bears three branches and five leaves; these leaves resemble those of the tree peony but are narrower and longer. The plant stands about one to two feet high. In the summer, it blossoms in several colors, including red, white, and purple. Its seeds are similar to those of the tree peony, only smaller. The roots are harvested in autumn and come in two varieties: red and white. 

Cui Bao <note>A scholar of the Jin Dynasty (266–420 AD)</note> states in his *Notes on Things Past and Present* <note>original: *Gujin Zhu*</note>: 
"There are two types of peonies: the **herbaceous peony** and the **woody peony**. The woody variety has larger flowers and deeper colors. While the common people call this 'tree peony' <note>original: 牡丹, *Mudan*</note>, that name is technically incorrect." <note>Historically, Chinese botanists debated whether the herbaceous and tree peonies were variations of the same plant or entirely different species. Cui Bao represents a school of thought that sought to unify them under the name *Shaoyao*.</note>

The plant is also known by the following names: 
* **Heli** <note>meaning 'Why Parting'—traditionally, lovers gave this flower to one another when saying goodbye</note> 
* **Baizhu** <note>meaning 'White Essence' or 'White Root'</note> 
* **Yurong** <note>meaning 'Lingering Countenance'</note> 
* **Lishi** <note>meaning 'Plough-Food'</note> 
* **Jiecang** <note>meaning 'Untying the Granary'</note> 
* **Ding** <note>referring to a metal rod or ingot, likely describing the straight shape of the root</note>

# Chrysanthemum

In the *Tang Materia Medica* <note>original: *Tang Bencao*; the world's first state-sponsored pharmacopoeia, compiled in 659 CE</note>, the chrysanthemum is also called "Lady’s Joint" <note>original: 女節, *Nüjie*</note> or "Lady’s Splendor" <note>original: 女華, *Nühua*</note>. Liu Meng’s *Chrysanthemum Manual* <note>original: *Liu Meng Jupu*; a Northern Song dynasty treatise</note> lists thirty-five varieties, and elsewhere another thirty-two. Fan Shihu’s <note>the literary name of Fan Chengda (1126–1193), a famous Song dynasty poet and horticulturalist</note> *Chrysanthemum Manual* records seventy-two types. Today, there is more than just one kind.

The **Sweet Chrysanthemum** <note>original: 甘菊, *Ganju*; likely *Chrysanthemum indicum*</note> has purple stems; its aroma is fragrant and its taste is sweet. Its flowers are deep yellow with single-layered petals. Those that possess a thin membrane-like sheath at the base of the flower <note>referring to the involucral bracts or calyx</note> are considered the genuine variety. It is excellent to harvest the flowers to make cakes or to brew them with sugar to create a beverage.

There are also the "Mandarin Duck" Chrysanthemum, the "Fifth-Month" Chrysanthemum, and the "Sixth-Month" Chrysanthemum. The poet Lu Guimeng <note>a Tang Dynasty scholar-recluse</note> composed the *Rhapsody on Wolfberry and Chrysanthemum* <note>original: 杞菊賦, *Qiju Fu*; a famous essay on the simple life of a scholar</note>. Zeng Duanbo <note>a Song Dynasty writer known for categorizing flowers as different types of friends</note> regarded the chrysanthemum as a "Fine Friend" <note>original: 佳友, *Jiayou*</note>.

# Orchid
<note>original: 蘭, *Lan*; specifically referring to the *Cymbidium* genus, which has been cultivated in China for over a millennium as a symbol of nobility and integrity.</note>

The Orchid is also known as the **Jade Paving-stone Flower** <note>original: 玉甃花, *Yuzhouhua*</note>. With leaves shaped like the spine of a sword, slender and long, those with purple flowers are the authentic variety from **Fujian**. The **Jiangxi Orchid** has leaves that are short and glossy, with white flowers.

There is also the **Pearl Orchid**, which has a large main stem and flowers resembling the standard orchid. There is also the **Forked Orchid** <note>original: 丫蘭, *Yalan*</note>; the Forked Orchid comes from **Guangdong**. There are also the **Winter Orchid**, the **Red Orchid**, and the **Hanging Orchid**. 

The Hanging Orchid is also called the **Wind Orchid** <note>original: 風蘭, *Fenglan*; likely an epiphytic variety</note>; it grows without soil. It is kept in small baskets and hung from trees. People call it the **"Immortal Herb"**; it has delicate flowers and a subtle fragrance.

Regarding the Orchid, **Shangu** <note>Shangu is the pseudonym of Huang Tingjian (1045–1105), a famous Song Dynasty poet and calligrapher</note> said: "That which has one stalk and one flower with an abundance of fragrance is the **Orchid** <note>original: 蘭, *Lan*</note>; that which has one stalk with several flowers and a less potent fragrance is the **Hui** <note>original: 蕙, *Hui*; often identified as *Cymbidium faberi*</note>." This statement captures the true essence of the Orchid.

The *Guangzhi* <note>An ancient gazetteer/encyclopedia of natural history</note> states: The *Hui* has green leaves and purple flowers; **Emperor Wu of Wei** <note>Cao Cao (155–220 AD), the famous general and statesman of the Three Kingdoms period</note> used it as incense for burning. 

The **Marsh Orchid** <note>original: 澤蘭, *Zelan*; likely *Lycopus lucidus* or a species of *Eupatorium*, historically categorized near orchids due to their fragrance</note> is also called the **Tiger Rush** <note>original: 虎蒲, *Hupu*</note>. The *Commentary* states: The Marsh Orchid is wide and long, its joints are red in the center, and it grows four to five feet high.

# Lotus

The **Lotus** <note>original: 荷, *He*</note> is also known as *Handan* <note>a name for the lotus flower before it opens</note> and is also called the *Shuifuqu* <note>an ancient name for the aquatic plant</note>. There are "thousand-petaled" varieties in yellow, white, and red. Some have red edges with a white center. There is the "Horse-Hoof" variety, which produces many large seeds, and the "Stacked Lotus"; both are considered excellent breeds.

On the summit of Mount Hua, there is a pond where "thousand-petaled" lotuses grow. It is said that if one consumes them, they will undergo **feathering** <note>original: 羽化, *yuhua*; a Daoist term for achieving immortality and ascending to the heavens</note>. This is what the poet Zheng Gu referred to in his verse: "The Jade Well Lotus on the summit of Great Hua."

In the South Seas, there are **water lilies** <note>original: 睡蓮, *shuilian*, literally "sleeping lotus"</note>; they rise at dawn to face the sun and sink low into the water at night. In years with abundant water, the lotus will bloom early. Jie Duanbo <note>likely referring to a scholar who categorized floral "friendships"</note> regarded the lotus as the **"Pure Friend."**

There are also varieties such as the Golden Lotus, the Velvet-thread Lotus, and the White-flowered *Taiyi* Lotus, which is very difficult to bring to bloom. Its stalk resembles a plantain and its leaves are like those of the taro; it is also called the **"Guanyin Taro"** <note>referring to *Alocasia*, named after the Bodhisattva of Compassion</note>. Some say the "Blue Lotus" is actually the Velvet-thread Lotus. During the Jin dynasty, the monk **Fotudeng** <note>a famous Buddhist master known for his miracle-working</note> once took a begging bowl, filled it with water, and burned incense while reciting an incantation; a Blue Lotus grew from the bowl, its radiance and color dazzling to all. The lotus typically blooms during the fourth and fifth months <note>of the lunar calendar</note>.

# Narcissus

The **Narcissus** <note>original: 水仙, *Shuixian*, literally "Water Immortal"</note> with a single layer of petals is known as the **Golden Cup on a Silver Stand** <note>original: 金盞銀臺, *jinzhan yintai*; referring to the yellow central cup and white outer petals</note>. There is also a variety with "a thousand petals" <note>a double-flowered variety</note> whose flower segments are curled, wrinkled, and densely packed; these are light yellow below and pale white above. This is the true Narcissus; its color is white and its fragrance is beautiful and lovely.

The poet **Shangu** <note>Huang Tingjian (1045–1105), a celebrated Song Dynasty poet known for his appreciation of the Narcissus</note> wrote in a poem:
> "When shall it be carried up to the **Purple Imperial Palace** <note>the most secluded part of the Emperor's residence</note> to be ranked alongside the court’s plum blossoms?"

## Jasmine

Jasmine <note>original: 茉莉, *mòlì*; specifically *Jasminum sambac*</note> is found throughout the regions of Guangzhou and Fujian. There are yellow and red varieties which are used to grow garden hedges. People in the Zhejiang and Zhili <note>Zhili refers to the administrative region surrounding the capital, modern-day Hebei and Beijing</note> regions can keep the plants for two or three years; however, they are only afraid of the east wind <note>In traditional Chinese gardening, the east wind was often associated with the unpredictable chills of early spring that could kill tender southern plants</note>.

The great poet Su Dongpo <note>Su Shi (1037–1101), a renowned Song dynasty polymath</note> regarded it as "Hidden Musk," while Zeng Duanbo <note>A Song dynasty scholar who famously assigned "ranks" and "friends" to various flowers</note> considered it a "Refined Friend."

According to the *Collected Talks on Investigating Things* <note>original: *Gewu Conghua*, a Ming dynasty compendium of natural philosophy and botanical lore</note>, the surface of the jasmine leaf is slightly wrinkled and lacks serrated edges. The plant by nature prefers warm soil, and southerners cultivate it in garden beds. Its blooming period occurs between summer and autumn, beginning to flourish most beautifully during the sixth and seventh months <note>Approximately July and August in the lunar calendar</note>.

## Flowering Crabapple <note>original: *Haitang*</note>

Flowering crabapples from Henan are considered the finest. Li Zanhuang <note>Li Deyu (787–850), a prominent Tang Dynasty statesman</note> states in his *Record of Flowers and Trees* that all contemporary plants with the character "sea" <note>original: *hai*</note> in their names originate from across the seas. This variety of *Tang* <note>a term for various species of wild apple or pear</note> is named "Sea" for this reason. There are three primary varieties: the "Stuck-to-the-Stem" <note>original: *Tiegeng*, often identified as the Flowering Quince</note>, the "West Mansion" <note>original: *Xifu*</note>, and the "Drooping Thread" <note>original: *Chuisi*</note>. They are highly praised in the Western Prefecture of Shu <note>modern-day Sichuan</note>, which is why the "West Mansion" variety earned its name.

The scholar Peng Yuancai once listed his "Five Regrets," one of which was that the flowering crabapple has no fragrance; only those found in Changzhou are said to be uniquely scented. The poet Zimei <note>the courtesy name of Du Fu (712–770), one of China’s most revered poets</note> never included poems about the crabapple in his collected works because his mother’s name was "Haitang" <note>It was a traditional Chinese custom to avoid using the characters of a parent’s name out of filial respect</note>.

Emperor Minghuang <note>Emperor Xuanzong of the Tang Dynasty</note> once compared the flower to his beloved consort, Taizhen <note>the famous beauty Yang Guifei</note>. When Taizhen was supported and led into his presence, not yet fully awake from her slumber, the Emperor laughed and remarked, "This is merely a flowering crabapple who has not yet had her fill of sleep."

If one wishes for the flowering crabapple to bloom with fresh and abundant flowers, it is best to wash the base of the roots with water mixed with distiller’s grains <note>the fermented byproduct of making rice wine</note> early on the day of the Winter Solstice. This method is also effective for the "Iron-stalk" <note>original: *Tiegeng*</note> variety. In the *Chronicle of the Peony’s Honor and Disgrace*, the double-petaled crabapple is referred to as the "Noble Lady of Flowers" <note>original: *Hua Mingfu*</note>, and the multi-petaled variety is called the "Imperial Relative of Flowers" <note>original: *Hua Qili*</note>. Jia Dan <note>a Tang Dynasty geographer and official</note> regarded it as the "Celestial Immortal among Flowers," while Zeng Duanbo considered it a "Famous Friend."

# Osmanthus Flower

The Osmanthus is also known as the <term>qin tree <gloss>qinmu</gloss></term> <note>An ancient name for the tree, often associated with its medicinal bark</note>. <term>Lu Dian</term> <note>A prominent Song Dynasty scholar (1042–1102) and author of the *Pi Ya*, an early Chinese dictionary of flora and fauna</note> stated: "The leaves of most trees have a single central midrib, yet the Osmanthus alone has three veins." In <term>Boluo</term> <note>A region in modern-day Guangdong province</note>, there are Osmanthus trees used to brew wine.

The <term>Osmanthus grub <gloss>guiduan</gloss></term> <note>An insect larva that lives within the wood of the tree</note> is shaped like a small mouse and surreptitiously feeds on honey. ## Azalea

The **Azalea** <note>original: 杜鵑, *Dujuan*</note> is also known as the <term>Mountain Pomegranate <gloss>shanshiliu</gloss></term>, the <term>Mountain Wanderer <gloss>shanzhizhu</gloss></term> <note>Literally "mountain hesitation," referencing a belief that sheep hesitate to eat the plant due to its toxicity</note>, or the <term>Mountain-Reflecting Red <gloss>yingshanhong</gloss></term>. Its nature is to fear heat and delight in the shade; it is not afraid of frost or snow. From early summer until late autumn, it is appropriate to water it daily with river water. 

There is a variety known as the **Mountain Azalea** <note>original: 山鵑, *shanjuan*</note> which has large flowers and sparse leaves. It blooms one month earlier than others. # Lily

There are three types of lily. One is called the **Sandalwood Lily**; its bulbs <note>original: 子, *zi*; literally "children," referring here to the fleshy scales of the bulb</note> can be cooked or steamed for food, and it benefits the vital energy <term>Qi: the internal life force or "breath" in traditional Chinese medicine</term>.

The Dubo <note>A historical tribe located in the northern regions near the Sayan Mountains, ancestors to the Tuvan people</note> do not know how to farm or sow crops. Their lands are rich in lilies, so they gather the roots to use as their primary food source <note>original: 糧, *liang*; grain or rations</note>.

# Magnolia Lily <gloss>Also known as the Wood-Brush</gloss>

The Magnolia Lily grows in the river valleys of Hanzhong <note>a region in modern Shaanxi province</note>, though today it is found in many places. The tree reaches a height of several *zhang* <note>a traditional unit of length; one *zhang* is approximately 3 meters or 10 feet</note>. Its leaves are similar to those of the persimmon tree, but are longer in shape.

In the first and second lunar months, it produces flowers that resemble small, fuzzy peaches. They are white in color with purple sepals at the base. When these flowers fall, they do not produce seeds. In the summer, the tree blooms a second time. When the buds first emerge, they look like the tip of a calligraphy brush; for this reason, people in the north call it the "Wood-Brush flower." 

There is another variety with branches and leaves that appear identical, yet it only blooms once per year. When its flowers fall in the fourth lunar month, it produces seeds resembling "love-seeds" <note>original: *xiangsizi* (相思子), the small, bright red seeds often associated with yearning or remembrance</note>. Some suggest that these are actually the same species, but that the tree must be ten or twenty years old before it begins to bear fruit. 

The timing of the flowering—whether early or late—depends on the seasonal climate and the warmth or cold of the northern and southern regions. In the ninth lunar month, the buds are harvested and dried in the sun for use <note>likely referring to their use in traditional medicine to treat sinus issues</note>. It is also known by the names *Xinshen* <note>original: 辛矧, meaning "Pungent Gums"</note>, *Houtao* <note>original: 侯桃, "Monkey Peach"</note>, and *Fangmu* <note>original: 房木, "Chamber Wood"</note>.

## Sweet Flag <note>original: *Changpu* (菖蒲)</note>

When the Sweet Flag is cultivated upon stones, it is called the "Stone Sweet Flag" <note>original: *Shichangpu* (石菖蒲)</note>. However, it may also be grown within containers made of broken pottery or porcelain. It is advisable to store plenty of "Yellow Plum water" <term>Yellow Plum water: Rainwater collected during the early summer rainy season when plums ripen, believed to be particularly nourishing for plants.</term> for irrigation. 

The specific method for its care is summarized as: "Spring stripping, summer pruning, autumn dewing, and winter sheltering." 

"Stripping" refers to plucking away the yellowed tips of the leaves after the Spring Equinox. 

"Pruning" involves cutting the old leaves back to the root on the fourteenth day of the fourth month; this allows the new leaves to emerge fine and slender. 

"Dewing" means allowing the plant to receive the autumn dew, which makes it lush and vibrant green. 

"Sheltering" means that after the First Frost, the plant should be covered with an overturned vat on the ground, ensuring that no mice are permitted to enter.

## Trumpet Creeper <note>original: *Lingxiaohua* (凌霄花)</note>

The **Trumpet Creeper** is the same as the **Chinese Trumpet Vine** <note>original: *Ziwei* (紫葳); *Campsis grandiflora*.</note>. Its blossoms are a reddish-yellow and are at their most abundant in the middle of summer.

### Golden Stalk Flower

The **Golden Stalk Flower** <note>original: *Jinjinghua* (金莖花)</note> is also known by the name **Galangal** <note>original: *Gaoliangjiang* (高良薑).
