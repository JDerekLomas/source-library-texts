### Researcher’s Note on the Illustration
The central feature of this folio is a large botanical drawing of an unidentified plant. It possesses a single, prominent <term>reniform</term> (kidney-shaped) leaf that is bright green with a distinct cleft at the base. The plant’s root system is unusual, depicted as a cluster of rounded, pebble-like <term>nodules</term> or bulbs.

Rising from this root is a slender, straight stem that terminates in a large, white <term>campanulate</term> (bell-shaped) flower. The flower is five-lobed and sits within a simple green <term>calyx</term> (the protective outer layer of a flower bud). 

<note>original: "peltate leaf"</note>
<note>The plant does not correspond to a known botanical species, though some scholars have compared the leaf shape to certain types of water lilies or asarum, despite the contradictory root and flower structures.</note>

# The Earth-Creeper with Azure Stars

The essence of this plant begins within its thick, knotted <term>rhizome</term> <note>a horizontal underground stem that sends out roots and shoots at intervals</note>, which crawls through the dark soil like a slumbering serpent. From this sturdy foundation, the vitality of the earth flows upward, branching into twin stalks that seek the light of the high meadows.

They are deeply divided into five distinct lobes, each serrated at the edge to catch the morning dew. These green canopies provide shade for the lower stem, ensuring the vital juices do not wither under the heat of the noon sun.

At the crown of each stalk, the plant offers its most precious gift: clusters of small, five-petaled flowers of a deep cerulean hue. These blossoms <note>the blue color often suggested a cooling property in medieval herbalism</note> are marked at their centers with golden dust, attracting the tiny winged messengers of the garden.

<margin>Gather the blossoms when the moon is in its second quarter for the strongest tincture.</margin>

To prepare the remedy, one must carefully unearth the <term>rhizome</term>, washing away the clinging earth with clear spring water. The root is then sliced thinly and dried until it becomes as hard as horn. When ground into a fine powder and mixed with wine, it is said to soothe the fires of the blood and bring tranquility to a restless mind.

The stalks themselves should be avoided, for their sap is bitter and may cause the skin to redden. Only the root and the azure stars hold the true virtue of the herb. <insert>Some healers suggest adding a drop of honey to the decoction.</insert>

Let the seeker find this herb in the damp shadows of the forest’s edge, where the soil remains moist even in the height of summer. It is a quiet dweller, unassuming in its stature but profound in its healing power.
