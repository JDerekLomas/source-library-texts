# To the Magnanimous Lorenzo de' Medici

Filippo Valori <note>Filippo Valori (1456–1494) was a Florentine politician and a close associate of the Medici family.</note> commends himself most highly to the magnanimous **Lorenzo de' Medici**, his patron and the savior of his fatherland.

Since order and rashness cannot be in harmony with one another, surely no one would think that fate and fortune could have any concord between them. Indeed, in fate, there is a necessary order of things; in fortune, however, there is rashness. But truly, contrary to everyone’s opinion, these two things—which otherwise are always at odds—seem at last to have conspired in me alone for my downfall.

For this year, fate threatened me with a heavy calamity in my affairs. Fortune, soon agreeing with fate, provided the occasion for a sudden loss through the death of Kings <note>original: "Regũ obitu"; likely referring to the deaths of major political figures that destabilized Valori's business or standing.</note>. Therefore, with adverse and contrary winds cruelly flowing together from both sides, we suffered a sudden shipwreck under the power of Juno and Aeolus <note>In Roman mythology, Juno (Queen of the Gods) and Aeolus (God of the Winds) conspired to create the storm that wrecked Aeneas's fleet.</note>. Nor was there any hope of safety remaining for me in my peril, had you not—acting as my Neptune <note>The Roman god of the sea who calmed the storm for Aeneas.</note>—appeased the swelling sea with your power, wisdom, and mercy, as if with a trident. What then, therefore, by your (if I may say so)...

# THE PREFACE OF MARSILIO FI-
# CINO THE FLORENTINE TO THE 
# BOOK ON LIFE, DEDICATED TO THE MAGNA-
# NIMOUS LORENZO DE' ME-
# DICI, SAVIOR OF HIS COUNTRY

**B**ACCHUS, the poets 
sing, was the highest 
overseer of priests 
and was born twice, 
signifying by this 
that it is necessary 

<margin>for one who is about to be initiated into the priesthood to be reborn immediately</margin> or that the mind of a perfected priest, at last thoroughly intoxicated by God, seems already to have been reborn. Or perhaps, in a more humble sense, the wine—the sprout of Bacchus—is generated once in the womb, as if from Semele <note>In Greek mythology, Semele was the mother of Bacchus (Dionysus) who died when Zeus revealed his true glory; the unborn child was then sewn into Zeus's thigh.</note>, when the clusters of grapes are ripened under Phoebus <note>The sun</note>; then it is regenerated again after the lightning-bolt of the vintage itself, the wine being held in its own vessel just as if in the thigh of Jove <note>Jupiter/Zeus</note> a second time. But we must not speak of sacred mysteries at this present moment, when we are soon to bring aid to the weary by means of physical medicine instead.
