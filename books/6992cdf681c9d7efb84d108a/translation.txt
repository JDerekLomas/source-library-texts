# Right Hand Thumb: The Posture of the Crying Crane about to Soar

---

**Bo** <gloss>Written in notation as "尸"</gloss>
<note>original: "擘" (bò). The shorthand symbol "尸" is derived from the top-left radical of the full character.</note>

**Tuo** <gloss>Written in notation as "乇"</gloss>
<note>original: "托" (tuō). The shorthand symbol "乇" is derived from the right-hand component of the full character.</note>

Using half the fingernail and half the flesh of the fingertip to strike inward toward the strings is called **Bo**.

Striking outward away from the strings is called **Tuo**.

Generally, any finger movement directed toward the player's body is called "inward" or "entering."

Movement directed toward the <term>hui<note>The thirteen mother-of-pearl circular markers inlaid into the surface of the Guqin, used to indicate finger positions for stopped notes and harmonics.</note></term> is called "outward" or "exiting." Other techniques follow these conventions.

<note>The "Crying Crane about to Soar" (鳴鶴將翔勢) is a poetic metaphor used to guide the player's hand shape. The thumb should be poised with the grace and potential energy of a crane preparing for flight, emphasizing a curved but firm structure that allows for the precise "half-flesh, half-nail" contact described in the text.</note>

# Right Hand Index Finger

## The Posture of the Crying Crane in the Shade
<note>original: "鳴鶴在陰勢" (míng hè zài yīn shì). This poetic name is derived from the *I Ching* (Book of Changes), suggesting a resonance that is felt even when the source is hidden. In Guqin practice, it refers to the elegant, curved shape of the index finger resembling a crane's neck.</note>

---

### Brushing Inward (Mo)
<gloss>Written in notation as the "wood" radical "木"</gloss>
<note>original: "抹" (mǒ). This is one of the most fundamental Guqin strokes.</note>
When the index finger moves inward toward the palm to strike a string, it is called "Mo."

### Glancing Outward (Li)
<gloss>Written in notation as the "cliff" radical "厂"</gloss>
<note>original: "歴" (lì).</note>
When the index finger moves outward away from the palm to strike a string, it is called "Li."

### Sweeping (Fu)
<gloss>Written in notation as "弗"</gloss>
<note>original: "拂" (fú). The term implies brushing away dust.</note>
Striking the strings in a downward motion without stopping on any single string is called "Fu."

### Passing (Du)
<gloss>Written in notation as the "broad" radical "广"</gloss>
<note>original: "度" (dù).</note>
A light, delicate version of the sweeping "Fu" technique is called "Du."

### Rolling Drum (Lei)
<gloss>Written in notation as "lightning" (電) or sometimes "sorrow" (哀)</gloss>
<note>original: "擂" (léi). This term often refers to the beating of drums.</note>
A continuous sweep starting from the seventh string <note>the highest-pitched string furthest from the player</note> across to the first string <note>the lowest-pitched string closest to the player</note> so that they sound as a single, resonant note; this is also known as "Lei."

# Right Hand: Index, Middle, and Ring Fingers
# The Posture of the Crowing and Dancing Pheasant
<note>original: "鴟雞鳴舞勢" (chī jī míng wǔ shì). The "Chī jī" refers to a long-tailed pheasant. In Guqin pedagogy, these animal metaphors help the student visualize the fluidity and "spirit" of the hand movement. This specific posture involves a flurry of notes suggesting the rapid, rhythmic movements of a pheasant in display.</note>

---

**Lock (Suo)** 
<gloss>Executed as three distinct sounds using the index finger to "pick" (outward) and "brush" (inward).</gloss>
<note>original: "鎖" (suǒ). This is a technical flourish where a string is struck multiple times in quick succession.</note>

**Reverse Lock (Fan Suo)** 
<gloss>Known as the "Reverse Kick" three-note sequence, using the index finger to "brush" then "pick."</gloss>
<note>original: "反鎖" (fǎn suǒ).</note>

**Short Lock (Duan Suo)** 
<gloss>Known as the "Kick" three-note sequence; it first produces two sounds, then follows with three sounds in quick succession.</gloss>
<note>original: "短鎖" (duǎn suǒ). This is a shorter version of the ornamental flourish.</note>

**Long Lock (Chang Suo)** 
<gloss>Produces three sounds: first by "picking" and "brushing" twice, then three sounds in succession for a total of seven sounds; or [in some variations] a total of nine sounds.</gloss>
<note>original: "長鎖" (cháng suǒ). A complex, rolling ornament that provides a rhythmic crescendo.</note>

**Rattling Lock (Li Li Suo)** 
<gloss>Executed as three sounds: first using the ring finger to "scrape" (Li), then the middle finger to "hook" (Gou), and then the ring finger to "scrape" again.</gloss>
<note>original: "擽擽鎖" (lì lì suǒ). The term "Li" (擽) mimics a rattling or scraping sound made by the fingernail.</note>

**Kick (Ti)** 
<gloss>Using the index finger to "pick" and "brush" followed by a long, sustained sound.</gloss>
<note>original: "踢" (tī). Not a literal kick, but a sharp, forceful outward stroke.</note>

# The Stance of the Mantis Catching a Cicada
## Right Hand: Index and Ring Fingers
<note>original: "螳螂捕蟬勢" (tángláng bǔ chán shì). This poetic metaphor describes the poised, bent shape of the fingers, resembling a mantis ready to strike. It governs techniques where the index and ring fingers play different strings simultaneously.</note>

---

**Simultaneous Pinching** <term>Qícuò</term> and **Reverse Pinching** <term>Fǎncuò</term>
In musical notation, the "Reverse" technique is written as a shorthand for "reverse" (反). <note>Traditional Guqin notation uses "Jianzifu," which are clusters of abbreviated characters representing finger movements and string numbers.</note>

Take the notation symbol <gloss>Zǎo</gloss> (早) as an example: if you are to perform a simultaneous pluck on the third and fifth strings, use the thumb to <term>pinch</term> <term>tāo</term> the index finger <note>original: "搯" (tāo). Here, the thumb applies pressure to the side of the index finger to create the necessary tension for a crisp strike.</note>. <term>Pick</term> <term>tiǎo</term> the fifth string with the index finger while using the ring finger to <term>strike</term> <term>dǎ</term> the third string. Both fingers should move downward in unison so that the two strings sound together as a single voice. Following the sound, release the thumb’s grip, but keep the ring finger resting against the fourth string.

If the notation indicates a **Reverse Pinching** (the "Reverse Zǎo" symbol), use the index finger to <term>brush inward</term> <term>mǒ</term> the fifth string, and the ring finger to <term>pluck outward</term> <term>zhāi</term> the third string, ensuring they both sound as one unified tone.

# Further Detailed Examination of Left-Hand Fingering Techniques

**Open String** <term>Sān; original: "散"</term> The right hand strikes the string with force while the left hand does not press down.

**Pressed Note** <term>Àn; original: "按"</term> <gloss>The middle finger rests on the tenth stud</gloss> Position the finger using both the nail and the flesh, pressing down firmly and moving upward.

**Wide Vibrato** <term>Náo; original: "猱"</term> Using the momentum of the note, pull the finger slightly away from the stud and then quickly return to it. <gloss>Náo</gloss> performed rapidly is called **Stored Vibrato** <term>Xù Náo; original: "畜猱"</term>. It involves a continuous, lingering back-and-forth movement that matches the musical intent.

**Tap** <term>Yàn; original: "罨"</term> The thumb presses a note at the tenth stud; a light, airy tap is called an **Empty Tap** <term>Xū Yàn; original: "虛罨"</term>.

**Scoop** <term>Dōu; original: "兜"</term> Simultaneously use the fingernail to hook the string lightly.

**Dot** <term>Diǎn; original: "點"</term> A responsive touch; either plucking downward or entering. This is also called **Pushing Up** <term>Tuīqǐ; original: "推起"</term>. In older manuals, this was called **Pushing Out** <term>Tuīchū; original: "推出"</term>. <note>Older instructional texts often used "out" and "in" to describe the direction of finger movement relative to the player's body.</note>

**Kneeling Finger** <term>Guì; original: "跪"</term> <gloss>To pull or lead</gloss> Positioning the finger outside the stud as indicated in old notes. <note>This technique involves bending the ring finger so the string is pressed by the knuckle or the back of the nail, used for higher pitches near the bridge.</note>

<margin>Stud</margin> **Two-Joint Pressed Vibrato** <term>Èrjié Àn Yín; original: "二節按吟"</term> Move the finger without allowing the sliding sound to become audible.

**Flying Vibrato** <term>Fēi Yín; original: "飛吟"</term> <note>A rapid, light vibrato that mimics the fluttering of wings.</note>

**Wandering Vibrato** <term>Yóu Yín; original: "游吟"</term> The finger moves slightly out of position, perhaps by half a stud.

<margin>Stud</margin> **Walking Vibrato** <term>Zǒu Yín; original: "走吟"</term> <gloss>Executed quickly</gloss> Vibrating the string while simultaneously leading the pitch upward; stop only when the sound is exhausted.

**Harmonic** <term>Fàn; original: "泛"</term> The finger floats on the surface of the string, vibrating and leading the sound upward.

<margin>Stored without a head</margin> **Tooth Harmonic** <term>Yá Fàn; original: "牙泛"</term> The "tooth" fingers (index and middle) are held upright.

**Strike** <term>Zhuàng; original: "撞"</term> Immediately after the note sounds, quickly move the finger upward a short distance. This is also called **Touch** <term>Chù; original: "觸"</term>.

**Upward Slide** <term>Chuò; original: "綽"</term> The sound begins from a lower position and slides up to the target pitch.

<margin>There is no fixed start or end to the stud note; the finger position is similar to the half-note and wandering styles.</margin> **Downward Slide** <term>Yì; original: "抑"</term> Sliding from a higher pitch down to a lower one.

# Methods for Fingernail Care

**Bamboo-like nails** are tough and rigid. **Water-like nails** <note>This refers to naturally thin or brittle nails.</note> are easily broken. Another method involves using **Crane Quills**. Cut the quill into the shape of a fingernail. Use **Isinglass** <term>Biào; original: "鰾"; a strong glue made from fish bladders</term> to stick and bind the quill to the natural nail. Let it set overnight; once cured, it will be so firm that it cannot be moved even with great force.
