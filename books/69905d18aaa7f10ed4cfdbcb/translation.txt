# SELF-MASTERY THROUGH CONSCIOUS AUTOSUGGESTION

Suggestion, or rather Autosuggestion, is a relatively new subject, and yet at the same time it is as old as the world. It is new in the sense that until now it has been studied incorrectly and, as a result, misunderstood; it is old because it has existed since the first appearance of humanity on Earth. In fact, autosuggestion is a tool that we possess from birth. Within this tool—or rather, within this force—resides a marvelous and immeasurable <note>original: "incalculable"</note> power which, depending on the circumstances, produces the best or the worst results. Knowledge of this force is useful to everyone, but it is especially essential for doctors, judges <note>original: "magistrates"</note>, lawyers, and those engaged in the work of education.

By knowing how to practice it *consciously*, it is possible, first, to avoid triggering harmful autosuggestions in others which may have disastrous consequences. Second, one can *consciously* encourage positive ones instead, thereby bringing physical health to the sick and mental <note>original: "moral"</note> health to the neurotic and the struggling—the unconscious victims of previous <note>original: "anterior"</note> autosuggestions—and to guide onto the right path those who had a tendency to take the wrong one.

### Will and Imagination

If we open a dictionary and look up the word “will,” we find this definition: “The faculty of freely determining certain acts.” We accept this definition as true and indisputable, although nothing could be more false. This will that we claim so proudly always *yields* to the imagination. It is an *absolute* rule that admits of no *exception*.

“Blasphemy! Paradox!” you will exclaim. “Not at all! On the contrary, it is the simplest truth,” I shall reply.

In order to convince yourself of it, open your eyes, look around you, and try to understand what you see. You will then come to the conclusion that what I am telling you is not a useless theory—the product of a diseased mind—but the simple expression of a *fact*.

Suppose that we place a plank on the ground, 30 feet long by 1 foot wide. It is evident that everyone is capable of walking from one end of this plank to the other without stepping over the edge. But now, change the conditions of the experiment and imagine this plank placed at the height of the towers of a cathedral. Who then would be capable of advancing even a few feet along this narrow path? Would you listen to my instructions? Probably not. Before you had taken two steps, you would begin to tremble, and *in spite of every effort of your will*, you would be certain to fall to the ground.

Why is it, then, that you would not fall if the plank is on the ground, but you would certainly fall if it were raised to a great height?

Let us now consider the case of a person suffering from <term>insomnia</term> <note>The inability to fall asleep or stay asleep.</note>.
