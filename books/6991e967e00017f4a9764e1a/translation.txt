> 28) That our codex is an autograph <note>A manuscript written in the author's own hand.</note> is evident and uncontested, and no proof is required. From the description and history of it, it also emerges in an absolute manner that the small notebook, minus the missing papers, has come down to us in the state in which it belonged to Leonardo. Ludwig, however (*Leonardo da Vinci, The Book of Painting, Volume III, p. 5* <note>original: "Das Buch von der Malerei"</note>), has expressed some doubts about the authenticity, not of the writing, but of the formation of the booklet, which he is inclined to believe is a patchwork of leaves belonging to different codices. >
> 1st: *The page numbers do not correspond to the number of the leaves.* The codex proves the opposite; the numbers correspond exactly to the leaves. (Compare page 18: Numerations, and 20: Missing leaves, and note 4.)
>
> 2nd: *Twice other numbers are superimposed on the original numbers.* There exists only one superimposition, which begins with No. 5 and continues until the last. (Compare page 18: 2nd Numeration, superimposed.)
>
> 3rd: *These superimpositions are made clearly with the intention of making the original numbers unrecognizable.* It is evident, as we have seen, that the intention was to correct a slip of the pen, not to alter the primitive numbers, which always remain legible and clear. (Compare note 4.)
>
> 4th: *The leaves are loose and can be arranged as one wishes.* That the leaves are now loose is true, but in all of them, and in the cover, there exist the holes, exactly corresponding to each other, of the ancient stitching that held them together originally (Compare page 18: Arrangement of the sheets), and they could not be arranged at will, because the arrangement they had and have is dictated: 1st by Leonardo's numbering, which would be upset by any other arrangement; 2nd by marks existing on every page, either intended by Leonardo or accidental, but from the time—indeed from the moment in which he wrote—such as the imprint from certain freshly written letters left on the opposite page; 3rd by the continuity of the subject matter; 4th by the dates written by Leonardo at the beginning and at the end of the codex. Emery in large pieces is broken by placing it upon a cloth folded many times; strike it on its side with the hammer, and thus it will gradually break away in flakes, and then it can be crushed with ease. If you were to hold it directly on the anvil, you would never break it, as it is so large.

# FLIGHT OF BIRDS — THE KITE

<margin>Fig. 28.</margin>
...the <term>compression</term><note>original: "detione" / "sione", likely referring to the compression of air beneath the wing</note> of which causes the bird to rise upward.

<margin>The kite.</margin>
The kite and other birds that beat their wings very little go searching for the currents of the wind; and when the wind prevails at a high altitude, then they are seen at a great height, and if it prevails low, they stay low.

When the wind does not prevail in the air, then the kite beats its wings several times in its flight in such a way that it rises upward and acquires <term>impetus</term><note>momentum or kinetic energy</note>. With this impetus, by then descending slightly, it travels a long distance without beating its wings; and when it has descended, it does the same again, and so it continues <insert>successively</insert>. And this descent without beating its wings serves as a way for it to rest in the air after the fatigue of the aforementioned wing-beating.

All birds that fly in "jolts" <note>intermittent flight</note> raise themselves upward with the beating of their wings, and when they descend, they come to rest, because during their descent they do not beat their wings.

<summary>Leonardo explains how the kite utilizes wind currents and alternating periods of active flapping and passive gliding to conserve energy during flight. He identifies the descent as a crucial period of rest for the bird, powered by the momentum gained during its ascent.</summary>
