->Master Mao says: Of the Five Rites <note>The traditional Chinese classification of rituals: Auspicious, Funeral, Military, Hospitality, and Festive.</note>, the military is one. In the system of Ritual, sacrifice is considered the most significant; thus, the **Ma sacrifice** <term>Ma <note>original: 禡; a specific ancient ritual performed by a commander to the god of war before a campaign or at the site of a battle.</note></term> is a practice of great antiquity. Our current dynasty <note>The Ming Dynasty (1368–1644).</note> is cautious and thorough in its service to the spirits. I have respectfully recorded these practices here, while also appending the regulations from the Song Dynasty. # Rituals for Sacrificing to the Banners and Standards of Our Dynasty

In the first year of the **Hongwu** era <note>1368 AD, the founding year of the Ming Dynasty.</note>, an imperial decree established the various rituals for when the Emperor personally leads a military expedition or dispatches his generals. It was determined that, according to the practices of the ancients, when the Son of Heaven <note>The Emperor.</note> personally leads a campaign, he must perform the **Lei** sacrifice <term>Lei <note>original: 類; a special sacrifice performed to the Supreme Deity (Shangdi) when the Emperor left the capital on an extraordinary journey or war.</note></term> to the Supreme Deity, report his departure at the ancestral temple, and perform the **Yi** sacrifice <term>Yi <note>original: 宜; a ritual dedicated to the Altars of Earth and Grain before a military operation.</note></term> to the God of the Soil. Furthermore, he should perform the **Ma** sacrifice at the destination of the campaign and offer sacrifices to the spirits of the mountains and rivers passed along the route. If generals are dispatched to lead an army, the departure must likewise be announced at the ancestral temples and the Altars of Earth and Grain, and the **Ma** sacrifice must be performed for the banners and standards <note>The large flags and command banners that represented the Emperor's authority and the spiritual heart of the army.</note>.

Following this, various scholars deliberated and submitted their proposals, decreeing that from this time forward, the **Ivory Banner** <term>Ivory Banner <note>original: 牙旗 (yaqi); the principal banner of a commander, historically named for the ivory ornaments that once decorated the flagstaff.</note></term> and the **Six Standards** <term>Six Standards <note>original: 六纛 (liudao); massive banners made of felt or hair that served as symbols of supreme military authority.</note></term> were to be stored within the Inner Palace. Their dedicated temple is located at the Altar of Mountains and Rivers. 

Every year, on the day of the mid-autumn sacrifice to the mountains and rivers, an official is dispatched to perform a sacrifice at the Temple of Banners and Standards. On the day of **Frost Descent** <note>A solar term occurring in late October; in the Ming military, this was a traditional time for seasonal drills and martial inspections.</note>, another sacrifice is performed at the drill grounds. At the end of the year, on the day of the grand offerings at the Imperial Ancestral Temple, yet another sacrifice is held outside the **Chengtian Gate** <note>The "Gate of Inheriting Heaven," the primary southern entrance to the Imperial City, later known as the Tiananmen Gate.</note>. All of these ceremonies are conducted by the commanders of the **Banner-Bearing Guard** <note>The Flag-Bearing Guard (Qishou Wei) was an elite unit responsible for the Emperor's ceremonial regalia and musical instruments.</note>.

After the **Yongle era** <note>1403–1424 AD, the reign of the third Ming Emperor.</note>, a separate "Sacrifice to the Divine Banner" was added, specifically to worship the **God of Fire and Thunder** <note>A deity associated with the power and destructive force of gunpowder weapons.</note>. On the first and fifteenth day of every month, the supervising official of the **Firearms Division** <term>Firearms Division <note>original: 神機營 (Shenji Ying); the "Divine Engine" Battalion, one of the three elite tactical camps of Beijing, specialized in musketry and artillery.</note></term> requests to perform a sacrifice at the drill grounds. Currently, the Supreme Commander of the Beijing Military District submits the formal petition; once the sacrifice is completed, he reports back to the Emperor to fulfill his commission.

### The Arrangement of the Seven Divine Seats
The ritual space is arranged for seven divine entities, <gloss>all facing South</gloss>:
*   The Great General of the Banner-Head.
*   The Great General of the **Six Standards** <term>Six Standards <note>original: 六纛 (liudao); massive hair-tufted banners representing the commander's authority.</note></term>.
*   The Gods of the Flags of the Five Directions.
*   The Presiding Deities of the Warships.
*   The Gods of the Gongs, Drums, Horns, Hand-Cannons, and Cannons.
*   The Gods of the Bows, Crossbows, Flying Spears, and Flying Stones.
*   The Deities of the Front and Rear of the Battle Formations, and the gathered assembly of the **Wuchang** <note>The Wuchang are fierce, martial spirits often invoked in local and military rituals to drive away malevolent forces and ensure victory.</note>.

### Ritual Offerings and Vessels
For the sacrifice, the following are to be provided: 
One calf, one sheep, and one pig. One *deng* <term>deng <note>A ceramic vessel used for holding sacrificial meat broth.</note></term>, two *bian* <term>bian <note>Bamboo baskets used for dry offerings like fruits or seeds.</note></term>, ten each of *fu* and *dou* <term>fu and dou <note>Square and round vessels used for grain and sauces, respectively.</note></term>, and two each of *fu* and *gui* <term>gui <note>Another type of round grain vessel.</note></term>.

Seven bolts of ritual silk are provided <gloss>two in black and five in white</gloss>, set out together. There are three wine vessels, three libation cups, thirty wine bowls, and one basket. These are placed to the southeast of the altar, facing West. A table for the **Ritual Invocations** <term>Ritual Invocations <note>original: 祝文 (zhuwen); the written prayers read aloud and then burned to communicate with the spirits.</note></term> is placed to the West of the altar.

### The Main Sacrifice
The ritual proceeds as follows: 
The Ritual Guides lead the Offering Official to the ablution station to perform the ceremonial washing of hands. The **Music Office** <term>Music Office <note>original: 教坊司 (jiaofang si); the state institution responsible for training musicians and performers for court and ritual events.</note></term> begins to play. The Master of Ceremonies calls out the commands, and the various Attending Officials each perform their designated duties. The Ritual Guides prompt the official to take his position. The Master of Ceremonies then calls out to begin the "Welcoming of the Spirits."

### The Sequence of the Main Sacrifice

**Sacred Music** is played. The music begins, then pauses. The **Master of Ceremonies** <note>original: 贊 (zan); the official who calls out the ritual steps.</note> and the **Guide** <note>original: 引 (yin); the official who physically leads the presiding officer through the space.</note> lead the participants in four prostrations. <gloss>Assistant sacrificial officials perform these alongside the leader.</gloss> The Master of Rituals announces the **Presentation of Silk**.

#### The First Offering
The **First Offering** is performed. Soft music is played. The attendants carry the ritual silk and the wine chalice forward. The Guide leads the Presiding Official to the front of the divine tablets. After the offering is presented, the Guide leads the official to the position for reading the prayer. The Master of Ceremonies commands everyone to kneel. The music stops.

Once the reading of the prayer is finished, the music resumes. The Master of Ceremonies commands everyone to prostrate, then rise and stand straight. The official is led back to his original position, and the music stops.

#### The Second and Final Offerings
The Master of Rituals announces the **Second Offering**. Music is played. The attendants carry the wine chalice, and the Guide leads the official to the divine tablets. Once the offering is complete, the music stops.

The Master of Rituals announces the **Final Offering**. <gloss>The ritual protocol is the same as the Second Offering.</gloss> The Master then announces the **Drinking of the Blessing** and the **Receiving of the Sacrificial Meat** <note>This "communion" signifies that the spirits have accepted the offering and are now sharing their favor with the worshippers.</note>. The remaining steps follow the standard ritual protocol.

## The Arrangement of the Ritual Site

On the day prior to the ceremony, the attendants shall set up the spirit altar <note>original: 神案 (shen'an); a table to hold the divine tablets of the spirits being invoked.</note> in the northern section of the temple hall. 

The military positions are to be established in the east, and the **Six Great Banners** <term>Six Banners (Liu Dao): large, ornate flags representing the supreme authority of the commander and the protection of the gods of war.</term> are to be positioned in the west. 

Twelve *bian* <term>Bian: bamboo baskets used for dry offerings like dates or chestnuts.</term> are to be arranged to the east of the spirit position in three rows, starting from the west. Twelve *dou* <term>Dou: lidded wooden bowls used for wet offerings like pickles or sauces.</term> are to be arranged to the west of the spirit position in three rows, starting from the east. 

Between the *bian* and the *dou*, place two *fu* and two *gui* <term>Fu and Gui: bronze or wooden vessels used specifically for holding sacrificial grains like millet and rice; the fu is rectangular and the gui is round.</term> with the *fu* to the east and the *gui* to the west. 

Three *xing* <note>original: 鉶; tripod vessels for meat stews.</note> and three *deng* <note>original: 登; ceramic or wooden vessels for meat broth.</note> are to be placed in front of the baskets and bowls. Three *zu* <note>original: 俎; sacrificial stands or "butcher blocks" upon which the meat of the primary animal sacrifices is laid.</note> are to be placed in front of the *deng* and *xing*. 

The incense altar is placed in front of the *zu* stands. The cup stand <note>original: 爵坫 (juedian); a small platform for the ceremonial wine cups.</note> is placed in front of the incense altar. A sand basin <note>original: 沙池 (shachi); used to catch wine during libations.</note> is placed in front of the cup stand. 

The station for the wine vessels is established in the eastern part of the temple courtyard. The chest for the silk offerings <note>original: 幣篚 (bifei); a basket for holding ritual fabrics given to the spirits.</note> is located to the north of the wine vessels. The station for washing the ceremonial cups is to the south of the wine vessels, and the station for the Emperor’s personal washing <note>original: 御洗 (yuxi); a basin for the ritual cleansing of the officiant's hands.</note> is to the south of the cup-washing station. 

A burial pit <note>original: 瘞坎 (yikan); a pit used to bury the remains of the sacrifice, such as hair and blood, to return them to the earth.</note> is prepared to the northwest of the spirit position. A mat is placed in front of the pit, upon which are set wine, five bowls, and **five roosters** to await the libation ceremony for the spirits. 

The Emperor’s position is established in the south of the temple courtyard, at the exact center and facing north. The Great General and the assisting ritual officials follow behind him, organized in rows according to their respective ranks.

### Civil Officials to the East, Military to the West; All Face North

On the day of the **Main Sacrifice**, at early dawn, the Ivory Banners and the **Six Great Standards** <term>Six Great Standards (Liu Dao): Massive regimental banners, often decorated with ox tails or feathers, symbolizing the supreme authority of the military commander and the protection of war deities.</term> are erected behind the spirit tablet; the banners are placed to the east and the standards to the west.

The Emperor dons the **Military Cap Dress** <term>Military Cap Dress (Wubianfu): A specific ceremonial uniform consisting of a red robe and a leather cap, worn by the Ming Emperor for military rituals or when reviewing troops.</term>. The escorting officials, together with the **Minister of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices** <note>The Minister (Taichangqing) was the high-ranking official responsible for overseeing state rituals, sacrifices, and court music.</note>, lead the Emperor through the left southern gate to his designated position. 

The Master of Ceremonies calls out to "Welcome the Deities," and the **Great Music** <note>original: 大樂 (dayue); the solemn, ancient style of music used exclusively for imperial state ceremonies.</note> begins to play. The Minister of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices requests the Emperor to bow. As the music plays, the Minister prompts the sequence of movements: "Bow, rise, bow, rise, and straighten the body." Then the music ceases. <gloss>The Assistant Ceremonialist performs the same duties.</gloss>

The Master of Ceremonies calls out for the **Presentation of the Ritual Silk** <note>Silk was a primary offering in imperial rites, symbolizing purity and high value.</note> and the commencement of the **Rite of First Offering**. 

The Minister of the Court of Imperial Sacrifices requests the Emperor to proceed to the **Ablution Station** <note>original: 盥洗位 (guanxi wei); a specific location where the officiant performs ritual hand-washing to ensure spiritual cleanliness before touching sacred objects.</note>. Upon reaching the station, the Emperor **tucks away his ritual tablet** <term>Jin Gui: The act of inserting the jade ritual tablet into the sash or belt to leave the hands free for washing or handling sacrificial vessels.</term>, performs the washing and drying of his hands, and then retrieves the tablet.

# Screen Hurdle
<note>original: "屏風笆" (Pingfeng Ba). A "Ba" is a hurdle or screen. The term "Pingfeng" refers to a traditional folding screen; here, it describes a protective barrier used to block the enemy's line of sight and fire.</note>

# Wooden Horse
<note>original: "木馬子" (Muma Zi). Literally "Small Wooden Horse." In a military and engineering context, this refers to a trestle or sawhorse used as a structural support or a portable obstacle.</note>

<term>Screen Hurdle (Pingfeng Ba 屏風笆): A specialized defensive panel typically woven from split bamboo. These were often used in siege warfare to create temporary "walls" that shielded soldiers from arrows and stones while they worked on fortifications or moved into position.</term>

<note>The diagram for the Screen Hurdle depicts a series of nested rectangular frames. The dotted patterns inside suggest the dense weaving of bamboo or reeds. These screens were valued for being lightweight yet remarkably resilient against low-velocity projectiles.</note>

<term>Wooden Horse (Muma Zi 木馬子): A heavy timber frame consisting of a main horizontal beam supported by diagonal, crossed legs. These served as the "bones" of many mobile military structures, providing a stable base for mounting the hurdles and screens described elsewhere in this manual.</term>

<note>The illustration shows a classic trestle design. In practical application, multiple "Wooden Horses" would be lined up to support long stretches of fencing, or used individually as "Hedgehogs" to block narrow passes and breaches in a wall, preventing cavalry or infantry charges.</note>

<summary>This page illustrates two fundamental components of Ming Dynasty field engineering: the Screen Hurdle, used for concealment and protection, and the Wooden Horse, a versatile trestle used to support larger defensive structures.</summary>

# Goose-wing Hurdle Support Bracket
<note>original: "撑鴈翅笆拐" (Cheng Yanchi Baguai). This is the functional handle or brace used to manipulate the "Goose-wing Hurdle" described in earlier sections. The "Goose-wing" hurdle was a protective screen with angled sides resembling a bird's wings.</note>

<term>Upper-headed Carriage Ladder (Shangtou Cheti 上頭車梯): A sophisticated mobile assault device. This ladder was not merely a climbing tool but a piece of heavy machinery. It was typically built onto a wooden frame with wheels, allowing it to be pushed toward enemy battlements under the cover of shields. The illustration shows a robust, double-railed construction designed to support the weight of multiple armored soldiers simultaneously.</term>

<note>The technical diagram for the Carriage Ladder highlights its mobility. In the context of the "Wujing Zongyao" <note>The "Complete Essentials for the Military Classics," a 11th-century Song Dynasty compendium</note>, such ladders were essential for "storming the heights" during a protracted siege.</note>

<term>Goose-wing Hurdle Support Bracket (Cheng Yanchi Baguai 撑鴈翅笆拐): A specialized T-shaped or L-shaped handle used by infantry. Because defensive hurdles (screens) were often heavy and unwieldy, these "brackets" or "cranks" provided the necessary leverage for soldiers to quickly raise, lower, or pivot the protection as the direction of enemy fire changed.</term>

<note>The illustration of the Support Bracket shows a simple but effective design. Its inclusion as a separate entry emphasizes the importance of ergonomics and manual handling in historical engineering; even the best defensive screen was useless if it could not be moved into position quickly by the men on the ground.</note>
