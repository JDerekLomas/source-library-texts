# The definition of a witch: the antiquity of witchcraft.
## Chapter 2.

The perfect and right knowledge of things is when a man knows their causes. A definition consists of those causes which give the whole essence and contain the perfect nature of the thing being defined; therefore, where that is found out, there is a very clear light. If it is perfect, that light is much greater; even if it is not fully perfect, it still gives some good light. For this reason, though I do not dare to claim that I can give a perfect definition in this matter—which is difficult to do even in well-known things, because the essential form is hard to find—yet I will give a definition which may at least provide notice of what manner of persons they are of whom I intend to speak.

<margin>What a witch is</margin>
A witch is one who works by the Devil, or by some devilish or occult <note>original: "curious art"—this referred to magic, alchemy, or any secret knowledge used to manipulate the natural or spiritual world</note> art, either hurting or healing, revealing secret things, or foretelling things to come, which the Devil has devised to entangle and snare men’s souls with into damnation. The conjurer, the enchanter, the sorcerer, the diviner, and whatever other sort there is, are indeed encompassed within this circle. The reason for this can be known neither from the word of **God**, nor from the rules of medicine <note>original: "phisicke"</note> or nature. For example, [thinking] that it is unlucky for one to put the left shoe on the right foot in the morning, to sneeze while putting on one's shoes, to have a nosebleed, to have a ringing in the ear, to suddenly bleed a few drops of blood, or to stumble on the right foot. 

On the other hand, [thinking] that it is good luck to find silver, to have a drink spilled on oneself, to have the left ear burn <note>referring to the sensation of one's ears "burning" when being spoken about</note>, or to dream of certain things. 

The same is said regarding the belief <margin>A fever or epilepsy <note>original: "falling sicknes"</note> may be driven away by a certain charm of words.</margin> that a fever <note>original: "the ague"</note> can be driven away by wearing a ring, or by a certain charm of strange and foreign-sounding <note>original: "barbarous"</note> words. It is foolish to think such power rests in herbs or in syllables just by pronouncing them; that was a small matter. 

Furthermore, [thinking] that the repetition of a word or saying it loudly can infuse herbs with a power <note>original: "faculty"</note> of healing diseases quickly—and other such things—should not be believed, even though they have often been recounted. For **God** permits them to appear successful so that those who see and hear such things may be tested, and it may appear what confidence they truly have in **God**.
