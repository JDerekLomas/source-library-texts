# Supplement to the *Compendium of Materia Medica*
<note>original: "本草綱目拾遺" (*Bencao Gangmu Shiyi*)</note>

This title identifies a seminal work of the Qing Dynasty (published circa 1765) by the scholar Zhao Xuemin. It was written to expand upon Li Shizhen’s world-renowned 16th-century medical encyclopedia, the *Compendium of Materia Medica*. Zhao Xuemin’s "Supplement" is highly regarded for documenting hundreds of new medicinal substances—including items introduced through maritime trade like chocolate and tobacco—as well as folk remedies that had been previously overlooked by the academic medical establishment.

<term>Materia Medica</term> (the body of collected knowledge about the therapeutic properties of any substance used for healing, particularly in <term>Traditional Chinese Medicine</term>).

**Golden Bean** <gloss>Appended with "Closing at Night"</gloss> <note>original: "金豆子" (*Jin dou zi*). "Closing at Night" refers to *Lespedeza cuneata*, a plant whose leaves fold at night.</note>

According to the *Mirror of a Hundred Herbs* <note>An earlier pharmaceutical reference text frequently cited by the author</note>, this is also called the "Golden Flower Leopard." Its sprouts emerge in the third month [of the lunar calendar] and it withers in the tenth month. Although it is a type of bean, it does not grow as a vine. The plant stands one or two inches tall, branching out to form a cluster. The leaves resemble those of the <term>Locust tree<note>original: "槐" (*Huai*). *Sophora japonica*.</note></term> but are slightly larger. 

During the "End of Heat" <note>A solar term in the traditional calendar occurring around August 23rd</note>, it blooms with yellow, five-petaled flowers; they resemble the **Chime-mouth Wintersweet** <note>original: "磬口蠟梅" (*Qingkou lamei*). A variety of wintersweet where the petals curve inward like the shape of an ancient stone chime.</note>. The seed pods point upward, resembling a red silk ribbon-fastener, though they are short, measuring only two or three inches. The seeds look like mung beans but are flat, with purple spots on the skin; they are slightly larger than mung beans and have a mild taste.

The seeds treat **carbuncles** <note>original: "癰" (*Yong*). A cluster of painful, pus-filled boils under the skin.</note> with miraculous effect.

**Leaves** 
These treat swollen toxins <note>Refers to localized inflammatory swellings, infections, or boils</note>. According to a formula passed down by the Fang family: dry the leaves in the sun and grind them into a powder. Mix this with vinegar and apply it as a poultice to a "Thunder Head" <note>original: "雷頭" (*leitou*). Likely referring to a "Thunder Head Wind" headache or a specific type of painful cranial swelling/lump</note>, and it will dissipate immediately. Alternatively, take two or three <term>mace<note>A traditional unit of weight, roughly 3.75 grams per mace.</note></term> of the powder orally with wine.

According to Fu Dan’an’s *Secrets of Herbs and Flowers*: "The **Golden Bean** blooms with yellow flowers, and its seeds resemble mung beans. When added to boiling tea, the flavor is pure and fragrant. This is actually the **Grass Cassia** <note>original: "草決明" (*Cao jueming*). *Senna obtusifolia*, often used for eye health and as a tea</note>."

In Prince Ningxian’s *Herbal for Relief of Famine* <note>original: "救荒本草" (*Jiuhuang bencao*), a 15th-century work by Zhu Xiao (Prince of Zhou) focused on edible wild plants for use during famines</note>, there is a listing for "Mountain Flat Bean," which is the same as the **Renmang Cassia** <note>original: "荏芒決明" (*Renmang jueming*). A variety of wild senna</note>. Its flavor is sweet and "slippery" <note>A texture associated with mucilaginous plants in traditional medicine</note>, and it can be used to make wine starter <note>original: "酒麴" (*Jiuqu*). A fermentation starter for alcohol</note>. It is colloquially called "Vat Occupier" <note>original: "獨占缸" (*Duzhangang*)</note>. The sprouts, leaves, flowers, and seeds can all be boiled as vegetables or steeped in tea for consumption. The physical description recorded there is identical to that of the **Golden Bean**. 

However, in the *Haohu Compendium* <note>original: "灝湖綱目" (*Haohu gangmu*)</note>, the entry for **Renmang** is appended to the section on **Cassia**, stating: "Its nature is balanced and non-toxic. When roasted over a fire and made into a drink, it is extremely fragrant. It eliminates phlegm, quenches thirst, prevents drowsiness, and regulates the 'Middle' <note>The digestive system or Middle Jiao</note>." This is the same plant that the Zen Master Sui Li collected to create the "Five-Colored Drink" to present to Emperor Yang of the Sui Dynasty <note>reigned 604–618 CE</note>. 

Because those earlier texts do not mention the treatment of swollen toxins or boils, I have preserved both sets of information here for comparative study and future verification.

# Night-Closing Gate
<note>original: "夜關門" (*Yeguanmen*). Usually refers to *Lespedeza cuneata*, known in English as Chinese bushclover.</note>

The leaves resemble those of the Chinese scholar tree <note>original: "槐" (*Huai*); *Sophora japonica*</note>. They close at night and open during the day. It produces yellow flowers. Many people in Jianqiao, Renhe <note>A district in modern-day Hangzhou</note>, cultivate it. 

Yu Xiaoyuan <note>A Qing dynasty physician or botanical scholar</note> states: "There are two varieties, one herbaceous and one woody. The woody variety is actually the **Silk Tree** <note>original: "合歡" (*Hehuan*); *Albizia julibrissin*</note>." It has the power to "dispel wind" <term>Dispel wind: In traditional medicine, this refers to relieving pain, numbness, or tremors believed to be caused by 'environmental' factors entering the body.</term>. The bark is harvested to treat lung abscesses that refuse to heal. It can also be steamed into a medicinal paste and applied to "toxins" <note>Refers to infected sores or boils</note> to promote the growth of new flesh and close the wound.

According to the *Compendium of Materia Medica* <note>original: "綱目" (*Gangmu*); referring to Li Shizhen's definitive 16th-century herbal</note>, the leaves of the **Sicklepod** <note>original: "馬蹄決明" (*Mati jueming*); *Senna obtusifolia*</note> also resemble the scholar tree and close at night. Its leaves are small at the base and broader at the tip. It blooms with yellow flowers in late autumn. This "Night-Closing Gate" may indeed be a variety of Cassia. However, the *Compendium* does not mention "hernia" under its entry for Cassia seeds. Both accounts are preserved here for reference.

**Pods** Used to treat <term>hernia<note>original: "疝氣" (*Shanqi*). In a historical context, this refers broadly to abdominal pain, swelling, or protrusions in the groin area.</note></term>.

### Immortal Peach Grass 
<note>original: "仙桃草" (*Xiantao cao*); botanically identified as *Veronica peregrina*, also known as Neckweed. The "peach" refers to the galls formed on the plant by the larvae of small weevils.</note>

The *Mirror of a Hundred Herbs* <note>original: "百草鏡" (*Baicao jing*)</note> states: **Immortal Peach Grass** is frequently found on the embankments of fields near water. After the **Grain Rain** solar term <term>Grain Rain: *Guyu*, a traditional Chinese solar term occurring around April 20th, marking a period of increased rainfall for crops.</term>, the sprouts emerge. The leaves are glossy and long, similar to the **False Daisy** <note>original: "旱蓮" (*Hanlian*); *Eclipta prostrata*</note>. It grows about a foot high. The stem is hollow; when broken, it does not turn black, nor does it have a fragrance. After the **Start of Summer** <term>Start of Summer: *Lixia*, a solar term beginning around May 5th.</term>, it produces small white flowers, also resembling those of the False Daisy, which form into spikes. 

The fruit it bears is as large as a bean and shaped like a tiny peach. It is hollow inside and contains small insects. These insects eventually grow wings, bore a hole through the fruit, and emerge. When harvesting, one must wait until the fruit is just about to turn red, but before the insects have grown wings and left. Only then is the medicinal potency complete. The effectiveness of this medicine depends entirely on the insects. One must sun-dry or roast the fruit to kill the insects inside. If it is merely hung to air-dry, there is a risk the insects will grow wings and escape, rendering the medicine useless.

<note>按：Zhao Xuemin's commentary follows</note> 
This grass must be harvested after **Grain in Ear** <term>Grain in Ear: *Mangzhong*, a solar term beginning around June 5th, signifying the time when seeds form.</term>. If the **Summer Solstice** <term>Summer Solstice: *Xiazhi*, the longest day of the year, around June 21st.</term> passes, the insects will bore through the fruit and transform into small gnats. The pods will then be empty and useless. 

Its nature is warm and its flavor is sweet and mild. It is used to "dissipate" <term>Dissipate: *Xiao* (消), a therapeutic method to reduce swellings, abscesses, or internal accumulations.</term> abscesses, swellings, and injuries from falls or blows. It is effective whether the juice is extracted or the plant is ground into powder for ingestion.

## Treating Liver-Qi and Harmonizing the Stomach

The *Collection of Heard Remedies* <note>original: "集聽" (*Jiting*)</note> states that another name for this is the **Eight-Trigrams Immortal Peach**. This herb grows in wild fields; its leaves resemble pomegranate leaves, and its fruit is like a peach, but extremely small. Those that contain small insects inside are the genuine variety. One should harvest the fruit and use it together with the insect. 

**A Prescription:** Specifically for treating **Liver-Qi** <term>Liver-Qi: In traditional Chinese medicine, this refers to the functional energy of the liver; "stagnation" here often causes emotional distress or physical pain.</term>, **Stomach-Qi** <term>Stomach-Qi: The energy responsible for digestion; its "rebellion" causes nausea or pain.</term>, and **small intestinal hernia** <term>Hernia: *Shan* (疝), a term covering various lower abdominal pains or protrusions.</term>. Grind these into a powder and mix with brown sugar to form pills the size of a mung bean. Every night, take about one *qian* <term>Qian: A traditional unit of weight, roughly 3.75 grams or 0.13 ounces.</term>. For even the most severe cases, two doses will "sever the root" of the illness.

It is then added to tonic medicines for use.

# Seven-leaf Chaste Tree

Also known as **Pig-lying Grass**, **Five-clawed Bead Grass**, **Crow-eating Grass**, **Crow-snake Grass**, and the **Seven-twisted Zither**; it is also called the **Seven-leaf Chaste Tree Vine**. It grows in shady spots at the base of earthen walls. Its leaves are long and pointed, arranged in opposing pairs of three or four rows to form a single leaflet. The stem is ridged, with a purple tint appearing in the hollows between the ridges. 

After the **White Dew** <term>White Dew: *Bailu* (白露), a solar term in the traditional Chinese calendar beginning around September 7th, marking the start of autumn's chill.</term>, it sends up a central stalk three to five feet tall. ### Plague-Warding Grass, Fish-Turtle Golden Star, and Phoenix-Tail Golden Star

This plant is also known as the **One-Legged Golden Rooster** <note>original: *Dujiao Jinji*</note> and the **Duck-Foot Golden Star** <note>original: *Yajiao Jinxing*</note>.
