# The Pictorial Compendium of the Three Realms

<note>original: "Sancai Tuhui" (三才圖會)</note>

<term>Three Realms<note>The "Three Realms" (or "Three Powers") refers to the traditional Chinese cosmological triad of Heaven, Earth, and Humanity. This encyclopedia seeks to categorize all existing knowledge within these three categories.</note></term>

<note>Compiled by Wang Qi and his son Wang Siyi during the Ming Dynasty (published around 1609), this massive work is one of the most important illustrated encyclopedias in Chinese history. It contains hundreds of illustrations covering subjects as diverse as astronomy, geography, biology, history, and daily life, serving as a "visual map" of the world as understood at the time.</note>

# Mount Tai

<margin>Page 34; Subject: Mount Tai; Sheet: 1</margin>

According to the records, Mount Tai is located in Tai'an Department, within the Jinan Prefecture of Shandong Province. It is the **Eastern Peak** of the <term>Five Sacred Mountains</term> <note>The Five Sacred Mountains, or *Wuyue*, represent the cardinal directions and the center in Chinese cosmology; Mount Tai is the most significant.</note>. The *Records of Diverse Matters* <note>original: "Bowu Zhi"</note> states that Mount Tai is also called the "Grandson of Heaven" <note>original: "Tiansun"</note>, meaning it is the grandson of the Celestial Emperor. It presides over the summoning of souls. Because the East is the direction where all things in the universe first come to completion, this mountain is said to know the length of every human life.

The *General Meaning of the Five Classics* <note>original: "Wujing Tongyi"</note> states that the mountain is also called <term>Daizong</term> <note>original: "岱宗"; "Dai" is an ancient name for the mountain, and "Zong" means ancestor or chief.</note>. This implies that when a sovereign receives the <term>Mandate of Heaven</term>, changes the ruling family name, reports his merits, and announces the successful completion of his tasks, he must do so at Daizong. The East is the place where all things transition from the old to the new. The term *Zong* refers to a chief or elder, signifying that this mountain is the leader of all peaks.

The *Comprehensive Discussions in the White Tiger Hall* <note>original: "Baihu Tong"</note> states that a king who receives the Mandate must perform the <term>Feng and Shan</term> <note>original: "封禪"; these were the most prestigious imperial rituals, performed to justify the emperor's rule to the cosmos.</note> sacrifices. To perform *Feng* means to increase the mountain's height, and to perform *Shan* means to broaden and thicken it. In both cases, the emperor carves stone to record his title and achievements as a testament to his own service. Since Heaven regards height as honorable and Earth regards thickness as virtuous, the height of Mount Tai is increased to show gratitude to Heaven, and the *Shan* ritual is performed at the base of Mount Liangfu to show gratitude to Earth.

The *Ceremonial Etiquette of Han Officials* <note>original: "Hanguan Yi"</note> and the *Record of Mount Tai* state that the winding paths twist and turn as they ascend, consisting of more than fifty switchbacks. From the base to the ancient site of the *Feng* and *Shan* sacrifices, the total distance is roughly forty *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance; 40 li is approximately 12.5 miles or 20 kilometers.</note>. One enters the mountain about two *li* after leaving the city of Tai'an.

From this point, the path twists and winds upward. The mountain peaks are steep and jagged, appearing as if they are snapping at one another. Along the sides are many mountain streams and springs; the sounds of the rushing water alternate with mournful echoes, harmonizing like the blowing of <term>reed pipes</term> <note>original: "jia"; a double-reed wind instrument used in ancient military or ritual music, known for its haunting, shrill sound.</note>.

After a long time, one reaches **Huima Ridge** <note>original: "Huima Ling," literally "Ridge where the Horse Turns Back," signifying the point where the terrain becomes too steep for horses.</note>. Traveling another four *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters or one-third of a mile.</note>, one arrives at **Yuzhang Rock** <note>original: "Yuzhang Yan"</note>. It is also called the Rock of the Imperial Regalia. This is the place where the honor guard stopped during the <term>Feng Sacrifice</term> <note>A supreme ritual performed by emperors to the Heavens at the summit of Mount Tai.</note> performed by the Song Dynasty Emperor at the Yongding Mausoleum. In front of the rock is a massive ravine. At the bottom of the ravine, the white stones are as flat as a whetstone and have the color of jade. Climbing up from the rock leads to a temple; its summit is quite spacious.

Traveling about another three *li*, one reaches **Huangxian** (Yellow Slope). The origin of the name Huangxian is unknown. Here stand five pine trees, which are the so-called **Five Grand-Officers** <note>original: "Wu Dafu." Legend says that the First Emperor of Qin, Qin Shi Huang, granted these trees the official rank of "Grand Officer" after they sheltered him from a sudden storm during his ascent.</note>. Because they are constrained by the rocky soil, they cannot grow lushly, yet they have taken on the gnarled shapes of coiled dragons and serpents.

Going up another *li* or so, one finds the **Hundred-Zhang Cliff**. The hollow of the cliff is as deep as a house. Beside it is a stone cave with a jagged mouth that descends into darkness; the depth of the pit is immeasurable. Having crossed the **Stone Wall Valley**, one encounters three sections of the **Eighteen Bends** <note>original: "Shiba Pan"; a series of steep, grueling staircases that are a signature feature of the climb to the summit.</note>. Passing through a central opening in the rock, one reaches the **Heavenly Gate** <note>original: "Tianmen."</note>.

After walking about another *li*, one arrives at the **Shrine of the Primal Lady** <note>original: "Yuanjun Ci"; dedicated to Bixia Yuanjun, the Sovereign of the Azure Clouds and the daughter of the Great Deity of Mount Tai.</note>. As for the Primal Lady, her origins are unknown. Some say she is the Jade Maiden of Mount Hua. Throughout the world, the prayers...

Those who come to pray for blessings flock to this place. The temple buildings are quite magnificent and grand. Behind them is a stone about three feet tall, carved with two lines of seal script by **Li Si** <note>original: "Li Si zhuan"; Li Si (c. 280–208 BCE) was the Prime Minister of the Qin Dynasty and a master of the Small Seal script.</note>. There is also a stone pool, approximately two feet in length, width, and depth; it is also called the **Jade Maiden’s Hair-Washing Basin** <note>original: "Yunu xitou pen."</note>.

From this point, turning left and ascending for about one *li*, one reaches the **Shrine of the Great Emperor of the Peak** <note>original: "Yuedi ci"; dedicated to the Great Emperor of the Eastern Peak (Dongyue Dadi), the presiding deity of the mountain.</note>. The structure is humble and cramped, unable to accommodate the vast volume of incense offered by pilgrims. Behind it, steep cliffs soar toward the heavens. To the left is the *Mount Tai Inscription of the Kaiyuan Imperial Records*. The characters are in Tang Dynasty **clerical script** <note>original: "Tang li"; a style of calligraphy that reached a peak of elegance during the Tang Dynasty (618–907).</note>, measuring about twenty inches across. They are beautiful, possessing a momentum that seems as if they are flying or in motion. 

To the left of that is the *Ode to the Eastern Sealing* <note>original: "Dongfeng song"; commemorating the ritual sacrifices to Heaven and Earth.</note> composed by the Duke of Yan <note>Referring to Zhang Yue (667–731), a prominent Tang statesman.</note>. The calligraphy is quite elegant and graceful, and it remains legible. There is also an inscription by **Yan Lugong** <note>The honorary title of Yan Zhenqing (709–785), one of the most celebrated calligraphers in Chinese history, known for his bold, upright style.</note>. 

From the basin, heading north for several hundred <term>paces</term> <note>original: "wu"; an ancient measurement of distance roughly equivalent to a double-step.</note>, one reaches the absolute summit, called the **Jade Emperor Shrine** <note>original: "Yuhuang ci"; dedicated to the Jade Emperor, the supreme ruler of Heaven in Taoist theology.</note>. In front of the shrine stands a square, yellow-colored stone pillar about ten feet tall. This is the so-called **Wordless Stele of the First Emperor of Qin** <note>original: "Qin Huang wuzi bei"; according to legend, the First Emperor left this stone blank because his achievements were beyond words, or because he was interrupted by a storm.</note>. This type of stone is not native to this mountain; some claim there is a separate stone tablet running through its center.

Turning east again and descending slightly for a hundred paces, then ascending for another hundred, one finds a stone chamber crowning the height, as tall as the Jade Emperor Shrine. Inside is a dark-colored stone about ten feet square, so lustrous and smooth it can serve as a mirror. It is said to be the place where **Emperor Wu of Han** <note>Reigned 141–87 BCE; he was famous for his numerous pilgrimages to Mount Tai.</note> hid his sacred records.

The *Records of the Gold Mud and Jade Slip* <note>original: "Jinni Yujian"; refers to the ritual documents used by emperors during the "Feng" and "Shan" sacrifices to Heaven and Earth, sealed with gold and jade.</note> states that when white clouds rise from within the sacrificial mound, it is a sign of spiritual resonance. In front of this site, the ground widens slightly; this is the location of the peaks known as **Sun View**, **Qin View**, and **Yue View**.

If one rises at the **fifth watch** <note>Between 3:00 AM and 5:00 AM.</note> to observe the sunrise, it is called the **Sun View** <note>original: "Riguan."</note>. Looking westward to see the lands of the ancient state of **Qin** <note>Modern-day Shaanxi province, hundreds of miles to the west.</note> makes it the **Qin View** <note>original: "Qinguan."</note>. Looking southward toward the lands of **Yue** <note>The coastal regions of Zhejiang and beyond to the south.</note> makes it the **Yue View** <note>original: "Yueguan."</note>.

When the sky clears, the sun plays with shifting colors. To the southeast, at the very limit of one's vision, there is a faint whiteness that shimmers and ripples—this is the **sea**. Directly north and then to the west, one can see a faint stretch of deep blue-green, resembling the battlements of the **Great Wall**; these are the **Taihang**, **Heng**, and **Song** mountain ranges <note>Major mountain systems of Northern and Central China.</note>.

Further off, the **Zi**, **Mian**, **Ji**, and **Si** rivers <note>Four major rivers of the Shandong region.</note> appear like a thousand flowing ribbons layered upon one another. Nearby, all the other mountains look like mere **anthills** <note>original: "Peilou"; a classical term for small mounds or hillocks, emphasizing the height of Mount Tai by comparison.</note>.

Suddenly, each of these nearby mountains emits a single wisp of white cloud, appearing like smoke rising from a tomb. As they rise higher, they grow as large as mats or tables. Hundreds of these paths of mist then race wildly toward one another, merging the moment they meet. Their rising is endless, and their wild racing and merging are the same. In an instant, the entire world below becomes a floor of **white jade** <note>Meaning a solid sea of clouds.</note>. Looking up, the sky remains a brilliant, translucent azure. The colors above and below are completely different. This is truly what is meant by the **"wild horses"** and the **"generative vapors"** <note>original: "Yema" and "Yinyun"; these terms come from the *Zhuangzi* and the *Book of Changes* respectively, describing the shimmering heat-haze and the primordial mist from which life emerges.</note>.

Following the northern shadow of Mount Tai, I arrived at the Divine Rock Temple <note>original: 靈巖寺, Lingyan Temple</note>. Following the mountain stream, I entered a narrow pass where the paths were lined with sandalwood trees. Their bark had peeled away to reveal gnarled, dragon-like knots. 

Upon entering the mountain gate, the forest seemed filled with red cranes. This commemorates an auspicious omen involving two cranes that appeared to the temple's founding master, Sengding <note>original: 法定, also known as Master Fading</note>. Master Sengding was a disciple of Fotudeng <note>A famous 4th-century Central Asian monk and thaumaturge active during the Later Zhao dynasty</note> during the era of the Stone Zhao. 

Originally, the temple had no water source. However, two cranes were seen perching at the foot of the mountain. The Master then performed the ritual of "planting the staff" <term>zhuoxi: the act of a wandering monk striking his staff into the ground to claim a site and, often, to miraculously find water</term>. In the exact spots where the cranes had stood and where the staff was planted, two pools of water bubbled up. These are now known as the "Twin Cranes Spring" and the "Planted Staff Spring."

The Main Hall is a grand structure built with three stories and twenty-eight corners. In the center is an altar representing Mount Sumeru <note>The sacred mountain at the center of the Buddhist universe</note>. The hall is arranged with Guanyin <note>The Bodhisattva of Compassion</note> to the south, the Medicine Buddha to the north, Shakyamuni <note>The historical Buddha</note> to the east, and Amitabha <note>The Buddha of the Western Paradise</note> to the west. Each deity guards their respective cardinal direction within the temple.

To the right of the Main Hall stands an ancient cypress tree; no one knows in what year it was planted. To the west of this broken cypress is a stone cavity in the ground. Its gate is bolted shut and cannot be opened; it is known as the Cave of Lu Ban <note>Named after the legendary patron god of Chinese artisans and engineers</note>. A few dozen paces to the north stands a pagoda thirteen levels high. Its base is said to connect with the cave below; this is the Pratyekabuddha Pagoda <note>original: 辟支塔, Pizhi Pagoda; a pagoda dedicated to "solitary realizers" who attain enlightenment on their own</term>.

Turning north from the "Dragon Sutra" Depository and passing the Thousand Buddha Pavilion, one encounters a mass of iron seven feet high. Its texture resembles the patchwork of a rice paddy field. It is said to be the fossilized robe left behind by an ancient Buddha, and it is known as the "Iron Cassock." To the east of the monastic kitchen <term>Xiangji Kitchen: named after the "Fragrant Accumulation" Buddha, whose realm is made of wonderful scents and food</term> stands a stone turtle six feet high.

The city of Qufu in the state of Lu remains grand and imposing. It sits upon a flat ridge with a circumference of approximately twenty *li* <note>A *li* is a traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters or one-third of a mile.</note>. The current city occupies its southwest corner.

To the south of the old city stands the current Watchtower, while to the north it leans against the Confucian Cemetery. The Temple of Confucius is properly gated. Inside these gates, one finds high walls and grand, spacious halls, with side corridors arranged in orderly wings like the feathers of a bird. Golden fittings adorn the gateways and carved stone monuments serve as foundations; the scale is in no way inferior to a royal palace. Within the Hall of Great Completion <note>The *Dacheng Dian*, the principal sanctum of the Confucian Temple.</note>, statues of the Sages and Worthies are arranged in their proper order. They are depicted wearing <term>Gunmian|The most formal ceremonial dress of ancient China, consisting of a heavy silk robe (gun) and a flat-topped crown (mian) with hanging beads.</term> and are seated. In front of the hall lies the Apricot Altar <note>The *Xingtan*, the site where Confucius is traditionally said to have taught his disciples under the shade of apricot trees.</note>.

Standing to the left of the gate is a juniper with left-spiraling patterns, said to have been planted by the hand of Confucius himself. Its height does not exceed the eaves of the building. During the previous dynasty, this tree miraculously returned to life twice, which is truly extraordinary.

Among the stone monuments inside and outside the gates, the most ancient are two particular steles: one with a twisted-dragon topper and one with a pointed top. #### Great Kunlun Mountain
Great Kunlun Mountain <note>Note that this is not the mythological Kunlun Mountain of the West, but a specific peak located in modern-day Weihai, Shandong Province, significant to the Quanzhen Taoist tradition.</note> is located forty *li* <note>A traditional unit of distance, roughly 500 meters or one-third of a mile.</note> southeast of Ninghai State in Dengzhou Prefecture. #### Mist and Cloud Cave
The Mist and Cloud Cave <note>original: *Yanxia Dong* (烟霞洞)</note> is located on Great Kunlun Mountain. During the Dading era of the Jin dynasty <note>1161–1189 CE.</note>, the Perfected Man Wang Chongyang arrived at Kunlun from Guanxi <note>The region "West of the Pass," referring to the Shaanxi area.</note>. He said to a local resident surnamed Yu: "I once practiced cultivation within the Mist and Cloud Cave on this mountain. Why don't we go up there?" Yu laughed and said: "My family has lived here for generations, and I have never heard of such a cave." They went together to seek it out and indeed found the cave. Above the cave entrance were the two characters for "Mist" and "Cloud." This was considered a great divine miracle, so a shrine was established and a stone monument was inscribed to record the event.

### Taibai Tower

The **Taibai Tower** <note>Named after the legendary poet Li Bai (701–762), whose courtesy name was Taibai.</note> is located within the prefectural city of **Jining**. ### Yanmen Pass

Yanmen Pass is located on Yanmen Mountain in Daizhou, Shanxi. The pass was established here because of the mountain. Among the forty-odd passes in Shanxi, all occupy narrow and strategic points for defense, yet for sheer height and majestic grandeur, Yanmen is the foremost.

Therefore, when <term>Li Mu<note>A prominent general of the State of Zhao (d. 229 BCE) during the Warring States period, famous for his defensive strategies against northern tribes.</note></term> of the Zhao State and <term>Zhi Du<note>A Han dynasty official (fl. 2nd century BCE) renowned for his strictness and his effective defense of the borders against the Xiongnu.</note></term> of the Han Dynasty prepared the border defenses here, the <term>Xiongnu<note>A powerful confederation of nomadic peoples from the Eurasian Steppe who were the primary rivals of early Chinese dynasties.</note></term> did not dare approach the fortress. These men were certainly the finest generals of their respective eras, but one cannot say that the treacherous terrain did not play a vital part in their success.

Leaving Daizhou and traveling north, the path consists entirely of climbing and winding roads. Streams gurgle along the way, and the local people live nestled against the mountains, building their houses at various heights—a scene so complex that it is impossible to capture in an illustration. 

At noon, after ascending the pass and turning west to tread upon the highest ridge, one can gaze out in all four directions. To the east tower the <term>Fanshi<note>Referring to the mountains of Fanshi County.</note></term> and Mount Wutai ranges; to the west, the various mountains of Ningwu wind like a belt. The peaks of Wangyang and Shigu stand tall to the south, while the borderlands of Shuozhou and Mayi lie to the north. Across the long slopes and steep banks, the view stretches boundlessly. One sees high flags fluttering atop the battlements, and the cold forests and ancient fortress lingering under the setting sun. This sight profoundly stirs thoughts of the "Yellow Sands and the Purple Border" <note>original: "紫塞" (Zisai). A classical poetic name for the Great Wall, referring to the purple-hued earth or the color of the fortifications under certain lights.</note>.

### Mount Taibai

Mount Taibai is located forty *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance, approximately 500 meters or one-third of a mile.</note> southeast of the city of Mei County in Xi’an Prefecture. It concentrates the spiritual essence of the <term>Western Metal Mansion</term> <note>Referring to the planet Venus, known in Chinese as *Taibai* ("Great White"), which is associated with the element of Metal and the direction West in traditional cosmology.</note>. Among all the mountains in the <term>Guanzhong</term> <note>The "Land Within the Passes," the fertile central plain of Shaanxi province.</note> region, none are higher than this. 

The mountain peak is so high and cold that no trees or plants grow there. Upon the summit, there are three cast-iron tablets dedicated to the mountain deities, named "Great Afu," "Second Afu," and "Third Afu." There is also a <term>Dragon Pond</term> <note>Original: *Jiuchi* (湫池), referring to high-altitude alpine lakes believed to be the dwellings of dragons or spirits.</note> where the ice remains frozen even during the <term>Three Volts</term> <note>The "Dog Days," the three ten-day periods representing the hottest time of the year.</note>. Because the mountain peak is perpetually covered in snow that never melts, it appears dazzlingly white even in the height of summer; for this reason, it is named "Taibai" (Great White). 

Whenever there is a drought in the Guanzhong region, people climb the mountain to fetch water from the pond <note>This refers to the ritual practice of "inviting water" to trigger rainfall.</note>. However, because the mountain is so high and frigid, with ice and snow constantly frozen, those who are physically weak or wearing thin clothing often die during the ascent. Local folklore claims that the God of Taibai "detains" people there, but this is incorrect <note>The author provides a rationalist explanation, attributing the deaths to hypothermia rather than the supernatural.</note>. At the foot of the mountain lies Ghost Valley; looking up from there, one can see the valley where the Chancellor of the Qin State, He Shu <note>Original: *He Shu* (闔術).</note>, conducted military hunts and maneuvers.

Pan Creek is located eighty *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters or 0.3 miles.</note> southeast of Baoji County in Fengxiang. This is the very spot where the Grand Duke of Zhou <note>original: "周太公" (Zhou Taigong), better known as Jiang Ziya, the legendary strategist who helped overthrow the Shang Dynasty.</note> used to fish. Upon the rocks there, one can still see the imprints left by his two knees. Within the valley, the stone walls are deep and precipitous, and the forests are dense and secluded. In the southeastern corner, there is a stone chamber which was presumably the residence of the Grand Duke. The waters of the creek are clear, cold, and possessed of a wondrous, spiritual quality; they flow northward and empty into the Wei River.

The *Wufu* <note>Likely referring to a specific literary rhapsody or collection; the following poem is famously attributed to the Song Dynasty scholar Su Shi.</note> contains a poem praising Pan Creek:

*Entering Pan Creek at night is like entering a mountain gorge,*
*The torches illuminating the path startle the monkeys, making them fall.*
*A solitary moon atop the mountain remains bright and shining;*
*At dawn, the cold waves crashing against the rocks grow even louder.*

There is also a poem regarding the stone:

*I have heard tell of the stone at Pan Creek,*
*Which still rests by the head of the Wei River.*
*Upon the dark cliffs, the knee-prints seem to remain,*
*But the great angler’s line originally had no hook.* <note>This refers to the legend that Jiang Ziya fished with a straight needle or a hookless line, claiming he was not fishing for fish, but waiting for a wise ruler to find him.</note>

Mount Chiuchi <term>Chouchi Shan; a mountain in modern-day Gansu province, historically significant as a stronghold for the Di people during the Six Dynasties period</term> is located one hundred *li* <note>Approximately 50 kilometers or 31 miles</note> west of Cheng County in Gongchang Prefecture. The mountain's peaks reach a height of ten thousand *ren* <note>An ancient unit of depth or height, roughly equal to 7 or 8 feet</note>, extending from the direction of Lizhou. Its eastern and western gates are connected by a winding road approximately seven *li* long.

The *Records of the Yuanhe Era* <note>A Tang dynasty geographical treatise</note> quotes the "Biographies of the Di People" from the *History of the Song Dynasty*, stating that Mount Chiuchi covers an area of one hundred *qing* <note>roughly 1,600 acres</note>. Its four sides are steep and sheer like a wall. At the summit is a high, level plateau extending over twenty *li*. There is a narrow, winding road known as a "sheep's gut path" that contains thirty-six switchbacks.

On the mountain, there is a spring of abundant water. The local soil can be boiled to produce salt.

Fan Ye’s *Book of the Later Han* <note>A 5th-century historical text covering the history of the Eastern Han dynasty</note> records that the Hechi area covers one hundred square *li*, and the people living in the surrounding area are known as the Baima <note>original: "White Horse"</note> tribe.

**Golden Ox Gorge** is located fifty *li* <note>Approximately 25 kilometers</note> north of Ningqiang Prefecture in Hanzhong. It is also known as the **Five Heroes Gorge** <note>original: *Wuding Xia*. The "Five Heroes" or *Wuding* were legendary strongmen of the ancient Shu Kingdom</note>.

King Hui of Qin <note>Reigned 338–311 BCE; the first ruler of Qin to style himself "King"</note> plotted to conquer the state of Shu <note>A wealthy, isolated kingdom in the Sichuan basin</note>, but he was troubled by the narrowness and danger of the mountain passes. He therefore had five stone oxen constructed, and every day he ordered gold to be placed beneath their tails. He spread a deceptive rumor that the oxen were capable of excreting gold <note>original: *fen jin*</note>, intending to use them as a "gift" to entice the King of Shu. 

The King of Shu, who prided himself on his physical strength but was possessed by greed, commanded five strongmen to carve a path through the mountains to lead the oxen into his territory. Qin subsequently dispatched the strategists **Zhang Yi** and **Sima Cuo** to lead their armies along this road to conquer and destroy Shu.

A poem by Hu Zeng <note>The text attributes him to the Song Dynasty, though he was a well-known poet of the late Tang Dynasty</note> says:

->A thousand peaks and mountain ranges surround the gates of Shu,<-
->Chengdu is a world unto itself.<-
->If the Five Heroes had not carved the Golden Ox road,<-
->How could King Hui of Qin ever have annexed the land?<-
