-># **Deng Yu**<-

**Deng Yu**, whose <term>courtesy name</term><note>The "style name" given at adulthood; original: 仲華 (Zhonghua).</note> was **Zhonghua**, was a native of Xinye in Nanyang. He served his mother with the utmost filial piety. 

When <term>Emperor Guangwu</term><note>The founder of the Eastern Han Dynasty, Liu Xiu (r. 25–57 CE).</note> was pacifying the regions north of the Yellow River, Yu set out on horseback to seek an audience with him. He advised the future emperor to recruit heroes from all over the land and to make it his primary mission to win the hearts of the common people. 

Emperor Guangwu was greatly pleased; he placed a portion of his army under Yu's command and dispatched him to pacify the Hedong region. When the Emperor formally ascended the throne, he appointed Yu as the <term>Minister over the Masses</term><note>One of the "Three Ducal Ministers," the highest civil administrative posts in the Han government; original: 司徒 (Situ).</note> and enfeoffed him as the **Marquis of Cuo**. After his death, he was granted the <term>posthumous title</term><note>An honorary name given to royalty and high officials after their death; original: 謚 (shi).</note> **Marquis Yuan**.

**Kou Xun**, whose <term>courtesy name</term><note>A name given at adulthood to be used by peers and colleagues; original: 字 (zi).</note> was Ziyi, once consulted and deliberated on strategy with <term>Deng Yu</term><note>The primary advisor to Emperor Guangwu; original: 鄧禹.</note>. Deng Yu was greatly impressed by his extraordinary talents. Later, when <term>Emperor Guangwu</term><note>The founding emperor of the Eastern Han Dynasty (r. 25–57 CE); original: 光武.</note> was pacifying the regions to the south and secured <term>Henei</term><note>A strategically vital commandery north of the Yellow River; original: 河內.</note>, he found it difficult to select a governor capable of defending it.

Deng Yu advised, "Kou Xun is fully equipped with both civil and military virtues; no one but this man can be entrusted with such a task." Guangwu then appointed Kou Xun as the Grand Protector of Henei. When Guangwu ascended the throne, the army’s food supplies were in dire shortage, but Kou Xun organized the transport of provisions so effectively that the flow never ceased.

When the Emperor’s carriage set out on a southern expedition, Kou Xun followed him as far as <term>Yingchuan</term><note>A region in modern-day Henan province; original: 穎川.</note>. The common people there blocked the path of the imperial procession and cried out: "We beg Your Majesty to lend us Lord Kou for just one more year!" <note>This "borrowing of a governor" became a famous historical idiom for a beloved official; original: 借寇 (jie kou).</note>

Later, Gao Jun, a general under the warlord <term>Wei Xiao</term><note>A rival leader in the west during the early Han transition; original: 隗囂.</note>, sent his military strategist <term>Huangfu Wen</term> to meet with Kou Xun. Huangfu Wen’s speech and etiquette were arrogant and unyielding. In a fit of rage, Kou Xun ordered his execution. Shortly after, Gao Jun came out of his fortifications and surrendered. Kou Xun remarked: "Huangfu Wen was Gao Jun’s heart and soul; by killing him, I caused Gao Jun to lose his courage. That is why he surrendered."

Kou Xun was profoundly learned in the Confucian Classics and refined in his conduct. His reputation was held in high regard at the Imperial Court, and his contemporaries considered him to have the <term>caliber of a Chancellor</term><note>Meaning he possessed the administrative and moral depth required to serve as the highest official in the empire; original: 宰相器 (zaixiang qi).</note>.

Ma Yuan, whose **courtesy name** <note>An additional name given at adulthood to be used by peers and colleagues; original: 字 (zi).</note> was **Wenyuan**, was a native of Maoling in Fufeng. His ancestors were the descendants of **Lord Mafu**, and the family took their surname from his title <note>original: 馬服君; Lord Mafu was the title of Zhao She, a famous general of the State of Zhao during the Warring States period. His descendants adopted the first character of the title, Ma (Horse), as their family name.</note>.

Ma Yuan was orphaned at the age of twelve. It was said that his eyes were as striking as a painting, and even from a young age, he possessed great ambition. **Emperor Guangwu** summoned him to serve as the **General of the Household**.

When the woman **Zheng Ce** <note>Known in Vietnam as Trưng Trắc, she and her sister led a major rebellion against Han Dynasty rule.</note> rebelled in **Jiaozhi** <note>An ancient Han province in what is now northern Vietnam.</note>, Ma Yuan was appointed as the **General who Subdues the Waves** <note>original: 伏波將軍 (Fubo Jiangjun). This became Ma Yuan's most famous title, and he is often referred to simply as "General Fubo."</note>. When news of his victory arrived at court, he was granted the title of **Marquis of Xinxi**.

During the **Yongping** era, Ma Yuan's daughter was established as Empress <note>She became the influential Empress Ma, known for her humility and refusal to grant titles to her own kin.</note>. During the reign of **Emperor Ming** (posthumously known as Xianzong), when portraits were commissioned to honor the meritorious officials who served during the **Jianwu** era, Ma Yuan alone was excluded because he was a **relative of the Empress** <note>original: 椒房親 (Pepper Chamber relative). To avoid the appearance of nepotism or favoritism toward the Empress's family, Emperor Ming did not include his father-in-law in the "Cloud Terrace 28 Generals," the official gallery of the dynasty's founding heroes.</note>.

When **Geng Yan** served as a general, Emperor **Guangwu** remarked that his achievements were comparable to those of **Han Xin**. <note>original: "耿弇" (Geng Yan) was a brilliant military strategist and one of the "28 Generals of Yuntai" who helped establish the Eastern Han Dynasty. "韓信" (Han Xin) was the most famous general of the earlier Western Han founding.</note>

In former times, when Han Xin met with **Gaozu**, he discussed the strengths of **Xiang Yu**, explaining how that apparent strength could easily be turned into weakness; he asserted that the region of the **Three Qins** could be pacified merely by sending out a formal proclamation. <note>original: "高祖" (Gaozu) refers to Liu Bang, the founder of the Western Han. The <term>Three Qins</term> (Sanquan) refers to the strategic heartland of the former Qin Empire, which had been divided among three surrendered generals.</note>

Similarly, when Geng Yan met with Emperor Guangwu, he requested to head north to gather the troops of **Shanggu**, pacify the rebel **Peng Chong** in **Yuyang**, capture **Zhang Feng** in **Zhuo Commandery**, return to secure **Fuping** and **Huosuo**, and then strike east against **Zhang Bu** to pacify the region of **Qi**.

When Gaozu was in **Hanzhong** and Guangwu was in **Chunling**, their respective states were extremely small and their armies were at their weakest. At that time, various warlords were expanding their power like owls spreading their wings and striking like falcons; among the many interconnected cities and towns, it was not yet known who would emerge as the victor. <note>The phrase "not yet known who would emerge as the victor" (original: "未知雌雄") literally translates to "not yet knowing which was female and which was male," a classic idiom for a contested struggle.</note>

These two generals [Han Xin and Geng Yan] both anticipated certain victory, and they did not merely speak of it—they executed their plans. They acted until success was achieved, and every single one of their predictions was verified. When Geng Yan first proposed his strategy, even Emperor Guangwu could not be certain of its success, yet Geng Yan alone remained convinced. In pacifying commanderies and storming cities, he never once suffered the slightest setback.

# Yang Zhen

**Yang Zhen**, whose courtesy name was **Boqi**, was a man who loved learning from a young age. <note>original: "楊震字伯起" (Yang Zhen zi Boqi). A "courtesy name" (zi) was a name given at adulthood to be used by peers and superiors as a sign of respect.</note> He mastered the Confucian Classics and read widely across many subjects. For many years, he lived as a guest in the region of **Hu**, where he refused to accept any official appointments or ritual invitations from the provincial or commandery governments. <note>original: "湖" (Hu) refers to the area of Lake Hongnong in modern Shaanxi province.</note>

It was not until he reached the age of fifty that the General-in-Chief **Deng Zhi** heard of his reputation for virtue and summoned him to office. <note>original: "鄧騭" (Deng Zhi) was one of the most powerful officials of the Eastern Han, serving as a regent for several years.</note> Through a series of five promotions, he eventually rose to the position of Governor of **Zhuo Commandery**. <note>original: "涿郡太守" (Zhuo Jun Taishou). Zhuo Commandery was a significant administrative region located near modern-day Beijing.</note>

By nature, Yang Zhen was impartial and incorruptible. He strictly refused to receive private visits or requests for favors. His children and grandchildren lived modestly, frequently eating only simple vegetable meals and traveling on foot rather than in carriages. When his old friends or elders suggested that he should establish some property or commercial interests to provide for his family's future, Zhen refused, saying: 

"If I allow future generations to refer to my descendants as the children of an **Incorruptible Official**, is this not a generous enough legacy to leave them?" <note>original: "清白吏" (Qingbai li). Literally translated as "clean and white official," this was a title of the highest honor for a bureaucrat, signifying someone who never took a bribe or used their position for personal gain.</note>

<summary>This page records the biography of Yang Zhen, an Eastern Han official famous for his refusal to use his political power for personal or family wealth. It highlights his philosophy of leaving a "legacy of integrity" rather than material riches to his descendants.</summary>

**Li Ying**, a celebrated official and intellectual leader of the **Eastern Han** dynasty, is depicted in this traditional woodcut illustration. <note>original: "李膺" (Li Ying). He was a pivotal figure during the "Partisan Proscriptions," a period of intense political struggle where he led a faction of scholar-officials against corrupt palace eunuchs.</note>

Known as a preeminent **illustrious scholar**, Li Ying was so deeply respected that younger students and fellow officials considered being accepted as his guest a supreme honor, famously comparing it to "climbing the Dragon's Gate." <note>original: "名士" (mingshi). This term refers to the "scholar-gentry" or "gentlemen of the elite" who were admired for their character and learning. The "Dragon's Gate" (Longmen) refers to a mythical achievement of great difficulty and prestige.</note>

# Portrait of Guo Linzong

**Guo Tai**, who is widely known by his **courtesy name** <term>zi; a name given at adulthood to be used by peers to show respect</term> **Linzong**, was a native of Jiexiu in Taiyuan <note>Present-day Shanxi Province</note>. <note>original: "郭林宗, 郭泰, 像, 名士, 東漢"</note>

Linzong was a man of great stature and presence. He was a leader of the **"Pure Critique"** <term>Qingyi; a movement of scholars and students who evaluated the moral conduct of officials to combat corruption in the imperial court</term>. Unlike others who sought to isolate themselves from the world, Linzong traveled extensively to the capital to lecture, where he was admired by thousands of students and high-ranking officials alike.

He was most famous for his ability in **"Character Appraisal"** <term>Renlun; the traditional practice of observing and judging a person's inner virtue and potential</term>. It was said that any young scholar who received a positive word from Linzong would instantly become famous throughout the empire.

One famous anecdote tells of a time Linzong was caught in the rain, and one corner of his headcloth became folded down. Because he was so deeply respected, the scholars of the capital began to intentionally fold their headcloths in the same way, creating a fashion known as the **"Linzong Kerchief"** <note>This "folded-corner headcloth" (zhejiaojin) became a lasting literary symbol of a refined and influential scholar</note>. 

Despite numerous invitations from the imperial court to serve as an official, he declined them all, preferring to remain a teacher and a moral compass for society. When he passed away, it is recorded that thousands of people attended his funeral, and the famous scholar **Cai Yong** composed his memorial tablet, remarking that Linzong was the only man whose virtues he could praise without feeling he was exaggerating.

# Guo Tai

**Guo Tai**, whose **courtesy name** <term>zi; a formal name given at adulthood to be used by peers</term> was **Linzong**, was a native of Jiexiu in Taiyuan <note>Located in modern-day Shanxi Province</note>. Fan Pang, whose courtesy name was **Mengbo**, practiced strict moral integrity from his youth and was highly respected by the people of his home region. He was appointed as a **Pure Proclamation Envoy** <term>qingzhaoshi; an imperial inspector tasked with rooting out corruption</term> to investigate the administration of Jizhou.

Later, in Runan, he was caught up in the **Proscription of the Partisans** <term>danghuo; a series of political purges where the ruling eunuchs arrested and executed scholar-officials</term>. He was summoned to prison for interrogation. Bound in **three-fold wooden shackles** <term>sanmu; heavy restraints for the neck, hands, and feet</term> and with a hood over his head, he was left exposed at the foot of the court steps.

He declared: "On the day I die, bury me at the foot of **Mount Shouyang** <note>Mount Shouyang is the legendary site where the sages Boyi and Shuqi died of starvation rather than serve an unjust ruler.</note>. When he was first arrested, his mother bid him a final farewell, saying: "Now that your name is ranked alongside the great scholars **Li and Du** <note>original: "李杜" (Li Du). Pang knelt to receive his mother's words, bowed twice, and departed. Turning to his son, he said: "If I were to tell you to do evil, you would know that evil is not to be done; yet if I tell you to do good—well, I have done nothing but good, and look what has become of me." Those who heard his words were moved to tears.

**Ban Chao**, whose courtesy name was **Zhongsheng**, was a man of great ambition. He was exceptionally diligent and articulate, having read widely across various historical texts and classics. ## Jia Fu <gloss>He possessed a prowess that could deter enemies from a thousand miles away. He defeated the **Qingdu** rebels at **Shequan** and crushed the **Five Garrisons** <note>rebel military groups during the early Eastern Han</note>; his military merits were vast.</gloss>

Although the various ministers collectively recommended that they should be appointed as Prime Ministers, the Emperor did not employ them in that capacity. They harbored no resentment. Is this not a perfect example of understanding the principle of when to advance and when to withdraw?

## Zhuo Mao

His virtue was paramount. He was broad-minded, benevolent, respectful, and loving. He was tranquil, found joy in the **Way** <note>the Dao</note>, and lived without competing with others. Although his fellow villagers differed in their conduct and abilities, they all loved and admired him. He once served as a local official and treated the people as if they were his own children. He promoted the good and educated the wayward; no evil word ever left his mouth. The officials and commoners loved him so dearly they could not bear to deceive him. When he first arrived in the county and abolished or established certain regulations, the local officials and people laughed at him. When the commandery of **Henan** appointed a supervisor to oversee him, Zhuo Mao felt no resentment and continued his duties with composure. After several years, his moral transformation of the people was so successful that **nothing dropped on the road was picked up by others** <note>an idiom indicating a state of perfect social order and honesty</note>. He was promoted to **Assistant to the Capital** <term>Jingdu Cheng</term>. The people of **Mi County** wept as they followed him all the way to the capital. During the regency of **Wang Mang**, he resigned due to illness. When Emperor Guangwu ascended the throne, he first sought out Zhuo Mao and appointed him as the **Grand Tutor** <term>Taifu; one of the highest honorary and advisory positions in the empire</term> to once again honor his great virtue.

## **Zhongchang Tong**

His style name was **Gongli**. He read widely in the records and histories. He possessed great character but did not pride himself on minor matters of etiquette. He was unpredictable in his speech and silence; people of his time sometimes called him a "mad scholar." ## **Shentu Pan**

His style name was **Zilong**. His family was poor, and he worked as a hired lacquerer. When Guo Linzong saw him, he found him extraordinary. Pan read the Five Classics extensively and was also well-versed in the apocryphal charts and prophecies <term>túwěi; mystical texts used to interpret the Confucian classics</term>. He lived at the Imperial Academy with **Wang Ziju** of Jiyin. When Ziju was on his deathbed, he entrusted his affairs to Pan. Pan personally pushed the funeral cart to return the remains to Ziju's hometown. Before this, the traveling scholars of the capital, such as **Fan Pang** of Runan, had been criticizing the imperial administration. The students at the Academy believed that Pan would be recruited into office along with the other "retired scholars," but Pan went into hiding between the regions of Liang and Dang. He built a house among the trees and lived as a common laborer. Two years later, Fan Pang and the others were indeed caught in the **Proscription of the Partisans** <term>dànggù; a series of political purges where scholars were banned from office</term>. Only Pan remained detached and escaped the judgment of the critics.

**Xie Lingyun** was exceptionally clever and insightful from the time he was a young child. Cheng Lingxi, whose courtesy name <term>zi</term> <note>The "zi," or courtesy name, was a name bestowed upon reaching adulthood, to be used by peers and colleagues as a mark of respect</note> was Xuandi, was a native of Haining in Xin’an. ## Master Wenzhong, Wang Tong

Wang Tong, whose <term>zi</term> <note>The "zi," or courtesy name, was a name given at adulthood to be used by peers as a mark of respect</note> was Zhongyan, was known posthumously as **Master Wenzhong**. In his youth, he was a devoted student, moved by a profound and passionate ambition to bring broad salvation and prosperity to the common people.

He traveled west to the capital of Chang’an to seek an audience with **Emperor Wen of the Sui** <note>The founding emperor of the Sui Dynasty (r. 581–604 AD), known for reunifying China</note>, where he presented his "Twelve Policies for Great Peace." However, his suggestions were not adopted by the throne. Despite this, the influential official **Yang Su** <note>A powerful general and statesman of the Sui period</note> held him in very high regard and treated him with great ceremony and respect.

Wang Tong eventually retired to the region of the **He and Fen rivers** <note>Located in modern-day Shanxi province</note> to teach his disciples. He dedicated himself to the study of the *Classic of Poetry* and the *Book of Documents* and worked to refine the **Six Classics** <note>The foundational texts of the Confucian tradition: Poetry, Documents, Rites, Music, Changes, and the Spring and Autumn Annals</note>. Prominent figures such as Dong Chang, **Fang Xuanling**, and **Du Ruhui** <note>Fang and Du later became the most celebrated chancellors of the Tang Dynasty, credited with its early success</note> all "faced north" <note>A traditional expression meaning to acknowledge someone as a master or teacher</note> to receive his teachings on the **Way of the King’s Advisor** <note>original: "王佐之道" (Wangzuo zhi dao); the art of statecraft and the moral philosophy required to serve as a high-level advisor to a monarch</note>. It is estimated that he had over a thousand such students.
