# PREFACE

<margin>The concealment of certain plans, and their silent delivery to friends.</margin>
We have heard and seen what many more recent authors have taught through numerous and most illustrious examples. Since these are well enough known to the learned—for whom these works are primarily published—we judge it unnecessary to bring them forward now. However, it is no small part of political, military, and other arts of that kind to hide those plans which, if they became known to the enemy, could harm us; and to be able to communicate them to friends in such a way that they cannot be discovered by any means, whether by a dishonest courier or by the enemies themselves, should the messages happen to be intercepted. For this reason, certain wise men, both ancient and modern, have devised several clever <term>strategems<note>original: "strategemata"; here referring to tactical ruses or methods of secret signaling</note></term> for that purpose, as well as for other ways of secretly signaling the secrets of the mind.

<margin>Commendation of Porta's books.</margin>
As for who has collected, devised, and published more numerous and ingenious methods, there is no one alive today, as far as I know, who surpasses the most learned man **Giambattista della Porta**. Indeed, regarding the published writings of the ancients on this subject, it is difficult to know what they were truly like. Without a doubt, very many of the ancients possessed this art, as can be seen in histories where it was occasionally put into actual practice; traces also remain in the writings of some, such as those of **Ovid**, which are also cited by **Porta**. <margin>Utility.</margin>
Certainly, the *Polygraphia* of Trithemius <note>Johannes Trithemius (1462–1516), author of the first printed book on cryptography</note> is not to be compared with these books of Porta, neither in the diligence of its method, nor in the multitude of its types, nor in the clarity of its explanation: although we would not deny that the reading of both works can be combined with profit. 

<margin>Utility</margin>
And indeed, if for no other reason, Porta must be read thoroughly by those especially eager for wisdom in military and civil affairs <note>original: "rei militaris civilisque"</note>, and even by all those who have a mind to peer into the secrets of others. For while it is indeed in our power not to use this art ourselves, we cannot prevent adversaries, enemies, and others from employing it. But if it should happen (a matter which those devoted to military and civil affairs must labor over greatly) that hostile stratagems or other writings of that kind should fall into our hands: with Porta’s help, we will be able to recognize and uncover them. <margin>4. Utility.</margin>
What of the fact that they render most clear very many passages of ancient writers speaking of this art, or derived from it, which are often quite obscure? This will, without a doubt, be most welcome to all right judges of matters and students of antiquity.
<margin>5. Utility.</margin>
Furthermore, merchants, chemists <note>original: "chymici" — likely referring to alchemists who used secret ciphers to hide their formulas</note>, and others who desire to cover their affairs in envelopes of darkness, will find here a much more abundant harvest for that purpose than before. But since these books will commend themselves enough and more, let us leave this part and say a few words now about the title, which has been changed by us. In this matter, we do not deny that it was indicated to us from what weighty authors Mr. Porta <note>Giambattista della Porta (1535–1615), the famous Italian polymath and author of this work</note> took the occasion for his title; nevertheless, because the translation of "Stealthy" <note>original: "furtivi" — referring to Porta's original title "De Furtivis Literarum Notis"</note> is less pleasing to readers, and the title of "Ciphers" <note>original: "Ziferarum"</note> was hardly necessary, we have taken care to substitute the one that pleased us, as it seemed both more acceptable and more complete.

Concerning certain examples.</margin> Furthermore, regarding what we said we would advise in the fourth place concerning certain examples, the matter stands as follows. For the author <note>original: "autor"</note> is accustomed to bring forward examples of two kinds: those of others and his own. As for those belonging to others—namely, those of ancient writers—he seems protected by the common right of all translators <note>original: "interpretum"</note>; for they are accustomed to present such things exactly as they found them and not otherwise. Indeed, if this were not done, they would be performing the role of authors rather than translators, or even that of forgers. However, those examples which he devises himself are sometimes of such a nature that we would have entirely preferred others. But since the law of Christian charity <note>original: "lex Christianæ charitatis"</note> commands that all things be interpreted in the best possible light, we judge that they were not brought forward so that anyone might seize an opportunity from them to dare similar things, but rather so that one might recognize the things practiced by the wicked <note>original: "improbis"</note> and might discourage and hinder them. For in this same way, many wicked things are reviewed in certain other excellent books: not so that one might imitate them, but so that we may learn to avoid and prohibit them with all the greater caution. Just as we point out deviations and dangerous places to those about to take a journey, lest otherwise, being ignorant of them, they might accidentally fall into loss or danger, or [fail to warn] others hurrying toward the same...
