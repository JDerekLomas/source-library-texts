# The Chou Tree

<term>Chou <note>original: "椆" (Chou). This refers to the *Quercus glauca*, commonly known as the Ring-cupped Oak or Blue Japanese Oak. It is an evergreen tree highly valued in Southern China for its incredibly hard, durable wood.</note></term>

According to the **Gazetteer of Xinhua County**, the *Classic of Mountains* <note>original: "Shanjing" (山經). An ancient Chinese text describing geography and mythology.</note> states that Hushou Mountain is home to many Chou trees. The *Shuowen Jiezi* <note>The first Chinese dictionary to analyze characters and their origins, compiled in the 2nd century.</note> simply defines it as a tree, while the *Leipian* <note>An 11th-century character dictionary.</note> notes that it endures the cold without withering. Today, its common name is the **Liangshan Tree**. It has many branches and its foliage stands tall and elegant like a canopy; the leaves are a deep, dark green and remain lush throughout the winter.

The **Gazetteer of Shaoyang County** records that there are two varieties of Chou: the Red and the White. The Red is considered superior, while the White is of secondary quality. The texture of the wood is hard, yet its nature is flexible. When crafting utensils or tools from this wood, it must be submerged in water for a full year to become truly solid and stable; if this is not done, the wood will quickly warp and crack after a short time.

The **Gazetteer of Chenxi County** also notes the Red and White varieties. The white type is locally called the <gloss>Haojing Chou</gloss> <note>Meaning "Brushwood" or "Common" Chou.</note>, while the red type is known as the <gloss>Yan Chou</gloss> <note>Meaning "Rock" Chou, implying a harder, mountain-grown variety.</note>. Its grain is straight and firm, making it suitable for carrying-poles and palanquins; the largest specimens are used to build heavy oil presses.

**Commentary:** Just as Camphor is the primary wood of Jiangxi, the Chou tree is the source of nearly all household utensils throughout Hunan, and its reputation has spread both near and far. However, while Camphor wood is found throughout the Jiangnan region <note>The lands south of the Yangtze River.</note> up until the southern mountain ridges, the Chou tree is virtually unheard of outside of Hunan. The character for its name is sometimes written as *Gou* (構), but the *Xinhua County Gazetteer* follows the *Classic of Mountains* by using *Chou* (椆), which is more accurate. The wood is heavy and hard, durable, and resistant to insects. Its leaves resemble those of the Camphor tree—though slightly smaller—and also bear a resemblance to the Camellia.

### The <term>Chou Tree <note>original: "稠" (Chou). Specifically the *Quercus glauca*, or Ring-cupped Oak. It is prized for its extremely hard wood, often used in making tools and axles.</note></term>

The bark of the trunk is smooth and grayish-black, while the wood grain resembles that of the Chestnut but runs at a slanted angle. The **Gazetteer of Shaoyang County** states that the wood must be submerged in water for a full year before it becomes truly hard and solid. However, it is not realized that all bamboo and wood intended for making utensils should ideally be soaked in water. This process allows the "vital energy" <note>Referring here to the raw sap and internal moisture of the living plant.</note> to be exhausted and the juices to drain away. Only after this treatment can the wood be properly worked with axes and chisels. Otherwise, if the wood is dried only by the wind, it will attract wood-boring insects; if it is subjected to damp heat, it will sprout fungi. This is a universal truth for all plants and is not unique to the Chou tree.

***

The **Gazetteer of Yongshun Prefecture** records that local paper is produced in all four counties of the region. It is made from the bark of the <term>Chu tree <note>original: "楮" (Chu). The *Broussonetia papyrifera*, commonly known as the Paper Mulberry. Its inner bark has been used for centuries across East Asia to produce high-quality paper.</note></term>. The better varieties are somewhat white, yet they remain coarse and rough, making them unsuitable for fine calligraphy. Nevertheless, this confirms that the Chu tree can indeed be utilized for papermaking.

# The <term>Chinese Pistache <note>original: "黃連木" (Huanglianmu). *Pistacia chinensis*. It is named for the yellow color of its inner wood, which resembles the medicinal Coptis root (Huanglian).</note></term>

The **Chinese Pistache** is found in abundance throughout Jiangxi and the Huguang region <note>A historical province covering modern Hubei and Hunan.</note>. It grows into a massive tree, reaching several *zhang* <note>One *zhang* is approximately 3.3 meters or 11 feet.</note> in height and requiring several arm-spans to encircle its trunk. Its leaves resemble those of the **Chinese Toon** <note>original: "椿" (Chun). *Toona sinensis*.</note> but are smaller. 

In early spring, the new buds appear with a faint reddish-yellow hue. People compete to gather these buds to pickle them for food or dry them in the sun to be used for beverages. The flavor is initially bitter but leaves a sweet aftertaste, much like an olive. During the sweltering summer months, it is effective for clearing internal heat and promoting the production of body fluids. The people of Hangzhou cook the buds together with licorice and green plums; when eaten this way, all five flavors are present.

According to the **Famine Relief Herbal** <note>original: "救荒本草" (Jiuhuang Bencao). A 15th-century treatise by Zhu Xiao describing plants suitable for consumption during food shortages.</note>, this tree is also called the **Huangdong Tree** <note>Literally "Yellow Pillar Tree."</note>. It grows in the southern mountains and wilderness of Zhengzhou. Its leaves are like newly sprouted Toon leaves but are extremely small; they also resemble the leaves of the **Chinaberry** <note>original: "楝" (Lian). *Melia azedarach*.</note>. The foliage is slightly yellow in color. It produces purple-red flowers and bears seeds the size of peas—green when young and turning red or purple when ripe. 

The leaves have a bitter taste. One must gather the tender buds and leaves and **scald** <term>original: "煠" (Zha). A culinary technique of briefly boiling or blanching ingredients in water to remove bitterness or prepare them for seasoning.</term> them until cooked, then soak them in water to draw out the bitter flavor. They can then be seasoned with oil and salt for a meal. The buds may also be steamed and sun-dried to be brewed as a tea. 

The physical form and utility of the tree are identical across regions, though it is considered unusual that its flowers and fruit are rarely seen in the south. The grain of the wood is solid and firm. The **General Gazetteer of Guangxi** records that the Chinese Pistache is produced in every prefecture and county and is renowned for its durability. This is the wood referred to in the **Miscellaneous Records of the Southern Ridges** as the "Shengdaifang" <note>original: "勝岱方". A regional term likely denoting a superior or highly durable grade of timber.</note>.

## Chinese Pistache <note>original: "黃連木" (Huanglianmu). *Pistacia chinensis*.</note>

Among various records, only the *Gazetteer of Xiangtan County* considers this tree to be the same as the **Kai Wood** <note>original: "楷木" (Kaimu). Historically, this refers to the trees planted at the tomb of Confucius, often identified as the Chinese Pistache.</note>; it is unknown what source they based this on. The people of the Chu region <note>Modern-day Hubei and Hunan provinces.</note> pronounce the word *lian* similarly to *rao*; the character is sometimes written as *jie* <note>original: "捷" (victory/quickness).</note> or *ou* <note>original: "鷗" (seagull).</note>. In the spring, villagers pluck the new buds to sell in the cities, calling them **Golden Oriole Buds** <note>original: "黃鸝芽" (Huangliya), likely named for their bright color.</note>.

According to the **Fivefold Miscellany** <note>original: "五雜俎" (Wuzazu). An encyclopedic book of observations by the Ming dynasty scholar Xie Zhaozhe.</note>, there is **Kai Wood** in the Kong Forest <note>The cemetery of Confucius and his descendants in Qufu, Shandong.</note> in Qufu, which tradition says was planted by the hand of **Zigong** <note>One of the most prominent disciples of Confucius, known for his loyalty and business acumen.</note>. That tree once measured over ten arm-spans in circumference, but it has since died. Its descendants have spread and grown quite numerously. 

The buds of this tree are fragrant and bitter; they can be brewed as a tea substitute, or dried and eaten as a preserved vegetable. Its wood can be used to make ceremonial tablets, pillows, and Go boards. It is said that when the wood is struck, the sound is very resonant and it does not crack—this is why it is so suitable for Go boards. Sleeping on a pillow made from it is said to prevent nightmares, which is why it is prized for pillows. 

Because this wood is a relic associated with sages and worthies, local officials daily harvest it to manufacture items to be given as gifts. Today, those that remain are few and far between. It is quite a pity that this tree, unlike the trees of **Shangqiu**, cannot live out its natural lifespan because it is too "talented" <note>A reference to a famous story in the *Zhuangzi* about a "useless" tree in Shangqiu that survives to a great age because its wood is too twisted for carpenters to use. Here, the author laments that the Chinese Pistache's usefulness leads to its destruction.</note>.

Note: The description of the buds' fragrant and bitter flavor seems to confirm this is indeed the **Chinese Pistache**. Perhaps the author of the *Xiangtan Gazetteer* was originally from the Lu region <note>Modern Shandong, the home of Confucius.</note> and thus recognized the tree's true identity.

### Blue Oak <note>original: "青岡樹" (Qinggangshu). *Cyclobalanopsis glauca*, also known as the Ring-cupped Oak.</note>

The *Materia Medica for Relief of Famine* <note>original: "救荒本草" (Jiuhuang Bencao). Among these trees, those that grow large and bear **acorn cups** <term>acorn cup (橡斗): The woody, cup-shaped base that holds the oak nut.</term> are known as **Oriental Oaks** <note>original: "橡櫟" (Xiangli). # Observations on Wild Silkworms and Tree Varieties

Silkworms of the **Pagoda tree** <note>original: "槐" (Huai). *Sophora japonica*, or the Chinese Scholar Tree.</note> are as small as ants, while the moths found on the **Elm** <note>original: "榆" (Yu).</note> resemble grasshoppers or leeches. The cocoons of both are as flimsy as spider webs and are entirely unfit for weaving.

The cocoons of the **Tree of Heaven** <note>original: "樗" (Chu). *Ailanthus altissima*, often called the "stinking cedar."</note> contain very little silk. These insects bind several leaves together to form a hollow nest and curl up inside. If you pluck these nests and toss them aside, the silkworms will continue to move, dragging their leafy dwellings along with them. How could silkworms such as these ever produce the delicate, **dangling threads** <note>original: "裊裊" (Niaoniao). Describes the graceful, slender, and swaying nature of high-quality silk.</note> required for fine fabric?

The **Oak** <note>original: "橡" (Xiang).</note> tree is sturdy and robust. Its wood is brown, and its leaves are stiff and glossy. Those varieties that do not bear fruit <note>meaning they do not produce acorns</note> are called **Blue Oaks** <note>original: "青岡" (Qinggang). *Cyclobalanopsis glauca*.</note>. Because the silkworms eat these leaves, they take on the tree's characteristics; consequently, the silk they produce is exceptionally strong and naturally brown in color.

Lu Yuanke <note>A scholar or local authority cited for his botanical observations.</note> stated: 
"The **Mountain Ailanthus** and the **Lowland Ailanthus** are essentially the same, yet people refuse to call them 'Ailanthus.' In explaining the nature of the 'Ailanthus,' some say it is *similar* to the Ailanthus but should not be categorized as such." 

If we were to follow Master Lu’s logic, it would be more appropriate to call such a tree the **Kao** <term>Kao (栲): A general term for various species of sturdy evergreen oaks or mangroves, often used for their durable wood and bark.</term>.

## The Treasure Tree

The **Treasure Tree** <note>original: "寶樹" (Baoshu). This name is often applied to rare or sacred trees found in temple grounds.</note> grows in the Buddhist temples of **Mount Lu** <note>A mountain in Jiangxi Province famous for its beauty and long history of Buddhist and Taoist practice.</note>. It stands tall and straight, with leaves resembling those of pines and firs, yet it possesses forked branches. Tradition holds that during the **Ming Dynasty**, it produced a single flower that looked like a lotus.

According to the **Miscellaneous Morsels from Youyang** <note>original: "酉陽雜俎" (Youyang Zazu). A wide-ranging collection of Tang Dynasty legends, flora, and fauna compiled by Duan Chengshi in the 9th century.</note>, a tree suddenly sprouted in a monk’s cell in Baling. A monk from a foreign land saw it and identified it as the **Bala tree** <note>original: "婆羅" (Poluo). This refers to the Sal tree (*Shorea robusta*), under which the Buddha is said to have been born and passed away.</note>. At the beginning of the **Yuanjia era** <note>424–453 AD</note>, it produced a lotus-like flower, suggesting it may belong to this category.

The **Study of Chinese and Foreign Flowers and Trees** <note>original: "華夷花木考" (Huayi Huamu Kao).</note> records that the **Bala tree** produces seven leaves on every branch. However, this is actually the **Celestial Master’s Chestnut** <note>original: "天師栗" (Tianshi Li). *Aesculus chinensis*, or the Chinese Horse Chestnut. It was often planted in Chinese temples as a substitute for the tropical Sal tree, which could not grow in northern climates.</note> and not the true **Bala tree**. The great physician **Li Shizhen** <note>The author of the *Compendium of Materia Medica* (Bencao Gangmu).</note> also confirmed this to be the case.

# The Buddhist Pine

The Buddhist Pine <note>original: "羅漢松" (Luohansong); *Podocarpus macrophyllus*.</note> has dense leaves that are long and lush like bamboo, yet they grow in clustered or rounded formations. It is frequently cultivated in pots for decorative enjoyment <note>original: "盆玩" (penwan); an early term for what is now commonly known as *Bonsai* or *Penjing*.</note>. Its fruit is shaped like the form of an Arhat <note>A "Luohan" or Arhat is a perfected Buddhist disciple; the seed resembles a bald head sitting atop a fleshy, robe-like base.</note>, which is why it is so named. Some say the fruit is edible. Others believe it to be the same as the **Bamboo Cypress** <note>original: "竹柏" (Zhubai); *Nagi podocarpus*.</note>. According to the *Records of Local Products in the Potted Section*, the Bamboo Cypress has dense, long leaves and its sheath <note>original: "籜" (tuo); usually referring to the protective sheath of a bamboo shoot.</note> resembles bamboo. If one considers the "sheath" to be its falling leaves, then the two species are very similar; however, if one interprets "sheath" as the bracts of a bamboo shoot, they are not alike at all. I have recorded this here to await further investigation.

The Buddhist Pine of Southern Yunnan <note>original: "滇南" (Diannan).</note> produces fruit as large as a thumb with a green head and a crimson base <note>original: "絳跗" (jiangfu). The "base" refers to the fleshy receptacle below the seed, which turns reddish-purple when ripe.</note>. Its shape is upright and pleasing. The base is tender and sweet in flavor, and it is particularly elegant when served on a display tray. Folk wisdom says that eating them can benefit the heart *qi* <note>original: "益心氣" (yi xin qi); in Traditional Chinese Medicine, this refers to nourishing the functional energy and spirit associated with the heart.</note>, as they likely possess the same medicinal efficacy as pine and cypress seeds.

# The Banyan Tree

<note>original: "榕" (*Rong*). The Chinese Banyan (*Ficus microcarpa*). It is one of the most iconic trees of Southern China, famous for its massive canopy and "walking" aerial roots.</note>

Banyan trees are extremely numerous throughout the provinces of Guangdong and Guangxi <note>original: "兩廣" (*Liangguang*), referring to the "Two Guangs."</note>. Although it is considered a wood of little practical use <term>useless timber <gloss>wood that is not suitable for construction or fine carpentry due to its twisted or soft nature</gloss></term>, its dense foliage provides essential shade for travelers, and its leaves can be used to fertilize the farm fields. 

As the wood grows very old, it is said to transform into a substance resembling *Kynam* incense <note>original: "伽南香" (*jianan xiang*). This refers to high-quality agarwood or aloeswood. While the Banyan is not a source of true agarwood, ancient texts often attributed aromatic properties to the decaying heartwood of very old trees.</note>. Its roots can grow to be as large as a house. 

The tree is found throughout southern Jiangxi and the Ganzhou region. However, if it is moved even slightly further north, it will wither as soon as it encounters the cold. Because of this, there is a local proverb: "The Banyan does not cross Ji" <note>original: "榕不過吉" (*Rong bu guo Ji*). This refers to Ji’an in Jiangxi province, which served as a traditional botanical boundary; the tree rarely survived the frosts further north of this line.</note>. 

Some people believe that this tree is the same as the *Kai* tree <note>original: "愷木" (*kaimu*)</note> found in Sichuan <note>original: "蜀" (*Shu*)</note>. However, the scholar Su Zimei <note>original: "蘇子美" (*Su Zimei*), the courtesy name of the Song dynasty poet Su Shunqin.</note>, who was a native of Sichuan and spent time in both Huizhou and Qiongzhou <note>Hainan Island</note>, never wrote a single word suggesting they were the same. Furthermore, Li Tiaoyuan <note>original: "李調元" (1734–1803), a famous Qing dynasty scholar and geographer.</note> describes the Banyan tree in great detail in his *Notes on the Southern Yue* <note>original: "南越筆記" (*Nanyue Biji*)</note>, yet he also does not identify it as the *Kai* tree. Li was also a native of Sichuan and would have been familiar with the local flora of his home.

## Cai Wood

<note>original: 蔡木 (*Caimu*). Historically, *Cai* refers to a variety of oak or a specific tree used in ancient divination rituals. Here, the author investigates its botanical identity by comparing it to common oaks and analyzing its name through classical philology.</note>

The **Cai wood** grows on Mount Wutai in Shanxi province; the local gazetteer records its presence there. Its branches and leaves are entirely similar to trees in the genera of the <term>oriental oak <note>original: 槲櫟 (*huli*); species of the Quercus genus.</note></term>, the <term>sawtooth oak <note>original: 桒栩 (*sangxu*); likely referring to types of oaks or mulberry-like wild trees.</note></term>, and the chestnut. 

Upon examining Master Duan’s <note>original: 段氏 (*Duan Shi*); refers to Duan Yucai (1735–1815), the famous Qing dynasty philologist who annotated the first Chinese dictionary.</note> commentary on the *Explaining Graphs and Analyzing Characters* <note>original: 說文解字 (*Shuowen Jiezi*)</note>, the word *Cai* is defined as "lush grass." The character for "lush" (*feng*) is pronounced like *jie*. Originally, the character for "lush" was missing from the dictionary, but it was later added to the fourth chapter, stating that "lushness describes the grassiness of *Cai*." Conversely, it is said here that "*Cai* is the lushness of grass." This relationship is known as <term>mutual explanation <note>original: 轉注 (*zhuanzhu*); one of the six classifications of Chinese characters where two words with similar meanings are used to define each other.</note></term>, referring to the way grass grows in a scattered and disorganized fashion. 

The words for "lush" (*feng*) and "Cai" (*cha*) are <term>alliterative rhymes <note>original: 疊韻 (*dieyun*); a phonological term for words that share the same final sound or rhyme.</note></term>. Since this tree has dense leaves and branching stalks, perhaps it was named *Cai* for this reason? 

The *Collected Rimes* <note>original: 集韻 (*Jiyun*)</note> contains the character *cha* (檫), describing it as a tree belonging to the <term>catalpa genus <note>original: 梓屬 (*zishu*).</note></term>. It is possible that "Cai" and "Cha" are phonetic or graphical errors of one another due to their similar appearance and sound. However, this tree is quite unlike the catalpa. 

Furthermore, when ancient writers composed these characters, they sometimes interpreted them as the <term>oriental oak <note>original: 柞 (*zuo*).</note></term> or the <term>sawtooth oak <note>original: 櫟 (*li*).</note></term>. Others interpreted it specifically as the "Zuo wood." The sawtooth oak is often unsightly, but its fruits are numerous and it is frequently used as <term>firewood <note>original: 薪槱 (*xinyou*).</note></term>. 

The sounds for *Zuo* and *Cai* are simply a single phonetic shift. In the western dialects <note>original: 西音 (*xiyin*); likely referring to the regional accents of Shanxi or Shaanxi.</note>, calling it *Cai* is considered an error. *Zuo* is also a <term>phonetic loan <note>original: 假借 (*jiajie*)</note></term> for the word *cha* (槎), meaning felled wood. It is highly likely that the author of the local gazetteer simply wrote the name as *Cai* based on the local dialect, unaware that it was actually the **Zuo tree** (oak). 

The *Huozhou Gazetteer* states: Regarding the **Zuo tree**, as the new leaves grow, the old leaves fall. It is a tough and resilient wood that is suitable for making cart axles.

# Amur Cork Tree
<note>original: 檗木 (*Bimu*). This is identified as the Amur Cork Tree (*Phellodendron amurense*), highly valued in traditional medicine for its bitter, yellow inner bark.</note>

The **Amur Cork Tree** was listed in the "Upper Class" of the *Classic of Materia Medica* under the name *Tanhuan*. 
<note>original: 本經 (*Benjing*), referring to the *Shennong Benjing* or "Divine Farmer’s Classic of Materia Medica"; 檀桓 (*Tanhuan*) is an archaic synonym.</note>
The *Miscellaneous Records* states that it grows in the mountain valleys of Hanzhong and Yongchang. 
<note>original: 別錄 (*Bielu*), referring to the *Mingyi Bielu* (Records of Famous Physicians).</note>
Today, it is found in great abundance within the mountains of Shanxi and Hunan. In common parlance, it is called the "Yellow Cork." The *Shuowen* dictionary defines it as a tree with yellow leaves. 
<note>original: 說文 (*Shuowen Jiezi*), the first century Chinese dictionary.</note>
It is a common error to add the "grass" radical to the top of the character, writing it as <term>蘗 (*bo*)</term>.

According to the writings of **Manqian**, 
<note>original: 曼倩 (*Manqian*), the courtesy name of Dongfang Shuo (c. 160–93 BCE), a Han dynasty scholar-official famous for his wit and association with the supernatural.</note>
fiction writers claim that throwing the leaves of the Amur Cork Tree into water can poison **flood dragons**. 
<note>original: 蛟龍 (*Jiaolong*). In ancient Chinese ecology, "dragons" were often used to personify the violent and destructive power of flash floods or turbulent river currents.</note>
This is similar to how **Wen Qiao** once lit a rhinoceros horn to illuminate the depths, which the ghosts and spirits detested. 
<note>Wen Qiao (288–329 CE) was a Jin dynasty official; legend says he lit a rhinoceros horn to see the supernatural creatures beneath the water of Niuzhu Fen.</note>

However, I have noted that when one suddenly encounters a boiling torrent in the mountains—locally known as "**Dragon Water**"—the force is so great that mountains split and trees are uprooted. Is it not possible that some Amur Cork wood would naturally be swept away and float along with such a current? The destruction caused by such "fragmenting waters" is truly beyond words. 

In recent times, there are those who speak of using the Amur Cork Tree to "strike down the dragon." 
<note>original: 檗伐蛟 (*bo fa jiao*).</note>
The intent behind such a claim is bold and magnificent, yet I have never heard of anyone actually testing it. In the *Rites of Zhou*, the **Official of the Jar and Pestle** was responsible for removing aquatic pests. 
<note>original: 壺涿氏 (*Huzhuo Shi*), an ancient ritual official mentioned in the *Zhou Li* (Rites of Zhou) tasked with clearing water-dwelling monsters or harmful insects.</note>
If they wished to kill the spirit of the water, they would take **male catalpa wood**, pierce it crosswise with **ivory**, and sink it into the depths. It was said the spirit would then die, and the deep pool would dry up to become a hill. 

How is this any different from the protective incantations used by later generations? If something can defend against great disasters and ward off massive calamities to the benefit of the people, then even if it is a "minor shamanic art," the Sages would surely have seen its value.

# Nut-thorn (Ruihe)

The **Nut-thorn** <note>original: 蕤核 (*Ruíhé*); the stone fruit of *Prinsepia uniflora*, a shrub native to Northern China often used in traditional medicine for eye ailments.</note> is listed as a **superior grade** substance in the *Classic of Materia Medica*.
<note>The *Classic of Materia Medica* (Shennong Bencao Jing) is the oldest known Chinese pharmaceutical text, categorizing "superior" herbs as those that are non-toxic and promote longevity.</note>

The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary/encyclopedia, dating back to the 3rd century BCE.</note> refers to the **Yu** as the **White Rui**. <gloss>The commentary states: It is a small woody plant that grows in clusters and possesses thorns. Its fruit resembles ear pendants and is purple-red in color and edible.</gloss>

Those who write commentaries on the *Materia Medica* believe this to be the very same Nut-thorn. The *Illustrated Classic of Materia Medica* <note>original: 圖經 (*Tujing Bencao*), an 11th-century work by Su Song.</note> states that its **leaves are as fine as Goji** (Wolfberry), its **flowers are white**, and the fruit grows attached to the stem, turning a purple-red hue. Upon examining its shape and characteristics, this description is indeed a perfect match. <gloss>Thus, the scholar Su Song identified it as the Cherokee Rose (Jinyingzi).</gloss>

The fruit is edible. Today, it is extremely common on the hillsides of **Shanxi**. <gloss>It is also found in the valleys; sometimes it is planted along the roadsides, where it grows vigorously in uneven clusters.</gloss> 

The poets of old described the physical nature of "flourishing firewood and stackable wood" <note>A reference to the *Shijing* (Book of Odes), the oldest existing collection of Chinese poetry. The terms "flourishing" (*pengpeng*) and "stackable wood" (*yupu*) evoke the image of dense, useful shrubs.</note>; the scholar Liu Liang also provided a self-account regarding the suitability of this plant's nature.

According to the *Gazetteer of Huozhou*, the **Yu** is also known as the **Rui**, which is the same as the "Brushwood" (*Yupu*) mentioned in the classics. It has small branches and grows in thickets; the stems are **hollow in the center**. The people of that prefecture who "drink smoke" <note>A traditional Chinese idiom for smoking tobacco, which became popular in the late Ming and Qing dynasties.</note> take these hollow stems to fashion into **smoking pipes**.

According to Lu Ji’s *Commentary on the Odes*, the *Yu* is actually a type of **Oak** (*Zuo*). He notes that the wood with a grain that is entirely white, lacking a red core, is the **White Rui**. This suggests that there are two varieties of *Yu*: red and white. 

Today, the plants produced in **Huozhou** <note>A region in modern-day Shanxi province.</note> have red patterns as intricate as embroidery, and a pith like that of the **Rice-paper plant** (*Tongcao*). Using these stems as tubes for pipes is exactly what the ancient poets meant when they wrote of the "Brushwood" (*Yupu*).
