# Introduction.

The imperfect reports regarding the details of the Battle of Austerlitz <note>Fought on December 2, 1805, also known as the "Battle of the Three Emperors."</note> that have reached the public are so contradictory, and so little satisfying for the military men of Europe, that it is believed one owes them the following clarification; it may serve to define their concepts regarding this memorable epoch.

Nations and the armies of all times and countries have been guided by public opinion. For this reason, it was always the policy of governments to amplify the brilliance of even the finest victories through everything that could inflame the national spirit, and to give a lighter color—through plausible reasoning <note>original: "Raisonnements"</note>—to accidents that were too well known to be suppressed.

Archduke Charles, forced into retreat by the unprecedented catastrophe of the German army, arrived safely in Hungary with his forces. This followed his earlier success at Caldiero, where he had rendered the French Italian army incapable of harming him.

The Austrian troops, therefore, fought well whenever their leaders intended for them to fight; it would be a very false and highly presumptuous judgment to attribute the misfortunes of the 1805 war to them. In this conflict, as in every other, this army distinguished itself through courage and devotion, through steadfastness in enduring unheard-of privations, and through their blind obedience.
