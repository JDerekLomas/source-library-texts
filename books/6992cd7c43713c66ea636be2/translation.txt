## Zhang Daoling

Zhang Daoling <note>The foundational figure of organized religious Taoism and the first "Celestial Master."</note>, whose courtesy name was Fuhan, was an eighth-generation descendant of Zifang <note>Zifang is the courtesy name of Zhang Liang, the famous advisor to the founding emperor of the Han dynasty.</note>. He was born at Mount Tianmu in the tenth year of the Jianwu era <note>34 CE</note> during the reign of Emperor Guangwu of the Han dynasty.

By the age of seven, he had already mastered the *Tao Te Ching* <note>original: "道德經" — The "Classic of the Way and Virtue," the central text of Taoism.</note> as well as the mystical "He-Luo" charts and apocryphal texts <note>original: "河洛圖緯" — Ancient cosmological diagrams and prophetic texts used to understand the divine order of the universe.</note>. His heart was set on the arts of cultivation. Among his disciples was the elder Wang Chang; together, they refined the "Great Elixir of the Dragon and Tiger." 

When the elixir was finally perfected, the <term>Perfected One<note>Zhenren (真人); a term for a high-level Taoist immortal who has transcended the physical world.</note></term> was over sixty years old, but upon consuming it, he regained the appearance of a man of about thirty. He traveled to Northern Mount Song where he encountered an "Emissary in Embroidered Robes" <note>A divine messenger or celestial official often sent by the heavens to deliver instructions to worthy seekers.</note> who spoke to him, saying: "In the stone chamber of the central peak are hidden the *Inner Script of the Three Sovereigns* and the *Yellow Emperor’s Nine-Tripod Great Clarity Elixir Scripture*. Obtain them and practice their teachings, and you shall ascend to heaven."

Thereupon, the Perfected One fasted and purified himself for seven days. He obtained the alchemical scriptures and devoted his mind to their cultivation. He became capable of "dividing his form and scattering his shadow" <note>The supernatural ability of bilocation—manifesting in several places simultaneously.</note>. He was often seen rowing a boat in a pond, reciting scriptures in the hall, leaning against a table to receive guests, and walking with a goosefoot staff while chanting poetry—all occurring at the very same time. People could not fathom the depth of his power.

### Mei Fu
<note>original: "梅福"</note>

**Mei Fu**, whose courtesy name was **Zizhen**, was a native of Shouchun <note>Located in modern-day Anhui Province.</note>. He served the Han Dynasty as the Commandant of Nanchang. Witnessing the rise of **Wang Mang** <note>A powerful official who eventually usurped the Han throne; he is often depicted in history as a symbol of political corruption and instability.</note> and his autocratic grip on the government, Mei Fu sighed and lamented:

->“Life is my cruel torture; it is my disgrace. Knowledge is my poison, and this physical body is my shackles.”<-

Thereupon, he abandoned his family to seek the path of the immortals. He wandered extensively through the **Yandang** and **Southern Min** mountain ranges. Upon reaching **Mount Xianxia**, he encountered the **Immortal Lord of Kongtong** <note>A legendary Daoist figure associated with the transmission of esoteric wisdom.</note>, who bestowed upon him the methods of **Inner and Outer Alchemy** <term>Alchemy<note>The practice of "Neidan" (Inner Alchemy) focused on spiritual cultivation and "Waidan" (Outer Alchemy) focused on the creation of life-prolonging elixirs.</note></term>. The Lord told Fu, “Your destiny lies on Mount Feihong.”

Fu subsequently went there, built a humble hermitage, and devoted himself to spiritual cultivation. Once his elixir was perfected, he returned to his home in Shouchun. One day, a purple mist filled the sky, and **Golden Boys and Jade Maidens** <note>Auspicious celestial attendants who herald divine arrivals.</note> descended from the clouds, carrying a divine decree and leading <term>simurghs<note>original: "鸞" (Luan); a mythical bird similar to a phoenix, often used as a celestial mount for those who have attained immortality.</note></term>. 

Fu bowed to the decree, bid farewell to his family, and rode a simurgh into the heavens. It is recorded that people saw Fu again much later, during the **Yuanfeng era** of the Song Dynasty <note>1078–1085 CE.</note>. He was eventually granted the title of the **Perfected Man of Shouchun** <note>original: "壽春真人" (Shouchun Zhenren). "Perfected Man" is a high-ranking title in the Daoist celestial hierarchy.</note>.

**Huang Chuping** was a native of Danxi <note>Located in modern-day Lanxi, Zhejiang Province.</note> during the Jin Dynasty. ## Sun Deng

Sun Deng, whose courtesy name was Gonghe, lived in a cave on the northern mountains of Ji County <note>located in modern-day Henan Province</note>. In the summer, he wove grass to make his clothes, and in the winter, he simply let his long hair fall over him to stay warm. He was highly skilled at **long whistling** <term>chángxiào (長嘯); a rhythmic, melodic sounding of the breath used by recluses to express spiritual transcendence</term>, and he took great pleasure in studying the *Book of Changes* <term>Yìjīng (易經); an ancient manual of divination and philosophy</term> and playing a one-stringed zither. His temperament was so steady that he never showed signs of joy or anger.

The scholar Ji Kang <note>Ji Kang (223–262 CE) was a leader of the "Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove" and a famous musician</note> followed and traveled with him for three years. Whenever Ji Kang asked about his goals or intentions, Sun Deng remained silent and would not answer. As they were about to part ways, Ji Kang said, "Sir, will you truly leave me without a single word?"

Sun Deng replied:

> "Do you understand fire? Fire is born with light, yet it does not 'use' its light; rather, its true power lies in how the light is applied. A person is born with talent, yet they should not 'use' that talent [recklessly]; rather, their success lies in how that talent is applied. Therefore, the secret to maintaining light is finding enough firewood—this is how its brilliance is preserved. The secret to using talent is recognizing the Truth <note>original: zhēn (真); referring to the Daoist 'True Self' or ultimate reality</note>—this is how one's natural lifespan is kept whole."

Ji Kang also requested to learn the zither from him, but Sun Deng refused to teach him, saying:

> "You have much talent but little wisdom. It will be difficult for you to escape the dangers of the current age."

Later, Ji Kang did indeed get caught up in the legal troubles of his friend Lu An. While in prison, he wrote a poem of self-reproach, saying:

> "Formerly, I felt shame before Liu Xiahui; now, I feel humbled by Sun Deng." <note>Ji Kang realized too late that Sun Deng’s silence and seclusion were necessary for survival in a treacherous political climate. Liu Xiahui was a legendary official known for maintaining his integrity despite being dismissed from office.</note>

Eventually, Sun Deng ascended to heaven in broad daylight <note>This indicates he achieved the highest level of Daoist immortality, leaving the physical world while still alive.</note>.

### The Biography of Zuo Ci

Zuo Ci, whose courtesy name was Yuanfang, was a native of Lujiang. He was highly proficient in the *Liujia* arts <term>Liujia (六甲); an ancient system of esoteric Taoist magic and divination used to command spirits</term> and was able to summon ghosts and deities. He could manifest a "traveling kitchen" <note>original: 行厨 (xíng chú); a magical banquet that appears out of thin air</note> while remaining seated and was capable of transforming into ten thousand different forms.

The warlord Cao Cao <note>A powerful minister and de facto ruler of the Eastern Han dynasty</note> summoned Zuo Ci and confined him in a single room, cutting off his supply of grain for an entire year. When Zuo Ci was finally let out, his complexion and appearance were exactly as they had been before.

Once, while Cao Cao was hosting a banquet for many guests, he remarked, "At such a grand gathering as we have today, the only thing we lack is perch from the Song River." 

Zuo Ci requested a bronze basin filled with water. Using a fishing rod, he cast his line into the basin and immediately caught a perch. 

Cao Cao said, "It is a pity we have no ginger from Shu <note>original: 蜀薑 (Shǔ jiāng); ginger from the region of modern-day Sichuan, prized for its quality</note> to go with it." 

Zuo Ci replied, "That is easily obtained." 

Cao Cao, fearing that Zuo Ci would simply acquire it from somewhere nearby, mentioned that he had previously sent a messenger to purchase silk in the region of Shu. He told Zuo Ci to instruct that messenger to increase the order by twenty bolts of silk as proof of his journey. Zuo Ci agreed. He then threw his cup into the air, transformed into a crane, and flew away. In just a short moment, he returned and produced the ginger from his sleeve. Later, when the man who had been sent to buy the silk returned, he indeed reported that on that very day, he had received an order to increase the purchase of silk.

Cao Cao grew to hate Zuo Ci’s powers and wished to kill him. However, Zuo Ci simply walked backward into a wall and vanished. Later, someone encountered Zuo Ci at the peak of Yangcheng Mountain. When pursued, he ran into a flock of sheep. Suddenly, an old ram <term>羝 (dī); a male sheep or ram</term> knelt on its two front knees, stood up like a human, and said, "Why such haste?" <note>original: 遽如許 (jù rú xǔ)</note>

The pursuers intended to seize him, but suddenly the entire flock of several hundred sheep all transformed into rams, stood up like humans, and said, "Why such haste?" Consequently, the pursuers no longer knew which one to take.

# The Deeds of Han Xiangzi

**Han Xiangzi**, whose courtesy name was Qingfu, was the nephew of Han Wengong <note>original: 韓文公 (Han Wengong); the posthumous name of Han Yu (768–824 AD), a preeminent poet and statesman of the Tang Dynasty known for his fierce defense of Confucianism and opposition to Buddhism and Taoism.</note>. He encountered the immortal Chunyang <note>original: 純陽 (Chunyang); the Taoist name of Lü Dongbin, one of the leaders of the Eight Immortals.</note> and followed him as a disciple. 

During his travels, Xiangzi climbed a peach tree, fell from its branches, and died; however, he achieved **corpse liberation** <term>shijie (尸解); a Taoist concept where a practitioner escapes the mortal coil by appearing to die, leaving behind an object like a sword or a cane in place of their body while their true self ascends as an immortal</term>.

Later, he returned to see Han Wengong. To demonstrate his spiritual resolve, he composed a poem that included lines describing his ability to "brew wine in an instant" and "command flowers to bloom in a moment." The Master (Han Yu) opened a wine jar, and indeed, it was filled with excellent nectar. Xiangzi then gathered some soil and, in a very short time, a single blue-green flower blossomed. Within the open petals, a couplet appeared in **gold characters**, which read:

->*Clouds straddle the Qin Mountains—where is my home?*<-
->*Snow blocks the Lan Pass—the horse will not move forward.*<-

The Master read the words but did not understand their meaning. 

Some time later, Han Yu submitted a stern memorial to the Emperor protesting the grand reception of the Buddha’s finger bone <note>Han Yu’s "Memorial on the Bone of the Buddha" is one of the most famous documents in Chinese history; it led to his near-execution and eventual exile.</note>. As a result, he was demoted and banished to the remote region of Chaozhou. While traveling through a blizzard on his way to his post, a man suddenly appeared through the snow. It was Xiangzi. 

Xiangzi asked, "Does the Master remember the lines found within the flower?" 

Han Yu inquired about their location and realized it was indeed **Lan Pass**. He stayed with Xiangzi at the Lan Pass post house that night. When it was time for Xiangzi to depart, he produced a gourd of medicine and gave it to Han Yu, saying, "Take one pill, and you can ward off the pestilence <note>The southern regions like Chaozhou were considered "miasmatic" and deadly to officials from the north.</note>." 

Han Yu felt a deep sense of sorrow. Xiangzi comforted him, saying, "You will soon return to the west (the capital) and shall be restored to your position." 

Han Yu asked, "Will there be a time for us to meet again after this?" 

Xiangzi replied, "What lies ahead cannot be known in advance," and then he departed.

### The Royal Uncle Cao

**Royal Uncle Cao** <note>original: 曹國舅 (Cao Guojiu)</note> was the younger brother of the Empress Dowager of the **Song Dynasty**. Because his own younger brother frequently committed unlawful acts and murdered people, Cao was filled with a deep sense of shame. Consequently, he withdrew from society and hid his tracks among the mountain cliffs, where he devoted his mind to the contemplation of profound <term>Taoist principles <note>original: 玄理 (xuanli); literally "mysterious logic," referring to the deep, esoteric truths of the Tao</note></term>. 

Wearing only rustic clothes and a simple hemp cap, he lived in such deep meditation that he would often go for ten days at a time without eating. One day, he encountered the two immortals **Zhongli** <note>Zhongli Quan</note> and **Chunyang** <note>The Taoist name of Lü Dongbin</note>. 

They asked him, “We have heard that you are practicing cultivation. What is it that you are cultivating?”

He replied, “I am cultivating the **Way** <note>original: 道 (Tao)</note>.”

They asked, “Where is the Way?”

The Royal Uncle pointed toward the sky.

They asked, “And where is the sky?”

The Royal Uncle pointed toward his heart.

The two immortals smiled and said to him, “The heart is the sky, and the sky is the Way. You have truly seen your **original face** <note>original: 本來面目 (benlai mianmu); a term for one's true, primordial nature before it is obscured by worldly desires</note>.”

They then bestowed upon him the **Secret Arts of Returning to the Truth** <note>original: 還真秘術 (huanzhen mishu)</note> and introduced him into the **ranks of the immortals**.

### Zhang Zhihe

->**Zhang Zhihe**<-

**Zhang Zhihe** (whose courtesy name was Budong) was a native of Jinhua during the Tang Dynasty <note>618–907 CE</note>. His birth was preceded by an auspicious omen: his mother dreamt that a maple tree grew from her womb. Later in life, Emperor Suzong <note>reigned 756–762 CE</note> promoted him through the *Mingjing* <term>Understanding the Classics; a prestigious imperial examination</term> and personally bestowed upon him the name **Zhihe** <note>literally "Will to Harmony"</note>. He was appointed as a scholar-in-attendance at the **Hanlin Academy** <term>an elite group of scholars who performed secretarial and advisory roles for the Emperor</term>.

After the mourning period for his parents ended, he chose not to return to official service. Instead, he spent his days wandering the rivers and lakes, referring to himself as the **Fisherman of the Mists and Waves** <term>Yanbo Diaotu</term>. He was also known by the title **Master of the Mysterious Truth** <term>Xuanzhenzi</term>. 

When he went fishing, he never used bait, for his true intention was not to catch fish <note>this is a common trope in Daoist hagiography, signifying that the practitioner is "fishing" for spiritual essence or simply being present in nature, rather than seeking material gain</note>. He was said to be able to drink three *dou* <note>a large ancient measurement; approximately 30 liters</note> of wine without becoming intoxicated. By guarding his inner truth and nurturing his *qi* <term>vital life force</term>, he became immune to the elements: he could lie in the snow without feeling the cold and submerge himself in water without getting wet.

Whenever he was merry with wine, he would spread a simple mat upon the surface of the water, sitting alone upon it to drink. The mat would drift across the waves as if it were a sturdy boat. Suddenly, clouds and cranes <note>traditional symbols of immortality and the celestial realm</note> would begin to circle and swirl above him. He would then straddle a crane and ascend into the heavens.

### Xuan Yuanji

->**Xuan Yuanji**<-

**Xuan Yuanji** was a Daoist practitioner of the Tang Dynasty <note>618–907 CE</note>. He resided on **Mount Luofu** <note>a mountain in modern-day Guangdong province, revered as the "Seventh Grotto-Heaven" of Daoism and a center for herbal medicine and alchemy</note>. 

Although he appeared to be a man in his thirties or forties, it was whispered by the locals that he had lived for several hundred years. His countenance remained youthful and vigorous, a sign of his mastery over internal cultivation. During the **Xiantong era** <note>860–874 CE</note>, Emperor Yizong heard of his extraordinary reputation and summoned him to the imperial court.

While at the palace, the Emperor questioned him regarding the secrets of longevity and the arts of the immortals. Xuan Yuanji responded with the wisdom of a true sage, suggesting that for a sovereign, the "elixir" of long life was found in the benevolent governance of the people and the practice of **non-action** <term>Wu-wei; the Daoist philosophy of acting in harmony with the natural flow of the universe rather than through force</term>. 

He was known for his command over the natural world. The woodcut illustration depicts him in his element: riding a fierce tiger as if it were a common mount, carrying a basket of medicinal herbs or spiritual talismans on a pole. He gazes downward at a snake, demonstrating his sovereignty over the dangerous creatures of the wilderness. This imagery serves to highlight his status as a **Zhenren** <term>a "Realized Person" or Perfected One who has transcended the limitations of the physical world</term>.

Eventually, weary of the constraints of the capital, he requested to return to his mountain retreat. It is said that he departed the mortal realm by "transforming his corpse" <note>shijie; a Daoist concept where an immortal appears to die but actually ascends, leaving behind an object like a sword or a staff in the coffin</note>, leaving the world behind to wander the celestial realms.

### Xuan Yuanji (Continued)

It is not known where **Xuan Yuanji** came from. Legend says he was several hundred years old, yet his complexion never aged. When he sat in a darkened room, the light from his eyes stretched out for dozens of feet. Whenever he gathered medicinal herbs in the rocky valleys, poisonous dragons and fierce beasts followed him as if acting as his protectors.

Among the common people, when families invited him as a **guest** <term>賔; an honored visitor or participant at a formal gathering</term>, even if there were a hundred such invitations at once, he would manifest his form at every location simultaneously <note>this refers to the Daoist feat of "bilocation" or "dividing the body"</note>. When drinking with others, he would produce a small flask from his sleeve that **barely** <term>纔; a term indicating a very small or limited amount</term> held two liters. Even with a house full of guests, he could pour from it all day without it ever running dry; yet he himself could drink a hundred liters without becoming intoxicated. If he encountered the sick, he would brush them with a cloth towel, and they would be healed instantly.

Emperor **Xuanzong** <note>reigned 846–859 CE; he was famously devoted to Daoist alchemy in his later years</note> summoned him to the palace and asked if longevity could truly be achieved. He answered:

> Sever your attachment to music and beauty, simplify your diet, and view both sorrow and joy as one. If your virtue is bestowed without bias, you will naturally align with the virtue of Heaven and Earth and match the brilliance of the sun and moon. If this is achieved, how could long life and lasting vision be out of reach?

After some time, he requested leave to return to the mountains. The Emperor ordered an imperial envoy to escort him. Throughout the journey, he was seen reaching into a single cloth bag to pull out money to give to the people. By the time they reached **Jiangling** <note>a historic city on the Yangtze River in modern-day Hubei province</note>, he had already distributed hundreds of thousands of coins, yet the bag’s supply remained inexhaustible. Before he even reached his mountain home, he suddenly vanished, and no one knew where he had gone.

# Chen Tuan

**Chen Tuan**, whose courtesy name was **Yunan** and pseudonym was **Fuyaozi**, was a native of Zhenyuan in Bozhou. At birth, he was unable to speak. When he reached four or five years of age, he was playing by the banks of the Wo River when an old woman in green clothing led him into her arms and breastfed him. From that moment, he was able to speak. His quick-wittedness and intelligence were far beyond those of ordinary people.

As he grew older, he read the Classics and Histories once and understood them completely, leaving nothing out. During the Liang and Tang dynasties, the scholar-officials admired his pure character; to know his face was like seeing a **Propitious Star** or **Auspicious Clouds** <note>These are celestial omens associated with peace and the presence of a sage</note>. However, the Master (Chen Tuan) did not associate with them.

Emperor **Mingzong** of the Tang dynasty personally issued a hand-written imperial decree to summon him. When the Master arrived, he offered a **deep bow** <term>長揖 (changyi); a formal bow where the hands are joined and raised, but without prostrating</term> rather than kneeling. Mingzong treated him with increasing reverence and gifted him three palace maids. The Master composed a poem to express his gratitude and decline the gift:

> Their skin is like snow and their cheeks like jade,
> I thank the Great King for sending them to me.
> But a hermit has no interest in dreaming of the **Wu Gorges**,
> It is a vain trouble for the **clouds and rain** to descend to the Yangtai.
<note>This poem uses classic literary metaphors: the "Wu Gorges" and "clouds and rain" are standard euphemisms for sexual intimacy, referencing a legend of a king meeting a goddess at Yangtai.</note>

He then fled to go into hiding at the Nine Stone Crags of **Mount Wudang**. There, he practiced **breath cultivation** <term>服氣 (fuqi); a Daoist technique of "ingesting" cosmic energy</term> and **grain-abstinence** <term>辟穀 (bigu); a fasting method where one avoids the "five grains" to purify the body</term> for over twenty years. Later, he moved his residence to **Mount Huashan**. By this time, he was already over seventy years old.

He would often close his door and lie down in sleep for months at a time without rising. During the **Xiande** era (954–959 CE) of Emperor Shizong of the Later Zhou dynasty, a woodcutter at the foot of the mountain saw a set of skeletal remains covered in dust. Approaching them—

# The Hidden Old Man Lei

The **Hidden Old Man Lei** <note>original: 雷隱翁 (Lei Yin Weng)</note>, whose given name was Ben, was characterized from his youth by an upright and unconventional spirit that set him apart from the crowd. Upon reaching adulthood, he pursued the path of a **scholar-official** <term>Jinshi</term> <note>original: 進士; the highest degree in the imperial examination system</note>. However, after failing the examinations twice, he abandoned his worldly ambitions. He took to sitting in silence for entire days. Some people, observing this, called him "foolish" or "dim-witted." The Old Man would only smile and reply, "In the end, I would never trade my 'foolishness' for your 'cleverness'."

One day, after passing on his secret arts and techniques to his son, he departed to travel and never returned. During the **Yuanyou era of the Song Dynasty** <note>1086–1094 AD</note>, a court official traveling through **Mount Luofu** <note>A mountain in Guangdong province famous in Taoist lore as a dwelling of immortals</note> encountered the Old Man sitting beneath a tree. He was heard chanting a quatrain to himself:

-> Coming and going for thirty years, <-
-> no trace remains within the human realm. <-
-> With merit achieved and practice full, I ascend to the heavens; <-
-> looking back, the moon above the mountain is perfectly round. <-
