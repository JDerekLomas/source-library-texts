# PREFACE.

*Although I provided a preface at the beginning of this work which I then deemed sufficient, I nevertheless realize that since its publication I have omitted several things of which the Reader ought to have been warned. Meanwhile, other matters pertaining to the illustration, expansion, or even correction of this History have come to light—either published by foreigners, communicated by friends, or encountered by myself during my reading. These require a new Preface and do not allow this second Volume to go forth "headless" <note>original: "acephalos"; meaning without an introduction</note>. Otherwise, a new preface would not have been required, as this volume is like the second half of the same body, divided into two parts for no other reason than to prevent the book from being too thick and difficult to handle if bound into a single volume.*

*First, therefore, let the Reader know that I have studiously avoided all disputes regarding nomenclature. I have not been overly anxious or industrious in seeking out the specific name by which every Plant was called by the Ancients. In many cases, because the recorded characteristics are so few, general, and common to many species, the name cannot be defined with certainty. In other controversial cases, the effort to attempt it would be greater than the reward. Furthermore, since others far more learned than I have already struggled abundantly in this arena <note>original: "palæstra"; literally a wrestling school or gymnasium</note>, and almost nothing remains to be said that has not been said before, it is necessary that there be some limit to Controversies. Hence, I have everywhere retained the accepted names approved by long usage; I have not thought it wise to rashly disturb or displace names that might perhaps have been wrongly applied in the beginning, lest they take legal action against us by right of long-standing prescription.*

*Next, since I hate the unnecessary multiplication of names no less than of things, even in those species which I have transferred from one genus to another, I have often left the ancient names without imposing any new ones. Because of this, I may perhaps seem to some to have refrained from even necessary <term>name-giving</term> <note>original Greek: ὀνοματοθεσίᾳ (onomatothesia)</note> out of fear of multiplication, and to have joined the ranks of those who, while avoiding one vice, run into its opposite. Nevertheless, for Indian and exotic names which might seem harsh and discordant to European ears, I have sometimes added headings or titles to the descriptions, which I hope will be neither useless nor ungrateful to the Reader.*

*In the next place, it is proper to warn that I do not sell as my own everything to which an Author's name is not attached. I both realize and regret that in many instances this was omitted through carelessness or haste: especially in the case of Jean Bauhin <note>A famous Swiss botanist (1541–1613)</note>, from whose History I have borrowed very many things without praising him. I acknowledge this here openly and honestly, lest anyone should deservedly charge me with the serious crime of plagiarism for secretly stealing the writings of others. In borrowed passages, I have scrupulously preserved the Authors' own words; I did not think I had the right to change the writings of others. Furthermore, I feared that if I did so (as it is easy to slip into error), I might corrupt the meaning and—through poorly understood sentences—attach a new meaning foreign to the Author's mind and perhaps even to the truth, and thus thrust it upon the Reader.*

*Furthermore, in the History of exotic Fruits—of which a vast number are described by Charles de l'Écluse, Jean Bauhin, Joannes de Laet, Ole Worm, and others—I confess that I am plainly a stranger, wandering as if in a Forest or Labyrinth from which I cannot extricate myself by any industry, nor reduce the individual species to their proper genera. Therefore, in arranging them, I preferred to follow the method of Caspar Bauhin <note>Brother of Jean Bauhin and author of the influential "Pinax" (1623)</note>, although it is not overly accurate, rather than exhaust myself in vain searching for a new and more convenient one. Others not recorded by Bauhin I have presented with almost no order preserved, just as they offered themselves. I have no doubt that some are the fruits of trees already described in the History, while others are repeated twice or thrice under different names. Those who live in the great Trading Centers and have the opportunity to buy the fruits themselves from sailors—and those to whom the Museums of the curious, filled with such treasures, stand open, allowing them to inspect them in person, compare them, and examine them carefully—will easily discern which fruits belong to which trees. They will see which ones, though the same, are mistaken for different ones due to variety of names and imperfect descriptions. Once all the similarities and differences are noted, they will arrange each in its place and bring them into order.*

*Finally, to hide nothing, while I review the sheets sent from the press, I detect errors made quite often by the Printing Works, sometimes by myself, and indeed more frequently than I would wish. Yet it is no wonder that in so long a work, sleep should sometimes creep upon a weary man through the tedium of long-lasting labor, and that in such great haste many things that should have been deleted escaped from a headlong pen. Those Errors I observed in the facts themselves you will find corrected in the Appendix; those in words and syntax you will find among the Typographical <term>oversights</term> <note>original Greek: παροράματα (paroramata)</note>. Indeed, beyond such <term>blunders</term> <note>original Greek: σφάλματα (sphalmata)</note> observed by me, I fear that errors were often made in the arrangement of Species, since I have not yet happened to see many of them, and those I saw long ago I did not contemplate so carefully as to investigate the characteristic marks of the genera (for at that time I did not even dream in my sleep of composing a History of Plants). In the descriptions of others, however, the principal parts from which the aforementioned marks [are derived...]*

# PREFACE

...must be taken from the aforementioned parts, namely the flowers and fruits, as well as their calyxes and seed-vessels, which are not infrequently omitted <note>The author is lamenting the lack of complete specimens in the sources he studied</note>: for which reason I have been forced to follow probable conjectures in these matters, and what I suspected rather than what I held as certain.

You will perhaps marvel, Reader, that the Appendix has grown to such a great bulk. This happened chiefly for two reasons; the first of which was my own forgetfulness and negligence. For when I was unrolling the *Histories of Plants* in order to patch this Work together, I found many species there arranged in the wrong places; I deferred them and omitted them for the time being, yet—trusting too much to my memory and willingly indulging in laziness—I failed to zealously extract them and keep them carefully noted on paper. Thus, when I reached the places where they ought to have been inserted, they had easily slipped from my mind and were omitted through forgetfulness. The other cause for the enlarged Appendix was the many new and not yet described Plants published after the first and second Volumes were finished and printed—some by others, but especially by that most celebrated man and by far the most skilled in herbal matters, Mr. Paul Hermann, Professor of Medicine and Botany in the Academy of Leiden, in the *Catalogue of Plants of the Medical Garden* of that Academy. Indeed, a large part of these is cultivated in the gardens around London. But for reasons not to be mentioned here, it was either not permitted or not pleasing for us to go to London. Indeed, even if it had been permitted, since I expected the edition of the recently praised Catalogue very soon, I would have deemed it better to spare my labor than to devote useless effort to describing them; for I was confident that I would find more accurate and fuller histories of them there than I myself could weave. For I was able to be present among them only once and for a short space of time, whereas Mr. Hermann always sees them <note>original: "τὰ ἀκμαῖα βλαστανῶν ἐκ τῆς γῆς, & ἀκμάζοντα, & θαυμαζόμενα"</note> <gloss>sprouting from the earth in their prime, and flourishing, and being wondered at.</gloss> We certainly owe many thanks to this most learned man, both for those things he has already provided in the aforementioned Work recently published, and for those things he has promised in the *Museum Zeylanicum* <note>The "Ceylon Museum," a work on the flora of Sri Lanka</note> to be published shortly. When he noticed that many, intent on profit for the sake of trade, sail to the East Indies every year with great fleets, but no one fit and suitable for the study of investigating Plants and promoting Natural History sets out thither—even though that region, with a sky and soil favorable to the generation of plants, pours forth almost innumerable species from its most fertile bosom, new, rare, and admirable in appearance and powers—he girded himself for the work. Magnanimously scorning the difficulties and dangers of so long a journey, he hastened across the immense sea to India, and staying there for several years and tenaciously pursuing his purpose, he curiously searched out those things which pertain to Natural History, and at last returned to Europe laden with the riches of the East.

But neither, while we are on this subject, should we keep silent about that most Illustrious Man worthy of immortal fame, Mr. Hendrik van Rheede van Draakenstein, who, though famous for things achieved in peace and war throughout the East Indies, did not think this sufficient for his praise. Instead, burning with a vast and <note>original: "ἀοιδίμῳ"</note> <gloss>renowned</gloss> zeal for illustrating and enlarging Botanical History, he spared no labor and no expense in seeking out, describing, and drawing the plants growing throughout the whole Kingdom of Malabar. By his benefit, we have not only the most beautiful icons <note>illustrations</note> of all that exist, but also descriptions no less accurate and faithful, and more species brought to light than I would have ever believed could arise spontaneously throughout the whole of India.

To these it is permitted to add a third, of no less name and fame in Botany, Mr. J. Banister, who has run the same course in what they call the West Indies, and is contemplating a *Natural History of Virginia*, where he now lives. Certainly, no one from our nation has hitherto set foot in the New World similar or second to him in the science of Botany and every kind of literature; no one has approached it better prepared with all the requirements for writing such a History.

By the industry of these men, the mountains, forests, valleys, shores, fields, meadows, and groves of both Indies have been surveyed, and their native and foster plants exposed to our eyes. From this we may learn that India differs from Europe no less in nature than in location, and that the character of that region differs so much from our own that if someone were transported from here to there while resting, upon waking he would scarcely believe he was conscious—so many things new, unusual, and admirable would meet his eyes everywhere. As, for example, the stupendous height of some trees, which equals or even exceeds three hundred feet; the enormous thickness of others, such that their trunks fill the extended arms of sixteen men embracing them. The leaves of this one are extended to such a length and spread to such a breadth that a single one suffices to shade twenty men and protect them from rain; the hardness of that one's wood rivals iron, nor is its weight much inferior. What is more wonderful than to behold a tree which, when it has produced neither fruit nor flower for thirty-five or forty years, then at last sends forth as if a new tree from its summit, and then shedding all its leaves, appears like some very tall ship's mast (for by nature it is <note>original: "μονόκλωνός"</note> <gloss>single-stemmed</gloss>, like the rest of the Palms) standing erect and raised on high; until this new tree gradually unfolds its foliage, and spreads very numerous branches and shoots in every direction, laden with an incredible multitude of flowers and the fruits succeeding them, which having been brought to maturity, the mother herself dies down to the roots.
