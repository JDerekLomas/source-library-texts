# PREFACE.

Although we are no longer in those unhappy centuries when nearly the entire world was plunged into the shadows of Idolatry, one can hardly do without a knowledge of the history of the Gods and the fables of <term>Paganism</term> <note>original: Paganisme; here referring to the polytheistic religions of the ancient world</note>. <term>Mythology</term> <note>original: Mythologie</note>, which teaches us to understand these fables and these gods, constitutes too significant a part of <term>Belles-Lettres</term> <note>original: Belles-Lettres; literally "fine letters," referring to the study of literature and the humanities</note> for it to be neglected. In fact, we read the works of the Greeks and Romans daily, especially those of their poets, whom it would often be very difficult to understand if one did not know the fables to which they make constant allusion.

Moreover, everything conspires to remind us of these ancient fictions: statues, bas-reliefs, and monuments of every kind. Indeed, what fills the books of <term>antiquarians</term> <note>original: Antiquaires; scholars who studied ancient artifacts and history</note> and the <term>cabinets of curiosities</term> <note>original: Cabinets des Curieux; private collections of notable objects, often precursors to modern museums</note> but images of <term>divinities</term> <note>original: Divinités</note>, sacrificial instruments, and everything that remains to us of ancient Paganism? Our galleries, our ceilings, our paintings, our statues—

<summary>This preface justifies the study of Greek and Roman mythology as an essential component of a classical education and a key to understanding Western art and literature.</summary>

<header>PREFACE.</header>

...ceaselessly represent the same objects to us; and as if <term>sacred and profane history</term> <note>original: l'Hiſtoire ſainte & profane; "sacred" refers to Biblical history, while "profane" refers to secular or non-biblical ancient history</note> did not provide enough interesting facts capable of inspiring virtuous sentiments in us, we borrow our subjects from <term>Fable</term> <note>original: la Fable; a term used in this period to refer to the collection of ancient myths and legends</note>, especially in our dramatic poetry.

Our theaters resound every day with the complaints of <term>Iphigenia</term> and <term>Andromache</term>, the rages of <term>Orestes</term>, and the outbursts of <term>Achilles</term> and <term>Clytemnestra</term> <note>Characters from Greek tragedy: Iphigenia was sacrificed by her father; Andromache was the widow of Hector; Orestes avenged his father Agamemnon; Achilles was the greatest Greek warrior; Clytemnestra was the wife and murderer of Agamemnon</note>; and let us not blush to admit it: we always view these heroes and heroines upon our stage with a renewed pleasure, while we often find it difficult to endure other characters more suited to excite a noble emulation within us.

It is therefore useful, and even in some sense necessary, to know Mythology; thus we see that those who are ignorant of it are considered to be lacking in education and the most necessary enlightenment for a <term>man of letters</term> <note>original: homme de Lettres; a person of broad scholarly and literary learning</note>. But when one comes to consider that the Fables are not pure fictions, as I prove at the beginning of this work; that they have a real relationship with the history of the first centuries; that they contain significant events; and that most of the Gods were actually men whose history forms part of that of the peoples who worshipped them—then Mythology becomes a more important object, and at the same time, more worthy of our curiosity.

It is this historical foundation, hidden beneath the cloak of Fable, which was the principal object of my research when I began to apply myself to the study of Mythology; and the public favorably received the *Explanation*...

<summary>The author argues that mythology is essential for an educated person because it dominates the arts and theater, and more importantly, because myths are actually distorted records of real historical people and events.</summary>
