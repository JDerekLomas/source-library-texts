### Roasted Meat Slices <gloss>The word "脯" (meat) is pronounced as "fu."</gloss>
<note>original: "fuzhi" (脯炙). While the character "fu" often refers to dried meat or jerky, here it describes fresh meat cut into small, uniform pieces for roasting.</note>

Meat from beef, mutton, elk, or deer may all be used. Cut the meat into square-inch chunks. Take the white parts of green onions and grind them until they are very fine. Mix these with salt and the liquid from fermented black beans; use just enough liquid to submerge the meat. Let it soak for a short time, then roast it immediately. If there is too much liquid or if the meat is left to soak for too long, it will become waterlogged and lose its texture.

Spread the coals open and bring the meat close to the heat. Turn it rapidly and roast it intensely. When the color turns white and it is cooked through, the meat will be juicy, smooth, and delicious. If you lift the meat away from the fire and put it back down repeatedly, or move it up and down too much, the fat will be scorched away and the meat will become dry. It will no longer be fit to eat.

### Roasted Liver

Liver from cattle, sheep, or pigs may all be used. Slice the liver into pieces one and a half inches long and half an inch wide. Prepare and season the liver using the same method of onions, salt, and fermented bean liquid as described for the **Roasted Meat Slices**.

## Roasted Beef Tripe

The tripe of an old ox is thick and fatty. Score the surface and pierce it with a skewer, scrunching the meat tightly together so it is compressed. Bring it very close to the heat and roast it rapidly until the surface splits open. Only then should you carve it; prepared this way, it will be crispy and exceptionally delicious. If you stretch the tripe out flat and roast it slowly over a low fire from a distance, it will become thin, dry, and rubbery.

# Fried Fish Cakes

To prepare this, fresh fish is required; "White Fish" <note>original: 白魚 (*baiyu*). Likely referring to the *Anabarilius* species or other pale-fleshed freshwater fish prized for their delicate texture.</note> is best. Catfish and carp are unsuitable for this purpose.

### Chapter 81: Making Preserved Meat and Grains-Packed Meat

## Method for Making Preserved Meat

Donkey, horse, and pork may all be used for this preparation. Those made during the twelfth lunar month <note>original: 臘月 (*layue*). This is the last month of the traditional Chinese calendar, known for being the ideal time for curing meats due to the cold, dry weather.</note> are of the highest quality. Meat preserved during this time will last through the summer without attracting insects. If you prepare this in other months, you must cover and protect it extremely carefully; if the seal is not airtight, maggots will inevitably grow.

For venison—specifically deer, elk, or muntjac <note>original: 麖 (*jing*). A small species of deer common in East Asia.</note>—keep the bones in if they are present. Chop the deer or elk meat into pieces. Mix together salt, <term>koji</term> <note>original: 麴 (*qu*). A fermentation starter made from grain colonized by molds, essential for brewing and curing.</note>, and wheat bran. Use your judgment to determine the total amount needed based on the quantity of meat.

As for the proportions: use equal parts salt and <term>koji</term>, and use half as much wheat bran as you used <term>koji</term>. Once the ingredients are thoroughly mixed, place the meat into a ceramic jar and seal the opening tightly with mud. Expose the jar to the sun for fourteen days <note>original: 二七日 (*er qi ri*), literally "two seven-days."</note>, at which point the meat will be cured and ready. Boil the meat before consuming.

## Method for Making Potted Meat
<note>original: 輿肉法 (*yurou fa*). While "yu" literally means a carriage or cart, in this culinary context it refers to a specific style of potted or preserved meat intended for long-term storage and convenience.</note>

Once the carcass has been prepared, singe it with fire until the skin turns a golden yellow. Use warm water to scrub and wash the hide; scrape it until it is perfectly clean.

Chop the carcass and remove the internal organs and the pig’s leaf fat. <term>Scald</term> <note>original: 燖 (*xun*). Next, take the lard that was rendered earlier and use it to cook the meat again. As a general rule for proportions, use two units <note>original: 升 (*sheng*). Strain the meat out and place it into a ceramic jar. Pour the remaining liquid fat into the jar over the meat so that it is entirely submerged and sealed. When you wish to serve it, boil a portion in water until it is tender and season it with aromatics as you would normally.

# Methods for Water-Pulled and Hand-Torn Noodles

Sieve the flour through fine silk to ensure it is pure. Prepare a seasoned meat broth <note>original: 臛汁 (*huozhi*). A savory, concentrated stock used here as the liquid base for the dough to add depth of flavor.</note> and allow it to cool completely before using it to knead the dough. 

For **Water-Pulled Noodles** <note>original: 水引 (*shuiyin*). Place them in a dish filled with water to soak. When ready to cook, hold the dough over the boiling cauldron and use your hands to stretch and flatten the strips until they are as thin as leek leaves, dropping them into the water as it boils.

Not only are they a lovely, bright white color, but they also possess an extraordinary smoothness and flavor that is truly exceptional.

### The Method for Preparing Flour "Sand-Grits" <gloss>pronounced *sha-shen*</gloss>

# Steamed Bulbs of Chinese Onion

Take one *shi* <note>a large volume measure, approximately 20-30 liters in this period</note> of glutinous millet <note>original: *shumi* (秫米)</note> and pound it thoroughly <gloss>thoroughly</gloss> until the grains are clean and fine and no longer sticky. Take three *sheng* <note>approximately 0.2 liters each</note> of fermented black beans and boil them to make a liquid. When it bubbles up, use a basket to strain it.

Take this liquid and pour it over the rice, making the mixture uniform so that the grains remain loose and separated. Once the shrimp <note>original: *xiami* (蝦米); in some versions of this text, this refers to small dried shrimp used for seasoning, though it may also refer to the texture of the broken grains</note> have been separated and strained out, let the rice sit in the fermented bean liquid for half a day in the summer, or a full day in the winter.

Remove the rice and add green onions and Chinese onion bulbs <term>Xiebai (薤白): the bulbs of *Allium chinense*, known for their sharp, garlic-like flavor and medicinal properties</term>, both cut into inch-long pieces, until you have a volume of about one *shi*. Add coriander cut into inch-long pieces until you have about one *sheng*. Combine this with five *sheng* of oil, mix well, and steam the mixture. This may be divided into two steaming vessels <term>Zeng (甑): a traditional earthenware steamer with a perforated floor</term> for the process.

When the steam rises, sprinkle five *sheng* of the fermented bean liquid over it. Repeat this three times in total, with three separate sprinklings. The process should last about as long as it takes to cook a standard meal <note>original: *yi chui jiu* (一炊久), a traditional measurement of time, roughly 30–40 minutes</note>. After the third sprinkling of bean liquid and when the dish is half-cooked, sprinkle another five *sheng* of oil over it.

Take it down immediately to serve while hot. If it is not to be eaten immediately, it must be re-steamed later. After the final oil is sprinkled and the steam has risen, do not leave the vessel sitting on the stove, or the oil will leak away and be lost. Re-steaming should not be done for a long duration.

# Method for Salting and Pickling Turnips, Chinese Cabbage, Mallow, and Sichuan Mustard

At the time of harvest, select the finest vegetables and bind them together using sedge grass <term>jianpu (菅蒲): a type of hardy reed or marsh grass used for tying bundles</term>. Prepare a brine and make it extremely salty. Wash the vegetables directly in this brine and then place them into a ceramic jar. If you were to wash them with fresh water first, the pickles would rot <note>The salt in the initial wash acts as an immediate antimicrobial agent, preventing the growth of spoilage-causing bacteria that thrive in plain water.</note>. Take the brine used for washing the vegetables, let it settle until clear, and pour the clear liquid into the jar until it completely covers the vegetables <gloss>once the vegetables are submerged, stop; do not add any further seasonings</gloss>. The color of the pickles will remain a vibrant green. To serve, wash away the salty brine with fresh water and cook them as a vegetable dish; they will be indistinguishable from fresh greens.

For the two specific varieties—turnip and Sichuan mustard—remove them from the jar after three days. Grind broomcorn millet into a powder to make a thin, clear porridge liquid. Pound wheat bran <term>kuai (糩): the coarse husks or chaff left over after milling grain</term> into a fine powder and sift it through silk.

Lay down one row of vegetables and lightly dust <term>ben (坌): an ancient term for sprinkling or covering something with a fine powder</term> them with the bran powder. Immediately pour the hot porridge liquid over them. Repeat these layers—vegetables, powder, and liquid—until the jar is completely full. When layering the vegetables, you must alternate the orientation of the stalks and leaves in every row <note>This "head-to-tail" packing method ensures the vegetables fit tightly and ferment evenly by balancing the bulk of the leaves with the thickness of the stems.</note>. Finally, pour the original brine back into the jar.

## Method for Preserving Melons from the *Dietary Classic* <note>original: 食經 (Shi Jing); a lost ancient culinary manual frequently cited in agricultural texts</note>
