# ¶ JACQUES LEFÈVRE TO HIS DEAR RAYMOND, GREETINGS.

<margin><unclear>Peter</unclear> <note>original: "Petrus." This marginal note appears to be a name, possibly a reader or owner's mark.</note> <image-desc>A small sketch of a manicule (pointing hand) towards the text</image-desc></margin>

<image-desc>Decorative woodcut initial 'C' containing a stag standing amidst stylized foliage.</image-desc>Since every state of the particular <term>religious orders</term><note>original: "religionum." Here referring specifically to monastic and mendicant orders like the Carthusians or Celestines rather than "religions" in the modern global sense.</note>, dearest Raymond, is a certain flight from the world and an approach to things beyond the world—a forsaking of the many and the divided, and a clinging to the One—it follows that each order possesses as much truth as it cleaves to things beyond the world, and to that One which is above all oneness. In this union and cleaving consists true contemplation and the sweetest fruit of the contemplative life. This fruit is known by few, as it is achieved not without secret <term>infusions</term><note>original: "immissionibus." In mystical theology, this refers to the "pouring in" of divine grace or knowledge directly into the soul.</note> and divine visitations. Through these, as far as is permitted to mortal man, the spiritual state is unveiled. 

For the blessed souls who have already cast off the garment of mortality live the spiritual state perfectly; those, however, who have not yet cast it off can indeed live in it, but not yet perfectly—not of themselves, but through a certain divine arrival. "I go," says the Savior, "and I come to you" <note>original: "Vado... & venio ad vos." A reference to the Gospel of John 14:18 or 14:28, often interpreted by mystics as the internal "coming" of Christ to the soul.</note>. And I believe there is no particular religious order in which there are not, or have not been, some who have perceived something of this sweetness, especially those that are more solitary, withdrawn, and strict. 

Therefore, I would not believe the <term>Carthusians</term><note>A monastic order known for extreme solitude and silence, founded by St. Bruno in 1084.</note> and <term>Celestines</term><note>A branch of the Benedictines founded by Peter of Morrone (later Pope Celestine V) that emphasized asceticism.</note> to hold the lowest places in this sublimity of the contemplative life; and rightly so, both because of their greater solitude and the strictness of their life, and because of their most watchful providence in defending their state from falling away. On this account, they also love the books of contemplative men most of all. This is not because books make contemplatives most perfect, but because they prepare and incite beginners; God, however, completes the rest. For in a higher way, a heavenly ray and a certain divine encounter make men contemplatives. 

Nevertheless, the Celestines of <note>Lefèvre lists several monastic houses in France.</note> Mantes, Sens, Marcoussis, and Paris, and the Carthusians of Saint-Omer, Mont-Dieu, Bourgfontaine, and the "Green Valley" of the Parisian solitude <note>original: "vallis viridis." Referring to the Carthusian monastery of Vauvert in Paris, located near the current Jardin du Luxembourg.</note>, have frequently shared most of these kinds of books with us, and they continue to share them most kindly every day as need requires; among whom...

You have avidly read the book shared with you regarding the adornment of the <term>spiritual marriage</term><note>original: "spiritualium nuptiarum." This refers to Ruusbroec's most famous work, *The Spiritual Espousals*, which describes the soul's journey toward union with God.</note>, which Jan van Ruusbroec—a devout and religious father of the <term>Augustinian Canons Regular</term><note>original: "canonicę obseruationis beati Augustini." A branch of the Augustinian order known for its strict adherence to the Rule.</note>—composed some time ago, around the era of <term>Boniface IX</term><note>Pope from 1389 to 1404. Ruusbroec actually died in 1381, but Lefèvre is providing a general historical timeframe.</note>.
<margin>mar-riage and other wed-dings</margin>
However, it troubled you while reading that <term>Gerson</term><note>Jean Gerson (1363–1429), the influential Chancellor of the University of Paris, who famously criticized Ruusbroec's mysticism for potentially leading to pantheistic errors.</note>—a devout man of grave authority, who bore a religious mind beneath a secular habit—judged the author of that work to be insufficiently learned, even rebuking him in certain matters as one in error. This should have moved you but little. For there is only one who finds fault, while a great crowd of holy men defends him. This fact cannot escape you if you read the devout works of <term>Thomas à Kempis</term><note>original: "Ioānis Quempiſij." Referring to Thomas à Kempis, the probable author of *The Imitation of Christ* and a fellow member of the Devotio Moderna movement that revered Ruusbroec.</note>, a man of blameless life.
<margin>By these things committed [to those] who believe [the] father [to the] brothers</margin>
Gerson, in the judgment of many, was both devout and possessed of much secular learning; and this man [Ruusbroec] was likewise devout and possessed of much spiritual learning. And both are good and to be revered: the latter in the cloister, the former in the world.

But indeed, that this book was first published in the native speech and the <term>vernacular tongue</term><note>original: "vernacula lĩgua." Ruusbroec wrote in Middle Dutch so that his teachings would be accessible to those without a university education in Latin.</note> is not sufficient proof that the author was of little learning. For even the most learned man can publish books in the vernacular—perhaps far better than an unlearned one.
<margin>It is difficult</margin>
And the grammarians who read this will judge the author to be remarkably elegant for that time; the rhetoricians will find him eloquent; the philosophers will see him as one skilled in the secrets of nature; the astrologers as a knower of the times; the physicians as a master of diseases and health; and the theologians as a master of divine matters.

<image-desc>A small decorative manicule (pointing hand) points to the next paragraph.</image-desc>

I think, therefore, that this happened to Gerson (an upright man indeed): that he happened upon a corrupt manuscript. This has happened to me elsewhere. But when I came upon a true and corrected copy, having previously judged the author unlearned, I changed my opinion and began to admire him. By a similar reasoning, I think he [Gerson] was mistaken. And later <term>Trithemius</term><note>Johannes Trithemius (1462–1516), a famous polymath and abbot who likely repeated Gerson's criticisms in his bibliographical catalogs.</note> followed him through his own writings, as one who wrote similar things based on hearsay. For neither the one nor the other was of such an unfair mind or such malignant judgment that (if they had seen the true volume) they would have ever accused the man of having little learning. Read, therefore, with confidence what Gerson himself would have read with confidence and even with delight. Farewell, progressing in piety.

->At Paris. On the <term>Nones of August</term><note>August 5th, 1512.</note><-
