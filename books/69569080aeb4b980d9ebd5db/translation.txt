## Usage guidelines

Google is proud to partner with libraries to digitize public domain materials and make them widely accessible. Public domain books belong to the public, and we are merely their custodians. Nevertheless, this work is expensive; therefore, in order to keep providing this resource, we have taken steps to prevent abuse by commercial parties, including placing technical restrictions on automated querying.

We also ask that you:

+ *Make non-commercial use of the files:* We designed Google Book Search for use by individuals, and we request that you use these files for personal, non-commercial purposes.

+ *Refrain from automated querying:* Do not send automated queries of any sort to Google’s system. If you are conducting research on machine translation, optical character recognition <note>OCR; the process of converting images of typed or handwritten text into machine-encoded text</note>, or other areas where access to a large amount of text is helpful, please contact us. We encourage the use of public domain materials for these purposes and may be able to help.

+ *Maintain attribution:* The Google “watermark” you see on each file is essential for informing people about this project and helping them find additional materials through Google Book Search. Please do not remove it.

+ *Keep it legal:* Whatever your use, remember that you are responsible for ensuring that what you are doing is legal. Do not assume that just because we believe a book is in the public domain for users in the United States, the work is also in the public domain for users in other countries. Whether a book is still in copyright varies from country to country, and we cannot offer guidance on whether any specific use of any specific book is allowed. Please do not assume that a book’s appearance in Google Book Search means it can be used in any manner anywhere in the world. Copyright infringement liability can be quite severe.

## THE
# PREFACE.

Laws and government are to the political bodies of civil societies what the vital spirits <note>original: "Vital Spirits"; in early medicine, these were thought to be substances that moved through the body to sustain life and function.</note> and life itself are to the natural bodies of living creatures. Those who study the anatomy of dead carcasses may see that the chief organs and the most delicate springs required to keep our "machine" <note>The author refers to the human body as a mechanical system, a common view in 17th and 18th-century philosophy.</note> in motion are not the hard bones, strong muscles, and nerves, nor the smooth white skin that so beautifully covers them. Instead, they are small, seemingly trivial membranes and little pipes that are either overlooked or seem insignificant to common eyes. 

In the same way, those who examine the nature of man—separating what is natural from what is learned through art and education—may observe that what truly makes him a sociable animal does not consist of his desire for company, his good nature, pity, politeness, or other graces of a pleasant exterior. Rather, his most "vile" and hateful qualities are actually the most necessary accomplishments to fit him for the largest and—according to the world—the happiest and most flourishing societies.

<summary>The author argues that just as microscopic biological parts sustain the human body, man's "vile" or selfish qualities, rather than his virtues, are the hidden foundations that allow large, prosperous societies to function.</summary>
