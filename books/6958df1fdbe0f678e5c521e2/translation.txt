# TO THE ZEALOUS READER 
## OF BOTANY <note>original: "Rei Herbariae," referring to the study of plants and gardens</note>

->Health and Greeting<- <note>original: "S. D." for "Salutem Dicit," a standard Latin epistolary greeting</note>

<image-desc>A large historiated woodcut initial 'C' containing a figure (possibly Hercules) wrestling a lion, surrounded by ornate foliate scrollwork.</image-desc>

Since many authors, both recent and ancient, have already discussed the different types of gardens, their utility, and their merits—as well as the honorable pleasure they provide—at great length in their writings, I have deemed it unnecessary to repeat all those points here. However, it seemed appropriate to briefly preface this work with a few matters pertaining to the nature of the subject we have now undertaken to treat and to our specific purpose, while reserving the remaining thoughts we are still contemplating on this genre for a more convenient time. Therefore, the "garden" <note>original: "Hortus"</note> of which we speak was understood in two ways among the ancients. First, in a more general sense, they understood it to mean the entire country estate <note>original: "universam villam"</note>, as the author Festus <note>Sextus Pompeius Festus, a 2nd-century Roman grammarian</note> asserts, and as the legal experts know from the Law of the Twelve Tables <note>The ancient legislation that stood at the foundation of Roman law</note>; Pliny affirms the same in Book 19, Chapter 4. Horace also seems to have used the term in this sense in Book II of his *Satires*, Poem VI:

> *This was among my prayers: a piece of land, not so very large,*
> *Where there would be a garden, and near the house a spring of ever-flowing water,*
> *And a bit of woodland besides. The gods have done more*
> *And better than I hoped. It is well; I pray for nothing more.*

In a more proper and specific sense, however, it refers to a place for various plants

For gardens were either furnished at great expense for the sake of pleasure and luxury alone, such as those which formerly existed for the most part among the **Egyptians**. According to **Athenaeus** <note>A Greek rhetorician and grammarian, circa 200 AD</note> in Book 5, they took peculiar care so that, due to the excessive mildness of the air, they might have an abundance of fresh flowers at every season of the year. In our own times, we see the **Turks** most zealously imitating these; from them, a not insignificant supply of rare and unknown plants is usually brought to us. Most sumptuous also were those **Hanging Gardens of Babylon**, which were counted among the wonders of the world, and are described quite fully by **Quintus Curtius** in Book 1 and **Diodorus Siculus**. Those who have traveled to **Babylon** assert that certain monuments of these still exist in our age. Regarding the gardens of **Gelo** around **Syracuse**, **Athenaeus** recorded more in Book 12, though it is certain that these have clearly perished, like many others.

In this category, perhaps, the gardens of **Epicurus** should also be placed. He was the first to move them from the countryside into the city of **Athens**, as **Cicero** writes in *On the Nature of the Gods* and others record; for this reason, he was called by the ancients the master of leisure and gardens. Properly speaking, among the **Romans** these were formerly called **Gardens** <note>original: "Horti" (plural)</note> in the plural number, such as the **Sallustian Gardens**, which on account of their splendor and pleasantness were used by later Kings as residences
