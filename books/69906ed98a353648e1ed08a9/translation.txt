### Here begins the treatise on the construction and use of the instrument called the Albion, published by the venerable master Johannes de Gmunden

<margin>
The book of Master Johannes Henricus of Hassurt, given as a gift by Lord Leonhard <unclear>Kersperm</unclear>, Canon of Boppard. <note>Boppard is a town in Germany; this note records the book's provenance and ownership.</note>
</margin>

<margin>
A. R.
I.
</margin>

The **Albion** is a geometrical instrument named an "Almanac of Astrometry." <note>Astrometry is the branch of astronomy involving precise measurements of the positions and movements of stars.</note> Although it serves various uses in measuring parts of the heavens, the Albion specifically incorporates a planisphere <note>A flat representation of the celestial sphere</note> of the entire celestial orb, including concentric and eccentric circles and epicycles <note>These are the geometric models used in the Ptolemaic system to explain the complex paths of planets</note> for the mathematical motions of all wandering and fixed stars, calculated with wondrous subtlety. It extends to every honorable motion of the heavenly bodies, showing the wandering stars <note>The planets</note> through their spheres, and the stars through their locations and aspects at any time, just as it equates their properties and daily motions through its own formulas. Indeed, it verifies the calculations of the fixed stars better than the **rectangulum** or the **torquetum**. <note>The Rectangulum and Torquetum were other complex medieval astronomical instruments; the author argues the Albion is superior.</note> Tables verify the various motions of the planets and fixed stars, and the astrolabe—which is called a "clock"—certifies the moments of time and hours; however, this instrument is called **Albion** because it adds the utilities and conveniences of each and every one of these into one single body, more briefly than any other could perhaps provide. Therefore it is named **Albion**, which is the same as "All-England" <gloss>that is</gloss> "all-by-one" <note>original: "to de bo"</note> <gloss>it is</gloss> called such in every interpretation. <note>The name "Albion" is a famous pun by its inventor, Richard of Wallingford. It refers both to "Albion" (the ancient name for England) and the English phrase "All-by-one," because the instrument performs all astronomical tasks in one device.</note> This instrument does not belong to any other more than to its own inventor, though by the ingenuity of many it could be said to have achieved even greater things. I intend at present to treat the construction and use of this instrument, not because anything new has been invented or added here, but because these things were spoken of in a certain ancient writing <note>A reference to the original 14th-century text by Richard of Wallingford.</note> in a very diffuse and incorrect script, lacking the figures and tables necessary for the understanding and work of the instrument itself. I aim to supply this for the sake of the proper course of study, following the books of the *Almagest* <note>Ptolemy’s foundational 2nd-century work on astronomy.</note> and Euclid, and the theories of the planets.

<column-break/>

...and tables and other instruments in which studious men are accustomed to weigh and hope for the measures of motions. <unclear>mici</unclear> <note>The text here is somewhat fragmented in the source.</note> But so that it might be applied to practical utility, this entire little work is to be brought together in four equal parts, first applying the origin of the motions so that a subtle subject for thinking is given. Some properties are permitted in its expositions, upon which the work of this instrument is founded, in order to express the moments of calculation. I shall subjoin the beginnings of the uses and versions of the instrument itself, starting with the first property as the first conclusion of this first part.

**The distance** of the center of the eccentric <note>A circle whose center is off-set from the Earth.</note> from the center of the Earth is expressed by the elongation of the Sun from its apogee. <note>Apogee is the point in an orbit farthest from the Earth.</note> This is found in the third book of the *Almagest*, chapter 4, and in the *Abridged Almagest*, book 2, chapter 11. It could not be understood otherwise except through the mediation of time through one half of the circle through the signs of the Zodiac, from the autumnal equinox to the vernal equinox. And observe through the mediation of time one quarter of the Zodiac, which is from the vernal equinox to the summer solstice. And observe from the solstice to the autumnal equinox, and by the mean motions of the Sun contained in the three aforementioned periods, a comparison is made as treated elsewhere. In the current year of our Lord 1430, the time from the vernal equinox to the autumnal equinox was 181 days, 0 hours, and 26 minutes...
