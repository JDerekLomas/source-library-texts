The wise kings of antiquity taught all under heaven through the <term>Six Arts (liuyi): the classical curriculum for an educated gentleman, comprising Rites, Music, Archery, Charioteering, Calligraphy, and Mathematics</term>, and it was in mathematics that these arts reached their essential completion. 

Its origins are indeed ancient. In later times, the clan of Rong Cheng <note>a legendary figure from the era of the Yellow Emperor, credited with creating the calendar</note> established it, and Li Shou <note>another legendary minister to the Yellow Emperor, credited with inventing the decimal system and calculation</note> developed it further. Later, the Duke of Zhou received this knowledge from Shang Gao <note>a mathematician of the early Zhou dynasty, famous for describing the Pythagorean theorem</note>, and from this point, the study of mathematics flourished greatly. 

From the *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art* <note>the foundational text of Chinese mathematics, compiled during the Han dynasty</note> to the works of Sun Zi, and then to Xu Yue <note>original: 徐氏 (Xu shi); a mathematician of the late Han or Eastern Wu period</note> who took charge of these teachings, the tradition has been passed down through the generations. It became an established subject for the <term>Erudites (boshi): imperial scholars or professors who specialized in specific classics</term> and other officials, all falling under the jurisdiction of the <term>Grand Astrologer (taishi): the high court official responsible for the calendar, astronomy, and historical records</term>. 

By the time of the Sui and Tang dynasties <note>original: 淳唐 (Chun Tang); likely a reference to the "Pure Tang," signifying the era's cultural height</note>, figures such as Liu Hui, Li Chunfeng, Zhen Luan, and Xiahou Yang were commissioned to revise, examine, and annotate the texts. All were eminent masters of their craft. At that time, each of these masters specialized in their own particular branch of study.

# Diagram of the Yellow Bell as the Foundation of All Things
<note>original: "黃鍾萬事根本圖" (Huáng Zhōng Wàn Shì Gēnběn Tú). In ancient Chinese thought, the Yellow Bell was the primary musical pitch pipe. Because its dimensions were fixed, it was used as the universal standard to define all other units of measurement.</note>

## The Yellow Bell gives birth to Linear Measures

The length of the Yellow Bell pipe is equal to the span of ninety medium-sized grains of black millet laid end-to-end.
One grain is defined as one *fen*.
Ten *fen* make one *cun* (inch).
Ten *cun* make one *chi* (foot).
Ten *chi* make one *zhang*.
Ten *zhang* make one *yin*.
<note>The "black millet" (秬黍, jùshǔ) served as a natural, reproducible constant for measurement, similar to how the "inch" was once defined by barleycorns in the West.</note>

## The Yellow Bell gives birth to Capacity Measures

The interior volume of the Yellow Bell pipe is sufficient to hold one thousand two hundred medium-sized grains of black millet.
This volume is defined as one *shao*.
Ten *shao* make one *he*.
Ten *he* make one *sheng* (liter).
Ten *sheng* make one *dou* (peck).
Ten *dou* make one *hu* (bushel).

---

## The Yellow Bell gives birth to Weight Measures

The weight of the one thousand two hundred grains contained within the Yellow Bell (one *shao*) is twelve *zhu*.
Two *shao* (twenty-four hundred grains) weigh twenty-four *zhu*, which is defined as one *liang* (tael).
Sixteen *liang* make one *jin* (catty).
Thirty *jin* make one *jun*.
Four *jun* make one *shi* (stone).
<note>By linking weight to the capacity of the pitch pipe, the system ensured that if you knew the musical pitch, you could verify the accuracy of a merchant's scale.</note>

## The Yellow Bell gives birth to Musical Pitches

The length of the Yellow Bell is nine *cun* (inches).
Its inner circumference is nine *fen*.
The product of its dimensions is one hundred and ten parts, which is the root of all pitches.
The other eleven musical pitches are derived from this through the process of "addition and subtraction."
<note>original: "損益" (sǔnyì). This refers to the 'Three-Part Addition and Subtraction' method (Sanfen Sunyi), where one creates the musical scale by alternately removing or adding one-third of the length of the previous pipe to find the next note.</note>
<note>The OCR reads "one hundred and ten" (一百一十分), though standard musical theory of this era often cites eighty-one (nine times nine) as the base area/volume for the Yellow Bell pipe.</note>

○ The **Method of Addition** involves retaining the base value <note>original: "身" (shen), referring to the number already set on the abacus</note> and increasing it; this is called <insert>Addition</insert>. Alternatively, the Method of Return-Division <term>Guichu: abacus division using mnemonic songs</term> may be used instead: take the total rice (principal plus wastage) as the dividend and use 1.07 as the divisor to perform the division.

### 〇 The Method of Simplifying Fractions
Whenever division is used, there are often remainders <term>Jiling: literally "odd zeros," referring to fractional remainders or decimals</term> that do not divide evenly. ### 〇 The Method of Finding Common Denominators
This refers to instances where the divisor or the dividend contains a remainder.
