### On the Casting of the Great Horse

The proportions of the horse must be calculated with the utmost precision, for the weight of the bronze in the rearing position threatens the stability of the pedestal. <note>Leonardo is likely referring to the "Sforza Horse" or the "Trivulzio Monument," both of which posed immense engineering challenges due to their size and the dynamic pose of the animal.</note>

->**The Method of the Mold**<-

To ensure the metal flows into every part of the limb, the vents <term>vents: small tubes or channels that allow air and gases to escape the mold as molten metal is poured in</term> must be placed at the highest points of the casting. If the air is trapped, the bronze will be pitted with holes, ruining the smooth surface of the flank.

<column-break/>

<margin>The iron framework must be forged in one piece to prevent shifting during the heat of the pour.</margin>

I have observed that when the wax is melted away <note>This refers to the "lost-wax" casting process, where a wax model is replaced by molten metal</note>, the space left behind must be perfectly dry. Any moisture remaining within the clay core will turn to steam and cause the mold to burst with great violence. <insert>This occurred during the trial at the foundry in the year of our Lord 1493.</insert>

#### Observations on the Metal Mixture

| Part of the Statue | Proportion of Copper | Proportion of Tin |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| The Main Body | 100 parts | 12 parts |
| The Delicate Features | 100 parts | 15 parts |
| The Base and Supports | 100 parts | 8 parts |

<note>The addition of more tin makes the bronze more fluid for fine details but also more brittle; less tin makes for a stronger, tougher metal for structural supports.</note>

The furnace must be stoked with dry wood of the oak or the hornbeam to reach the necessary heat. <gloss>Use only seasoned wood to avoid excessive smoke.</gloss> When the metal runs like water, then and only then should the plug be pulled.

# Study for an Equestrian Monument

<note>This small, irregularly shaped fragment of paper is pasted onto a larger sheet. It contains a dynamic pen-and-ink sketch by Leonardo da Vinci, likely a study for an <term>equestrian monument</term> (a statue of a rider on horseback), such as the Sforza or Trivulzio monuments.</note>

The fragment depicts a rider on a horse in a <term>rearing</term> <note>original: "cabre"</note> position. The horse is shown in profile, facing right, with its front legs raised. Below the horse's hooves, there is a suggestion of a fallen figure, a common motif in Leonardo's designs for military monuments symbolizing triumph. The sketch is executed with quick, confident strokes of pen and ink.

<column-break/>

### Technical Vocabulary
*   **Equestrian** <note>original: "equestre"</note>: Relating to horse riding or statues depicting a person on a horse.
*   **Horse** <note>original: "cavallo"</note>: The central subject of Leonardo's anatomical and sculptural studies.
*   **Monument** <note>original: "monumento"</note>: A large-scale commemorative work, often cast in bronze.
*   **Sforza**: The ruling family of Milan; Leonardo spent years designing a massive bronze horse for Ludovico Sforza.
*   **Trivulzio**: Gian Giacomo Trivulzio, a military commander who later commissioned Leonardo for a similar tomb monument.

# Anatomical Study of a Horse's Foreleg

<note>This sheet contains a series of precise silverpoint drawings on blue-tinted paper, a technique Leonardo favored for its delicacy. These sketches focus on the musculature and bone structure of a horse's front limb, likely intended to ensure the "rearing" pose of his monuments was anatomically perfect.</note>

The central drawing illustrates the <term>humerus</term> <note>the upper bone of the forelimb</note> and the complex network of tendons surrounding the <term>carpus</term> <note>the "knee" of the horse, which anatomically corresponds to the human wrist</note>. Leonardo's mastery of light and shadow is evident in the way he defines the tension of the muscles.

<margin>The measurement of the joint must be taken when the limb is fully extended.</margin>

->**Observations on Movement**<-

When the horse lifts its leg to strike, the muscle marked **A** <insert>the brachiocephalicus</insert> contracts significantly. This movement is not merely a lifting of the weight, but a coiled spring of energy. <note>Leonardo often compared the mechanics of animals to complex machines.</note>

<column-break/>

### Comparative Anatomy
*   **Tendon** <note>original: "corda"</note>: Leonardo uses the word for "cord" or "string" to describe the fibrous tissues that connect muscle to bone.
*   **Proportion** <note>original: "proporzione"</note>: The mathematical relationship between the length of the hoof and the height of the shoulder.
*   **Nature** <note>original: "natura"</note>: The ultimate teacher; Leonardo insists that the artist must observe the living animal rather than following traditional artistic "rules."

<margin>Note how the skin folds at the point of the elbow when the leg is bent.</margin>

It highlights his belief that a successful monument must be grounded in a deep understanding of internal anatomy and mechanics.</summary>

# On the Construction of Scythed Chariots

When these chariots pass through the ranks of the enemy, the scythes must be positioned at such a height that they strike the limbs of the infantry with the greatest force. <note>Leonardo is calculating the optimal "kill zone" for the rotating blades to ensure they do not simply pass over a fallen soldier.</note> It is necessary that the horses be armored with plates of iron, for if the horses are wounded, the entire machine becomes a danger to its own side.

->**The Mechanism of the Axle**<-

The motion is derived from the main axle of the wheels.
