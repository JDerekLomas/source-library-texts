**Fang Xuanling**, whose courtesy name <note>A name given at adulthood to be used by peers; original: "Zi Qiao" (字喬)</note> was Qiao, was alert and quick-witted from his youth, showing a great talent for literary composition. Whenever he accompanied **Emperor Taizong** <note>The second emperor of the Tang Dynasty (reigned 626–649 AD), often considered one of China's greatest rulers</note> on military expeditions, his primary concern was to recruit talented individuals to serve in the prince's command center.

The King <note>Referring to Taizong before his formal ascension to the throne</note> once remarked: "In the past, when Emperor Guangwu of the Han dynasty gained the service of **Deng Yu** <note>A famous advisor and general who helped restore the Han Dynasty; he was known for recommending talented people</note>, his inner circle became even more devoted. Now that I have Xuanling, he is to me what Deng Yu was to him."

Xuanling brought in **Du Ruhui** <note>Together, Fang and Du became the most famous ministerial duo in Chinese history; Fang was known for his planning and Du for his decisive judgment</note> to assist in determining grand strategies for the empire. When Taizong ascended the throne and evaluated the merit of his subjects, he ranked Xuanling as the foremost contributor. 

He served as **Chancellor** <term>The highest-ranking civil official in the imperial government</term> for fifteen years. His daughter was married to a King and his son married a Princess. Realizing that his power and the imperial favor he enjoyed had reached their absolute zenith, he repeatedly offered his resignation, but the Emperor would not grant it.

When his illness became critical, the Emperor ordered a section of the imperial garden wall to be breached so that he could more easily send messengers to inquire after the minister's health. The Emperor personally held his hand to say a final farewell, and then he passed away.

Du Ruhui, whose **<term>courtesy name</term>** <note>A name traditionally bestowed upon reaching adulthood, used in formal social circles</note> was Keming, was spirited and brilliant in his youth. He had a great love for books and prided himself on his refined, scholarly character. Internally, he was a man of great integrity, and when faced with critical situations, he was known for his immediate and firm decisiveness.

He followed **Emperor Taizong** on his military campaigns, where he frequently provided counsel from within the <term>strategic tent</term> <note>The "tent" or "curtain" (帷幄) refers to the inner sanctum of military planning</note>. Later, he managed the affairs of the imperial government alongside **Fang Xuanling**. He died at the age of forty-six. The Emperor wept for him in profound grief and issued an edict commanding **Yu Shinan** to compose and engrave a commemorative text upon a stone monument.

Ruhui excelled at making final decisions, while Xuanling was gifted at strategic planning. Because the two men shared a deep mutual understanding, they were able to work with one heart to fulfill their plans, thereby achieving a period of prosperous and stable rule <note>This celebrated partnership is the origin of the historical phrase "Fang's planning and Du's decisiveness" (房謀杜斷), representing the perfect administrative balance</note>.

**Li Jing**, <term>courtesy name</term> **Yaoshi**, was a native of Sanyuan in Jingzhao <note>located in modern-day Shaanxi Province</note>. His physical appearance was imposing and extraordinary. He once remarked to others, “A man of great character should achieve wealth and status through merit and renown; why would he stoop to being a mere scholar of <term>pedantic commentary</term>?” <note>The original text refers to "Zhangju Ru," scholars who focused on rote memorization and literal line-by-line interpretation of the Classics rather than practical application.</note>

His maternal uncle, **Han Qinhu** <note>a famous general of the Sui Dynasty</note>, once discussed military strategy with him and remarked, “He is someone who can truly be compared to **Wu Qi**.” <note>original: "Wu"; referring to the famous strategist of the Warring States period</note> Upon meeting him, the official **Niu Hong** observed, “This is a talent fit to assist a king.”

During the <term>Zhenguan era</term> <note>627–649 AD, the reign of Emperor Taizong of the Tang Dynasty</note>, Li Jing served both as a general in the field and as a chancellor at court. He was eventually enfeoffed as the **Duke of the State of Wei**.

**Yuchi Jingde** <note>original: 尉遲敬德</note>

**Yuchi Jingde**, whose given name was Gong but who was known by his courtesy name <note>A courtesy name, or "zi," was an honorary name given to a man at age twenty; using it was a sign of respect from his peers.</note>, initially served as a <term>Lieutenant General</term> under the warlord Liu Wuzhou. 

In the second year of the **Wude** era <note>619 AD; the Wude era (618–626) marked the beginning of the Tang Dynasty under Emperor Gaozu.</note>, he and Xun Xiang surrendered their territories and defected to the Tang. He was subsequently appointed as a <term>Commander of the Right Guard</term>. 

When Xun Xiang later betrayed the Tang and fled, the various generals became suspicious of Jingde’s loyalty. However, **Taizong** <note>Emperor Taizong (r. 626–649), born Li Shimin, who was at this time the Prince of Qin and the primary military leader of the Tang forces.</note> dismissed their fears, stating: "If Jingde intended to rebel, would he really have waited until *after* Xun Xiang had already left?" 

Ultimately, Jingde helped finalize the plan to execute the Crown Prince <note>This refers to the Incident at Xuanwu Gate in 626 AD, a palace coup where Li Shimin eliminated his rivals, the Crown Prince Li Jiancheng and Prince Li Yuanji, to secure the throne.</note> and was eventually enfeoffed as the **Duke of E** <note>The title "Duke of E" (E Guo Gong) was one of the highest honors for a Tang official, signifying his status as a founding hero of the dynasty.</note>.

**Zhangsun Wuji**, whose courtesy name was **Fuji**, was naturally brilliant and possessed a wide-ranging knowledge of history and literature. He was enfeoffed as the **Duke of Qi**. 

Because he was a relative of the imperial family by marriage <note>Wuji's sister was the virtuous Empress Zhangsun, wife of Emperor Taizong</note>, he feared that holding the high rank of the <term>Three Ducal Ministers|The three highest-ranking civil service positions in the imperial government</term> would cause resentment or suspicion of nepotism. He firmly declined the position of Chancellor, though the Emperor refused to accept his resignation.

Initially, when the former heir apparent, **Chengqian**, was deposed, the Emperor wished to name the **Prince of Jin** <note>Li Zhi, who would become Emperor Gaozong</note> as the new successor but remained hesitant. A single word of support from Wuji settled the matter. 

Later, following the Emperor's death, Wuji received the final testamentary edict to serve as a senior advisor to **Emperor Gaozong**. However, because he opposed the Emperor’s plan to promote **Wu Zhaoyi** <note>The consort who would later become Empress Wu Zetian, the only woman to rule China as an emperor in her own right</note> to the position of Empress, he fell from favor. His official ranks and noble titles were stripped away, and he was exiled to **Qianzhou**, where he ultimately died.

**Yu Shinan** (whose style name was **Boshi**) and his elder brother **Yu Shiji** both received their education under the master **Gu Yewang** in the region of Wu. At the time, Yu Shinan's reputation was so great that he was compared to the <term>Two Lus of the Jin Dynasty<note>Referring to the famous brothers Lu Ji and Lu Yun, who were legendary literary figures of the 3rd century.</note></term>. When **Emperor Taizong** ascended the throne, he appointed Yu as a Scholar of the <term>Institute for the Advancement of Literature<note>The Hongwen Guan, an imperial academy and library where the elite studied and preserved classical texts.</note></term>.

The Emperor once commanded a transcription of the *Biographies of Exemplary Women*. At that time, no physical copy of the manuscript was available. Yu Shinan, however, was able to transcribe the entire work from memory; when he finished, not a single character was found to be in error. 

The Emperor frequently praised Yu for possessing the **"Five Excellences"**:
1. **Virtuous conduct**
2. **Loyalty and integrity**
3. **Vast learning**
4. **Literary skill**
5. **Calligraphy**

Upon his death, he was granted the posthumous title **Wenyi** <note>original: "文懿" (Wényì), meaning "Cultured and Admirable."</note>.

Chu Suiliang, whose **courtesy name** <note>original: "字" (zi); an honorary name given in adulthood to be used by peers and superiors.</note> was Dengshan, was widely read in literature and history. **Emperor Taizong** <note>Reigned 626–649 AD; he was a great patron of the arts and a foundational figure of the Tang Dynasty.</note> once intended to perform sacred ceremonies at **Mount Tai**, but Suiliang advised him to stop because of an omen in the stars <note>In ancient Chinese political thought, unusual astronomical events (star changes) were viewed as signs of Heaven's displeasure with the ruler’s plans.</note>. **Yu Zhining**, courtesy name <term>Zhongmi: A "courtesy name" was a formal name given to an adult in Imperial China to be used by peers and colleagues</term>, was a native of Gaoling in Jingzhao <note>modern-day Gaoling District, Shaanxi Province</note>. In the third year of the Zhenguan era <note>629 AD</note>, he served as the Deputy Head of the Secretariat. 

Emperor Taizong once held a banquet for his close ministers and asked, "Where is Yu Zhining?" The officials reported that the decree specified summoning only officials of the third rank, whereas Yu was of the fourth rank. The Emperor issued a special decree for him to attend the banquet and, as a result, promoted him to Cavalier Attendant-in-Ordinary, Left Mentor to the Crown Prince, and Duke of Liyang County.

At that time, there was a debate regarding the establishment of the Seven Imperial Ancestral Shrines. The assembled officials requested that King Wuzhao of Liang <note>Li Gao, the founder of the Western Liang state</note> be designated as the First Ancestor. Yu Zhining argued that the Liang state was not the true origin of the current imperial enterprise; he stood alone in his advice, opposing the others. 

Later, the Emperor ordered that meritorious officials should be granted hereditary positions as Prefects. Yu Zhining submitted a memorial stating that "the present and the past are different times," and argued that pursuing the "empty names" of ancient systems while ignoring "actual risks" was not a strategy for long-term stability. The Emperor followed his advice in both instances.

He also assisted the Crown Prince, Li Chengqian, frequently offering loyal and blunt criticisms, though his advice was not followed. When the Crown Prince eventually fell from power, the palace officials were all punished or dismissed, but Yu Zhining alone was spared and even received praise and encouragement for his efforts. He was later granted the title of Duke of Yan and supervised the compilation of the national history. 

He died in the fourth year of the Xianqing era <note>659 AD</note> and was given the posthumous title "Ding" <note>meaning "The Settled" or "The Stable"</note>. He also worked alongside Li Ji, the Minister of Works, to revise and finalize the *Materia Medica* <term>Bencao: A comprehensive encyclopedia of medicinal plants, animals, and minerals</term>, including illustrations, totaling fifty-four volumes. This book became widely influential.

# **Yan Shigu**

**Yan Shigu**, whose courtesy name was **Zhou**, was widely read from his youth and possessed a great talent for composition. During the reign of Emperor Gaozu <note>The founding emperor of the Tang dynasty, r. 618–626</note>, he rose through successive positions to become a **Secretariat Drafter** <term>Zhongshu Sheren: A high-ranking official responsible for drafting imperial edicts and handling confidential state documents</term>, where he was specifically entrusted with classified matters. 

Shigu was naturally quick-witted and highly experienced in the art of governance. During periods when military and state affairs were pressing, most of the imperial edicts and decrees were issued directly from his hand. His skill in drafting petitions and memorials to the throne was such that no contemporary could match him.

When Emperor Taizong ascended the throne, Shigu was appointed **Vice Minister of the Secretariat** and enfeoffed as the **Baron of Langya County**. The Emperor once lamented that the transmission and study of the *Five Classics* <note>The foundational texts of Confucianism: the Books of Songs, Documents, Changes, Rites, and the Spring and Autumn Annals</note> had become stagnant and riddled with errors over time. He issued an edict for Shigu to go to the **Palace Library** <term>Bishusheng: The imperial department responsible for the custody and collation of books and records</term> to critically examine and standardize the texts for distribution throughout the empire. Scholars everywhere relied upon his work. 

He was soon appointed **Deputy Director of the Palace Library**, specializing in the collation and correction of ancient texts and unusual characters <note>original: "奇字" (qizi), referring to archaic or rare variants of Chinese characters</note> that had long confused the public; his analyses and explanations were always thorough and clear. He also produced a commentary for Ban Gu’s *Book of Han* <note>The standard history of the Western Han dynasty</note> and presented it to the Crown Prince, Li Chengqian. People of the time compared him and Du Yu <note>Referenced here by his title "Du Zhengnan," a famous general and scholar who annotated the Zuo Zhuan</note> to the original authors Zuo Qiuming and Ban Gu, calling them their "loyal ministers" for their dedication to preserving the original intent of the texts.

He was later promoted to **Director of the Palace Library** and **Scholar of the Hall of Literary Greatness**. In the nineteenth year of the Zhenguan era <note>645 CE</note>, he died of illness while accompanying the imperial expedition against the Liao <note>The Goguryeo kingdom in the Liaodong region</note>. He was granted the posthumous name **Dai**. His annotations for the *Quick Access Primer* <note>original: "Jijiu Zhang," a Han-dynasty literacy textbook</note> became as famous and widely circulated as his work on the *Book of Han*. He also authored the eight-chapter work *Rectifying Errors and Correcting Vulgarities* <note>original: "Kuangmiu Zhengsu," a work on philology and correct usage</note>.

Su, whose <term>courtesy name</term> <note>A name given at adulthood to be used by peers and superiors</note> was Shichang. When the founding Emperor Gaozu pacified the rebel leader Wang Shichong, he captured Shichang and appointed him as the Supervisor of the Yushan Agricultural Colony. The Emperor had long been an old acquaintance of his, and so he granted him an audience at the <term>Xuanwu Gate</term> <note>The northern gate of the Forbidden City in Chang'an, famous as a site for imperial business and later a bloody palace coup</note>. # Xu Jingzong

**Xu Jingzong**, whose courtesy name was **Yanzu**, was a native of Xincheng in Hangzhou. From a young age, he was exceptionally skilled in literary composition. Emperor Taizong heard of his reputation and, during the <term>Zhenguan era (627–649 CE)</term>, appointed him as an Editorial Director and concurrent compiler of the national history.

During the campaign against <term>Goguryeo</term> <note>A kingdom located in the northern Korean Peninsula and parts of Manchuria; Emperor Taizong led a famous expedition against it in 645 CE.</note>, the official Cen Wenben passed away. The Emperor summoned **Xu** via the relay post system to serve in his current capacity as the Acting Vice Director of the Secretariat. While stationed at Mount Zhuwa, following the defeat of the enemy, the Emperor commanded **Xu** to draft an imperial edict immediately in front of his horse. The Emperor greatly admired the elegance and polish of his prose <note>original: "zaoqing," referring to ornate literary embellishment</note>, and from that point forward, **Xu** was exclusively in charge of drafting imperial decrees and mandates.

When the Crown Prince **Chengqian** was deposed, all the officials serving in the eastern palace were stripped of their ranks. **Xu Jingzong** spoke up in their defense; the Emperor realized the merit of his argument and reinstated many of them.

By the reign of Emperor Gaozong, **Xu** was promoted to Minister of Rites. However, he married his daughter to a tribal chieftain <note>This was viewed by historians as a corrupt act, trading his status for personal gain among non-Han groups.</note> and encouraged the Emperor to install the **Talented Consort Wu** <note>The future Empress Wu Zetian.</note> by deposing Empress Wang and the Crown Prince, and instead elevating the Prince of Dai. Furthermore, he secretly conspired with the Empress to bring about the death of **Zhangsun Wuji**, the Prince of Liang. For these reasons, official histories place him within the <term>Biographies of Treacherous Officials</term> <note>The "Jianchen Zhuan," a category in traditional Chinese historiography reserved for ministers who were seen as disloyal, corrupt, or harmful to the state.</note>.

# Li Gang

Li Gang, whose courtesy name was Wenji, was known from a young age for his magnanimous spirit and his deep commitment to moral integrity. While he was serving in the <term>Eastern Palace<note>The official residence and administrative office of the Crown Prince.</note></term>, the Crown Prince took an excursion to the Hot Springs. During the trip, someone presented fish as a gift, and the Crown Prince ordered that they be prepared into <term>Kuai<note>original: 膾 (kuài). A traditional dish of finely sliced raw fish or meat, similar to sashimi.</note></term>.

Two officials, Tang Jian and Zhao Yuankai, both boasted of their own proficiency in slicing the fish. The Crown Prince, however, remarked: "When it comes to wielding the knife to slice the carp, you gentlemen are indeed skilled; but as for the duty of <term>assisting and harmonizing<note>original: 弼諧 (bìxié). Because Li Gang rooted his conduct in the principles of the *Book of Changes*, whenever he spoke to present matters of state, he was resolute and his determination could not be shaken.

### Li Deyu

Li Deyu (courtesy name Wenrao) was the son of Li Jifu. He entered government service through ancestral privilege <term>yin; a system where the sons of high-ranking officials were granted positions without undergoing the standard imperial examinations</term> and was appointed as a Rectifier of Books.

During the reign of Emperor Muzong <note>the 15th emperor of the Tang Dynasty, reigned 820–824 CE</note>, he served as the Surveillance Commissioner of Zhexi. Under his administration, the harmful local customs were greatly reformed. When Emperor Jingzong <note>reigned 824–827 CE</note> ascended the throne, his expenditures were extravagant and without limit; the messengers sent to demand tribute were so numerous that they followed one another in an endless stream <note>literally: "their backs and necks were in view of each other"</note>. Li Deyu offered remonstrances against these actions one by one and succeeded in putting a stop to them.

During the reign of Emperor Wenzong <note>reigned 827–840 CE</note>, he was summoned and appointed as the Vice Minister of the Ministry of War. When Emperor Wuzong <note>reigned 840–846 CE</note> ascended the throne, he was summoned to serve as Chancellor <term>Tong Pingzhang Shi; a title for a high minister functioning as a de facto Prime Minister</term>. His first priority was to urge the Emperor to distinguish clearly between the wicked and the upright officials. When the warlord Liu Zhen <note>a military governor who rebelled against the Tang central government</note> usurped authority, Li Deyu resolved to launch a military campaign to execute him. For his strategic success and meritorious service, he was enfeoffed as the Duke of the State of Wei.

# Famous Ministers of the Later Five Dynasties

## The Liang Dynasty
<note>Also known as the Later Liang (907–923 CE), this was the first of the five short-lived dynasties that controlled northern China after the fall of the Tang.</note>

### Jing Xiang
His courtesy name was Zizhen. As a youth, he was a dedicated student and was highly skilled in the art of calligraphy and drafting military dispatches. While a guest in the state of Liang, he often composed letters and petitions for others; his work was so well-regarded that he was appointed as a circuit official for the postal stations. Xiang was a man of profound and contemplative character, possessed of great strategic vision. He followed the Emperor Taizu <note>The founding emperor of the Later Liang, Zhu Wen.</note> through more than twenty years of military campaigns. Every administrative matter, whether minute or monumental, had to pass through his personal review.

### Wang Yanzhang
His courtesy name was Ming. <note>He is popularly known in history and folklore as "Iron Spear Wang."</note> He was a man of extraordinary valor and physical strength; it was said he could walk barefoot over thorns for a hundred paces. He wielded an iron spear and would charge into battle on horseback with a swiftness that seemed as if he were flying. When the state of Jin <note>The rivals of the Liang, who would later found the Later Tang.</note> captured Yunzhou, the Last Emperor asked how long it would take to defeat the enemy. Yanzhang replied: "Three days." Within three days, he had indeed broken the enemy. Later, when the Tang troops attacked and he was defeated and captured, the Emperor Zhuangzong of Tang sent envoys to console him and persuade him to switch sides. Yanzhang declined, saying: "How could I be one who lives merely for the sake of living?" He was subsequently executed.

---

## The Later Tang Dynasty
<note>The Later Tang (923–937 CE) was founded by the Shatuo Turks who claimed to be the legitimate successors to the original Tang dynasty.</note>

### Guo Chongtao
He was widely praised for his administrative and governing talents. Holding the powers of both a general and a chief minister, he took the welfare of the entire empire as his personal responsibility. He once advised the Magistrate of Henan, Luo Guan, to maintain personal integrity and uphold the law. However, several court actors <note>Professional performers often held significant, and sometimes corrupt, influence over the Later Tang emperors.</note> slandered Luo Guan. Despite Chongtao’s persistent and forceful defense of the magistrate, Luo was eventually killed. During the campaign to conquer the Shu region, once the territory was pacified, the army monitor Li Congxi falsely accused Chongtao of harboring rebellious ambitions. He was killed by means of a forged imperial edict.

---

## The Jin Dynasty
<note>The Later Jin (936–947 CE) is often remembered for its subservient relationship to the Khitan Liao Empire to the north.</note>

### Sang Weihan
His courtesy name was Guoqiao. When he first sat for the imperial examinations, the chief examiner disliked his surname because "Sang" <gloss>mulberry</gloss> is a homophone for "Sang" <gloss>funeral or mourning</gloss>. To demonstrate his unwavering resolve, Weihan had an inkstone cast from iron and showed it to others, saying: "Only when this inkstone is worn through will I consider changing my career path to a different profession." He eventually succeeded in passing the examinations and attained the *jinshi* degree. <note>The "iron inkstone" became a famous symbol of scholarly persistence in Chinese culture.</note>

# The Later Han Dynasty

**Yang Bin** In his youth, he served as a low-level registrar in a provincial office. He did not like literary men and lacked an understanding of the broader principles of statecraft <note>literally "the great substance," referring to the strategic vision required for high office</note>. For this reason, those in power considered him unsuitable. When a consort died, the Emperor wished to bury her with the ritual honors of an Empress; Bin opposed it. When a favorite sought the high office of Commissioner of the Palace Commission <note>original: "Xuanhuishi," a powerful administrative post</note>, Bin again stopped the appointment, deeming it inappropriate. Because of this, those close to the Emperor took advantage of the friction to frame him, and he was killed.

---

# The Later Zhou Dynasty

**Wang Pu** His courtesy name was Wenbo. In his youth, he passed the highest imperial examinations. He initially relied on Yang Bin of the Han, but seeing that the Han dynasty would inevitably fall into chaos, he departed. He was known for being exceptionally sharp and multi-talented, possessing a deep understanding of the arts of *Yin* and *Yang*, musicology, and calendrical systems. He was brought in to deliberate on the affairs of the realm. He edited and finalized the "Calendar for Honoring Heaven" <note>original: "Qintian Li"</note> and corrected the "Elegant Music" <note>The formal ritual music of the court, essential for legitimizing a dynasty's mandate</note>; his systems were so precise that they continued to be used well into the Song dynasty.

---

# The Southern Tang Dynasty

**Liu Ranshan** His courtesy name was Shouyu. As a general, he treated wealth lightly and valued his soldiers, and his military discipline was strict and solemn. He was well-versed in the classic military manuals.
<gloss>Ranshan served the Southern Tang and was praised for his governance wherever he went. When Emperor Shizong of the Later Zhou reached the Huai River, Liu's son, Chongjian, plotted with other generals to surrender.</gloss>
When Liu discovered the plot, he immediately ordered his son's execution.

---

**Han Tong** He served the Later Zhou dynasty as the Deputy Commander-in-Chief of the Palace Guards. During the Mutiny at Chen Bridge <note>The 960 CE event where the troops of the Later Zhou acclaimed Zhao Kuangyin as the first Emperor of the Song dynasty</note>, he attempted to resist. The military officer Wang Yansheng pursued him. Tong galloped back to his residence in a desperate rush, intending to organize his troops. Before he could even close his gates, he was murdered by Yansheng. His wife and children were killed alongside him.
