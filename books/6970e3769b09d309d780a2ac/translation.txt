### 225 S. P. PLESSIUS <note>Plessius refers to Philippe de Mornay (1549–1623), a prominent French Huguenot leader and theologian often called the "Huguenot Pope."</note>

Whoever the reader may be, I ask him that what was recently permitted to Camerarius <note>original: "Camerario"; likely referring to the influential humanist scholar Joachim Camerarius.</note> at the beginning of the passage <note>original: "Strichs," which can mean a stroke, line, or a specific section of text.</note>, might be granted to me here at the end: namely, to speak the truth. For in the other book, because I was otherwise occupied, I was unable to do so. I have not written all this for my own sake, but was moved by shameful circumstances to somewhat embellish—using rhetorical tactics <note>original: "Practicken," implying clever maneuvers or political schemes.</note>—myself and my fellow believers, who are mocked <note>original: "stumpffiret," meaning to be slighted or treated with contempt.</note> everywhere by everyone, if indeed I could do no other. 

I did not do this in the hope that I might completely persuade anyone, for it would be in vain to attempt to achieve with others that which I cannot even obtain for myself. For though I can indeed affirm my innocence with words, cunning, and tactics, I cannot truly perceive it within myself; yet I desire so eagerly to perceive it in others. However, I achieve nothing anywhere; I am who I am, and I am regarded as such. Let the reader not wonder that I confess this myself; for even if I did not, this book of mine would confess it; which, when it is read, must itself

->be ashamed.<-

->THE END.<-

This is all the more necessary because all those who advise in favor of it are foreigners, and are either heretics, such as the Dutch, or otherwise bad Christians, such as the Venetians; or those whose ultimate salvation and welfare consist in arms, such as the exiled princes in Germany. Now, all of these do not <term>urge</term> <note>original: "Vrgiren"</note> for war so that any benefit should arise for us, but rather because it will be powerfully profitable for them and their own designs.

Those in the Empire who advise for it are either openly known <term>Huguenots</term> <note>French Protestants, often viewed with suspicion by Catholic authors during this period</note>, whose intent is directed toward this: that if their brothers in the neighborhood should attain such power, they might then be able to oppose the King all the better (should necessity demand it). Or, they are the Huguenots' good friends and patrons who secretly give them every assistance; they certainly direct everything toward this same end, but much more discreetly. Therefore, they are all the more harmful. Firstly, our Old Man <note>Likely referring to King James I of England/VI of Scotland, who died in 1625</note> has less and less power and authority as time goes on, for there are many more who worship the rising sun than the setting one. Thirdly, in Scotland nothing is more certain to be expected, for the Scots will not want to pay homage to the young King <note>The future Charles I</note> in any other way than as they see fit, and they will venture to prescribe certain formulas for this.
