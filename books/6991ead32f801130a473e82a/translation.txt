### The logic of winnowing grain
<note>Winnowing is the agricultural process of separating grain from chaff by throwing it into the air so the wind blows away the lighter debris.</note>

Let us say there are 4 <term>ratios</term> <note>original: "rati"; here Leonardo uses the term to describe terms in a numerical sequence</note>: one of 1, the second of 2, the third of 3, and the fourth of 4. And if there are 2 ratios, one of 1 and the other of 2, this will be 3. And if there are 3 ratios—one of 1, the second of 2, and the third of 3—this will be 6. And if there are 4 ratios—one of 1, the second of 2, the third of 3, and the fourth of 4—this will be 10. Or rather, say there are *n* ratios: the first of 1, the second of 2, the third of 3, the fourth of 4, up to *n*. Say that the sum of all these ratios will be half of the number of ratios multiplied by the number of ratios plus one. For example, if the ratios were 4, say that half of 4, which is 2, multiplied by 5, will be 10. And so if the ratios were 100, say 50 multiplied by 101 will be 5050. And if the ratios were 1000, say 500 multiplied by 1001 will be 500,500. And so it will be for whatever number you wish for the ratios: say that half of that number multiplied by that *n* + 1 will give you the sum of the ratios.
<note>Leonardo is describing the formula for the sum of an arithmetic progression: S = n(n+1)/2.</note>

---

<note>Small horizontal sequence of letters used as variables in the following paragraph.</note>
->r . o . s . t . u . x . y . z .<-

If you want to know how much space there will be between *r* and *o* ratios, assuming the ratios are 4: it means that one of those ratios will be 10 <term>braccia</term> <note>A Florentine unit of measurement, roughly 23 inches or 58 cm</note>, the second 20 braccia, the third 30 braccia, and the fourth 40 braccia. And if you want to know how much space there will be between one and the other of these ratios...

---

Regarding that which falls from above, of that weight which falls from above: say that it falls in the center. The wind from below pushes it from above so that it falls in the center. And if it falls from above, say that it falls in the center; the wind from below pushes it from above so that it falls in the center. And if it falls from above, say that it falls in the center; the wind from below pushes it from above so that it falls in the center. And if it falls from above, say that it falls in the center; the wind from below pushes it from above so that it falls in the center. And if it falls from above, say that it falls in the center; the wind from below pushes it from above so that it falls in the center.
<note>This repetitive passage likely reflects Leonardo's observation of how air resistance or an upward draft affects a falling body, specifically grain during winnowing.</note>

This means that the ratios of that half, of that number multiplied by that number + 1, will be half of the number of ratios multiplied by that number which is plus one. # ON THE MOTIONS OF THE NECK

The muscles that move the neck are numerous, for it must perform many functions: it must bend forward, backward, to the left, and to the right; it must rotate, and it must also stabilize the head when the body is in motion.

<margin>The cervical vertebrae <note>The seven bones of the neck</note> act as a flexible pillar for the skull.</margin>

### OF THE TENDONS AND LIGAMENTS

# ON THE WINDPIPE AND THE LUNGS

The **windpipe** <note>original: "aspre arteria" (trachea)</note> does not increase or decrease in size when the lung expands or contracts, because it is composed of cartilaginous rings that are incomplete circles. These rings are joined by a flexible membrane that allows them to widen or narrow slightly, but their primary purpose is to remain open so that the passage of air is never restricted.

The water that falls perpendicularly into a basin of a certain depth will penetrate more deeply into the water of that basin the greater the height from which it falls. # On the Motion of the Shoulder and Neck

The shoulder is the most mobile of all the joints in the human body, for it can move in a complete circle. This is because the head of the humerus <note>original: "osso del braccio"; the bone of the arm</note> is seated within a shallow socket, allowing for a vast range of motion that no other limb possesses.

### On the Nature of the Air and the Wing

The air that is struck by the wing is compressed, and the air that is compressed resists the wing. This resistance is the cause of the bird's support in the air. If the wing is moved with great speed, the air becomes so dense that it offers a resistance similar to a solid body.

<margin>
The wing must be wider at the base than at the tip so that it may strike a greater quantity of air near the body, where the strength of the bird is greatest.
</margin>

<note>Drawing 'e': A sketch of a bird's wing extended, showing the tapering from the shoulder to the primary feathers.</note>

->**The Proportion of the Wing to the Weight**<-

If the bird weighs two pounds and the air is still, the bird must move its wings with such force that the resistance of the air becomes equal to two pounds of weight. In this way, the bird remains in equilibrium <note>original: "bilico" - a state of balance or poise</note> and does not descend.

<margin>
When the bird wishes to rise, it must strike the air with more force than its own weight, and when it wishes to descend, it strikes with less force.
</margin>

<column-break/>

<margin>
The bird does not always beat its wings in a vertical line, but often strikes the air obliquely <note>at an angle</note> to move forward while staying aloft.
</margin>

<note>Drawing 'g': A diagram showing the angle of a wing stroke relative to the horizon, with arrows indicating the direction of the bird's movement.</note>

The movement of the wing is always more rapid at the tip than at the base, because the tip describes a larger circle in the same amount of time. Therefore, the air is more compressed at the tips of the wings than near the body.

<margin>
This is why birds with long wings are better suited for soaring, as they can find more resistance in the air with less effort.
</margin>

# Summary of the various movements and motions of the heart, the lungs, and the other viscera

It is seen by experience how the lungs, by expelling the air, expand
and refresh the air that is in the vessels of the heart, and the vessels of the other viscera
contract, and the heart also contracts and remains without blood in it;
and then, by the contrary action of these lungs, the viscera return to their former state.

---

Now see how the air that enters into the lungs through the <term>trachea</term> <note>original: "trachea arteria"; Leonardo uses the classical term for the windpipe</note> makes its motion
through the recesses of the branches of said trachea which terminate in the connective tissues of the lung.
It is seen that this air has no other passage through which it can pass,
because the skin <note>membrane</note> of the lung has no opening through which the air could pass
into the vessels of the other viscera; and this is proven by the lung of a man,
or of chickens, or of other animals, which by experience are seen to inflate
like a bladder if you blow into them, and nothing escapes except through the mouth.
Therefore, we hold that this air does not pass through the skins of the lung and does not go into the chest;
so the air that vapors and refreshes the heat of the heart is that
which touches the arterial vein <note>the pulmonary artery</note> and the venous artery <note>the pulmonary vein</note> which are
continuous with the branches of the trachea and join with it;
and by means of this skin the air is refreshed without the air passing into the branches
of the trachea in the vessels of the heart, nor the blood passing into the branches of said trachea.

# On the Nature of the Human Body

## Book the First: Concerning the Vital Spirits

->**Introduction**<-

The study of the human frame is not merely an investigation of flesh and bone, but a contemplation of the divine architecture. As the ancients often remarked, <note>original: "Anima est forma corporis"</note> the soul is the formative essence of the body, and without understanding the one, we cannot hope to grasp the workings of the other. 

In this chapter, we shall examine the <term>pneuma</term> <note>A Greek term referring to the "vital breath" or spirit believed to circulate through the arteries to sustain life.</note> and how it is distributed from the heart to the furthest members. <margin>See the diagrams in the second volume regarding the arterial tree.</margin> It is essential to note that the <insert>superior</insert> ventricles play a primary role in this refinement of the blood.

### Of the Heart and its Vessels

The heart, being the sun of our microcosm, radiates heat and life. <gloss>The Greeks call this the "arche" or the beginning.</gloss> We must observe the following structures:

| Part Name | Function | Quality |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Left Ventricle | Generation of spirits | Hot and Dry |
| Right Ventricle | Nourishment of lungs | Hot and Moist |
| Aorta | Distribution of life | Temperate |

However, our dissections suggest a more unified flow. <unclear>The membrane</unclear> separating these chambers is porous, allowing for a subtle transpiration of vapors. 

<margin>Note: Compare this with the findings of the Paduan school.</margin>

We must proceed with caution, for to look upon the inner workings of a man is to look into a mirror of the cosmos itself.
