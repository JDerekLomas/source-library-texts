## Archery Methods

The *Biographies of Exemplary Women* <note>original: "Lienü Zhuan" (列女傳). A traditional Chinese biographical work compiled by Liu Xiang around 18 BCE. Its inclusion here shows that archery principles were often illustrated through classical moral literature.</note> states: "Draw the bow with a vigorous spirit; release the arrow with a calmed breath." Drawing with a vigorous spirit <note>original: "nuqi" (怒氣). In this context, "nu" does not mean anger in the modern sense, but rather a surge of vital energy or focused martial intensity.</note> ensures the strength is heroic and the draw is full. Releasing with a calmed breath <note>original: "xiqi" (息氣). This refers to a state of settled, rhythmic breathing.</note> ensures the mind is steady and one's considerations are thorough.

To measure one's strength to tune the bow, and to measure the bow to craft the arrows—this is of the utmost importance. Thus, the philosopher Xunzi <note>A Confucian philosopher (c. 310–235 BCE) who emphasized ritual and discipline.</note> said: "If the bow and arrows are not harmonized, even the Great Archer Yi could not hit the target with certainty." <note>Yi (羿), often called Hou Yi, is the mythological god of archery in Chinese culture, famous for shooting down nine of the ten suns to save the Earth.</note> Mencius <note>One of the most famous Confucian thinkers (c. 372–289 BCE).</note> also observed: "When Yi taught others archery, he insisted they reach the full draw." <note>original: "gou" (彀). This term refers to the point where the bow is drawn to its maximum intended capacity, just before release.</note> Students must also strive to reach the full draw; this is an essential rule for all schools of archery.

In holding the bow and arrow, one must be "deliberate and firm." To be "deliberate" <note>original: "shen" (審).</note> means to examine every detail with care. To be "firm" <note>original: "gu" (固).</note> means to maintain a grip that is steadfast and unyielding.

Whenever the bowstring strikes the archer’s sleeve, it is always because the grip is not steady. <note>This "sleeve strike" is a common technical error where the bow rotates inward or the arm is poorly positioned, causing the string to slap the clothing and ruin the shot's accuracy.</note>

Whenever an arrow wobbles in flight and lacks power, it is because the arrowhead is not resting against the finger. <note>original: "zhu bu shang zhi" (鏃不上指). This refers to the technique of drawing the arrow back until the head (pile) touches the index finger of the hand holding the bow, ensuring a consistent draw length and stability.</note>

**Methods of Archery**

One method states: "If the <term>arrowhead</term> <note>original: "zhù" (鏃). The metal tip of the arrow.</note> does not reach the finger, there is no logic to hitting the target." If the finger does not "know" the arrowhead, it is the same as being without eyes. This word "finger" refers specifically to the tip of the left middle finger. For the fingertip to "know" the arrowhead means that the finger itself senses that the arrowhead has arrived at the full draw, without needing to rely on the eyes to look. One must wait until the fingertip senses the arrowhead before the draw is considered <term>full</term> <note>original: "gōng mǎn" (弓滿). The state where the bow is drawn to its maximum intended capacity.</note>. Only when every shot begins with the finger sensing the arrowhead can one truly begin to discuss the art of <term>archery</term> <note>original: "shè" (射). One of the Six Confucian Arts, encompassing both physical skill and moral discipline.</note>.

Another point regarding <term>Scrutiny</term> <note>original: "shěn" (審). While often translated as "aiming," in this manual it refers to a deeper state of deliberation, examination, and mental composure.</note>: One must practice scrutiny at the precise moment when the bow is full and the arrow is about to be released. Most people today practice their scrutiny when the arrow is only half-drawn; what possible benefit can that provide?

Regarding <term>Scrutiny</term>: People today all believe that "scrutiny" simply means looking at the target. They do not realize that looking at the target is merely one small part of the process. When the bow is at full draw, the archer's spirit is already taxed to its limit and the limbs are strained and "empty." If the arrow is released abruptly in this state, then whether the arrow flies straight or hits the mark is not determined by the command of the mind. Therefore, one must apply "scrutiny" <note>The text suggests that "scrutiny" is the mental anchor that prevents a panicked or unconscious release when the physical exertion of the draw is at its peak.</note>.

# The Canon of Boxing
<note>original: "拳經" (Quán Jīng). This text is heavily influenced by, and in parts directly quotes, General Qi Jiguang’s "New Book of Effective Discipline" (c. 1560), which revolutionized Ming Dynasty martial arts by codifying folk styles for military training.</note>

Boxing methods may seem to have no direct application in the grand maneuvers of great battles; however, the way they loosen the hands and feet and accustom the limbs to constant exertion makes them the **essential gateway** for the beginner to enter the martial arts. Therefore, these methods are preserved here at the end to complete the curriculum of this school. 

In learning boxing, the **body method** must be agile and adaptable, the **hand methods** must be convenient and efficient, and the **footwork** must be light yet solid. Advancing and retreating must be timed perfectly, and the legs should be capable of leaping and soaring. 

The cleverness of this art lies in maneuvers like **Overturning and Inserting Upside-Down** <term>Diānqǐ Dàochā (顛起倒挿): Advanced grappling or throwing maneuvers where the opponent's momentum is used to upend them</term>. Its speed is seen in **Lively Capture and Pointing to Heaven** <term>Huózhuō Cháotiān (活捉朝天): Rapid transitions from a grappling hold to a high strike or throw</term>. For these reasons, the thirty-two best postures of boxing have been selected. Each posture flows into the next; when facing an enemy to secure victory, the changes are infinite. So subtle and unpredictable, so profound and mysterious that no observer can perceive the underlying logic—this is what is called "divine." 

As the common saying goes, "a strike delivered when the opponent is unaware is like a **thunderbolt so fast one cannot cover their ears**" <note>original: "迅雷不及掩耳" (Xùnléi bùjí yǎněr); a classic Chinese idiom emphasizing that speed and surprise leave the defender no time to react</note>. By recording broadly, studying widely, and planning with many strategies, one achieves victory. # The Closed Gate Posture
<note>original: "關門勢" (Guānmén shì). In martial arts, a "gate" refers to the defensive lines of the body. To "close the gate" is to create an impenetrable barrier that forces an opponent to stall or redirect their attack.</note>

This is the **Subtle Shuttle** spear method, where the body follows the spear’s advance.
<note>original: "微梭" (Wēi suō). The term "shuttle" refers to the back-and-forth sliding motion of the spear shaft through the lead hand, much like a weaver's shuttle. This allows the practitioner to change the spear's reach instantly.</note>

Once the stance is established, one employs the techniques of **piercing**, **blocking**, and **lifting**. 
<note>The text uses "Cù" (剳 - piercing/stabbing), "Lán" (欄 - horizontal parrying), and "Tí" (提 - upward flicking or lifting). These are the "Big Three" foundational movements of Ming dynasty spear play.</note>

When attacking the opponent’s "hard" or strong positions, the classic manuals say the **Six Straights** hold the secret. 
<term>Six Straights (六直 - Liù zhí): A technical requirement in spear fighting where the spearhead, the front hand, the back hand, and the practitioner's alignment must maintain "straightness" to ensure the force is direct and the point is difficult to parry.</term>

The profound logic of this art is this: **length is valued for its "short" application, and shortness is valued for its "long" application.**
<note>This is a classic martial paradox. A long weapon is most effective when it can be used in tight, "short" spaces, while a short weapon is most effective when it can strike from a "long" (unexpected) range.</note>

Using the "short" within the "long" creates a technique that cannot be resisted. If an opponent faces a long weapon and the wielder cannot land a hit because the distance has closed (making the weapon "too long" to use), the wielder is instead undone by their own length. 

Therefore, when using a long weapon, one must apply it in **sections**; these segments are dangerous and subtle.
<note>original: "節節" (Jié jié - section by section). This refers to the ability to use the spear at various lengths by shifting hand positions, effectively treating a ten-foot pole as a series of shorter, more manageable weapons.</note>

When the technique is applied within a foot or so of the body, the method becomes even more unpredictable. When the opponent sees my long spear, they develop a **delusional mind**, thinking: *"If I can just get past the tip and move in deep, his spear will be useless."* 

But I suddenly engage them with these "short" sectional movements. Their strategy is defeated, their mind becomes flustered in the haste of the moment, and they find themselves unable to respond. This mastery of using "long" weapons in "short" ways is a topic the ancients surely deemed essential for debate.
