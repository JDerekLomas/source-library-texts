# TWENTIETH MEDITATION.

HEALTH is natural. That which is natural is in harmony with orderly law. All law is beneficial; therefore, the degree of harmony in anything is the test of how natural it is. God is the Author of nature, and natural law is the divine method. Disharmony, disease, and unhappiness are unnatural because they are discordant with all that is normal and ideal. Nature does not *know* any lack of conformity. Man is naturally, generically, and potentially whole. All illness is a deviation and comes from artificiality. Thought, the human creative faculty, misses the mark.

The spiritual should mold the material, the inner the outer; and all human experience proves the reasonableness of such an ordered relationship. It follows that human vitality can be increased only from within, and that medication with drugs is abnormal and adds no new life. It deals only with effects and is clearly unnatural and irrational. Man must hold before him an undeviating pattern and thereby grow into its likeness. Nature is not "sickly," and for man—its crowning feature—to be disordered proves his *continual* "fall" into materialism. Nature, rightly interpreted, is spiritual. The universal order speaks only of wholeness and harmony.

# Studies in the Thought World
## or Practical Mind Art

->By HENRY WOOD<-
->Author of "Ideal Suggestion," "God's Image in Man," "Edward<-
->Burton," "The Political Economy of Natural Law,"<-
->etc. Cloth $1.25<-

Mr. Wood is a visionary as well as a thinker. He searches to find the secrets of the spirit, and thereby discovers many of the mysteries of life. His pages abound in sayings of wisdom and truth. They are crowded with compelling suggestions and rich in inspiring statements. His style is clear, penetrating, brilliant, and impressive, just like his thought. He ranks with the foremost writers and thinkers of the time. — *Boston Courier.*

We doubt very much if in the whole range of English literature we have ever read anything more fascinating than his chapter on "The Divinity of Nature." It has all the beauty of Emerson—another idealist—and all the sympathy of Thoreau. — *The Minneapolis Tribune.*

The series of papers is filled with intellectual ozone, mental exhilaration, and great spiritual invigoration. The author makes the somewhat difficult philosophy of the higher life very clear in his able treatment of the subject from a scientific standpoint. — *The Call, Philadelphia.*

The result of reading this book is to acknowledge Mr. Wood as an original thinker and an idealist, and to see that he possesses the faculty of presenting these questions—which are growing more important to the general thinker all the time—in a way that is graphic and interesting. He has no superior as an essayist. — *Boston Times.*

Mr. Wood has the faculty of presenting vital topics in an interesting and very graphic manner, and has here ably treated the higher development of humanity from a scientific standpoint. — *Detroit Free Press.*

There is not a page in it that does not contain matter for a fascinating controversy. — *Saturday Evening Gazette, Boston.*

Thinkers have a treat in this work, and once begun, one's interest increases to the end. — *Detroit Journal.*

Mr. Wood is a strong thinker, and he says a great many fine things. — *Boston Beacon.*
