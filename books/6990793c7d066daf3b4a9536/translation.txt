The subject of the present book is the formation of the idea of the magical and the sacred: we stop at the moment where magical-sacred forces <note>The author uses the term "magico-sacrées," reflecting the early 20th-century sociological view that magic and religion share a common origin in "mana" or sacred power.</note> begin to personify and be conceived as individual wills. We shall leave the study of sacred personifications <note>Essentially, the transition from vague spiritual forces to distinct gods or spirits.</note> for a later work, which is already in preparation. Later still may come the study of abstract representations, and then that of religious society considered from the point of view of its organization.

We have excluded from the scope of this volume several subjects that one might have expected to see treated here. The study of magical tradition, alchemy, and astrology seemed to us to belong more to a history of Islamic sciences <note>original: "sciences musulmanes"</note> than to a history of religion in North Africa; the study of prayer and its origins ought to have been treated here, but an insufficient refinement of the topic has forced us to postpone it until a bit later, as it seemed to us that it could be more advantageously linked to the study of the divine person.

# THE LAW

...this primitive character of the religious nature of institutions, but Islam has further reinforced it: perhaps none of the great religions embraces societies with such a broad reach, none invades private and public life so completely.

The strict Muslim is bound by multiple obligations: not only do ritual prayers repeat throughout the day, but his words, gestures, and steps are subject to a multitude of rules; the precepts of etiquette almost all have the value of religious prescriptions and are sufficiently complicated that it is nearly impossible for a European disguised among Muslims not to quickly betray his identity: hence the numerous failures of travelers who tried to pass themselves off as Muslims during their explorations. <note>This likely refers to the "Great Game" era of exploration where figures like Richard Burton attempted to infiltrate sacred cities in disguise; the author suggests the complexity of daily ritual was the primary barrier to a successful ruse.</note>

The law is entirely religious; prayer, inheritance, and personal grooming are regulated in the same way and on the authority of the same sources of divine law. Morality is often conflated with law; indeed, <term>jurisprudence</term> <note>original: "fiqh"</note> regulates all actions and classifies them as **obligatory** <note>original: "ouâdjib"</note>, **recommended** <note>original: "mandoûb"</note>, **permitted** <note>original: "moubâh’"</note>, **discouraged** <note>original: "makroûh"</note>, and **forbidden** <note>original: "h’arâm"</note>; human reason is incapable on its own of discerning good from evil, say the Muslim scholars <note>The original uses "docteurs," referring to the *Ulema* or doctors of sacred law</note><sup>(1)</sup>; everything is regulated, actions by jurisprudence, and beliefs by the science of **creeds** <note>original: "aqâ’ïd"; refers to the articles of faith</note>; the union of these two sciences is the "law" or <term>Sharia</term> <note>original: "charî‘a"</note>. # ART

...amplifications that were made of them (<term>tripling <note>Arabic: *tethlîth*; adding three lines to a couplet</note></term>, <term>quadrupling <note>Arabic: *terbî'*</note></term>, <term>quintupling <note>Arabic: *tekhmîs*</note></term>, etc.) have remained the models for this genre, which is as monotonous as it is abundant.

The fine arts in Islam are likewise dependent on religion; as images are proscribed <note>meaning forbidden by religious law, specifically to avoid idolatry</note>, painting and sculpture do not exist, or exist only as negligible exceptions; geometric design alone was able to develop and has, moreover, enjoyed a brilliant career: from the Great Mosque of Cordoba to the Alhambra of Granada, and from the Koutoubia <note>the famous 12th-century mosque in Marrakech</note> to Sidi Boumediene <note>a sacred complex in Tlemcen, Algeria</note>, geometric interlacing has been the sole resource for decoration. Even when it borrows models from nature from antiquity, it does so to turn them into purely ornamental and geometric motifs. For the same reason, the artist rejects deep modeling and prefers ornamentation consisting entirely of cut-out patterns, devoid of relief<sup>(1)</sup>.

Finally, the organization of society itself is entirely religious; the sovereign is merely the vicar (<term>Caliph <note>Arabic: *khalîfa*, meaning "successor" or "deputy"</note></term>) of Muhammad; he is the <term>Imam <note>The primary leader of prayer and the community</note></term> *par excellence*. # SURVIVALS WITHIN ISLAM 1

...imitate God by making an effort to create living beings.

It would be easy to multiply these examples; the most typical and well-known is the preservation, within the ceremonies accompanying the pilgrimage to Mecca, of an entire block of pre-Islamic rites: the circumambulations around the Ka’ba, the kissing of the Black Stone, the running between Safa and Marwa, and the festivals of Arafat^(1). One can also cite this curious concept of the *Fitra*^(2) <term>Fitra: the innate human nature or "natural religion" believed to be inherent in all people from birth</term>, that is to say, of natural religion, including a certain number of non-Quranic prescriptions, but contained in the *Hadith* <term>Hadith: the recorded traditions, sayings, and actions of the Prophet Muhammad</term>, that is to say, tradition; among these are circumcision, hair removal, and the trimming of nails and mustaches.

Orthodoxy attributes these prescriptions to Abraham <note>Abraham is considered a foundational prophet and the "first Muslim" in Islamic tradition, credited with establishing many monotheistic practices</note>, whereas modern ethnography finds here again universal and exceedingly ancient customs—rites known in primitive societies to be rites of initiation or purification. These latter play a considerable role in the Muslim religion; the chapter on *tahara* <term>tahara: the state of ritual purity required for religious duties</term> is the first and one of the most important in the books of *fiqh* <term>fiqh: Islamic jurisprudence; the human interpretation of divine law</term>, and we know the place that ablutions <note>ritual washing</note> occupy in the life of the believer. Now, the distinction between the pure and the impure is one of the fundamental themes of all primitive religions, and one can no longer treat this part of the Muslim religion without referring to the considerable works of contemporary sociologists on this subject^(3).
