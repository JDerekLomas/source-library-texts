...reader eager to learn, to read these exercises of ours attentively, so that he may be able to examine difficult passages and ancient secrets; and thus look into things previously veiled with a thick curtain, now revealed. And whereas before it was only given to look at the surface itself and the bark, he may now rightly rejoice to see God <note>original Greek abbreviation: "Θῠ" (Theou)</note> himself.

Nor should anyone justly find fault that the author is titled the *Revealer*, as if we were claiming for ourselves some fanatic enthusiasm, or an Apocalypse or revelation of mysteries like John the Theologian, or as if we visionaries were claiming certain visions for ourselves; for everyone knows that these extraordinary gifts have now ceased. Nevertheless, who will deny that God daily reveals many things to His elect which were previously hidden? And although the gift of prophecy or interpretation was once extraordinary—just like the Spirit of prayer and other gifts—these gifts still thrive in the Church today, even if not with the same abundance and success.

Hence, just as among the Hebrews, *Shlomo Yitzhaki* <note>Commonly known as **Rashi** (1040–1105), the preeminent Jewish commentator on the Bible and Talmud.</note> is remembered as *Parshandata* <note>original Hebrew: פרשנדתא</note> or *Meforesh HaDat* <note>original Hebrew: מפרש הדת</note>, that is, the *Interpreter of the Law*, <term>par excellence<note>original Greek: "κατ' ἐξοχήν" (kat' exochen), meaning "pre-eminently" or "above all others."</note></term>, because many things were revealed to him that lay hidden to others. A single passage, **1 Peter 3:19**, *concerning the spirits in prison*, provides proof of this. Also, **Philips van Marnix, Lord of Saint-Aldegonde** <note>A Flemish-Dutch nobleman, diplomat, and theologian, famous for his role in the Dutch Revolt and his biblical scholarship.</note> was the first to correctly interpret the passage in **Acts 19:5**, *on hearing this, they were baptized* <note>original Greek: "ἀκούσαντες δὲ ἐβαπτίσθησαν" (akousantes de ebaptisthesan)</note>, from the original Greek text.

Since it is the most upright rule and standard of justice, whatever God wills and commands obtains both the name and nature of justice.
