By Royal Authority it is decreed that no one may print the works of the *Emperor Julian*, in Greek and Latin <note>original: "græ. lat."</note>, as edited by *Pierre Martin of Morentin in Navarre*, *Charles de Chanteclair, Royal Councilor and Master of Requests in the court, and Théodore Marcile*, or sell them elsewhere if printed outside this kingdom: except for *Denis du Val, bookseller and printer of the University of Paris*, for the nine-year period <note>original: "novennium"</note> following the completion of the edition. Whoever shall do otherwise will be punished by the confiscation of the books and by the penalties declared in the Royal decree. Given at Paris, the sixteenth of December, 1580.

<header>TO OTHO II <note>Odet de Coligny (1517–1571), a high-ranking French prelate and brother of the Huguenot leader Gaspard de Coligny. Odet famously converted to Protestantism while retaining his title as Cardinal.</note></header>

# TO COLIGNY
## CARDINAL OF CHÂTILLON
## PIERRE MARTIN
### of Morentin in Navarre, sends greetings.

<image-desc>Decorative woodcut initial letter 'S' featuring leafy scrollwork and architectural motifs.</image-desc>

I TRULY hope (most noble Cardinal) that no one will be justly angry with me if I publish the works <note>original Latin: "monumenta," literally 'monuments' or 'records'</note> of the Emperor Julian for the public. For while I grant that the crimes of impiety and tyranny, in which that wretched man miserably entangled himself, are wicked and worthy of hatred; yet certainly the most brilliant lights of his eloquence and genius do not deserve, I believe, to be extinguished along with his sins. Otherwise, let Demosthenes, Cicero, Homer, Virgil, Plato, Aristotle, and in a word all the authors of elegant and refined literature—poets, orators, philosophers—be removed from our midst; finally, let all the arts of humanity and wisdom be removed, because they were mostly handed down by superstitious or impious men. If that were to happen, what kind of republic, I ask, would we hold today? But among the Latins, Lucretius, and among the Greeks, Porphyry and Lucian are read in Greek and Latin editions: and yet I believe there is no one who complains that their works exist. For it is primarily relevant to the value of history and the knowledge of antiquity, not only that those men once lived, but also to know who and what kind of people they were. But you may say they were impious. So be it, since the examples of these men are effective for deterring people and calling them back from impiety; hence that unhappy Theseus cries out in the underworld as an example of his own misery, as it is written in Virgil’s *Aeneid*, Book 6:

> Learn justice, be warned, and do not slight the gods. <note>original Latin: "Discite justitiam moniti, & non temnere divos." This is a famous line from Virgil describing the eternal punishment of the wicked.</note>

Moreover, the history of Julian is worthy of a certain special attention and observation. In him were exceptional gifts of nature and talent, great virtue and nearly singular skill in administering the affairs of war and peace; to these many good qualities was added the knowledge of true piety <note>Julian was raised as a Christian before his "apostasy" or return to paganism.</note>, adorned even by ample testimonies of his life. But the man, burning with a desire for power, and captured by the deceits and arts of magic, renounced Christ; and having attained the power of the highest command, he most cruelly harassed the Christians, banned them from schools, removed them from magistracies, despoiled them of their goods, expelled Athanasius <note>St. Athanasius of Alexandria, a leading figure in early Christianity and a fierce opponent of Julian.</note>, and finally in all ways disturbed and afflicted the Church. It will therefore be useful to recall from these records both what the state of those times was and what the feelings of good men were.

<header>EPISTLE.</header>

Truly, what a great joy it will be for the Church, having finally endured and been freed from such great evils, not only to remember her past labors, but also to look upon the enemies by whom she was so often wounded now driven out, their camps deserted, and their leader cast down and lying defeated? In this same way, the Trojans, exhausted by the weariness of their long siege, rejoiced when they saw the enemy had moved away, as the same poet <note>The "poet" is Virgil, the most famous of the Roman epic poets.</note> says in the second book of the *Aeneid*:

> Therefore all Troy releases itself from long grief:
> The gates are opened; it is a joy to go and see the Greek camps,
> The deserted places, and the abandoned shore:
> Here the troop of Dolopians <note>A group of Greek warriors from Thessaly.</note> stayed, here fierce Achilles used to pitch his tent.

Therefore, this pleasure in reading these texts will be added to the practical utility of history. For this reason, even if no eloquence or charm of genius appeared in these writings, no one, I believe, should take it poorly that these benefits of utility and pleasure have been shared with them through our efforts.

As for the reliability of this edition, I obtained a single manuscript copy from the library of Pierre Ramus <note>Pierre de la Ramée (1515–1572), a famous French humanist, logician, and Martin’s teacher.</note>. Because that copy was faulty in several places, we have changed the old reading where it seemed to be in error according to our best judgment; however, we have provided notes <margin>how it was read previously</margin> to show how the text appeared before our corrections.

You will receive, therefore (most distinguished Cardinal), both this modest gift of an edition and myself, its author, with that kindness with which you are accustomed to receive all those in whom some sign of virtue and good will appears. Your honors are many and illustrious in every field, but one in particular will be praised and carried to the heavens as long as literature exists: that you protect, adorn, and enlarge the Academy of Paris; that by your generosity you support the studies of many; and finally, that you embrace good and learned men with singular zeal and love.

Because you hold Pierre Ramus, my teacher, in such high esteem, I say that you bind not only me but all people throughout the world who love true education—and who have been awakened and helped by his late-night labors—to yourself forever. You draw them to love you and to serve you with every duty. I also have several other works, including a Hebrew grammar we have developed using logical principles <note>Martin is referring to "Ramist" logic, a method of organizing knowledge into diagrams and definitions.</note>, which will soon be published in your name if I learn that these current efforts have not been unwelcome to you. Farewell. Paris <note>Original Latin: "Lutetiae," the Roman name for Paris.</note>, the first of May, 1566.

->P. MARTIN<-

...worshipped above all, so that he might have been able to enjoy much. For indeed, after the death of his brother Constantine <note>Constantine II, who died in 340 AD.</note>, Julian’s father was crushed by the successors of the empire; his mother, however, departed this life only a few months after Julian was born. Thus, while still quite a young boy, he was left destitute of all hope of comfort or protection, along with his brother Gallus—his only paternal sibling—who was himself of fragile and nearly broken health. Indeed, the same ruin that had previously overtaken their father would have crushed both sons as well, had not Julian’s youth and Gallus’s ill health spared them from the immediate danger.

These, then, were the seeds of his nature sown amidst the splendor of his lineage and parentage. Yet, in his mind and body, nature’s disposition was quite varied. His mind was indeed most volatile; thus (as Marcellinus <note>Ammianus Marcellinus, the Roman historian.</note> states in Book 22), he allowed his prefects and associates to confidently restrain his impulses with timely admonition whenever they strayed further than was proper; he frequently demonstrated that he grieved for his errors and took joy in being corrected. 

The modest build of his body is described by the same author in his twenty-fifth book: *“His physical frame,”* he says, *“was as follows: he was of medium height, with hair that was soft, looking as though it had been combed, and a shaggy beard tapering to a point. He possessed eyes of a flashing beauty that betrayed the restless intensity* <note>original: *angustias*—literally "narrowness" or "straits," here referring to a crowded, anxious, or shifty state of mind.</note> *of his mind. He had handsome eyebrows and a perfectly straight nose, a slightly large mouth with a cleft lower lip, and a thick, arched neck with broad, powerful shoulders. From his head to the very tips of his toes, the alignment of his frame was straight and well-knit, giving him great strength and speed in running.”* 

In this mention of "restless intensity," the same volatility noted above is observed; however, to this is added a physical hardiness, from which a great measure of his subsequent military glory was derived.

Yet on this entire subject, the discourse of Gregory of Nazianzus <note>A prominent 4th-century Christian theologian who studied with Julian in Athens.</note> is more detailed and outspoken. He encountered Julian as a young man in Athens and, from the disposition of his mind and body, foresaw his future character. Thus he speaks in his second book *Against Julian*: *“At that time,”* he says, *“I realized I was no poor judge of character, even though I am not naturally gifted in those arts of divination. But it was his mental instability and extraordinary agitation that made me capable of foresight—for indeed, the best prophet is one who knows how to guess correctly* <note>This is a Latin rendering of a Greek proverb: "The best seer is he who guesses well."</note>. *It seemed to me no sign of anything good to see that bowed neck, those twitching and shrugging shoulders, a wandering eye that rolled with a wild gaze, feet that were unstable and stumbling, and a mocking expression that breathed forth contempt and...”*

->ON JULIAN’S *MISOPOGON* <note>The *Misopogon* (Beard-Hater) is a satirical essay written by Emperor Julian himself to mock the citizens of Antioch who had ridiculed his appearance and ascetic lifestyle.</note><-

...contempt, ridiculous facial expressions, the same kind of unrestrained and excessive laughter, irrational nodding and shaking of the head, a hesitant speech broken by breaths, silly and foolish questions, and answers no better than those, colliding into one another, inconsistent, and not following a scholarly order. Why should I list each one? I saw him as such before his works, as I came to know him in his works. These are his words, from which we understand the perpetual levity and inconstancy implanted and innate in this man, so that the primary cause of the error that followed is already grasped. Therefore, such was his nature.

What of his education, by what masters, and by what kind of studies and arts was it handled and cultivated? After his seventh year of age, he was handed over to the tutor Mardonius the eunuch, regarding which he himself addresses the people of Antioch in the *Misopogon* in this manner: "Do you wish (he says) for me to bring forth the name and race of my pedagogue? He was a barbarian and a Scythian by race, of the same name as he <note>Mardonius was the name of the Persian general who persuaded Xerxes to invade Greece in 480 BCE.</note> who led Xerxes to war in Greece and to noble Argos. This name, however, which before my twentieth year was used out of honor, is now used as a slur. He was a Eunuch, educated under my grandfather, to explain the poems of Homer and Hesiod to my mother. Since she died a few months later, having left me as her first and only son, I was then like an orphaned and deserted maiden, rescued from many calamities, a girl and tender [child], and after my seventh year, I was handed over to him." 

From this teacher, therefore, he was led to the public schools in Constantinople, and there he spent his childhood years in grammatical and rhetorical studies: he had Nicocles the Lacedæmonian <term>Lacedæmonian: another name for a Spartan.</term> as his master of the art of grammar, and Ecebolius the sophist as his master of rhetoric.

...it was frequently said that he was fit for governing the empire. And so, lest any occasion for revolution might arise from that reputation, he departed from this first school for Nicomedia. 

<margin>Libanius the Rhetorician</margin>
Libanius the Rhetorician <note>Libanius (c. 314–393 AD) was one of the most famous teachers of rhetoric in the later Roman Empire and a lifelong friend of Julian.</note> was flourishing in that city at the time, held in great praise for his eloquence, but he was particularly devoted to pagan superstition. For this reason, Julian at first kept away from his school and only read his books at home, because he was wonderfully delighted by them. But later, gradually corrupted by familiarity with Libanius, he began not only to frequent the school but even dared, after the Greek fashion, to declaim against Christians, as if for the sake of exercising his talent he wanted to make some incredible thesis seem plausible through speaking. This was Julian’s second school: a school, I say, the mistress of error and impiety. Indeed, the Apostle Paul rightly says, quoting the poet Menander:

> “Bad company corrupts good character.” <note>original Greek: Φθείρουσιν ἤθη χρηστὰ ὁμιλίαι κακαί; Latin: Corrumpunt mores bonos congressus mali. This is a quote from the playwright Menander, famously cited by St. Paul in 1 Corinthians 15:33.</note>

Nevertheless, in this school, such were his exercises in eloquence. There he also learned philosophy. At that time, philosophy was not entirely Platonic or Aristotelian, but a mixture of both; Porphyry <note>A Neoplatonist philosopher and student of Plotinus, known for his anti-Christian writings.</note> a little before, and Iamblichus then, were considered the leaders of this school.

<margin>Iamblichus, the greatest philosopher</margin>
Iamblichus held the highest authority among all: thus, Julian especially cultivated and loved this philosopher, bestowing great honors and rewards upon him. He held philosophy of this sort: hence in his letters he calls himself "a zealot for the doctrines of Plato and Aristotle" <note>original Greek: τῶν πλάτωνος καὶ ἀριστοτέλους ζηλωτὴν δογμάτων</note>. In this matter, he also made much use of the labor of that eunuch teacher I mentioned before, just as he himself declares to the people of Antioch in the *Misopogon* <note>The "Beard-Hater," Julian's satirical defense of his lifestyle.</note>, when he shifts the blame for his life upon him with a certain irony. "Often (he says) these names, I believe, have been heard—so much, clearly, have they been criticized and mocked—Plato, Socrates, Aristotle, Theophrastus. When that old man [Mardonius] had followed them with excessive folly and simplicity, and later finding me as a boy burning with the desire for learning, he persuaded me that if I wished to follow in their footsteps, I would become a better man—perhaps not better than other men (for there was no competition between me and them), but certainly better than my former self. But I (for what else could I have done?), having obeyed the old man, can no longer change anything, however much I might desire it; and I reproach myself for [giving in to] all..."
