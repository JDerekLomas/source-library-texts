# PREFACE. <note>The original French heading "Avertissement" translates literally as "Warning" or "Notice," but in 18th-century publishing, it serves as the author's or editor's preface.</note>

Without the immense labors of M. de Sainte-Palaie <note>Jean-Baptiste de La Curne de Sainte-Palaye (1697–1781), a pioneering French scholar of medieval history and linguistics.</note>, the literary history of Europe, and of France in particular, would have always remained incomplete. Only the author of the *Memoirs on Ancient Chivalry* <note>Sainte-Palaye’s most famous work, which helped spark the 18th-century revival of interest in the Middle Ages.</note> could have snatched the troubadours from the tomb where their fame was buried along with their works. This respectable academician, sacrificing everything—health and fortune—to the deepest research into our national antiquities, has succeeded in discovering everything one could reasonably desire in a field of study bristling with thorns and capable of daunting even the most passionate thirst for knowledge. The Public will judge that this is not merely a discovery of idle curiosity, nor of fruitless erudition.

To know the troubadours, these ancient Provençal poets <note>While "Provençal" specifically refers to the region of Provence, it was historically used as a catch-all term for the Occitan language of Southern France used by the troubadours.</note>, the fathers of modern literature, it was necessary to find and explain their works. The King’s Library <note>original: "bibliothèque du Roi"; the royal collection that became the modern National Library of France (BnF).</note> possesses only four manuscripts of them. Italy possesses a great many. Although a love for antiquity and the arts had already led M. de Sainte-Palaie there, he undertook a second journey to collect so many unknown or neglected monuments... <note>The text ends mid-word with "négli-," the first part of "négligés" (neglected), continuing on the next page.</note>

<summary>This preface introduces the work of Jean-Baptiste de La Curne de Sainte-Palaye, creditng him with rediscovering the lost literature of the medieval troubadours through extensive archival research and travel to Italy.</summary>

In the month of February 1740, the *Literary News of Florence* <note>original: *Nouvelles littéraires de Florence* (Novelle Letterarie di Firenze), a prominent 18th-century scholarly journal.</note> celebrated his project and his work. Four years later, the learned Doctor Lami <note>Giovanni Lami (1697–1770), an influential Italian librarian and scholar.</note> celebrated them once again in an eloquent dedicatory epistle, where he likewise did justice to the qualities of his heart and those of his mind. (See *Delights of the Erudite* <note>original Latin: *Deliciæ eruditorum*, a multi-volume collection of historical documents published by Lami.</note>.)

If the French academician has exhausted, so to speak, all the libraries of Italy, this species of conquest required incredible activity, expense, and care. Let one judge this by a particular fact. The Fathers Mabillon and de Montfaucon <note>Jean Mabillon (1632–1707) and Bernard de Montfaucon (1655–1741) were giants of 17th-century scholarship; their inability to access these texts highlights the difficulty of Sainte-Palaie's task.</note> had been unable to obtain permission for certain manuscripts in Rome to be shared with them: M. de Sainte-Palaie, to obtain access to them, required a papal brief <note>A formal letter or decree from the Pope; here, it served as a high-level security clearance for restricted archives.</note>; whether a misguided literary jealousy or a self-interested policy had placed obstacles in the way of the progress of our knowledge.

After having collected about four thousand pieces, and the original lives of several poets; after having verified that the fragments scattered in various places, numbering twelve hundred, were all found in his collections; he still had the greatest difficulties to overcome. How was he to properly understand the troubadours? Men of Letters, though familiar with the Provençal tongue <note>The Occitan language of Southern France, which was the medium of the troubadours.</note>

<summary>This page describes the immense scholarly and diplomatic effort Sainte-Palaie undertook in Italy, including obtaining a papal brief to access restricted manuscripts that had been denied to previous famous scholars. He eventually compiled over four thousand pieces of troubadour poetry and biography.</summary>
