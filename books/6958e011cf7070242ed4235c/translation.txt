# On the Powers of Herbs

About to speak of the powers of certain herbs in song,
Artemisia <note>Mugwort (Artemisia vulgaris)</note> was given the name "Mother of Herbs,"
To which I think it right to first apply
The Greek name.
Diana is said to have first discovered its aid;
She is called *Artemis* in Greek, and from her the herb
Takes its name, because she is said to be its discoverer.
This herb heals women’s diseases especially;
A decoction of it, when taken, draws out the menses.
The womb <note>original: "matrix"</note> does the same if it is often bathed in this,
Or if the raw herb is ground and drunk with pure wine,
Or if, when green and crushed, it is bound over the belly at night.

->¶ About to speak of the powers of certain herbs, etc.<-

The poet begins his song with Artemisia, first declaring the origin of its name. Some wish to claim that Artemisia is derived from Artemisia, the wife of Mausolus, King of Caria <note>Artemisia II of Caria (died c. 350 BCE), a botanist and researcher</note>, who first gave her name to this herb. This Artemisia (as literary monuments testify) built a tomb for her husband—which she wished to be called the *Mausoleum*—of such magnificence, beauty, and ornament that it is numbered among the Seven Wonders of the World. Concerning this, Martial <note>Roman poet, 1st century CE</note> wrote: *Nor the Mausoleums hanging in the empty air.* Others say Artemisia is named after Artemis, that is, Diana <note>original: "arthemys ei.g. dyana latine fignat" - Artemis in Greek signifies Diana in Latin</note>, because just as Diana was invoked by those in labor (as can be seen in comedies), so too this herb specifically heals the ailments of women, as Pliny <note>Pliny the Elder, Roman naturalist</note> testifies. Indeed, Juno is so named from "helping" (*iuvando*) and Lucina because she brings the birth into the light (*lucem*). For Diana, Juno, and Lucina all signify the same thing <note>These goddesses were often conflated in Roman religion as protectors of childbirth</note>. As I am about to speak—that is, wishing to describe the virtues—meaning the powers and certain properties—of some herbs, I think—that is, I judge—it to be just—meaning consistent with law and reason—to set down—that is, to describe—first, in the first place, the Mother of Herbs. For it is just that a mother be preferred over her children. To this herb, the Greek language gave—that is, bestowed—a name, namely *Artemisia*. Diana <note>The Roman goddess of the hunt and childbirth, known as Artemis in Greek.</note> is said—that is, reported—to have been the first to discover the aid—meaning the strengths and properties—of that herb. Since Diana is called *Artemis* in Greek, the herb holds—that is, retains—its name from her for the reason that Artemis herself, namely Diana, is called the discoverer of this herb.

This herb heals—that is, assists—chiefly, by a certain special nature, the diseases of women—that is, of females—as will be clarified hereafter. A decoction <note>A liquid preparation made by boiling the herb in water.</note> of this herb, when taken, draws down—that is, provokes—the menses, which are the bloody superfluities of women. 

¶ The menses are indeed named from "month" <note>Latin: *mensis*</note> because women are accustomed to emit these flows through their private parts every single month, unless they have not yet reached the age of childbearing, or have already passed it, or have become pregnant. 

¶ For the menses occur in women, as Galen <note>A prominent Greek physician and philosopher in the Roman Empire (c. 129–216 AD).</note> testifies, by the intention of generative nature. ¶ Therefore, if the menses do not fail to reach a just quantity, or do not exceed it, they are causes of health; but if they either fail or exceed, they are causes of sickness. Whence Hippocrates <note>The ancient Greek physician often called the "Father of Medicine."</note> says: 

¶ When the menses occur in too great a quantity, illnesses happen; and when they do not occur, illnesses happen from the womb <note>Latin: *matrice*</note>. 

But let us return to the literal text. The decoction—that is, the water from the boiling of this herb—draws down the menses if they have not flowed at the time appointed by nature; and this herb does this—that is, draws down the menses—if the womb—that is, that member in women appointed for generation—is fomented—that is, warmed with a medicinal compress—of the same herb.
