# Discourse on the Defense of the Zhejiang Seas

At the beginning of the dynasty <note>Referring to the founding of the Ming dynasty in 1368.</note>, four Regional Commanders <term>Bazong: a military rank responsible for coastal defense and naval coordination</term> were established along the coast of Zhejiang. The first command oversaw Dinghai, Linshan, and Guanhai. The second command covered Songmen, Haimen, and Changguo. The third command was stationed at Jinxiang and Panqian, and the fourth was dedicated to Haining. While all other commands were equipped with large warships, Haining alone was not. Why was this the case?

I once traveled to Dinghai, where I climbed the heights and meditated upon the terrain. Beyond it lies the Ningbo Sea, which sits directly across from the Suzhou Sea to the north, separated by only a few hundred <term>li: a traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters</term>. To the east of Zhejiang are the prefectures of Ningbo and Shaoxing; to the west is Jiaxing. Hangzhou, however, sits at the very end of the western inlet; it is effectively an interior "belly" land and not part of the open sea. When an alarm is raised at sea, the warships from the other commands immediately deploy to intercept and block the path. How could the pirates <note>original: "賊" (zei); specifically referring to the Wokou or "dwarf pirates" who raided the Chinese coast during the Ming period</note> strike directly at the heart of the region?

Furthermore, the waters at Haining are shallow and sandy, lacking deep-water harbors where ships could anchor. Therefore, by simply fortifying the "outer doors" <note>A metaphor for the outer coastal defenses and islands</note>, the "inner chambers" of the province remain naturally secure.

In my humble estimation, the various islands and mountains of the Zhejiang seas are divided into three boundaries. These include: Huangniu Mountain, Mamu, Changtu, Cezi, Jintang, Daxie, Lanxiu, Jianshan, Shuangyu, Shuangtang, Liuheng, Jiushan, and Tanshan... <note>This list identifies key strategic islands in the Zhoushan Archipelago and surrounding waters that served as the frontline for coastal defense.</note>

In coastal defense, nothing is more urgent than the naval fleet <term>zhoushi: literally "boat masters," the traditional term for a maritime military force</term>. Under the old system, the Chenghai Guard produced various warships categorized by their capacity, including those of 700, 500, 400, and 300 *liao* <term>liao: a traditional Chinese unit of ship measurement based on its cargo capacity or the amount of timber used in construction</term>, as well as specialized "pointed boats." Because the pirate vessels were formerly not large, the production of the 700-*liao* ships has long been discontinued. 

The remaining vessels, such as the 500-*liao* class, were also found to be inconvenient for naval combat and were remodeled into Fuqing-style ships <note>A type of sturdy, deep-hulled warship originating from Fujian province, better suited for the open sea</note>. The military also operates eight-oar boats equipped with firearms to launch surprise ambushes, as well as netting boats. These forces are divided into four regional commands and six mobile corps to patrol and guard the various ocean ports.

## Discourse on the Preparedness of Shandong

The pirate troubles <note>original: "倭患" (Wohan) — referring to the Wokou, or "dwarf pirates," maritime raiders (mostly Japanese, but later including Chinese outlaws) who plagued the Ming coast.</note> began from Nagasaki <note>original: "嶺崎" (Lingqi), likely referring to the Japanese port of Nagasaki.</note> in the north and reached all the way to the Huai and Yang regions <note>modern-day Jiangsu province</note>; there was nowhere that did not suffer their harm. Yet, why is it that Shandong alone has not been reached by this calamity? Is it because the pirates have no interest in this place? 

It is also because the people of Shandong are comfortable with saddles and horses but are not skilled with boats and oars. There have been no locals who have "crossed over to the barbarians" <note>original: "過番" (guofan) — a term for traveling to foreign lands, often used in the context of illegal trade or emigration during the Ming maritime bans.</note> or gone to sea to serve as guides or provide supplies for the raiders. 

However, in recent years, many soldiers from the regions of Qing and Qi <note>ancient names for Shandong</note> have been captured by the pirates. Who is to say that among them there are none who, now knowing the sea routes, might lure the enemy back? In my humble observation, the character of the people in the various prefectures of Shandong is fierce, and they take pleasure in combat. The short weapons of the pirates are not enough to withstand our long spears and powerful crossbows; the pirates' infantry tactics are no match for our organized ranks of heavy cavalry. Should they ever happen to arrive here, it would be their own destruction. 

What concerns me is that Dengzhou and Laizhou <note>the two major prefectures of the eastern peninsula</note> jut out into the sea and are surrounded by enemies on three sides, making them difficult to guard. Our dynasty has specifically established one <term>Regional Commander for Wokou Defense <note>original: "備倭都指揮" (Beiwo Duzhihui) — a high-ranking military post dedicated to anti-piracy.</note></term> and one <term>Vice Commissioner for Sea Patrol <note>original: "巡海副使" (Xunhai Fushi) — a civilian official with military oversight of coastal security.</note></term>, who are stationed separately to defend the guards of these two prefectures.
