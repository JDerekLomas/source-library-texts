Regarding **back-erupting carbuncles** <term>fabei<note>original: "fabei" (發背). These are severe, deep-seated subcutaneous infections or clusters of boils. In the Ming Dynasty, when this text was compiled, they were considered extremely dangerous because of their proximity to the spine and internal organs.</note></term>: they occur when <term>wind-heat<note>original: "fengre" (風熱). A traditional medical category for pathogens that cause sudden inflammation, fever, and redness.</note></term> and evil toxins pour into the spaces between the ribs and manifest within the **meridians** <term>jingluo<note>original: "jingluo" (經絡). The network of channels through which vital energy is believed to circulate.</note></term>. 

When a person experiences deep sorrow and bitterness, or indulges in excessive pleasures, the **nutritive and defensive energies** <term>ying-wei<note>original: "ying-wei" (營衛). "Ying" refers to the blood-nourishing energy, while "wei" is the body's outermost immune-like defense.</note></term> both become hollowed and weak. If phlegm is present and the **true qi** <note>The body's essential life force and constitutional strength.</note> is exhausted and depleted, the toxins will seize the opportunity to attack and erupt.

### Method of Treatment

The protocol for healing begins with decocting **Angelica and Forsythia Powder** <note>original: "Danggui Lianqiao San" (當歸連翹散). A classic formula used to move the blood and clear internal heat.</note> taken together with **Detoxification Pills** to clear and unblock the internal pathways. Afterward, the patient should take **Frankincense and Astragalus Powder** <note>Astragalus is used here to "prop up" or support the body's ability to push the infection outward and grow new skin.</note> to support the healing of the back. 

On the opening of the sore itself, apply **Toad Venom Pills** <note>original: "Chansu Wan" (蟾酥丸). Derived from toad secretions, this was a potent, specialized medication used for its intense anti-inflammatory and anesthetic properties.</note>, adjusting the amount based on the size of the ulcer. On the swollen areas surrounding the four sides of the wound, apply **Frankincense Toxin-Extracting Powder**. 

If the sore has already burst and is discharging:
*   Apply **Toxin-Chasing Obsidian Powder** <note>original: "Wujin San" (烏金散), literally "Black Gold Powder." This likely contained charred ingredients intended to "eat away" dead tissue.</note> to remove the putrid or necrotic flesh.
*   Apply **Peach Blossom Powder** <note>original: "Taohua San" (桃花散). A red-tinted powder used to dry the wound and encourage the skin to close and scar over.</note> to facilitate the final contraction and healing of the skin.

<summary>This page explains that back carbuncles are caused by a combination of external environmental "heat" and internal depletion caused by emotional or physical excess. It outlines a comprehensive treatment starting with internal "unblocking" herbs and ending with topical powders to remove dead flesh and close the wound.</summary>

Regarding **head-erupting carbuncles** <term>fanao<note>original: "fanao" (發腦). A severe, deep-seated inflammatory lesion or carbuncle appearing on the back of the head or the nape of the neck.</note></term>: these are caused by **suppressed yang** <term>fuyang<note>original: "fuyang" (伏陽). In traditional medicine, this refers to internal heat or "yang" energy that becomes trapped within the body rather than circulating or venting naturally.</note></term> becoming stagnant and blocked, causing **evil toxins** <note>Pathogenic factors that cause disease, often associated with infection or inflammation.</note> to congest and surge upward. 

They may arise either at the edge of the **Jade Pillow** <term>yuzhen<note>original: "yuzhen" (玉枕). This refers to the occipital bone at the base of the skull or the specific acupuncture point located there (BL9).</note></term> or in the area of the **Wind Pool** <term>fengchi<note>original: "fengchi" (風池). A vital acupuncture point (GB20) located in the depressions on either side of the neck at the base of the skull.</note></term>.

In appearance, they resemble **hard boils** <term>yingjie<note>original: "yingjie" (硬癤). A firm, localized, and painful lump.</note></term> that gradually **ulcerate** <note>To break open, fester, or become an open sore.</note> and spread. The pain is as intense as a **knife’s stab**. The affected area is **red and swollen**, yet it typically contains **no pus**. 

Generally, **head-erupting carbuncles** are to be managed and cured using the **same therapeutic methods** as **back-erupting carbuncles** <term>fabei<note>original: "fabei" (發背). Large, dangerous carbuncles on the back, which were the subject of the previous page.</note></term>.

# Illustration of Temple Sores
<term>fabinchuang<note>original: "fabinchuang" (發鬢瘡). A medical term for inflammatory swellings, abscesses, or carbuncles occurring in the temple area, specifically where the sideburns grow.</note></term>

The illustration accompanying this heading depicts a gentleman dressed in the formal robes and "scholar's cap" <note>original: "zhuangyuan mao" (狀元帽). The headwear associated with the top scorer of the imperial examinations, used here to represent a person of status.</note> of the Ming era. He is seated on a stool, reaching his right hand up to touch his temple. 

In traditional Chinese medicine, the location of a skin eruption was crucial for diagnosis. **Temple sores** are associated with the **Lesser Yang** <note>Shao Yang</note> networks—specifically the Gallbladder meridian—which travels along the sides of the head. These sores were thought to be caused by "fire" or "stagnant heat" in these specific pathways. Practitioners used these diagrams to distinguish **temple sores** from other cranial conditions like **head-erupting carbuncles** <note>fanao (發腦), located at the back of the head</note> or **cheek-erupting sores** <note>fayi (發頤), located near the jaw</note>.

Regarding the eruption of **mumps-like swelling** <term>fazha<note>original: "fazha" (發痄). 3. One must first **promote drainage** <note>original: "shuli" (疏利). A clinical strategy to clear the body's pathways of toxins, often via the bowels or by dispersing stagnation.</note>.
4. Afterward, administer **Wind-Eliminating Powder** <note>original: "Xiaofeng San" (消風散). A classic formula for treating skin conditions caused by wind-heat.</note> to remove the lingering wind-heat.

For the opening of the sore itself, apply **Poison-Expelling Powder** <note>original: "Zhuidu San" (追毒散).</note> topically.

Regarding **Withered Jaw sores** <note>original: "Qiaosai" (癄顋). A historical term for a painful, hardened swelling or abscess on the lower jaw or chin, often involving the salivary glands or deep tissue inflammation.</note>, these are caused by deficiency-heat in the Spleen and Stomach, combined with "evil wind" <note>In traditional medicine, "evil wind" refers to external environmental pathogens that invade the body.</note> from the Heart and Lungs attacking upwards. 

The condition originates at the edge of the <term>Heliao point <note>original: "Heliao" (禾髎). An acupuncture point located on the upper lip, lateral to the philtrum; its name literally means "Grain Bone-hole."</note></term> and is mostly found at the side of the <term>Chengjiang point <note>original: "Chengjiang" (承漿). An acupuncture point located in the center of the groove below the lower lip; its name literally means "Saliva Container."</note></term>. Its appearance is **shaped like a "sheep's thorn"** <note>likely referring to a jagged, prickly, or sharply protruding inflammation</note>, and the four surrounding sides are swollen and hard. 

The pain is nearly unbearable; when yellow fluid flows out, the patient may experience numbness and increased chills. The nature of this ailment is "rebellious" <note>meaning it is stubborn and difficult to treat, often progressing against the desired healing direction</note>.

Regarding **Throat-Closure** <note>original: 喉閉 (Hóubì). Previously defined as a severe blockage or constriction of the throat that prevents swallowing or breathing.</note>: this condition occurs when **Wind-Heat** <term>Wind-Heat (風熱): A pathological pattern in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) where external environmental factors—often associated with infectious diseases—combine with heat, causing rapid-onset inflammation and fever.</term> and the body's **Blood and Qi** congeal together. This condition originates at the **root of the tongue** and pours into the **Double Tower** <note>original: 重樓 (Chónglóu). A traditional anatomical metaphor for the trachea and esophagus, named for the tiered, "stack of towers" appearance of the cartilaginous rings of the windpipe.</note>.

When the entire body is in a state of disharmony, the **Nutritive and Defensive systems** <term>Ying and Wei (榮衛): The Ying (Nutritive) Qi flows within the blood vessels to nourish the organs, while the Wei (Defensive) Qi flows outside the vessels to provide immunity. Their balance is essential for health.</term> suffer injury. Because the **hundred vessels** <note>original: 百脉 (Bǎimài). A term referring to the entire complex network of circulatory channels and meridians that connect the body’s internal systems.</note> are all interconnected—numbering thirteen thousand five hundred in total—this localized throat condition can affect the patient's whole being.

**The Method of Treatment**

The protocol for healing is as follows:

1.  First, the patient must ingest **cooling medicines** <note>original: 涼藥 (Liángyào). Herbal formulas with a cold or cool property designed to neutralize "Fire" and reduce swelling.</note> to **"wash the heart"** <note>original: 洗心 (Xǐxīn). In this medical context, it refers to a therapeutic strategy of clearing pathogenic heat from the heart and chest area to soothe the spirit and reduce <unclear>internal inflammation</unclear>.</note>
