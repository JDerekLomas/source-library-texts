## Preface.

This small study on the **headless god** <note>In ancient magic, the "headless god" (Akephalos) was a powerful, often terrifying figure invoked in spells for protection or cursing.</note> constitutes an excerpt from the collections of material gathered during my many years of work with the **Greek magical papyri** <note>A collection of ancient texts from Egypt dating from the 2nd century BCE to the 5th century CE, containing spells, rituals, and hymns.</note>. It had been in preparation for a long time along with others—such as the study regarding the **snake biting its own tail** <note>the Ouroboros, a symbol of eternity and the cyclical nature of the universe</note>—before I encountered the stimulating work of <term>A. Delatte</term>, *The Headless God* <note>original: "ΑΚΕΦΑΛΟΣ ΘΕΟΣ"</note>, which collected valuable material after the end of the war; his work provided the impetus to develop my study as a parallel to his. Although my interpretation and reading of the various traditions concerning the Headless One mostly deviate from <term>Delatte’s</term>, I gladly admit that I owe much to his lines of thought and results, even in areas where I have not progressed beyond preliminary findings and hypotheses. It seems regrettable throughout that <term>Delatte</term> did not resolve to build his contribution upon a thorough re-examination of the papyrus texts themselves. Such a review would have protected him from the false conclusions that inevitably followed his use of <term>C. Wessely’s</term> sketch of the magical image in the **Mimaut Papyrus** <note>original: "Pap. Mimaut"; a famous magical manuscript held in the Louvre Museum</note>.

One may consider and excuse the digression regarding the Roman **curse tablets** <note>Thin sheets of lead (defixiones) inscribed with magic spells intended to "bind" or influence the actions of others, often buried in graves or thrown into wells.</note> of the **Kircherian Museum** <note>original: "Museo Kircheriano"; a historic collection of antiquities in Rome</note> as a detour lying directly along the path. Their "Sethian" origin—the name given to them by <term>Richard Wünsch</term>, their first editor and commentator who earned great merit in the study of ancient magic—seemed to require a re-examination of certain decisive questions. The engagement with <term>Delatte’s</term> headless god, which led to **Seth-Typhon** <note>A syncretic deity combining the Egyptian god Seth with the Greek monster Typhon, often depicted as a chaotic or adversarial power in magical texts</note>, brought about this late **critical assessment** <note>original: "Epikritik"; a formal scholarly review or follow-up analysis</note> quite naturally. It should be expressly noted that this is intended merely as an objective statement of my own results and conjectures, and in no way a polemic against <term>Richard Wünsch</term>, whom I highly esteemed as both a scholar and a man; his untimely death—he too [fell in the war]...

## The Headless God in Folklore.

The headless figure—a horror to us, yet a familiar figure to the religious conceptions of ancient civilized peoples as well as today's indigenous peoples <note>original: "Naturvölker," a historical term used by 19th and early 20th-century scholars to describe tribal or non-industrialized societies</note> across the entire globe. Europeans from the North and South alike feared the spirits of the headless, who haunt even into our own time: indeed, **GOTTFRIED KELLER'S** <note>Gottfried Keller (1819–1890) was a famous Swiss author; his novel *Green Henry* (Der grüne Heinrich) is a masterpiece of German-language realism</note> *Green Henry* learns from his patroness Frau Margreth how lively things were with such ghostly business in her youth, "when she had to walk through field and forest by day and night, especially in the countryside. There, headless men had walked by her side for hours, drawing closer the more fervently she prayed, . . . and she described in moving words the distressing state she found herself in when she could not resist addressing the uncanny companions from the side, even though she knew that this was highly harmful." . . . To the Germanic peoples, the headless were the ghosts of criminals who had suffered execution by the sword or deserved it by law but escaped it in this life: they must wander headless or carrying the head they paid with under their arm until their redemption. So too the <term>movers of boundary stones</term> <note>original: "Verrücker von Flursteinen." In agrarian societies, moving a boundary stone to steal land was considered a grave sin and a crime against the community</note>: for them, the plowshare tears the head from the body, whether they met this punishment in this life or only after death. All those who lost their heads to the executioner’s axe or the weapon of an opponent or murderer can become headless haunting spirits. All characteristic examples collected so far from the legends and folk traditions of the northern countries of Europe point to this¹.

The South also knows the headless ghost figures of decapitated villains. Plutarch <note>A famous Greek biographer and essayist (c. 46–119 AD) known for his "Lives" and "Moralia."</note> tells of a "strange" festival on Crete: there, an image of a headless man, Molos, was shown to the crowd. He had violated a young girl, and after the deed, he was found without a head. It is uncertain whether divine or human vengeance overtook him. Furthermore, a headless Triton <note>A Greek sea god, usually depicted as a merman with the upper body of a human and the tail of a fish.</note> stood in the Temple of Dionysus <note>The god of wine, fertility, and ritual madness.</note> in Tanagra, as Pausanias (9, 20, 5) <note>A Greek traveler and geographer of the 2nd century AD who wrote a famous "Description of Greece."</note> reports in his writings. As the reason for his **acephaly** <term>acephaly: the condition of being headless (from the Greek "akephalos")</term>, a legend cites the decapitation of the sea demon by a man from Tanagra: the Triton had stolen livestock, was captured by the inhabitants using wine, and was overpowered in a drunken sleep. Before this, however, Pausanias shares another, older version of the legend: it tells of an attack by the Triton on women who were taking their ritual bath in the sea. Their pleas summoned Dionysus, who fought the demon down. No decapitation is explicitly mentioned here; however, in both traditions, we are dealing with headless depictions of two "violators of women." S. Eitrem (in *Contributions 2, 48*) suspected that phallic concepts might play a role in the Molos story, and the Triton legend seems to support his view. ...had to die at the <term>nuptial bed</term><note>original: "Beilager"; refers to the formal consummation of a marriage</note>. This idea—that it represents a symbolic killing of growth—should be approached only with caution. The public display of the headless image of **Molos** <note>In Cretan myth, Molos was a son of Deucalion who was punished for a sexual transgression</note> likely pointed, in its most ancient origins, to the punishment that threatened the violator and rapist of virginity on Crete, regardless of whether the equation between the "head" and the "phallus" actually existed at that time. Furthermore, the image of Molos does not need to have been merely a puppet prepared specifically for a festival, as **Nilsson** <note>Martin P. Nilsson (1874–1967), a highly influential scholar of Greek religion</note> suggests. The <term>spirit-image</term><note>original: "Eidolon"</note> of this headless figure could well have consisted of a cult image from the most ancient times, much like the statue of the **Triton of Tanagra**.

However, there is no report of any of these "headless ones" wandering about as a <term>haunting demon</term><note>original: "Spukdämon"</note> or appearing as something uncanny to the living. They were "heroized" in the ancient sense of the word; they did not yet belong to those heroes of a later period who wandered about as restless ghosts in the night. This is also true of **Dionysos Kephalen** <note>original: "Kephalen" from the Greek "kephale" (head); a specific form of Dionysos worshipped at Methymna</note> of Methymna, **Orpheus** of Antissa, **Orion**, and others who, according to myth, had lost their heads. Only their heads served here and there for miraculous prophecy. For example, the <term>Gorgon's head</term><note>original: "Gorgoneion"</note> appears in the sea off the coast of Asia Minor according to medieval legend; depending on how it moves, it predicts a calm or a storm. The finned head of the **Gorgon** creates the demonic impression of a <term>chthonic</term><note>relating to the underworld or the depths of the earth</note> deity or a dangerous sea spirit (O. GRUPPE, *Greek Mythology*, 1903, 837, 3; Index under "Head")... but even here, we are not dealing with "headless spirits" in the true sense, any more than with those severed heads in Italy that possessed a tradition of prophetic power (W. FURTWÄNGLER, *Ancient Gems*, 3, 1900, 251 f.).

The fact of decapitation manifests differently in cult and legend: it is not the "headless body," but rather the "head" as a being unto itself that plays a role in the figure’s history. Similarly, **Mimir** <note>A figure in Norse mythology known for his great knowledge; after he was beheaded, Odin preserved his head to consult it for secrets</note> does not live on as a <term>headless one</term><note>original: "Akephalos"</note> after his death; instead, the myth provides us with his prophetic head. Consequently, such figures were not depicted as headless gods or demons; only their prophetic heads were occasionally rendered in art. The depiction of <term>headless</term><note>original: "akephaler"</note> beings was only possible where the headless person was thought to be still active *within* their mutilation—that is, acting specifically without a head. This was the case in the world of **magic** and its literature; the different types of examples, such as Molos and Triton, must be excluded from this category.

Lucian, in his work *The Lover of Lies* (Chapter 29), considers the possibility of spirits of the hanged, the decapitated, and the crucified reappearing. However, it is unlikely that Tertullian's <note>Tertullian (c. 155–220 AD) was a prolific early Christian author from Carthage.</note> reproach against the pagans in his *Apology* (Chapter 12)—"Your gods are without heads!"—refers to this. Nor does his report in *To the Nations* (Book 1, 10) that Varro <note>Marcus Terentius Varro (116–27 BC) was an ancient Roman scholar and writer.</note> speaks of "three hundred Jupiters without heads." One wonders if such mockery refers to truly headless religious figures or rather to ancient statues of gods that lost their heads through accidents and were left in temples in their mutilated state due to their antiquity, without being repaired by human hands. Such statues do not count as headless demons, any more than Varro’s Stoic "round god," who is described as "without head or foreskin"—one who has achieved his roundness through <term>akroteriasmos (the removal of extremities, in this context implying a loss of limbs to form a perfect sphere)</term>, and with whom the Emperor Claudius in Seneca’s *Pumpkinification* <note>A satire by Seneca on the death and deification of Emperor Claudius.</note> (Chapter 8) must not be compared!

Similarly, the figure of the "Original Man" from the Samothracian mysteries, as recorded by Hippolytus <note>Hippolytus of Rome (c. 170–235 AD) was a Christian theologian who wrote against heresies.</note> in his *Refutation of All Heresies* (5, 8, 13 f.), clearly cannot be included among the headless gods. The description of this <term>Archanthropos (Original Man)</term>—called Korybas by the Thracians and Phrygians and depicted in the sanctuary with an erect phallus and hands raised toward heaven—is not easily grasped: "He begins his descent from the heights above—hence his name Korybas, 'the High-Strider'—and from the featureless brain, to [direct] all the principles of the things lying beneath in a way incomprehensible to us. "...menoi" <note>original: "menoi," likely the suffix of a Greek term like *biothanatoi* (those who died by violence) or *omenaoi*</note> of the curse tablets from Cyprus¹: they are summoned alongside the souls from mass graves, suicides, victims of rape, those who died prematurely, the unburied, and the aborted² and are compelled into the service of **Black Magic**. It is these spirits who are believed to possess magical powers after their death: **Aretaeus of Cappadocia** mentions in the 2nd century AD that the blood of the beheaded is a particularly healing drink for epileptics <note>original: *de curat. morbor.* 1, 4—referring to the treatise "On the Cure of Diseases"</note>, a folk superstition that has survived even into our own time: Andersen <note>the famous author Hans Christian Andersen</note> recounts in his autobiography that in 1823 he attended an execution in Skelskör: here, parents had their epileptic children drink a cup of blood from the beheaded...

The sinners of the Greeks who were punished by decapitation only in the afterlife may also—like those of the Germanic underworld—have become headless ghosts, even if direct evidence is hard to find: Aeschylus already writes of the goddesses of justice <note>The Furies or Eumenides</note> who, in Hades, carry out the punishment of beheading or blinding upon the condemned, depending on their misdeeds³.

To the ancient civilizations, as well as to numerous "primitive" tribes, the death-demon appeared in the exact form of its corpse; wounds and mutilations to the body and limbs reveal the nature of its death. However, this specific view of the *headless* spirit likely belongs to a later era. Yet it is not absent even in the most recent times: according to a report by **FR. W. H. MYERS**⁴, the spirit of a man beheaded by the Chinese in battle visited his sister in Europe; she saw her brother standing headless by her bed, and next to him, the dead man's coffin with his head resting upon it. This seems like a haunting survival of the oldest beliefs in demons⁵.

Headless goats are among the procession of Germanic ghost-animals; and the headless snake in the Greek starry sky finds its explanation in the myth of the struggle between Heracles and the Dragon, whose head the hero cuts off. Zeus then secures the creature's immortality in the celestial sphere by transforming it into a constellation in this, its final earthly form¹. The headless dove, which appears alongside the headless snake <note>original: "akephale Ophis"</note> in the sign of Capricorn, may perhaps be traced back to a similar legendary foundation unknown to me.

In reality, however, the original concept of the "headless one" is traced back to ancient burial customs. Germanic tribes often did not inter the body intact, but only after detaching individual parts. In some places—the Moselle region and Northern France—the skull is missing; in others—Thuringia—everything is cremated except for the head. A high-ranking follower of King Dagobert fell terminally ill; the king rushed to his side, and it was decided—though it did not come to pass—to cut off the sick man’s head "according to heathen custom" and burn his body²... From such customs³, which sought to prevent the return of the dead to the realm of the living, the headless demon was born. Death itself, or Hel <note>The Norse goddess of the underworld and the realm of the dead itself</note>, is headless according to Holstein legends. This is also true in Byzantine lore: before a great plague in Constantinople, the gods of death were seen as "black men without heads in bronze ships," sailing toward the future sites of disease⁴. Persons marked for destruction appear headless, or a headless spirit appears to them, and whoever casts a shadow only with their body <note>implying the shadow is missing its head</note> must soon die: these were originally cases where the person affected lost their life through beheading.

In ancient Italy, too, it was the custom to cremate the body of someone who died while traveling or at war on the spot, but to preserve one limb—sometimes the head—in order to bury that specific part at home⁵.

In Egypt, the corpse was dismembered <note>The ritual practice of taking a body apart after death</note> only to be reassembled and embalmed later¹. In ancient times, this custom was not entirely forgotten, though it may have been practiced less frequently during the era of the pyramid builders. Bodies have been found that were decapitated before burial. There were also ritual formulas in which the gods were requested to return the deceased's severed head to them in the afterlife. This custom was most closely linked to the myth of the dismemberment of Osiris. The body of the murdered god was scattered in many pieces across Egypt by Seth and his accomplices: in Abydos, the head of Osiris was found and revered as a relic <note>A sacred object, often a physical remain of a deity or holy person</note>. According to one version of the legend, the god received his limbs and his head back before Isis restored him to life.

However, there was also an old legend stating that every year a head made of papyrus <note>A material made from the pith of the papyrus plant, commonly used for writing or construction</note> would travel from Egypt to Byblos in a divinely guided sea voyage. Lucian, who recounts this miracle in *On the Syrian Goddess*, Chapter 7 <note>original: "De Dea Syria"</note>, witnessed it himself and examined the papyrus head. According to Lucian, this floating head was the reason some people in Byblos assumed that Osiris was buried in their city, and that the festivals for Adonis held there were not actually for Adonis, but rather exclusively for the Egyptian god. Apparently, no prophetic, speaking, or other miracles are reported from this sanctuary, unlike the floating heads in similar legends of antiquity: in those cases, the head itself played a miraculous role, whereas in Egypt, the miracle was the revived, headless Osiris².

# Doubtful evidence for the Headless One.

->1.<-

Perhaps a trace of the Headless One has also been preserved in the magical papyri. One such instance is found in a passage of the P IV <note>The Great Paris Magical Papyrus, a famous 4th-century Greek manuscript of magic spells.</note>, which scholars have previously attempted to clarify through various conjectures. It appears in the great <term>Agôgê</term> <note>A ritual "attraction" or binding spell intended to bring a lover to the magician.</note>, a practice intended to bring the beloved to the practitioner with the help of Hecate-Artemis-Selene (lines 2441–2707). Within it is a well-known example of <term>Diabolê</term> <note>A ritual technique of "slander" or "defamation" where the magician tells a god that the target of the spell has committed an impiety, hoping to provoke the god's wrath against them.</note>, where "N. N." (the desired woman) is to be made unpopular with the goddess through certain false accusations. There, she is accused (line 2477) as follows: "She slanderously betrayed your—the goddess’s—holy secrets to men. It is N. N. who said it was I who said: 'I saw the greatest goddess leaving the celestial pole, on the earth without sandals, sword-bearing, calling the faceless one by name' ..." <note>original Greek: ἄοπον ὀνομάσασαν (aopon onomasasan)</note>. Given a syncretistic <note>The blending or merging of different religious beliefs.</note> identification of the moon-goddess Selene with Isis, this could refer to the search for the god Osiris <note>Osiris was famously dismembered; Isis searched for his remains.</note>, if one accepts my interpretation. Another possible interpretation would be: "I saw the goddess... on the earth... sword-bearing, calling out facelessly," if one allows the word for "calling by name" <note>original: ὀνομάσασαν</note> to be used intransitively. In that case, Selene would be the goddess who has become headless due to a lunar eclipse and has left her heavenly seat. One must choose between these two possibilities: either the goddess Selene-Isis calls upon the headless Osiris, or she herself, headless, emits cries during the night on earth. The first case would fit the theme of slander: that N. N. had betrayed or profaned the "mysteries" of the goddess. All modifications of this passage through academic conjectures seem unnecessary to me¹.

# The Acephalos in the Sky.

FRANZ BOLL, in his work "Sphaera" (1903), published and discussed the astronomical treatise of Teucer <note>original: "Teukros"</note>, a Greek writer who likely lived in the first century AD. This scholar identifies and names the "Headless One" among the constellations that rise alongside each sign and <term>decan</term> <note>a subdivision of the zodiac into ten-degree increments</note> of the zodiac. Regarding Capricorn, he reports: next to the third <term>decan</term>, there rise the half of the wheel, the tail of the Great Fish, the Altar of Incense, and the headless spirit <note>original: "Dämon"</note> who carries his own head¹. Similarly, a later astronomer who was familiar with Teucer records for Capricorn: in its sign appear Nereus, the Raven, and the Satyr who hides his head.

According to BOLL’s view (p. 221), both authors have the same constellation in mind. They likely knew it from visual depictions but probably interpreted it somewhat differently, or the star maps each saw varied slightly in their imagery. Such traditions are not lacking in the Greco-Egyptian world. BOLL treated the zodiac <note>original: "Sphaera"</note> of the Temple of Dendera from the early Roman Imperial period in great detail, and in doing so, he was able to point out other ancient zodiacal images with great success. Could this figure not be the headless demon of Clement <note>Clement of Alexandria, an early Christian author</note> who sits before the star "of the Wanderer"? According to an Egyptian legend, Horus wandered with Isis while fleeing from Seth until they reached the region of Apis in the Libyan **nome** <term>nome (an administrative district in ancient Egypt)</term>; the details of this wandering are reported with great precision by the Metternich Stela¹. Thus, the headless god of the Dendera decan-circle could perhaps find its explanation as Osiris, and the report by Clement might also gain clarity². Furthermore, it is possible that the headless demon of Teucer <note>Teucer of Babylon, an astrologer from the 1st century</note> could be interpreted from this perspective: it is perhaps he who originally stood as a star between Capricorn and Aquarius and was later replaced, as in the circular zodiac of Dendera, by the headless four-legged animal, taking his place among the decans. This much, however, seems certain to me: if the headless demon of Teucer belonged to the "barbaric" (in this case, Egyptian) **Sphaera** <term>Sphaera (the celestial sphere or a collection of star charts)</term>, rather than to its Hellenistic components, then he likely would have originally been Osiris, whom I recognize in the headless decan of the circular zodiac.

Reference has already been made to other headless figures in the starry sky of the Egyptians and Hellenes. There was a headless dove and snake, and a headless, unidentifiable four-legged creature: these likely all originate from mythology, partially from the cycle of Osiris.
