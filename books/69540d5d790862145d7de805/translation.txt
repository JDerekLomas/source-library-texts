## Marsilio Ficino of Florence humbly commends himself to the Most Reverend Father in Christ, Lord Giovanni de' Medici, Cardinal of the Holy Roman Church. <note>Giovanni de' Medici (1475–1521) later became Pope Leo X. Ficino was the head of the Platonic Academy in Florence.</note>

When I had decided to write a letter to you to congratulate you on the Cardinal's dignity recently bestowed upon you, and was considering to whom I might best give it to be delivered to you—someone who would make our congratulation much more pleasing—I suddenly betook myself to the Academy, hoping that at least in its innermost sanctuaries some greeter most acceptable to you would not be lacking. There, by a certain divine chance, I first encountered Iamblichus <note>A 4th-century Neoplatonist philosopher known for his interest in theurgy and ritual.</note>, known among the Academics as "the Divine," and a great priest there. To him indeed I said, "Greetings, Iamblichus, great priest. At present (as you see) I am about to congratulate Giovanni de' Medici, a new prelate of the Christian religion; I pray you, if I ask what is right, that you will make our otherwise meager congratulation fuller with your divine words and mysteries." He assented to my prayers, and with this sentiment that great priest comes to you: "Giovanni, great priest." You, therefore, joyfully receive such a great greeter, and after the first greeting, after reading the letter, listen more attentively, if you please, to what that divine man says. For he promised that, to speak in a manner worthy of both his person and yours, he would express to you in brief what the Egyptian and Assyrian priests felt concerning religion and divine matters.

### ARGUMENT <note>A summary or introduction to the contents of a book.</note> ON THE BOOK OF IAMBLICHUS.

Porphyry <note>A student of Plotinus and a major Neoplatonic philosopher.</note>, who among the Platonists is called "the Philosopher" on account of his excellence, sent a long letter to Egypt to the priest Anebo, full of various and weighty questions looking toward all parts of philosophy, especially toward God, as well as angels, demons, and souls; toward providence, fate, prophecies, magic, miracles, sacrifices, and vows. Iamblichus, his pupil, responded to Porphyry's questions. He introduces Abamon, an Egyptian priest, responding to Porphyry on behalf of his disciple Anebo, to whom Porphyry had sent the letter. Therefore, of this entire inquiry and truly divine response—as if still too occupied with Plotinus <note>Ficino had recently completed a massive translation and commentary on Plotinus.</note>—I have translated almost every thought rather than every word. Thus it should not be thought strange if the speech appears disjointed in places, especially among the very beginnings of the disputation where the loss of words is lighter. From here on, the context of the words will appear more continuous. I was, however, occasionally forced to insert some word of my own for the sake of continuity or understanding. Furthermore, Pierleone <note>Pierleone Leoni, a famous physician and scholar close to the Medici.</note>, a man most desirous of all secrets, was also a cause for this swifter edition. But that which I especially admire is that he always seems to strive for those things which he already possesses. ## On the Knowledge of Divine Matters

Mercury is the god of wisdom and eloquence. Pythagoras, Plato, Democritus, Eudoxus, and many others approached the Egyptian priests. The doctrines of this book belong to the Assyrians and Egyptians and are taken from the Pillars of Mercury. Pythagoras and Plato learned philosophy from the Pillars of Mercury in Egypt. The Pillars of Mercury are full of teachings. Before any use of reason, there is naturally implanted in us a notion of the gods. Indeed, there is a certain "touch" of divinity that is better than mere knowledge, from which the natural appetite for the Good is incited, as well as reasoning and judgment. The essential knowledge of divine things is perpetual to the soul; and in truth, this is not merely "knowledge" by which we enjoy God. For in knowledge there is "otherness" <note>The philosopher distinguishes between knowing *about* something (subject vs. object) and a direct, unified experience</note>, but this is a certain essential and simple contact. For we cannot reach Unity itself except by something most unified and by a unity of the mind, which exists above the specific properties of the soul and the mind. The Unity of the gods itself unites souls to itself from eternity through their own unities, according to a proximity so proper and effective that it appears to be a continuity. The divine Intellect gives being to the soul through its own essential act of understanding. Therefore, the being of the soul is a certain understanding—namely, of God, upon whom it depends. Our being is to know God, because the principal being of the soul is its intellect, in which "being" is the same as "understanding divine things" in a perpetual act. From that principal being, however, the discursive powers of the soul are derived. After the gods, we place demons, heroes, and pure souls; these three orders are the attendants of the gods. We cannot reach these attendants of the gods—the demons, heroes, and pure souls—by the usual wanderings of human reason. Instead, it is necessary to rise up to essential and eternal intelligence.

<margin>}</margin>
Just as the gods are always reached by an innate notion, so the spirits that attend the gods are only reached when the soul has laid aside its mobile mode of cognition—which belongs to the rational power formed by the intellect and intelligence, which they call the "acquired intellect" <term>intellectus adeptus: a technical term in medieval and Renaissance philosophy for the human intellect when it has been perfected by receiving universal truths</term>. For the first knowledge consists in the "active intellect." Since knowledge occurs through a process of making things equal <note>the mind conforming to the object</note>, it follows that we reach eternal and immobile things through an eternal, immobile, and simple notion. The knowledge by which we know "separate substances" <note>beings without physical bodies, like angels or celestial intelligences</note> is of a different kind than that by which we know other things.

The knowledge of divine things was always in the soul through a simple intuition.

Some people think they will attain knowledge of separate substances <note>The "separate substances" refer to immaterial beings like angels or celestial intellects that exist apart from physical matter.</note> through a certain method of opposition—namely, that material things are mobile, local, and subject to suffering, and so on; therefore, separate substances are immobile, and so on. ### On the properties of the one cause of the higher beings

If any composition exists in contrast to separate and separable substances, it does not consist of a genus that is indeterminate in itself and a terminating difference <note>In Aristotelian logic, a definition is usually made of a "genus" (a broad category) and a "difference" (the specific trait that narrows it down). The author argues divine beings are too simple for this kind of "parts-based" definition.</note>. For those substances are complete species, entirely in a determinate and complete act. If separable substances, being simple, do not differ among themselves through specific differences under the same genus, then they do not differ through individual differences under the same species either. Therefore, each one is, as it were, a single genus or species existing by itself. They differ, however, by themselves and by the order of their grades, and insofar as they are referred to the First <note>The "First" refers to the Neoplatonic "One" or God, the ultimate source of all existence.</note> in different ways. Intelligences do not agree or differ among themselves by genus or any differences—for they are most simple—but only insofar as they are referred to the First by either the same or a different rationale; some are arranged in prior grades of perfection, others in subsequent ones. 

The differences of things among themselves in actions and passions <note>In this context, "passions" refers to being acted upon or receiving an impression, rather than emotion.</note> must everywhere be resolved into differences of powers, and these into differences of essences. One must not rest anywhere in subsequent things, but must proceed to the prior. Neither in the higher substances nor in our soul is there any passive motion, but the whole is an effective act. The motion of the soul, by which it is moved from itself, is not divided into a mover and a moved, or into "moving" and "being moved"; rather, it is an essential act proceeding temporally. Accidents <note>Technical term for non-essential qualities, like color or size, which can change without changing what a thing is.</note> belong to composite things and forms that exist in a subject; they do not suit immaterial forms. Whatever is in separable forms is "being" and is essential by a certain principal and equal lot. Neither is any accident or consequence found there. 

Motions and actions are different because of the differences of natures, not the other way around. If we were to say there is one single genus of separate things, we would confound theology. Separable substances do not agree in one certain genus in which they might be distinguished by differences, but are distinguished by themselves and by the order of their grades, insofar as they are referred to the First through different grades of perfection. Those things are more closely related to one another which are referred in a more similar manner to the first and the last. Goodness is twofold: that which is above essence <note>original: "super essentiam"; referring to the Neoplatonic "One" which transcends being</note>, and that which is according to essence. Essence is the property of all the gods. But the Good itself is above essence: and it is called the cause of good—namely, the principle of essential good. Accordingly, the ranks of the gods are distinguished among themselves according to the degrees of essence. In the lowest rational souls, essential good is not present as it is in the gods, but rather a participation in the good according to intellectual beauty; yet this is present in them immovably.

### On the order of the superior beings.

<margin>a</margin><insert>a</insert>Our souls are counted among the genera of divine beings. Heroes are greater than men, therefore demons are much grander. The first of the divine beings is the Good itself, and the gods following it. The last are the particular rational souls. Between these two are the intermediates: namely, heroes near to the souls, and demons near to the gods, just as air and water exist between fire and earth. Although heroes excel us in power, virtue, beauty, and magnitude, they nevertheless greatly agree with our soul and its life. Demons are superior to heroes, and are the ministers of the gods—who act as architects—in the worldly office. Those things which in the gods are ineffable and hidden, the demons express and make manifest. Demons and heroes adapt the universal, simple, and immovable gifts of the gods for inferior beings. They reconcile all things, and are the authors of harmonic consent and the mutual compassion <note>term: "compassion" (compassio); here meaning a cosmic "sympathy" or resonance between different parts of the universe</note> of all things. They translate divine things to us, and equally lead our affairs up to the divine. Furthermore, they equally distribute the grades in the gifts of the superiors and in the preparation of those receiving them.

The diverse genera of divine beings differ among themselves through a triple mode of properties: namely, as pertains to their essences, their virtues <note>note: "virtues" here refers to inherent powers or capacities</note>, and their actions. The property of the gods is unity, permanence in themselves, and an eminent providence that is the immovable cause of motions, having nothing in common with those things for which it provides; and this property is preserved in them according to essence, virtue, and action. The property of souls is an inclination toward multitude and motion, and a conjunction with the gods, as well as receiving something from others and being filled by them with vital motion, and agreeing everywhere with all things—partly with those that remain, and partly with those that flow—and tempering all things in and through itself. This property is preserved in them according to essence, virtue, and action. The properties of demons are to contain within themselves the gifts of the gods in a manner lower than the gods, but to contain the conditions of inferior things under the form and reason of those gifts; namely, while they are indeed multiplied, they are mixed unitedly, and though mixed, they remain unmixed. Finally, they are moved, but stably...

Heroes, on the other hand, possess unity, identity, stability, and excellence under the condition of multiplicity, motion, and mixture. Daemons and heroes are closest to the extremes: the former to the gods, the latter to souls. ### How the actions of superior beings are distinguished among themselves.

<margin>d</margin>
The gods hold the highest rank of beings, souls the lowest. The gods are capable of all things, united in a single moment; souls, by contrast, are capable neither of all things, nor in a single <margin>ti.</margin> moment, nor are they united. The gods act upon and care for all things without being inclined <note>original: "non inclinati" - meaning they act without losing their transcendent nature or becoming burdened by the material world</note> toward them. Souls act with a certain inclination and turning toward their objects. The gods are the primary causes; souls depend perpetually upon the will of the gods. The gods, with the swiftest vigor, encompass their innermost ends together with their beginnings. Souls proceed from one thing to another, from the imperfect to the perfect. The gods cannot be grasped by any measure or form. Souls are held back and overcome by inclination, habit, and will; they are touched by a desire for lesser things and by familiarity with them, and they contract certain qualities from that contact.

# ON THE MYSTERIES

<note>original: "DE MYSTERIIS"</note>

...distinguishing separate essences by their relationship to bodies—namely, gods by their relationship to ethereal bodies, demons to airy bodies, and souls to earthly bodies. This is incorrect because primary things and causes should not be defined by what comes after them or by their effects. Incorporeal substances are not *in* bodies; rather, they lead them from the outside and give something to them without receiving anything in return. Therefore, they are neither changed along with them, nor are they distributed according to the distribution of those bodies. For they are not "habits" <note>In this context, 'habits' refers to accidental qualities or states of being</note> of bodies or material forms, but are self-subsisting and separable. Indeed, they are already separate and live before bodies exist. Separate and separable substances do not become such because they chose a certain lot or province, or were assigned one; rather, it is the other way around. Just as a soul, before entering a body, brings forth from itself a certain life and form into action, so it obtains a corresponding organic body and a nature following its own, which is subjected to the perfection of that soul's life. The rational soul <note>original: "anima rationalis"</note> enters into a body that is already alive, but only imperfectly so. Divine beings naturally generate within themselves their entire distinct work before they bring it forth. Among those things which hold the status of a universal principle, the lower things exist within the higher: corporeal things within the incorporeal, and the works within the creators. They are directed within those higher beings that contain them in a circular fashion. Therefore, the celestial circuits are reduced to the circuit of the celestial souls. Truly, the souls of the worlds—that is, of the spheres and the stars—as they approach their own intellect, are comprehended by it inwardly and are first generated within it. The particular intellect is contained in the universal intellect, and that in turn in higher things. Since subsequent things are always turned toward the first and higher things as their models, and the lower are led by them, certainly essence and form are produced by the higher and are present in the lower. In all cases, subsequent things are produced first within the better things. Therefore, order and measure in subsequent things depend on the higher, and not the other way around; thus, the properties of the higher are not to be assigned based on the lower. Therefore, an incorporeal essence cannot be distinguished by the qualities of bodies, with which it has nothing in common.

Divine principles grant to their works not only essence, but their entire distinction and formal order. An essence that is present to bodies non-locally is not distinguished by physical places; and that which is not restrained by the particular boundaries of subjects is by no means contained particularly by the parts of the world. An incorporeal nature is suddenly present wherever it wishes, with nothing anywhere prohibiting it.

Every divine and incorporeal thing is everywhere.

**P**LATO therefore (to begin with the prince of philosophers <note>The author refers to Plato as "their prince," reflecting the high status of Platonic thought in the Renaissance</note>), since he divided the soul into two parts—namely, the mind and the senses—attributed <term>gladness</term> <note>original: "læticia"</note> and <term>joy</term> <note>original: "gaudium"</note> to the mind, and <term>pleasure</term> <note>original: "uoluptas"</note> to the senses. But he believes these first two differ from each other because all "joy" is worthy of praise, whereas "gladness" is partly to be praised and partly to be blamed. For he says that gladness is a certain elevation of the mind in the possession of some good, which can either exceed or maintain moderation. Joy, however, is that very delight received from contemplation or some other practice of the virtues. And so, in his *Phaedrus* <note>One of Plato's famous dialogues concerning the soul and love</note>, when he speaks of that first and true life which the soul enjoys in its heavenly seat, he says it is nourished by the contemplation of truth and "rejoices." For this reason, he often calls joy the "sustenance of the mind." For he wishes truth itself to be like a field, and the contemplation of that truth to be like the crops. Then, the sustenance taken from these two is the joy by which the mind fully enjoys the contemplation of truth. Therefore, in that same *Phaedrus*, when he discusses those shadows into which the soul has been cast and submerged from the highest and true light, he says: "where there is great effort to see where the plain of truth is; for from there comes the suitable food for the soul, and the nature of the wings by which the soul is raised up is nourished by this." Next, the soul itself inquires whether it can easily obtain what it desires—a concern and anxiety we call **solicitude**. If the soul finds it is less able to achieve its goal, this very opinion is called **mistrust**, which is followed by **despair**. This occurs when the soul no longer expects to easily enjoy what it desires. From this arises **sorrow** <note>original: "tristitia"</note>; that is, a depression and casting down of a despairing and mistrusting soul. But if someone believes they will easily attain what they desire, this very faith is called **confidence**. **Hope** then follows confidence; that is, a firm and easy expectation of a future good. From this is born **gladness** <note>original: "laetitia"</note>, a certain lifting up and elation of a soul hoping that it will, without any doubt, possess what it has sought. Indeed, a certain confidence and a very firm hope—along with the intense contemplation of that very thing we hope for—represents the object of our quest to the soul in such a way that it is not far from appearing entirely present.

The result is that something very similar to pleasure is born from this hope and thought. I believe **Aristotle** called this **gladness** a certain image and appearance of future pleasure. **Lucretius** the Epicurean <note>Lucretius (c. 99–55 BC) was a Roman poet and philosopher who explained Epicurean physics and psychology in his poem *On the Nature of Things*.</note> called it a "drop of Venus" <note>original: "guttam ueneris"</note>, because it is a small and light perception of the beloved thing; whereas **pleasure** <note>original: "uoluptas"</note> is the perfect and absolute enjoyment of the desired thing (to speak in the manner of **Plato**). On the other hand, when the appearance of something that seems to be a future good is offered, the very opinion of it impels one to attain it (to speak in the Stoic manner). When this is done with constancy and prudence, we call such a desire the **will** <note>original: "uoluntatem"</note>; they believe this exists only in the wise person and should in no way be called a disorder. They define it thus: **will** is that which desires something in accordance with reason. Furthermore, when we are moved in such a way that we enjoy some good, it happens in two ways. We have already declared in a long series of arguments Aristotle’s judgment that the pleasure of the mind is superior to the sweetness of the body. There now remains another discussion by Aristotle against Eudoxus <note>A Greek astronomer and mathematician who argued that pleasure is the supreme good because all creatures seek it.</note> and the **Cyrenaics** <note>A school of philosophy founded by Aristippus of Cyrene, which taught that the pleasure of the moment, especially physical pleasure, is the only certain good.</note>, through which he demonstrates that the pleasure of the body is by no means the "highest good" <term>summum bonum (the ultimate goal or end of human existence)</term>.

Indeed, by the authority of all philosophers, the highest good must be of such a kind that there is nothing better beyond it, nor is it referred to anything else as a means to an end. However, the pleasure of the body is considerably inferior to the joy of the mind (as we showed a little while ago), and it is desired for the sake of the mind and the other things we have mentioned. Moreover, that which is the highest good in itself ought to always appear good and desirable to everyone, and be approved in all its parts. Yet, not every physical delight is sought always, nor is it sought by all people. For what often provides pleasure to one person frequently provides distress to another; what seems pleasant to children seems bothersome to men; and what we crave while we are sober, we later find nauseating to even look at once we have been filled. 

To this we must add the fact that such is the nature of the highest good that whatever we do, we choose it for its own sake, as if compelled by necessity. Yet there are many most laborious tasks and duties full of danger which—even if we know for certain they will bring us no physical pleasure—we do not hesitate to undertake for the sake of honor, virtue, or the joy of the mind. Indeed, many of the bravest and wisest men have committed themselves to certain death for the sake of what is honorable, for their country, or for their parents. If anyone should say they did this because they were ensnared by physical sweetness or inflamed by lust, or if a critic prefers the judgment of the common crowd and of beasts over the character of these men, he must surely be judged the most foolish of all.

Furthermore, anyone, even the most abject person, can enjoy physical pleasures no less than the best of men. But no one would say that "happiness" <term>felicitas (the state of flourishing or true well-being)</term> belongs to the abject. For the nature of goods is twofold: they both fill the mind and free it from all distress. This kind of fulfillment and liberation is, without a doubt, tranquility itself.

<margin>d</margin> Since this follows the possession of other goods, it follows that it should rightly be seen as their end and ultimate limit, and for that reason, the highest. For the mind never rests unless it has attained all goods. Now, rest and tranquility are one and the same. It follows, therefore, the possession of other goods. Thus, all goods seem to be sought for its sake, as if this were the ultimate and best end to be desired by all. For this reason, Epicurus judges that a happy life consists in the tranquility of the mind—specifically, in that most blessed tranquility which contains the <term>absence of pain<note>original: "indolentiam"; referring to the state of *aponia* (physical) and *ataraxia* (mental).</note></term> of both mind and body simultaneously. <margin>e</margin> But lest I seem to assert these things regarding Epicurus on my own authority, first...

# ON PLEASURE

That pleasure, which is the companion of motion, is not the highest good <note>original: "summum bonum"</note>, he himself testifies in his own words in his letter to Menoeceus <note>Epicurus’s *Letter to Menoeceus* is a foundational text of Epicurean ethics, focusing on how to achieve a happy life.</note>, saying: "Therefore, when we say that pleasure is the goal, we do not mean the pleasures of the dissolute, nor those found in the taste [of food], as some who are ignorant and disagree with our opinion think, but we mean the absence of pain in the body and being tranquil in the mind, and to be free from disturbance. For it is not banquets and revelries, nor the company of boys and women, nor the consumption of fish and other things offered by a costly table, that produce a sweet life; but rather sober reasoning, searching out the causes for why everything should be chosen or avoided, and driving out opinions through which the greatest turmoil occupies the soul." Likewise in the same letter: "The absence of pain and disturbance," he says, "are constant pleasures. But joy and gladness are said to be actions according to motion <note>The author distinguishes between "kinetic" (moving) pleasures, like eating, and "static" pleasures, like the state of being full or at peace.</note>. The highest pleasure is the privation of pain, and the pleasures of the mind are indeed greater than those of the body. For the flesh is bathed only in present sweetness, but the mind rejoices together in the thought of past, present, and future things." Likewise in the same letter: "One cannot live pleasantly unless one lives prudently, honorably, and justly; nor prudently, honorably, and justly, unless one lives pleasantly." From these things Epicurus shows that the highest good does not consist in pleasures according to motion, nor in those taken from shameful actions, but that these two—namely, <term>indolentia<note>Literally "painlessness." In this context, it refers to the Greek *aponia*, the absence of physical pain.</note></term> of the body and tranquility of the mind—produce the most blessed life. The Epicurean Lucretius also testifies to the same in the second book of *On the Nature of Things* <note>original: "De rerum natura"</note>. There, while he censures the empty desires and luxurious delights of men, he says:
