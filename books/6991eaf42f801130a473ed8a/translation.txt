# On the Movement of the Human Body

The soul, which governs the body, does not act without the instruments of the nerves and muscles <note>Leonardo often viewed the body as a complex machine where the soul acted as the primary mover</note>. Just as the weight of a clock is the cause of its movement, so the contraction of the muscles provides the force that moves the bones at their joints.

The entire space of the air that faces the eye is filled with infinite **images** <note>original: "similitudini"; these are the "visual species" or likenesses of objects that Leonardo believed traveled through the air</note> of the things placed in that air, all of which intersect through the pupil of said eye within that pupil. Thus, the forms of said air are all reduced into said pupil as if into a single point, and from said point they expand through the **crystalline humor** <note>the lens of the eye</note> and strike the surface of the **spherical humor** <note>the vitreous humor</note> and then pass to the **common sense** <note>term: "senso comune"; the central faculty of the brain where all senses were thought to meet and be processed</note>.

But here a doubt arises, and it is this: that if these images wish to penetrate through the pupil as far as the common sense—which said images intersect in said pupil—it follows that the images of things placed on the right would go to the left, and those on the left to the right. And likewise, the form of a man who stands upright would go to the common sense upside down. Therefore, reason teaches us that by necessity of said intersection, these images must be **re-inverted** <note>original: "risvolgere"; to turn back or flip again</note>, and this must be done by the concavity of the crystalline humor, which by its spherical shape re-inverts said images. And this reasoning shall be proven in its proper places with experience and with demonstrations.

The movement of the heart arises from nothing other than the lung, which, in cooling itself, draws air into itself, and in heating itself, expels it again. Therefore, this lung contracts due to the cooling and expands due to the heating; and by this movement, the ventricles <note>The chambers of the heart</note> of the heart come to open and close. Thus, the movement of the lung and that of the heart are one and the same thing, and the movement of the heart is for the same reason as that of the lung—that is, because of the coldness of the air that enters and the heat of the air that leaves the lung. ## On the Function of the Diaphragm

The diaphragm <term>diaphragm: the muscular partition separating the chest from the abdomen</term> is a powerful muscle that serves as the primary engine of respiration. When it contracts, it flattens and descends, thereby increasing the volume of the chest cavity and drawing air into the lungs. Conversely, when it relaxes, it rises and presses against the lungs, forcing the air out.

<margin>
Note how the liver and the spleen are moved by the action of the diaphragm; for when the diaphragm descends, it pushes these organs downward, and when it rises, they follow it back up.
</margin>

### The Relationship of the Heart to the Liver

The heart is placed above the liver, and between them lies the diaphragm. The heat of the heart warms the liver, and the liver, being the source of the blood <note>Following the Galenic tradition, Leonardo believed the liver was the site of blood production</note>, sends the blood to the heart to be vitalized. This heat is tempered by the air drawn in by the lungs, as previously stated.

## On the Function of the Diaphragm

The diaphragm <term>diaphragm: the muscular partition separating the chest from the abdomen</term> is a powerful muscle that serves as the primary engine of respiration. When it contracts, it flattens and descends, thereby increasing the volume of the chest cavity and drawing air into the lungs. Conversely, when it relaxes, it rises and presses against the lungs, forcing the air out.

<margin>
Note how the liver and the spleen are moved by the action of the diaphragm; for when the diaphragm descends, it pushes these organs downward, and when it rises, they follow it back up.
</margin>

### The Relationship of the Heart to the Liver

The heart is placed above the liver, and between them lies the diaphragm. The heat of the heart warms the liver, and the liver, being the source of the blood <note>Following the Galenic tradition, Leonardo believed the liver was the site of blood production</note>, sends the blood to the heart to be vitalized. This heat is tempered by the air drawn in by the lungs, as previously stated.

### Of the Balance and the Beam

If you place a weight of ten pounds at the end of a lever <term>lever: a rigid bar resting on a pivot, used to help move a heavy load with one end when pressure is applied to the other</term> that is four cubits <note>original: "braccia"; a traditional Italian measure of length, roughly the length of an arm</note> long, and you wish to balance it with a weight placed at only one cubit from the center of the movement <note>the fulcrum or pivot point</note>, you will find that the smaller distance requires a much greater weight. 

<margin>Proportion of weight to distance.</margin>

For as many times as the shorter arm of the balance is contained within the longer arm, so many times more must the weight of the shorter arm exceed the weight of the longer one to achieve equality. <insert>This is the fundamental rule of the steelyard.</insert> Therefore, if the four-cubit arm holds ten pounds, the one-cubit arm must hold forty pounds to remain in a state of rest. 

<column-break/>

### On the Nature of Heavy Bodies

Every heavy body desires to move toward the center of the world by the shortest path. <note>Leonardo is describing gravity before the formal laws of Newton were established.</note> If a weight is suspended by a cord, it will never come to rest until it is perfectly perpendicular to the center of the earth. 

**The movement of the weight** is always more powerful at the beginning of its fall than at the end, provided there is no resistance from the air. <gloss>The speed increases as it descends.</gloss> 

->All weight tends to fall, and all fall tends to follow the straightest line.<-

<summary>Leonardo explains the mathematical proportions of the lever and the behavior of falling weights, noting that balance is achieved when weight and distance are inversely proportional.</summary>
