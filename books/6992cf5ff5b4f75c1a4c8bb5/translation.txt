# <insert>Illustrated Treatise on the Maritime Kingdoms</insert> <note>original: 海國圖志 (Hǎiguó Túzhì)</note>

<term>Illustrated Treatise on the Maritime Kingdoms</term>: Compiled by the scholar <term>Wei Yuan</term> <note>1794–1857</note> during the <term>Qing Dynasty</term>, this is one of the most significant works of 19th-century Chinese geography. It was written following China's defeat in the First Opium War to help the "Middle Kingdom" understand the military technology and geography of the Western world.

<term>Wei Yuan</term>: A prominent Chinese scholar and government official who advocated for "learning the superior techniques of the barbarians to control them."

<term>Geography and Cartography</term>: This work combined traditional Chinese knowledge with Western maps and accounts to create a comprehensive guide to the world's oceans and nations.

<term>Title Label</term> <note>original: 題籤 (tíqiān)</note>: The vertical slip of paper pasted on the front cover of a traditional book, usually featuring calligraphy that identifies the work.

## Nations of Central India

According to the *Records of Things Seen and Heard in the Western Regions* <note>original: 西域聞見錄 (Xīyù Wénjiàn Lù). This was a 1777 geographical text written by a Manchu official named Qi-shi-yi, describing the lands and cultures of Central Asia and beyond.</note>, <term>Hindustan</term> <note>original: 溫都斯坦 (Wēndūsītǎn). Historically used in Chinese texts to refer to Northern India or the Mughal Empire.</note> is also one of the great Islamic nations of the <term>Western Regions</term> <note>A traditional Chinese term for the lands to the west of China, ranging from modern Xinjiang to Central Asia.</note>. Traveling southwest from <term>Yarkand</term> <note>original: 葉爾羌 (Yè’ěrqiāng). A major city in present-day Xinjiang that served as a key hub on the Silk Road.</note> by horse for over sixty days, one reaches <term>Kashmir</term> <note>original: 克食米爾 (Kèshímǐ’ěr).</note>. Traveling southwest again from Kashmir for over forty days, one reaches Hindustan.

Access by water is also possible; many traders between these two locations rely on boats, which travel back and forth without end. They call their king a <term>Khan</term>. The capital city is magnificent, with a circumference of over sixty *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance; sixty *li* is roughly 30 kilometers or 18 miles.</note>. It has jurisdiction over more than 370 large and small Islamic cities. 

The people there have deep-set eyes, high noses, and full beards. The pupils of their eyes are black and white and shine brilliantly like <term>lapis lazuli</term> <note>original: 琉璃 (liúlí). While often meaning glass or glaze, in ancient descriptions of eyes, it implies a clear, gem-like quality.</note>. Their faces are dark and their lips have a bluish tint. Their speech sounds like the chirping of birds; even other Muslims <note>original: 回子 (huízǐ). A historical term for practitioners of Islam, particularly those from Central Asia.</note> cannot distinguish the meaning of their words.

Their clothes are open at the front, and their heads are wrapped in floral cloth <note>This refers to the practice of wearing patterned turbans.</note>. The soil is black and fertile, but the land is extremely hot and humid, where **miasma** <note>original: 瘴癘 (zhànglì). A historical term for "swamp fever" or malaria-like illnesses thought to be caused by bad air in tropical climates.</note> causes much harm. Some people have tumors or growths on their heads or faces; these grow long when pulled and shrink back when released.

For plowing the land and pulling carts over long distances, they rely entirely on elephants. They have cattle and horses but lack camels, sheep, mules, and donkeys, as they do not understand the practices of nomadic herding. 

Non-glutinous and glutinous rice, as well as melons, fruits, and vegetables, are all cultivated in great abundance. Betel nut trees, <term>yuan-palms</term> <note>original: 杬榔 (yuánláng). Likely a variety of areca or fan palm used for its fruit or timber.</note>, palm trees, oranges, and pomelos form forests everywhere; their leaves do not wither even in winter. 

The people are skilled in crafts, producing gold lacquer and engravings of exquisite quality. The jade items they make are as thin as cicada wings, with patterns as fine as hair. They draw gold and silver into threads to weave silks, satins, felt, and cloth, which are sold throughout the nations of the <term>Western Regions</term> <note>Historically referring to Central Asia and the lands along the Silk Road.</note> and various Islamic cities. 

Their dwellings are excavated deep into the ground, several <term>zhang</term> <note>A traditional Chinese unit of length; one *zhang* is approximately 3.3 meters or 11 feet.</note> deep. They dig side-tunnels into the earth to create rooms, which are exceptionally refined and decorated with gold and jade. They never build houses or gardens on top of the soil. Consequently, their cities and villages appear from a distance like vast, empty stretches of land with no signs of human life. Outside the city walls lies a great marsh, where the mountains and waters are quite beautiful.

Flowers and trees grow in lush profusion. The inhabitants often take their families aboard boats, spending months or weeks at a time traveling upon the waters. They possess many fine liquors and place great value on banquets, often spending hundreds of pieces of gold on a single gathering. They also frequently load their wine onto boats to drift across the marshes. 

In this land, all affairs—both public and private—along with the labors and trades of farmers, artisans, merchants, and travelers, are conducted at night. When the sun rises, the people retire to rest. As the nation is wealthy and populous, their customs are quite extravagant. 

The region also contains mountains of jade. However, **silver** <note>original: 白金 (báijīn), literally "white gold." In this context, it refers to silver.</note> is uniquely scarce, and its value exceeds that of gold. They prize Chinese **porcelain** <note>original: 磁器 (cíqì).</note> above all else. Should anyone bring it to their country, the people compete to trade white jade plates and bowls for it before the traveler can depart, fearing they might miss the opportunity.

This is because for every disease or skin ulcer found in that land, a dose of rhubarb brings immediate recovery; it never fails. A term used historically in China to refer to various Muslim populations.</note> must possess at least half a **tael** <note>original: 兩 (liǎng). A traditional unit of weight, roughly 37.5 grams.</note>...

Alternatively, these goods are transported in bulk to Yarkand <note>An ancient oasis city in modern-day Xinjiang, a major hub on the Silk Road.</note> and from there find their way back into China. For instance, the **Muslims** <note>original: 回子 (huízǐ), a historical term used to describe the Hui or Turkic Muslim peoples of Central Asia.</note> of Kashgar once purchased a bolt of Zhangzhou velvet <note>A famous silk textile produced in Fujian province.</note> that still bore the "Tianshun" era brand <note>The Tianshun reign (1457–1464) of the Ming Dynasty. In the western corner of this kingdom lies a colossal marsh several thousand *li* in circumference. Within this marsh stands a mountain over a thousand *li* around, with ten thousand peaks soaring upward, piercing the very clouds and sky. Some claim this is the highest mountain in the world of men. It is named Mount **Qiangelimadandalasi** <note>original: 牽各里麻膽達喇斯 (Qiāngèlǐmǎdǎndálǎsī). This likely represents a phonetic transliteration of a local name for a peak in the Himalayas or the Hindu Kush.</note>. 

This mountain is the habitat of the **lion** <note>original: 獅子 (shīzi). While called a "lion," the physical description that follows—patterned like a tiger and reaching massive proportions—suggests a blend of travelers' tales and local mythology.</note>. When the autumn moon shines bright and clear, the lions often carry their cubs upon their backs, moving back and forth through the mountain passes. They have massive heads with curly manes, and their tails are shaped like brooms. Their bodies have a yellow ground with black markings, resembling the skin of a tiger, and they reach a length of sixty or seventy feet. From time to time, they scale the highest peaks and gaze at the moon, salivating as they roar and leap about. In a fierce frenzy, they fly upward as if to swallow the moon, sometimes leaping through the air for eight or nine *li* <note>About 4 to 4.5 kilometers.</note>.

Near the city are the tombs of ancient kings, which rise as high as tiered pagodas. At this time, the neighboring states are all at peace, and there has been no destruction from war; as a result, commerce is flourishing once again. In the thirteenth year of the **Daoguang** era <note>1833 AD</note>, this location was established as a capital.

Traveling north for more than two hundred *li* <note>A traditional Chinese unit of distance, roughly 500 meters or 1/3 of a mile</note>, one reaches **Yinggong**. Most of the people live in wooden plank houses; they do not lock their doors at night, and there are no thieves or violent brawls.

There are no punishments of execution or corporal flogging. It is truly a land of happiness within the South Seas.
