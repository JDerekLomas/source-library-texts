# To the Most Reverend Father in Christ:

## THE MOST ILLUSTRIOUS PRINCE, OTTO, HEREDITARY STEWARD <note>original Latin: Dapifero</note> OF THE HOLY ROMAN EMPIRE; BARON IN WALDBURG; CARDINAL OF THE HOLY ROMAN CHURCH, WITH THE TITLE OF ST. ALBINA, AND THE MOST WORTHY AND DISTINGUISHED BISHOP OF AUGSBURG.

### Glarean sends many greetings.

**T**imotheus of Miletus, a most famous author of the musical art, most Reverend FATHER, is recorded by Athenaeus in his 14th book (citing Artemon) as having been rebuked by the Spartans as a corruptor of ancient and primitive music, because he used a system that had more strings than the *Magadis* <note>The magadis was an ancient Greek stringed instrument; here it refers to the traditional limit of strings allowed in strict Spartan society.</note>. Furthermore, the Sainted Severinus <note>Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius (c. 477–524 AD), whose "Fundamentals of Music" was the standard textbook for a millennium.</note> asserts in his preface to music that Timotheus was even expelled from Laconia <note>The region of Greece where Sparta was located.</note> because he added one string to the customary ones and made music more effeminate. Boethius reports in Greek words the decree <term>Psephisma<gloss>A formal proposal or decree passed by an assembly.</gloss></term> made concerning him, which stated: for this reason the Spartans were angry with Timotheus of Miletus, because by rendering music "manifold" he obstructed the minds of the boys he had received to be educated, and hindered them from the modesty of virtue. This is what Boethius says of Timotheus from the Greek sources. 

If these things are as the authors worthy of trust have handed down, what do I think will become of me? I, who to the eight modes of musical song—celebrated among all for so many centuries—now add four more, and from an *Octochordon* <note>An eight-stringed work or system; Glarean refers to the traditional eight-mode system used in Gregorian chant.</note> make a *Dodecachordon* <note>A twelve-stringed work; Glarean's title for his theory of twelve modes.</note>. I seem to accuse all antiquity of ignorance and condemn it for negligence; indeed, I now—if it pleases the Gods, as the proverb goes—recall, restore to legal status, and resuscitate to life two modes rejected by all the ancients, hissed off the stage, relegated to exile, and as if dead. I would truly be worthy of being hissed at, cast out, and driven away, not just from one Spartan land, but from the entire world wherever rational men dwell. 

Unless I clear myself of these harsh objections and grave accusations, and show the reader that the matter is different than how I am accused, what remains but that, defeated, I yield my hands <note>An idiomatic expression meaning "to admit defeat" or "surrender."</note> and pay the penalty for my audacity? But truly, most venerable Father, since no one was ever condemned before a fair judge without his case being heard, I bid all those who wish me to be found false and to lose my case to have good hope. For I shall easily refute the arguments of the opposing side, if I first speak concerning that Spartan decree. Indeed, Athenaeus says in the same place I mentioned that Timotheus, when...

# Preface.

<meta>Catchword: the right of age</meta>

A certain Spartan, wishing to cut the redundant strings from a Lyre, pointed out that there was another among the Spartans who used a Lyre with the same number of strings; upon saying this, he was dismissed. Thus, since history is varied on this point and it is not sufficiently certain—though it matters much—whether authors are speaking of the strings of the Lyre or the Cithara <note>both are types of ancient Greek stringed instruments similar to harps</note> when they write that he was condemned; and since he even earned praise for adding strings to the Cithara, as he is proclaimed by all professors of this art to have made an eleven-stringed instrument <term>Hendecachordum</term> out of a nine-stringed one <term>Enneachordo</term> along with Hestiaeus of Colophon, this point may be deliberately omitted, and let us come to the matter itself. 

Those who cry out that we have introduced a "new" commentary on the **Twelve Modes** <note>original: "XII Modis"</note>—truly, they show themselves to have been only very lightly engaged in the reading of ancient authors. Indeed, they are content with the usage of these few centuries, in which eight or nine modes sufficed for the singing of the Psalms. But we must bring forward authors—few indeed, but those to whom credit is deservedly given. 

**Plato**, although he frequently mentions the Modes, calls them "Harmonies" <note>original: "Harmonías"</note>. Certainly, in the third book of the *Republic*, he names six principal Modes; if we give to each of these its own plagal <note>a "plagal" mode is a variation of a scale starting a fourth lower; original: "plagios"</note> version, who could deny that there are **twelve Modes**? From the same author, and from his no less learned disciple **Aristotle**, it is clear enough that in that era, knowledge of the Modes was in very frequent use among all the learned. We have not read **Aristoxenus** ourselves, but all admit he was the author of the twelve Modes, having even added a nomenclature for them, which we have determined should be followed in the second book of these Commentaries. I pass over here innumerable Greeks, some of whom have even been translated into Latin. 

Among the Roman writers, **Boëthius**—the true light of this art—established seven species of the *Diapason* <term>the octave</term>, from which twelve (or if you prefer, fourteen) Modes are easily gathered through arithmetic and harmonic division. For in more than one place, he admits that the eighth species is the same as the first, and was added by **Ptolemy** to complete the maximum system of the **double octave** <note>original: "δὶς διὰ πασῶν"</note>. Otherwise, there would be sixteen, but we, along with Aristoxenus, set them at twelve (excluding two "bastard" modes). **Martianus Capella** shows us fifteen, of which we shall speak in the proper place. The **Roman Church** uses nine intonations in the singing of the Psalms, but its chants nevertheless contain thirteen Modes, as we shall show in the same second book with examples taken from the Church Choir. **Georgius Valla** was satisfied to review everyone's opinion and pile up whatever he had read written anywhere about musicians. Thus, he himself has more than twelve. 

It is established, therefore, that this assertion of ours concerning the **Twelve Modes** is not a new thing, but a proper restoration of antiquity. But let the reader not believe this until I have shown the matter itself with firm arguments through mathematical ratios, and the precepts through the clearest examples—which we are fully persuaded we have done in the two subsequent books. Furthermore, I often tend to marvel that, while in our age no one uses the **Chromatic** or **Enharmonic** <note>two of the three "genera" of Greek music, involving semi-tones and quarter-tones</note>—two types of melody frequent among the ancients—nevertheless all...

# Preface.

<meta>Catchword: the right of age</meta>

...of each age <note>The page begins mid-sentence: "ius ætatis," likely completing "cuius ætatis," referring to musicians of every era</note> to repeatedly emphasize, and almost more anxiously give instructions about them, than about the **Diatonic** <term>the standard musical scale consisting of five whole steps and two half steps</term>, which is the only one we use now. As far as possible, we have been careful of this in these books, and once the Elements of this art were simply delivered in one book, we attached the Modes themselves—for whose sake we undertook this troublesome labor—along with examples, in the two following volumes. But it is more pleasing to marvel at certain people who write professionally about Music, who, as often as they proclaim from **Apuleius** <note>a 2nd-century Roman philosopher and rhetorician</note> the simplicity of the Aeolian and the variety of the Iastian, or from **Lucian** <note>a Hellenistic satirist and rhetorician</note> the **divine nature of the Phrygian, the Bacchic spirit of the Lydian, the solemnity of the Dorian, and the elegance of the Ionian** <note>original: "τῆς φρυγίη τὸ ἔνθεον: τῆς λυδίης, τὸ βακχικόν: τῆς Δωρίου, τὸ σεμνόν. τῆς ἰωνικῆς, τὸ γλαφυρόν"</note>; yet when they are asked to distinguish the Aeolian from the Dorian, or the Ionian from the Lydian, and to show this with some example of Song, they respond with nothing more **than if they were hard flint, or stood as a Marpessian crag** <note>original: "Quàm ſi dura ſilex, aut ſtet Marpeſia cautes"; a quote from Vergil's Aeneid, Book VI, comparing their silence to unmoving stone</note>, but instead they refer the matter back to the singers. These people indeed seem to me very much like those who, having praised some healthy herb, but being asked to point it out, either answer nothing or send the asker to the Pharmacists—being themselves more unlearned than those who asked them. It pertains to the same point that, when I solicited even the **Princes of Letters** <note>likely referring to the leading scholars and humanists of the time, such as Erasmus</note> in our age concerning this matter—not so much obscure as nearly despaired of—they mostly replied that it was not of their profession.

But enough of these things now. I return to the business at hand. I have dared, I confess, Most August Father, a thing which when I first began (for I have been **turning this stone for no less than twenty years** <note>original: "XX annis hoc ſaxum uorſo"; an allusion to the myth of Sisyphus, indicating a long and laborious task</note>), I plainly laughed at my own attempt; and yet, as it happens, I was lured by the hope of obtaining my goal, moved especially by that saying of the Comic poet: **Nothing is so difficult that it cannot be investigated by searching** <note>original: "Nihil tàm difficile, quin quærendo inueſtigari poſsiet"; a quote from the Roman playwright Terence</note>. Having often called upon Christ, that He might prosper a work begun for His glory, I sought help from Him alone, hoping for a reward from Him alone: for from men one can almost expect nothing but slander, and that for any slight reason. For to some we shall not seem polished enough, as if indeed we were teaching Rhetoric here; by others I will undoubtedly be blamed for being too long; by some, on the contrary, too concise. But who can please everyone? I judge that man to speak brilliantly enough, in whatever art is to be delivered, who teaches the matter itself, as it is, naturally, candidly, and sincerely, abstaining from painted and deceptive speech, and who desires to teach more than to seem learned. Nor, however, would I wish to seem to have commended one who speaks in a filthy or barbaric manner, who stains the art with foul stammering. But we shall speak more copiously of these things elsewhere. 

In the defense of this work, this seemed worthy of warning: for no small difficulty was thrust upon us by the fact that **Ecclesiastical chants** <term>the traditional plainchant and liturgical music of the Church</term> are today wonderfully varied across different nations, and moreover changed by dioceses and orders, and finally, badly corrupted by many. But unless I am mistaken, the **Order of Preachers** <note>the Dominicans</note> has a purer chant than some other orders—I speak of that which pertains to the essentials of the Mass; we have mostly followed that in the second book, although I do not follow it everywhere. There is a monastery of the **Benedictine profession** at the head of the **Black Forest** <note>original: "Hercyniæ ſyluæ"</note> (they call it **St. George’s**), where another source of the **Danube** rises nearby...

# Preface.

...is called "Prygen" <note>This refers to the "Baar" region or specifically the area around the St. George Abbey in the Black Forest</note>; it seems Herodotus called it Pyrene. Its site is located between Freiburg in the land of Breisgau and Rottweil and Villingen, two magnificent towns of the **Hercynian Forest** <note>The Black Forest</note>, yet it is closer to Rottweil and Villingen than to Freiburg. Over that monastery the Reverend Father and Lord, Lord Johann Kern, presided as Abbot in my time. With him was a codex which contained various treatises on all the sciences—what the Greeks call an **encyclopedia** <note>original: *ἐγκυκλοπαιδίαν* (*enkyklopaedian*)</note>. 

In it were five volumes by Saint Severinus <note>Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius, the most influential musical authority of the Middle Ages</note> on Music; furthermore, certain works of Guido of Arezzo, Berno, Wilhelm, Otto, Bishop Theoger, and John, who was later the Supreme Pontiff, the twenty-second of that name <note>Pope John XXII, who wrote on ecclesiastical music</note>. Through those books I was made bolder—especially through the works of Boethius, which had previously been incomplete in my possession but were there somewhat cleansed of errors. I cannot deny this, even if I found the full nomenclature of the Modes in none of them. Therefore, it was necessary to consult the Greeks besides. 

Nor was that undeserved, since we ought to credit them for whatever disciplines exist anywhere. And among the other disciplines, this one [music] is entirely Greek, having all its themes and all technical terms in Greek words—except that the Latins later approached it by a more concise path and made it easier with much shorter words and technical terms, as must be shown by us in the work itself. 

Therefore, I submit these things to you, Most August **FATHER**, together with the most reverend assembly of Cardinals, as the moderators of all ecclesiastical law, to be examined. I have truly undertaken this labor for the praise of **CHRIST**. Let those, therefore, to whom the flock of **CHRIST** has been committed, judge it. As far as I am concerned, I trust I have handled this business in such a way that all fair-minded judges will perceive that I have wished to consult Christian piety and ecclesiastical dignity from my heart. If this affection does not achieve its desires, certainly the will to increase our holy religion was praiseworthy. But enough words; the matter itself will speak.

->Farewell, at Freiburg im Breisgau. In the year from the birth of Christ, 1547.<-

# Heinrich Loriti of Glarus, a Swiss Nobleman
## Dodecachordon, Book I.
### Chapter I. On the division and definition of Music.

<image-desc>A large woodcut historiated initial letter 'M' at the beginning of the text. Within the body of the 'M', two men dressed in scholar's robes are in a room; one plays a lute and the other looks on. A window shows a building and trees in the distance.</image-desc>

**Music** is of two kinds: theoretical and practical. Theoretical music is concerned with the contemplation of musical matters. According to the author Boethius <note>Annius Manlius Severinus Boethius, a 6th-century philosopher whose "Fundamentals of Music" was the standard textbook for a millennium</note> (Book 1, Chapter 2), this is threefold: First, Worldly <note>original: "Mundana"</note>, which considers the harmony of the entire universe and the parts of the world. Second, Human <note>original: "Humana"</note>, which treats the proportions of the body and the soul, and how these parts relate to each other. Third, that which is said to consist in certain instruments <note>original: "Instrumentalis"</note>; the same author discusses this in five books. Practical music consists of the performance of song. This is found in rhythms, in meters, and in sounds. And sounds are found partly in instruments (which are themselves varied) and partly in the human voice, which we will now attempt to discuss most specifically—namely, concerning "regulated" music, which pertains to song. Furthermore, song is of two kinds: one is simple and uniform, which is now commonly used in churches, and this is the subject of plainchant, which they call Gregorian; the other is varied and multiform, which some now call figured music <note>original: "figuralem"</note> and others call mensural music <note>referring to music with precise rhythmic notation, as opposed to the free rhythm of chant</note>. Since nothing certain concerning this [mensural] song is found among the ancients, as far as I know, we shall follow the tradition of the authors of our own age in later chapters.

->Definition of Theoretical Music<-

> **Music** is the faculty of weighing the differences between high and low sounds by means of sense and reason. **Boethius, Book 5, Chapter 1.**

->Definition of Practical Music<-

> **Music** is the science of correct melody-making <term>modulandi</term>. **Saint Augustine.**

# On the elements of Practice
## Chapter II.

Since all mathematical learning consists in demonstration, and because neither the things themselves can enter into a debate nor can sounds <note>original: "voces"</note> be written down, musicians invented signs for sounds. These consist partly of figures, which they now call notes, and partly of the naming of syllables, of which six are received by common use: **Ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la.** They now call these "voices" <note>original: "voces"</note> with a more frequent word, using the sign for the thing signified. However, they call the locations of these voices "keys" <note>original: "clauies"; these are the positions on the musical staff or the hexachord system</note>.

# Dodecachordon
<meta>Catchword: worm</meta>

...sounds are distinguished by **lines** and **spaces** in songs. Although the parallel lines are drawn at equal distances to the eye, the voices themselves are not all separated by an equal interval <note>In musical notation, the physical distance between lines doesn't change, but the musical "distance" (pitch interval) between notes can be either a whole tone or a half-step (semitone).</note>, as will be demonstrated more extensively later when we discuss intervals.

Ancient musicians called voices *phthongos* <note>original: "phthongos," a Greek term for a musical sound of a definite pitch</note> and called keys *strings*. However, Guido of Arezzo <note>A famous 11th-century monk and music theorist credited with inventing the musical staff and the "ut-re-mi" system</note>—a man of exceptional learning whom our age still follows—arranged these keys into an order like a ladder <term>scala</term>. He placed the voice "ut" on the lowest parallel line, marking it with the third letter of the Greek alphabet, **Gamma (Γ)**. He did this so that we would not be unmindful that this discipline, like all others, came from the Greeks.

Next, in the space above the first parallel line, he placed the voice "re," preceded by the letter **A**. Then, on the second line, he placed the voice "mi" with the letter **B** written before it. Some believe this "B" should be drawn with a square body so that the right side descends below the base and the left side rises above the top, in this form: **♮** <note>This is the origin of our modern "natural" sign, used to indicate B-natural, which was called "hard B" because of its square shape.</note>. Franchino Gaffurio <note>original: "Franchinus Laudenis," a contemporary of Glarean and a major music theorist of the Italian Renaissance</note>, the leader in this art in our time, taught this. He was moved by the reason that there should be a distinction between the two voices "mi" and "fa" at the eighth key, **b-flat** and **b-natural** <note>original: "b fa ♮ mi"</note>.

Furthermore, in the space above the second line, Guido placed two voices, "fa" and "ut," marked with the letter **C**. This begins a new order of six voices <term>hexachord</term> to take over where the previous order ran out while ascending. It does not start at the very end of the previous order, but in the middle, so that voices that are similar by nature are placed in the same key. Our musicians agree there is a certain natural similarity every fourth voice: "ut" with "fa," "re" with "sol," and "mi" with "la." However, I believe the reason for this division was to ensure the semitone <note>the smallest interval in this system</note> always occurred in the third position, between "mi" and "fa," following the form of the **diatonic genus** <note>The standard musical scale of Western music, consisting of whole tones and half-steps.</note>, which we will treat later.

I do not know on whose authority they teach that "ut" and "fa" are "soft" voices, "re" and "sol" are "natural," and "mi" and "la" are "hard," unless we prefer to look at common usage rather than the art itself. For what song is more tearful and "soft" than one where **mi** reigns? For example, some have composed harmonies for the *Lamentations of Jeremiah* and the complaint of the Holy Magdalene at the tomb of the Lord: "They have taken away my Lord" <note>original: "Tulerunt dominum meum"</note>. ## Chapter III. What beginners of this art should consider in the Guidonian model.

First, it should be known that it was not done without reason that letters are placed at the beginning of the keys, for this is very useful to those learning. There are seven keys distinct by nature, marked with the seven letters **a b c d e f g**. Through repetition, however, twenty are formed, drawn by musicians with this distinction: the first seven after **Γ (Gamma)**-ut are in capital forms; the next seven are in small letters. Furthermore, the final five are doubled. From this we say "Great" or "Grave" **A**, likewise "Small" or "Acute" **a**, "Double" **Aa**, and in the same way for the remaining keys.

Second, the distances between the keys must be considered. For a key is a **second** from the one nearest to it; a **third** from the next; a **fourth** from the one after that, and so on. Generally, letters of the same species are separated from their nearest neighbor by an **octave**. For example, **Γ**-ut is an octave from **G**-sol-re-ut; **A**-re from **a**-la-mi-re, and the rest should be estimated in the same way. Here a rule should be noted: the judgment regarding octaves is the same, understood not only concerning the voices but no less concerning the nature of the song. For whatever voices are in **G**-sol-re-ut can also be correctly sung in **Γ**-ut; and those in **a**-la-mi-re, the same in **A**-re; those in **b**-fa-♮-mi, the same in **♮**-mi, and the highest notes should be judged similarly.

Third, it should be known that this arrangement of keys could be extended to infinity, provided the law hinted at in the aforementioned rule is observed. Nevertheless, it was necessary that there be a beginning somewhere, and likewise an end. Let this be an example: if some note, by chance (as often happens), is placed below **Γ**-ut, you ask what should be sung there? I say, one must look at the key an octave from it. Just as below **G**-sol-re-ut is **F**-fa-ut, so below **Γ**-ut will be **f**-fa-ut <note>meaning a low F below the standard scale</note>. In a similar way, if a note occurs beyond **ee**-la, it also comes to be judged by the logic of the octave key. The human voice, however, does not go beyond these limits. Nonetheless, four-voice songs and musical instruments themselves frequently exceed this arrangement.

Nor would anyone correctly call those voices "fictitious" <term>musica ficta</term> which are found in the octaves; rather, you would call those "fictitious" which are contained neither by these keys (where license is usurped) nor by their octaves. For example: **mi** in **F**-fa-ut; **sol** in **E**-la-mi; **fa** in **a**-mi-re, etc. <note>These are notes that require accidentals not found in the standard Guidonian hand.</note> Finally, one must become accustomed to the keys so that we may commit them sharply to memory, knowing even without preparation which voices each key holds, lest we sing **mi** where **fa** must be sung, or vice versa. The rest will be easily learned through practice.

There are seven keys distinct by nature, marked with the seven letters **a b c d e f g**. Through repetition, however, twenty are formed, drawn by musicians with this distinction: the first seven after **Γ** <note>Gamma</note>-**ut** are in capital forms; the next seven are in small letters. Furthermore, the final five are doubled. From this we say "Great" or "Grave" **A**, likewise "Small" or "Acute" **a**, "Double" **aa**, and in the same way for the remaining keys. 

Second, the distances between the keys must be considered. For a key is a **second** from the one nearest to it; a **third** from the next; a **fourth** from the one after that, and so on. Generally, letters of the same species are separated from their nearest neighbor by an **octave**. For example, **Γ-ut** is an octave from **G-sol-re-ut**; **A-re** from **a-la-mi-re**, and the rest should be estimated in the same way. Here a rule should be noted: the judgment regarding octaves is the same, understood not only concerning the voices <note>the solmization syllables</note> but no less concerning the nature of the song. For whatever voices are in **G-sol-re-ut** can also be correctly sung in **Γ-ut**; and those in **a-la-mi-re**, the same in **A-re**; those in **b-fa-♮-mi**, the same in **♮-mi**, and the highest notes should be judged similarly. 

Third, it should be known that this arrangement of keys could be extended to infinity, provided the law hinted at in the aforementioned rule is observed. Nevertheless, it was necessary that there be a beginning somewhere, and likewise an end. Let this be an example: if some note, by chance (as often happens), is placed below **Γ-ut**, you ask what should be sung there? I say, one must look at the key an octave from it. Just as below **G-sol-re-ut** is **F-fa-ut**, so below **Γ-ut** will be **f-fa-ut** <note>referring to the pitch an octave below Gamma</note>. In a similar way, if a note occurs beyond **ee-la**, it also comes to be judged by the logic of the octave key. The human voice, however, does not go beyond these limits. Nevertheless, four-voice songs and musical instruments themselves frequently exceed this arrangement. 

Nor would anyone correctly call those voices "fictitious" <term>musica ficta</term> which are found in the octaves; rather, you would call those "fictitious" which are contained neither by these keys (where license is usurped) nor by their octaves. For example: **mi** in **F-fa-ut**; **sol** in **E-la-mi**; **fa** in **a-mi-re**, etc. <note>These are "accidental" notes that fall outside the standard hexachord system.</note> Finally, one must become accustomed to the keys so that we may commit them sharply to memory, knowing even without preparation which voices each key holds, lest we sing **mi** where **fa** must be sung, or vice versa. The rest will be easily learned through practice.

The first tetrachord begins after the **added note** and is the tetrachord **of the principals** <note>original Greek: τῶν ὑπατῶν (ton hypaton)</note>, that is, of the first or principal strings. These are the four: **the principal of the principals**, **the next-to-principal of the principals**, **the index-finger of the principals**, and **the principal of the middles** <note>In the Greek system: Hypate hypaton, Parhypate hypaton, Lichanos hypaton, and Hypate meson.</note>. In Guido’s scale, these are **B, C, D, E**. 

The second tetrachord is the one **of the middles** <note>original Greek: τῶν μεσῶν (ton meson)</note>, which is connected to the first because they share a common string <note>The note E acts as the end of the first tetrachord and the start of the second.</note>. Its four strings are: **the principal of the middles**, **the next-to-principal of the middles**, **the index-finger of the middles**, and **the middle note**. In Guido's system: **E, F, G, a**. 

The third is the one **of the connected notes** <note>original Greek: τῶν συνημμένων (ton synemmenon)</note>; it has these four strings: **the middle**, **the third of the connected**, **the next-to-last of the connected**, and **the last of the connected**. In Guido’s system: **a, b-flat, c, d**. 

The fourth is the one **of the disjunct notes** <note>original Greek: τῶν διεζευγμένων (ton diezeugmenon)</note>, for this is not joined with the previous ones, but is separated from the **middle note** <term>mese</term> by a whole tone. Its strings are: **the near-middle**, **the third of the disjunct**, **the next-to-last of the disjunct**, and **the last of the disjunct**. In Guido’s system: **B-natural, c, d, e**. 

The fifth is the one **of the excellent notes** <note>original Greek: τῶν ὑπερβολαίων (ton hyperbolaion)</note>, having a common string with the preceding tetrachord. Its strings are: **the last of the disjunct**, **the third of the excellent**, **the next-to-last of the excellent**, and **the last of the excellent**. In Guido’s system: **e, f, g, aa**. 

Indeed, the third tetrachord is called "connected" <term>synemmenon</term> because it is joined with the **middle note** <term>mese</term>. The name has been corrupted among authors, such as in <term>Vitruvius</term> <note>A Roman architect and engineer (c. 80–15 BC) who wrote on the acoustics of theaters.</note> Book 5, Chapter 4, where "synemmenon" is read incorrectly in common manuscripts. Conversely, the "disjunct" <term>diezeugmenon</term> is so named because it is separated from the middle note by a tone, as we said. <term>Aulus Gellius</term> <note>A 2nd-century Roman author.</note> also interprets these in the same way in Book 16, Chapter 8. Musicians called the **last note** <term>nete</term> <note>original Greek: νήτη</note> (from *neate*) the extreme and highest string of the tetrachord. The **next-to-last** <term>paranete</term> is so called because it is next to the last. The **third** <term>trite</term> is the third from the last. Finally, the **middle** <term>mese</term>, and the **near-middle** <term>paramese</term>. The **index-finger note** <term>lichanos</term> is the "distinguishing" note, a name which <term>Suidas</term> <note>The author of a massive 10th-century Greek Byzantine encyclopedia.</note> believes is derived from the word "to speak" or "to point." 

Since two connected tetrachords do not fill an octave, a tone was added below the two lowest tetrachords (the "middles" and "principals")—namely, from the principal of the principals to the **added note** <term>proslambanomene</term>. Likewise, another tone was added at the two highest tetrachords ("excellent" and "disjunct") from the near-middle to the middle; thus, on both sides, the **octave system** <term>diapason</term> is completed. When these are joined together, they constitute the **double octave** <term>diadiapason</term> from the added note to the last of the excellent—the greatest system in music. 

Furthermore, in Guido's scale, the numbers placed in the middle explain the ratios of the strings. For the **added note** has a duple

Therefore, the ratio from the added note to the highest note of the excellent is **quadruple** <note>A 4:1 ratio, representing two octaves.</note>. <term>Severinus</term> himself uses such large numbers because of the **schismas** <note>Extremely small musical intervals, smaller than a comma.</note> and parts of the **comma** <note>The small discrepancy in pitch resulting from tuning by perfect fifths versus octaves.</note>, which we will discuss more broadly in later sections when we treat the division of the whole tone. 

Otherwise, I am not unaware that this order of strings established by Guido seems to many to be inverted and entirely contrary to the natural course of the heavens, from which this formula was taken. This is because the higher bodies of the celestial orbs are said to produce a deeper sound, as they are larger <note>This refers to the "Music of the Spheres," where planets were thought to produce pitches based on their size or distance.</note>. Saint Severinus also seems to have observed this everywhere in his demonstrations. To which we could briefly respond that others have seen it differently. For example, that the higher celestial bodies produce a *higher* sound because they move more swiftly; this was the reasoning (as the same Severinus relates in Book 1, Chapter 27) of **Marcus Tullius Cicero** in the sixth book of *On the Republic*.

The first tetrachord begins after the **added note** and is the tetrachord **of the principals** <note>original Greek: τῶν ὑπατῶν (tōn hypatōn)</note>, that is, of the first or principal strings. These are the four: **the principal of the principals**, **the next-to-principal of the principals**, **the index-finger of the principals**, and **the principal of the middles** <note>In the Greek system: Hypate hypaton, Parhypate hypaton, Lichanos hypaton, and Hypate meson.</note>. The second tetrachord is the one **of the middles** <note>original Greek: τῶν μεσῶν (tōn mesōn)</note>, which is connected to the first because they share a common string <note>The note E acts as the end of the first tetrachord and the start of the second.</note>. Its four strings are: **the principal of the middles**, **the next-to-principal of the middles**, **the index-finger of the middles**, and **the middle note**. In Guido's system: **E, F, G, a**.

The third is the one **of the connected notes** <note>original Greek: τῶν συνημμένων (tōn synēmmenōn)</note>; it has these four strings: **the middle**, **the third of the connected**, **the next-to-last of the connected**, and **the last of the connected**. In Guido’s system: **a, b-flat, c, d**.

The fourth is the one **of the disjunct notes** <note>original Greek: τῶν διεζευγμένων (tōn diezeugmenōn)</note>, for this is not joined with the previous ones, but is separated from the **middle note** <term>mese</term> by a whole tone. Its strings are: **the near-middle**, **the third of the disjunct**, **the next-to-last of the disjunct**, and **the last of the disjunct**. In Guido’s system: **B-natural, c, d, e**.

The fifth is the one **of the excellent notes** <note>original Greek: τῶν ὑπερβολαίων (tōn hyperbolaiōn)</note>, having a common string with the preceding tetrachord. Its strings are: **the last of the disjunct**, **the third of the excellent**, **the next-to-last of the excellent**, and **the last of the excellent**. In Guido’s system: **e, f, g, aa**.

Indeed, the third tetrachord is called "connected" <term>synemmenon</term> because it is joined with the **middle note** <term>mese</term>, <gloss>from the Greek "to be joined together."</gloss> The name has been corrupted among authors, such as in <term>Vitruvius</term> <note>A Roman architect (c. 80–15 BC) who wrote on the acoustics of theaters.</note> Book 5, Chapter 4, where "synemmenon" <note>original Latin/Greek: συνέμμενον</note> is read incorrectly in common manuscripts for "synēmmenon" <note>original: συνημμένον</note>. Finally, the **middle** <term>mese</term>, and the **near-middle** <term>paramese</term>. # On the transposition of signed keys,
->Chapter VII.<-

The custom has remained among musicians of this age to notate a song on no more than five lines, and quite often on four. Yet a song sometimes exceeds these limits. Therefore, **transposition** <term>transpositio</term> via **keys** <note>original: *claues*. Here meaning "clefs," like the C-clef or F-clef.</note> was invented. These clefs are moved on the lines, and as they yield their position, the notes vary along with them. In this matter, the only thing to observe is where the previous note stood in relation to the clef, and where the subsequent one stands. Then, by making a comparison, the distance will be easily recognized. For example, if the first note was in **F-fa-ut** and the second in **c-sol-fa-ut**, it is certain that they are a fifth apart. Those who transpose the clefs at the margins <note>Changing the clef at the beginning of a new line of music.</note> act more cautiously whenever possible, for in this way the song is run through without the singer making an error. There is also a rule of our fellow countrymen <note>Likely referring to Swiss or German music theorists.</note> for this matter which is not useless, if it does not confuse the student. How much...

The **clef** <note>original: *clauis*</note> (they say) ascends, just as much as the note descends. And conversely, as much as the clef descends, the note ascends. This they plainly understand regarding the harmony or the sound, not the figures <note>i.e., the physical shapes of the notes on the page</note>; that is, the note ought to be sung that much higher or lower. Furthermore, this practice is now almost absent among our countrymen, for they prefer either to place a line below or add one above <note>This refers to the development of ledger lines.</note> rather than to perform a transposition. Furthermore, it was arranged quite elegantly for four voices by **Jean Mouton** <note>A celebrated French Renaissance composer (c. 1459–1522).</note>, a Frenchman and an excellent **composer** <term>Symphoneta</term> of our age; it is presented here by us in a simple [monophonic] version for the progress and exercise of beginners, just as it was first published. However, it has been moved from the ancient place of the Ionian, which was C, by a fourth; for its proper seat was C.
