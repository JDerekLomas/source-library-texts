# TO THE MOST ILLUSTRIOUS AND MOST CHRISTIAN
## PRINCE AND LORD, LORD JOACHIM, ELECTOR OF THE HOLY
## ROMAN EMPIRE, MARGRAVE OF BRANDENBURG,
## Duke of Stettin, Pomerania, the Cassubians, and the Slavs, Burgrave
## of Nuremberg, and Prince of Rügen, his most clement
## Lord, LEONHART
## Fuchs, Physician, sends his greatest greetings.

<margin>That herbal medicine has always obtained much admiration and praise.</margin>
ALTHOUGH all of medicine has been considered glorious and divine in every age, Most Illustrious and most noble PRINCE, nevertheless, in this its highest splendor, that part of it which investigates and explores the natures of plants has always obtained more praise, admiration, and weight. This is partly because it is the most ancient by virtue of its age—which has always held the greatest authority and veneration among all people—and partly because the knowledge of it is pleasant, useful, and necessary.

<margin>The divine origin of plants.</margin>
For while almost all other arts were discovered as soon as man was created by God the Best and Greatest, and were subsequently increased by the industry of many, plants alone—immediately after the elements were established and while no human yet existed—burst forth from the hiding places of the earth together with their divine powers, at God's command. So for this reason, the discovery of no thing or art is more truly assigned to God than that of medicine and plants. But if anyone should impudently dare to object to what we have asserted, we shall oppose him with Moses, the most ancient witness of all who have written (for you will find no other writer before him, neither among the Chaldeans, nor the Egyptians, nor the Phoenicians, nor the Greeks, which are nonetheless the older nations), who openly affirms in his book of Genesis what we have said.

<margin>Moses, the most ancient writer of all.</margin>
Nor is there any reason why anyone should insolently and insultingly reject or despise his testimony, as he is one whom not only our people, but even all secular writers have always admired and extolled with the highest praises; whom antiquity itself makes venerable and truthful.

<margin>The authority of antiquity.</margin>
Indeed, this antiquity itself, as Cicero witnesses, comes closest to the gods; therefore, the closer it is to the beginning and the divine progeny, the better it retains those things which are true. Hence, not rashly have as many men as ever existed constantly asserted that faith should be given to venerable antiquity. Furthermore, not only Moses, but also as many poets and historians as followed him, being refined by Egyptian learning, attributed the origin and discovery of herbs to the gods.

<margin>Homer and Hesiod, the most ancient writers of the Greeks.</margin>
For the most ancient poets of the Greeks, Homer and Hesiod (for the Greeks have nothing they can cite before them), openly testify that the origin of plants is divine. The latter <note>Hesiod</note> indeed relates that men are ignorant of how much is hidden in Mallow and Asphodel. For by those words, he implies quite obscurely that the powers of herbs are hidden in their innermost marrow and are unknown to men, and perceived only by the gods themselves, who are their discoverers. The former <note>Homer</note> truly records that the discovery of the highly praised herb *Moly* <note>A mythical herb mentioned in the Odyssey</note>—wonderfully effective against the sorceries of Circe—is credited to Mercury, the messenger of the gods, and writes that it is difficult for men to find, but easy for the gods. Moreover, in his *Theriaca*, Nicander recorded that *Panaces* <note>A legendary universal remedy or "panacea"</note>—which by its very name promises remedies for all diseases—was discovered by the gods themselves, and for this reason was called *Chironium*, *Asclepium*, and *Herculeum* <note>Named after the centaur Chiron, the god Asclepius, and the hero Hercules</note>. What the Greek poets asserted specifically and about almost every individual kind, Ovid—whom alone I have now brought forward as a witness for many—wished to affirm universally about all of them among the Latins, when he imagines Apollo speaking of himself in this way, saying:

> Medicine is my discovery, and I am called
> its worker throughout the world, and the power
> of herbs is subject to us.

Therefore, I think it is now clear to everyone that if we look at antiquity and divine origin, nothing in herbal medicine is anywhere more praiseworthy, more splendid, or more honorable. Because—

<margin>The utility of herbal medicine.</margin>
If you truly weigh in your mind the magnitude of the usefulness that this science brings to mankind, I do not see what could be more excellent. For by no other means do we more effectively preserve, protect, and sustain good bodily health than by the aid of plants of every kind. Moreover, nothing exists that is better, more excellent, or more desirable than good health, since without it neither power, nor riches, nor any other pleasant things can be of use; as was famously said by our Hippocrates: <note>original: "οὐδὲν ὄφελός ἐστιν οὔτε χρημάτων, οὔτε σώματος, οὔτε τ' ἄλλων οὐδενὸς, ἄτερ τῆς ὑγιείας"</note> *There is no benefit in riches, nor in the body, nor in anything else, without health.* Nor do we only protect our present health by the benefit of herbs, but we also correct, amend, and restore health when it has been weakened or shattered by these very same means. For just as bees collect material from the flowers of plants from which they fashion honey, so do we gather remedies against diseases from the immense variety of those same plants.

<margin>The necessity of herbal medicine.</margin>
Furthermore, since there are almost innumerable diseases that lay snares for us from the front, the back, and the sides, I think it will be obscure to no one, but rather agreed upon by all, that an exquisite knowledge of plants is also supremely necessary. For it will not be possible for us to drive away diseases without their divine powers.

<margin>The delight of knowing plants.</margin>
Truly, there is no reason for me to explain at length how much joy and delight the knowledge of plants possesses, since there is no one who does not know that nothing in this life is more pleasant or more delightful than to wander through forests, mountains, and fields, wreathed and adorned with various and most elegant little flowers and herbs, and to gaze upon them with intent eyes. Indeed, that pleasure and joy increase not a little if a knowledge of their properties and powers is added. For there is no less sweetness and delight in knowing them than in looking at them. Therefore, when those ancients perceived that the origin of plants was divine, and that in such great fragility of human life they not only possessed immense use and fruit, but were also supremely necessary, they began not only to think honorably of this part of medicine, but they even considered that there was nothing in nature itself more worthy of admiration, or more beautiful to investigate and scrutinize, or more pleasant to know. Consequently, they applied themselves entirely, in both mind and body, to the inquiry and investigation of the same. And this zeal for tracking down plants was held not only by common people and those of lowly lot, but also by men from every class who were outstanding in power, riches, wisdom, and learning. For the more famous they were for nobility of birth, and the more excellent in wisdom and learning—and thus able to judge more incorruptibly concerning this part of medicine—the more their minds were excited and inflamed to pursue this study.

<margin>Kings of old were wonderfully occupied with the study of scrutinizing plants.</margin>
This is why many Kings, excelling in power and wisdom, meanwhile neglecting the honor of royal pomp, did not find it burdensome to go with their entire royal retinue through the vast solitudes of many regions, to wander over the pathless ridges of mountains, to survey inaccessible lakes, to scrutinize the hidden fibers of the earth, and the gaping caves of valleys. They were invited to do this not so much by the immense joy and pleasure which they then enjoyed when they visited various places adorned with various flowers and herbs, as they were driven by a certain ardor to deserve the best from the human race, and thus from all posterity, and to achieve an immortal name. Therefore, they fought no less fiercely and strenuously to claim for themselves those plants which each had found than they did for their empire, or for life itself, than which nothing is dearer: a fact to which the names of many plants bear witness. For the herb *Polemonia* <note>Greek Valerian</note> was called *Philetæria* for no other reason, according to Pliny, than because of a contest between kings regarding its discovery.

<margin>Gentius, King of the Illyrians.</margin>
Does not *Gentiana* <note>Gentian</note> itself, to which the name was given by Gentius, King of the Illyrians, its discoverer, sufficiently show how studious of herbs he was? For he did not shrink from devoting effort to their investigation even in the midst of arms <note>i.e., during war</note>, and from searching out the powers of Gentian, for no other reason than to win for himself by this means a perpetual and never-dying memory.

<margin>Lysimachus, King of the Macedonians.</margin>
Furthermore, it was not so much greatness of soul and the glory of his strength that made Lysimachus, King of the Macedonians, noble, as it was the herb *Lysimachia* <note>Loosestrife</note>, which retains his name to this very day.

Mithridates surely, the greatest King of Pontus and the Parthians, who [spoke] no fewer than twenty-two...

# TO THE MOST ILLUSTRIOUS PRINCE, LORD JOACHIM

But I shall finally cease weaving this catalog of illustrious men who have thought and written most honorably concerning <term>herbal medicine</term><note>original: "herbaria medicina"; the study of plants for healing</note>, once I have added to them one of the most distinguished women: *Artemisia*, the wife of *Mausolus*, the petty king of *Caria*. She did not shrink from this study of investigating plants. Indeed, the plant *Artemisia* <note>Mugwort or Wormwood</note>—which retains her name to this day, though it was previously called *Parthenis*—speaks to the incredible passion and desire she burned with for knowing herbs. Thus, it should now be clear to everyone that people of every age and gender, including those most outstanding in nobility and learning, have not only held herbal medicine in high regard but have also contributed a vast amount of work, study, diligence, and labor to master it thoroughly.

<margin>What the ancients achieved through the study of investigating plants.</margin>
And yet, perhaps someone who is not well-disposed toward this branch of medicine will desire to know from us: what, in the end, did those figures we have commemorated in such a long list achieve through this study of investigating plants? Truly, they achieved a most excellent thing, one not to be ranked below any riches or pleasures: namely, an immortal and everlasting memory among posterity. For their memory will remain firm and unshaken for as long as the earth, the faithful and best parent of all things, continues to produce *Gentian* <note>named for King Gentius</note>, *Lysimachia* <note>named for King Lysimachus</note>, *Scordium*, *Eupatorium* <note>named for Mithridates Eupator</note>, *Euphorbium* <note>named for the physician Euphorbus</note>, *Clymenon*, *Polemonia*, and other plants of that kind upon which the names of most distinguished kings have been bestowed. Thus, their names live today in the grateful memory of men—names that otherwise would have perished with them the moment they departed from the living, had they not left them behind imprinted upon plants. And certainly, this most noble and most healthful study and rivalry of such great kings and learned men is deservedly extolled by us with praises up to the heavens, whenever we remember how they adorned our lives with the best gifts and how excellently they have deserved our gratitude. Wherefore *Pliny* most truly said:

> Certain individual discoveries have added their authors to the number of the gods, and they have made the lives of all men more bright by giving their names to herbs, with memory thus kindly returning the favor.

When I reflect on this, I am moved to wonder why almost none of our own princes strive to imitate the example of the ancient kings and heroes, or even take it into their hearts to assist those who are possessed by this passion for investigating herbs. I know, of course, that they are so entangled in troublesome and laborious affairs—provided they wish to properly guard the <term>Sparta</term><note>A classical proverb meaning "the lot or duty one has inherited"</note> which has fallen to them—that they themselves cannot devote effort to investigating plants.

<margin>Princes ought to support herbalists or provide funds for investigating herbs.</margin>
Nevertheless, it was nonetheless their duty to construct gardens planted with the best herbs, even those sought from afar, and to support at their own expense herbalists who might continually cultivate, increase, and preserve them—just as we mentioned above that the *Roman Emperors* once did. This should be done chiefly because there is no matter from which they can hunt for more praise and glory for themselves, or better deserve the thanks of all posterity, than if they strive to preserve the knowledge of plants in this manner. I would wish, therefore, that they would apply at least some part—and clearly a very small one—of their expenses (which they mostly spend on fleeting and momentary things, to say nothing more severe) toward illustrating this part of medicine, which now, as it were, raises its head out of the deep darkness.

<margin>The physicians of our time shrink from the study of investigating herbs.</margin>
But by the immortal Gods! What wonder is it that kings and princes do not greatly respect this study of investigating plants, when even the physicians of our age so shrink from it that among a hundred, one can scarcely find one who has an exact knowledge of even a few herbs. I judge this happens for no other reason than that they do not think that part of medicine which hands down the knowledge of plants pertains to them; or they deem it too cheap and dishonorable to allow it to concern them. For they think their dignity and reputation will be greatly diminished if they handle those things which the <term>druggists</term><note>original: "seplasiarij"; sellers of perfumes and ointments</note> and certain others—if the gods permit!—have now claimed knowledge of for themselves.

<margin>Herbal medicine today is in the hands of the ignorant common people.</margin>
Hence it is that they reject this entire care onto the pharmacists—a class of men, by Hercules, largely unlearned—and onto foolish and wonderfully superstitious women. To such an extent that in our time, the whole of herbal medicine is in the hands of peasant women, old hags, and uneducated men, who [perpetuate] error...
