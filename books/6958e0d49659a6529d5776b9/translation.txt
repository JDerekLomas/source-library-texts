# PREFACE.

IN the year of the Christian Era 1688, I published the *General History of Plants*; in which I included all species published up to that time, with the exception of a few omitted through carelessness or forgetfulness. Since that time, by the wonderful industry of the Botanists of this century, the *History of Plants* has grown almost immeasurably through new additions every day. Indeed, since that Work was published, there have appeared in the light of day:

The last six volumes of the **Garden of Malabar** <note>original: *Horti Malabarici*, a monumental work on the flora of the East Indies</note>;

The second *Preliminary Exposition* <note>original: *Prodromus*</note> of **Mr. Jacob Breynius**;

The *Flowers of the Dutch Flora* by **Mr. Paul Hermann**, and the *Preliminary Exposition of the Dutch Paradise* by the *same author*; and finally, after his death, the *Paradise* itself;

The *Botanical School* of **Mr. Joseph Pitton Tournefort**; the *Elements of Botany* by the *same author*, written in French; likewise the *Institutions of the Herbal Art* in Latin; as well as the *History of Plants growing around Paris* in French;

The *Descriptions of American Plants* by **Mr. Charles Plumier**, composed in French;

Three parts of the *Phytographia* <note>literally "Plant-Engravings"</note> by **Mr. Leonard Plukenet**; also the *Botanical Almagest* <note>a "great book" or storehouse</note> by the *same author*, a most rich Repository of rarer plants; also the *Supplement to the Botanical Almagest*, containing more than a thousand newly discovered plants;

The *Museum of Rare Plants of Sicily, Malta, Corsica, Italy, Piedmont, and Germany* by **Mr. Paul Boccone**, in Italian; the *Physical and Experimental Museum* by the *same author*, in which there is much concerning Plants, in Italian;

The *Images and Descriptions of Rare Plants of the Amsterdam Medical Garden* by **Mr. Commelin**, in folio, two Volumes;

The third part of the *Universal Oxford History of Plants* by **Mr. Robert Morison**, completed and finished by **Mr. Jacob Bobart**;

The *Catholic Garden* <note>meaning "Universal Garden"</note> with a triple Supplement by the **Reverend Father Mr. Francesco Cupani**. In this Work many species not yet published or described are contained, discovered for the most part in Sicily by the Author and his friends. Not a few of these—specifically those of which **Mr. Sherard** shared dried specimens with us—we have inserted into this Supplement; the rest, known to us by name only, we have omitted, because we have been informed that their images and descriptions have already been sent to the press;

The *Nuremberg Flora* of **Mr. Johann Georg Volckamer**, or a *Catalogue of Plants* both growing spontaneously in the Nuremberg territory and nourished in Gardens and Pleasure-grounds;

The *New Method of Plants* according to the differences of the flowers, both in respect to the number of petals and to their regular or irregular shape, by **Mr. Augustus Quirinus Rivinus**, Professor of Physiology in the Academy of Leipzig: to say nothing of other books of lesser note.

->MOREOVER, the Plants contained in these books fall into three categories.<-

I. Some, growing in their native places, were observed, described, and drawn from life by their editors while they were in their natural state and vigor, adorned with flowers and burdened with fruit.
II. Others, cultivated outside their native places in Gardens—whether public or the private gardens of the curious—were viewed by the Authors and in like manner either expressed in a painting and described in words, or distinguished only by names or descriptive labels.
III. Finally, others were seen neither alive nor flourishing, but only dried <note>original: *exsiccatæ*</note>; and for the most part not whole, but only as torn-off branches. Of these, either images drawn and engraved from the dry specimens were published with titles prefixed, or only general names were issued, with epithets added to determine the species.

# PREFACE

In assigning names to plants of this final genus, it can scarcely be avoided that even the most cautious and circumspect botanists—who are truly <term>Lyncean</term><note>original: "Lyncei"; a reference to the Lynceus of Greek mythology, famed for his keen sight. It refers here to the members of the Academy of the Lynxe (Accademia dei Lincei), an early scientific society.</note> in discerning and judging plants—might sometimes err or grope blindly. This is because dried branches are occasionally brought to us lacking fruit; and even if fruit is present, the form, structure, and number of petals of the flower in some, or the shape of the fruit, the consistency of the seed-vessel, and the number of cells into which it is divided in others, cannot be discerned or distinguished. Consequently, it cannot be determined with certainty to which genera they should be referred. Nevertheless, when I have had no knowledge of a certain plant from elsewhere, I have not dared to omit or change anything based on conjecture; for I have deemed it better to ensure that nothing was missing than to worry lest something be redundant or placed in the wrong location.

Furthermore, so that I may give you, kind *Reader*, some account of this work, know that I have included in it all the new species, not previously published, which are contained in the aforementioned books.

Next, I have increased the accounts of *Seaweeds*<note>original: "Fucorum"</note> and other underwater plants, *Mushrooms*, and *Mosses* by about three hundred species, partly observed by myself and partly communicated by friends. However, because the mosses are so numerous, I have divided them into several sub-genera for the sake of clearer understanding and easier memory.

Before I proceed, it might perhaps be worthwhile to address an objection that could be raised against me. There will surely be someone who accuses me of vain curiosity, because I have investigated things so cheap and lowly, of no use in life, and have spent so much time and effort in describing them.

To such a person I respond: These are the works of God in the contemplation of which I exercise myself. These are the effects of Divine Art and Power, to the searching out of which I devote my spare hours. It was He who introduced me into this world, furnished and adorned with such inexplicable variety of things; He placed them before my eyes, which He gave me, to be seen, and before my mind to be considered. It results, therefore, in an insult to God that you should affirm these things to be superfluous and useless, which you do not dare to deny He created.

Furthermore, it is the opinion of theologians that this sublunary world and all things in it were created for the sake of man; therefore, so too were *Seaweeds*, *Mushrooms*, and *Mosses*. And what better use do they provide for men than that they should contemplate them, and observe and marvel at the traces of Divine Art in their formation? But if you refer everything to the Body, and contend that all things were prepared for it either for feeding, or clothing, or adorning, or healing, even these genera have their own use in these matters, as would be easy to show by running through them all.

You will say, "There are greater and more necessary studies which require the whole man." I respond: I bestow greater care upon those greater things, yet in the meantime there is no need for me to utterly neglect these smaller and lighter matters. I am sufficient for both tasks; there is plenty of time for both, provided I dispense it prudently, and provided I take care that no part of it slips away entirely empty. *We have not received a short life* (as <term>Seneca</term><note>Lucius Annaeus Seneca, the Roman Stoic philosopher.</note> rightly says), *but we have made it so; we are not poor in it, but prodigal with it.* Furthermore, even the stricter theologians allot some portion of time to recreations. I recreate and delight myself with these studies and inquiries. What others spend on hunting, fowling, chatting, and games, I spend on investigating, cultivating, and contemplating plants. Whatever one takes delight in recreates and refreshes the mind, even though it may be laborious.

But to return from this digression to the path: I have not only collected from the writings of botanists published after our *History* was issued whatever was lacking in it, but I have also added many not yet described, communicated by friends. In the first place, from the most rich manuscript *History of the Plants of Jamaica* by the most accomplished physician and botanist Mr. Hans Sloane, our most esteemed friend—which the highly praised author kindly lent me for my use, granting also, without jealousy, permission to pluck new flowers from it and to transfer into our *Supplement* whatever might lead to its perfection and adornment—I have transcribed descriptions of many rare and new plants, and those full and accurate, with the uses of some in food, medicine, and mechanics also added. Hence it is that I do not blush to recommend this work of ours to the <term>Philobotanist</term><note>A lover of plants.</note> reader on the grounds of both novelty and utility.
