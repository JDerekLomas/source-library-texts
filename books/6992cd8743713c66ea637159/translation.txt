# <insert>Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms</insert>

<note>original: "三才圖會" (Sāncái Túhuì). This title refers to a massive encyclopedia compiled by Wang Qi and his son Wang Siyi during the Ming Dynasty, first published around 1609. The "Three Realms" (Sancai) refers to the traditional Chinese view of the universe as a triad of Heaven, Earth, and Humanity.</note>

<term>Three Realms (Sancai): A cosmological concept encompassing everything in existence. In this encyclopedia, it organizes knowledge into categories like astronomy, geography, biology, and human history.</term>

<term>Ming Dynasty (1368–1644): The era during which this work was produced, characterized by a great flourishing of literature, art, and the printing of illustrated books.</term>

<term>Thread-bound book (Xiànzhuāngshū): A classic style of Chinese bookbinding where the pages are folded and sewn through the spine with silk thread, typically featuring a dark blue indigo-dyed cover as seen here.</term>

<summary>This page serves as the cover and title label for the "Sancai Tuhui," a comprehensive Ming Dynasty encyclopedia. It introduces a work intended to archive all known knowledge across the spheres of Heaven, Earth, and Humanity.</summary>

->**The People of Tubo**<-
<note>original: "吐蕃" (Tǔbó). This is the historical Chinese name for the Tibetan Empire and its people, used from the Tang Dynasty through the Ming Dynasty.</note>

The Tubo are also called the "False Western Fan."
<note>original: "假西蕃" (Jiǎ Xīfān). "Fan" was a generic term for groups on the borders of imperial China. The prefix "False" likely serves as a taxonomic distinction used by Ming geographers to differentiate this specific group from other "Western Fan" peoples closer to the border.</note>
They were originally of the Western Qiang <note>original: "西羌" (Xīqiāng). An ancient ethnic group in western China; Chinese historians traditionally viewed the Tibetans as descendants of these nomadic tribes.</note> and lived along the Zhi River <note>original: "支水" (Zhī Shuǐ). Likely referring to the Yarlung Tsangpo River or its tributaries in the Tibetan heartland.</note>. Their ancestral surname was Bosuye <note>original: "勃窣野" (Bósùyě). A phonetic transcription of an early Tibetan clan or lineage name.</note>.
In their customs, "strong and heroic" is called *Zan*; "great man" is called *Pu*.
Therefore, they call their sovereign ruler the *Zanpu*.
<note>original: "贊普" (Zànpǔ). This is the Chinese transcription of the Tibetan title "Tsenpo," the term for the Emperor or King of Tibet.</note>
Among their later rulers were those called Lunzan and Nongzan.
<note>original: "論贊" (Lùnzàn) and "弄贊" (Nòngzàn). These refer to early kings of the Yarlung Dynasty. "Nongzan" is a common historical Chinese transcription for Songtsen Gampo, the famous 7th-century ruler who unified Tibet and married the Chinese Princess Wencheng.</note>

<note>original: "汪輦國" (Wāngniǎn Guó). This is a phonetic transcription. Scholars often identify this with "Onor" (Honavar) or other coastal regions of the Malabar Coast in Southern India, as described by Ming-era maritime travelers.</note>

<note>original: "印度" (Yìndù). This is the historical Chinese term for India.</note>

## The State of the Suspended Crossing
<term>original: 懸渡國 (Xuándùguó). A historical and legendary name for a region in the Hindu Kush or Pamir Mountains, so named because the terrain was so steep that travelers had to be "suspended" from ropes to cross it.</term>

The **State of the Suspended Crossing** is located to the **west of Wuhao** <note>original: 烏耗 (Wūhào). Likely referring to a region near the Wakhan Corridor or the Bolor mountainous area in modern-day Afghanistan/Tajikistan.</note>. 

The **mountain streams and valleys** are **impassable**; the only way to traverse them is by **pulling oneself along ropes** to cross the voids. 

The local inhabitants **stack stones** among the rocky cliffs to **construct their dwellings**. They **drink by joining hands** <note>This refers to forming a human chain to reach water sources in deep, inaccessible ravines.</note> and **pulling one another along**. This method of movement is what is popularly called **"monkey-pulling"** <note>original: 猿引 (yuányǐn). This vividly describes the primate-like agility required to navigate the vertical rock faces and rope bridges of the region.</note>.

## The People of the Kingdom of Wuchang
<term>original: 烏萇 (Wūcháng). Historically known as Udyana, an ancient kingdom located in the Swat Valley of modern-day Pakistan. It was a significant center of Buddhist culture and was frequently visited by Chinese pilgrims like Fazhao and Xuanzang.</term>

When a person among the people of **Wuchang** commits a **capital crime**, the authorities **do not execute** them. Instead, they are merely **exiled to the desolate mountains**, where they are left to **fend for themselves** <note>original: 飲啄 (yǐnzhuó). In legal cases where the **evidence is ambiguous** or suspicious, a **medicinal substance** is administered to the accused. Their **innocence or guilt** <note>original: 清濁 (qīngzhuó), literally "purity or turbidity." This refers to a trial by ordeal where the body's reaction to the medicine was believed to manifest the person's internal moral state.</note> will **reveal itself** naturally. The final verdict is then **decided based on the severity** of the matter at hand.

<term>original: 波斯國 (Bōsī Guó). Historically referring to the Sasanian Empire or subsequent Persian territories. During the Ming dynasty, this name encompassed the broad cultural and geographical region of modern-day Iran.</term>

The people of this nation have **dark-colored skin** and **wrap their bodies** in cloth decorated with **golden flower patterns**. 

The kingdom has **no walled cities** <note>original: 城郭 (chéngguō). This suggests a nomadic or decentralized settlement pattern, contrasting with the heavily fortified urban centers of Ming China.</note>.

The land yields **rare treasures** and other exotic items.

# The Kingdom of Bomeili 
<term>original: 撥枚力國 (Bōméilì Guó). Historians identify this as Berbera, located on the Somali coast of East Africa. It was known to Chinese maritime explorers through the voyages of Zheng He and earlier Song Dynasty accounts.</term>

This nation is located within the **Southwestern Sea** <note>original: 西南海 (Xī nán hǎi). A general geographical term used in the Ming dynasty to refer to the Indian Ocean and the seas surrounding the African continent.</note>. 

The people there do **not know the five grains** <note>original: 五穀 (wǔgǔ). A traditional Chinese grouping of staple crops like rice, wheat, and millet. To Ming readers, a society without "grains" was a significant marker of a non-agricultural, pastoral lifestyle.</note>. 

They subsist **entirely on meat**. They frequently **insert needles** <note>original: 針 (zhēn). The text and illustration describe the practice of drawing blood from live animals without killing them.</note> into their **cattle** to draw **blood**, which they **mix with milk** and **drink raw**. 

On their bodies, they have **no clothing**; they only use **sheepskin** to **cover themselves** from the waist down.

# The Kingdom of Kunwu
<term>original: 昆吾國 (Kūnwú Guó). In ancient Chinese mythology, Kunwu was a tribe or state famous for its copper and smithing. By the Ming Dynasty, this name was often associated with distant lands known for producing exceptional steel.</term>

The Kingdom of Kunwu produces **precious iron**. 

When fashioned into **blades**, this iron can **cut through jade** as if it were **soft mud** <note>original: 切玉如泥 (qiē yù rú ní). A classic Chinese idiom describing a legendary sharpness, specifically associated with the "Kunwu Sword."</note>.

In this country, the **pagodas** <note>original: 浮圖 (fútú). A term derived from the Sanskrit word for Buddha, used here to describe a stupa or a tiered religious monument.</note> consist of **three levels**. 

**Dried corpses** <note>original: 屍乾 (shī gān). This likely refers to mummified remains or those that have undergone natural desiccation.</note> are placed on the **top level**, while **fresh corpses** <note>original: 屍濕 (shī shī), literally "wet corpses."</note> are placed on the **bottom level**. 

They believe that **proximity to the burial** <note>original: 近葬 (jìn zàng). This may refer to keeping the deceased near the living or the tiered closeness of the family within the structure.</note> constitutes **filial piety**. 

The people gather at a **great altar** <note>original: 大檀 (dà tán). A large platform or sanctuary used for communal rites.</note> in the **center** of their community. There, they **hang clothing and silk fabrics** <note>original: 絲繒 (sī zēng). General terms for fine silk textiles.</note> as offerings, **weeping** as they perform **sacrifices** to the deceased.

### The Kingdom of Poluozhe
<term>original: 婆羅遮 (Póluózhē). This name likely refers to a region in Central or Southeast Asia. In historical Chinese records, "Poluozhe" was also the name of a specific foreign musical suite or masked dance that reached China during the Tang Dynasty.</term>

The people all wear **dog-head masks** <note>original: 狗頭 (gǒu tóu). These masks were characteristic of certain "Western Regions" performances.</note>. 

Both the **men and women** have **faces like monkeys** <note>original: 猿面 (yuán miàn). This likely describes either the appearance of their ritual masks or a Chinese artistic convention for depicting foreign ethnic groups.</note>. 

They **sing** and **dance** without ceasing, **day or night**. 

In the **eighth month**, on the **fifth day**, they hold an **elephant procession** <note>original: 行象 (xíng xiàng). A ceremonial parade common in Buddhist cultures where elephants carry sacred relics or deities.</note>. 

As part of their **entertainment**, they perform **tightrope walking** by stretching **ropes across elephant tusks** <note>original: 牙透索爲戲 (yá tòu suǒ wéi xì). A remarkable feat of acrobatics where performers balanced on ropes anchored to the tusks of live elephants.</note>.

# The Kingdom of the Wuxi Man
<term>original: 五溪蠻 (Wǔxī Mán). The "Five Streams" refers to a region encompassing parts of modern-day Hunan and Guizhou provinces. "Man" was a historical Chinese term used to describe the diverse non-Han indigenous ethnic groups of the south.</term>

When a person experiences the **death of their parents**, they perform **drumming and rhythmic dancing** <note>original: 鼓踏歌 (gǔ tà gē). A communal ritual where participants sing and step in time to the beat of a drum to honor the deceased.</note>. 

The **relatives** gather for **drinking and feasting**, which lasts for **one full month**.

They produce **coffins** known as *langfan* <note>original: 榔籓 (láng fān). This likely refers to a specific type of wooden burial vessel or enclosure used by these groups.</note>. 

In the **high mountains** overlooking the **rivers**, they **carve out stone niches** <note>original: 龕 (kān). A reference to the "hanging coffins" or cliff-side grotto burials found in southern China.</note> in which to **bury** the dead. 

For **three years** following the funeral, they **do not eat salt** <note>A profound gesture of mourning and self-denial, as salt was essential for health and flavor.</note>.

# The Kingdom of Meilugu
<term>original: 眉路骨國 (Méilùgǔ Guó). Scholars have identified this as a phonetic transcription likely referring to Meroë (in modern-day Sudan) or perhaps a region in the Maghreb (North Africa), transmitted through earlier Arab geographical accounts.</term>

The **Kingdom of Meilugu** possesses a **city with seven layers of walls** <note>This describes a city with concentric fortifications, a hallmark of legendary or highly fortified ancient capitals.</note>. These walls are **constructed using polished black stone**.

In this land, there are **more than three hundred tombs of the foreigners** <note>original: 番人塚 (fānrén zhǒng). "Fanren" was a general term for non-Chinese peoples, particularly those from the West or maritime regions.</note>. 

The **Westerners** <note>original: 胡 (hú). A historical term for people from Central or Western Asia.</note> refer to these tombs as **"pagodas"** <note>original: 塔 (tǎ). In a Chinese context, this refers to a Buddhist pagoda, but here it is used to describe foreign reliquaries, stupas, or possibly small pyramids, as seen in the illustration.</note>.

The people use **woolen cloth** <note>original: 毛段 (máo duàn). This refers to heavy, woven fabrics or felt, which would have been a distinct commodity compared to Chinese silk.</note> for their **clothing**. Their **diet** consists of **meat and flour-based foods** <note>original: 麪 (miàn). This refers to wheat products like bread or noodles, contrasting with the rice-staple diet of the Chinese heartland.</note>.

They use **gold and silver** as **currency**.

# The Kingdom of Zang
<term>original: 藏國 (Zàng Guó). A historical Chinese name for Tibet. During the Ming dynasty, this region was recognized for its unique Buddhist culture and strategic importance on the western frontier.</term>

In the **Kingdom of Zang**, there are **walled cities, moats**, and **houses**. 

The **local produce** of this land includes **great willow trees** <note>Willows hold cultural significance in Tibet; for instance, the "Princess Willow" at the Jokhang Temple in Lhasa is traditionally said to have been planted by the Tang dynasty Princess Wencheng.</note>.

To travel from this kingdom **to Yingtian Prefecture** <note>Modern-day Nanjing, which served as the capital at the start of the Ming dynasty and remained a vital administrative and cultural center.</note>, it takes **a journey of one year and two months**.

<note>The accompanying woodblock illustration depicts a large weeping willow tree—the "local produce" mentioned in the text—looming over a seated figure.
