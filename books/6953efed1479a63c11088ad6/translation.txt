# Preface.

---

Following John Locke’s investigations into the foundations of knowledge, this second volume of the collection presents Shaftesbury’s inquiry into the foundations of moral action. Just as Locke opened new paths for psychology and the theory of knowledge <note>original: Erkenntnistheorie</note>, Shaftesbury stands in the front rank of those thinkers who undertook to give ethics and aesthetics their own laws, free from foreign elements.

Among the best-known works in which Shaftesbury has received treatment appropriate to his significance is Hettner’s *Literary History of the Eighteenth Century* (Vol. I, 5th ed., pp. 172–187). For a more detailed study of our author, recent monographs to be mentioned include G. Spicker’s *The Philosophy of the Earl of Shaftesbury* (Freiburg 1872) and especially G. von Giżycki’s *The Philosophy of Shaftesbury* (Leipzig and Heidelberg 1876); these works also list the older literature. One can learn about Shaftesbury’s connection with the intellectual currents of the eighteenth century in England—besides the accounts by Hettner or Windelband—particularly through Leslie Stephen’s *English Thought in the Eighteenth Century* (London 1881, 2 vols.). The influence of English philosophers on German philosophy of the eighteenth century is treated in the prize-winning essay by G. Zart (Berlin 1881).

The text of the *Inquiry Concerning Virtue or Merit* is based on the new two-volume edition of the *Characteristics* by John M. # Preface

To assist the reader, I have decided to provide a short summary of the *Inquiry* beforehand; within the text itself, the most important results are emphasized through spaced lettering <note>original: "Sperrdruck," a common German method of emphasis similar to italics</note>. According to the principles expressed in the preface to Volume I of this collection, the annotations are intended primarily to clarify the subject matter. Thus, they aim to promote an understanding of the philosophical world of concepts—its content and development—by returning to the original sources. To us, the content is more important than the outward dress of the language; the internal history—the adoption of new concepts and the transformation of existing thoughts—is more significant than the evolution of sounds and word forms. Purely linguistic remarks, therefore, take a secondary position here and are otherwise left to the discretion of the teacher and the needs of the class. For a better overview, a glossary of explained words has been added to the end of this volume.

Hettner lamented <gloss>1</gloss> that the present day concerns itself so little with Shaftesbury’s world of thought. Nearly fifty years have passed since he wrote the words cited below, and no one who has familiarized themselves with the spirit of Shaftesbury’s writings will claim that the call for "more Shaftesbury" is any less justified today than it was then. Perhaps, however, our era—with its increased interest in art and intellectual culture—has become more receptive to his way of seeing things. Perhaps today we are better prepared to pay the debt of gratitude, which has not yet been settled, to this great English apostle of pure and free humanity. May this attempt to introduce Shaftesbury into the reading curriculum of the senior classes <note>original: "Prima," the highest grade level in the German gymnasium</note> of our neo-humanist institutions be regarded as a contribution toward this goal, and simultaneously help to silence the complaints regarding the "inferiority" of school reading materials.

# The Life and Writings of Shaftesbury

## Outlines of His Philosophical Views

Anthony Ashley Cooper, who became the third Earl of Shaftesbury after the death of his father, was born in London on February 26, 1671. His grandfather—Lord Ashley, who rose to the rank of Earl of Shaftesbury shortly after this birth—was eagerly determined to secure the honor and fame of the family. Consequently, starting in 1674, he took over the care of his first-born grandson in place of the boy's father, who was poorly suited for the task. The grandfather placed the boy's education in the hands of his close friend, John Locke <note>John Locke (1632–1704) was a foundational Enlightenment philosopher and family physician to the Ashleys; his theories on education emphasized practical learning over rote memorization.</note>. Locke conscientiously supervised both the physical development and the intellectual progress of the boy entrusted to him. However, at Locke’s recommendation, the actual instruction was assigned to a young lady, Miss Elizabeth Birch. She had received an excellent education and philological <term>philological: relating to the study of language and literature</term> training from her father. She succeeded brilliantly in introducing "Master Anthony" to the classical languages—he learned Latin and Greek as if they were living languages and, by the age of eleven, had already attained complete fluency in their use.

After the death of his grandfather in 1683, the close relationship with Locke was also interrupted. The boy's father sent him to Winchester School for another three years, where he suffered greatly from the spiteful behavior of his classmates <note>Shaftesbury's classmates likely bullied him because of his grandfather's controversial political reputation as a Whig leader.</note>. Afterward, he spent several years traveling through Holland, France, and Italy with a companion. This was an extraordinarily important period for the development of the gifted youth’s worldview and philosophy of life. During this time, with the youthful enthusiasm that shines through all his writings, he found the opportunity to pursue his strong inclinations for literature and art. Shortly after the accession of William of <note>original: "Wilhelm von," referring to William of Orange, who became William III of England in 1689.</note>

# The Life and Writings of Shaftesbury

With the arrival of William of Orange in 1688 <note>original: "Oranien"; referring to the Glorious Revolution when William III took the English throne.</note>, Shaftesbury also set foot once again on his native soil. He continued his philosophical and literary studies for five years before taking his seat in Parliament in 1695 at the age of 24. However, his weak health soon forced him to give up his political career. For his recovery, he lived another year primarily in Holland, where he maintained active contact with Jean Le Clerc and Pierre Bayle <note>Pierre Bayle (1647–1706) was a French philosopher and pioneer of the Enlightenment, best known for his *Historical and Critical Dictionary*.</note>.

At the end of 1699, he returned to England. Following the death of his father, he was prompted to participate in political affairs once again as a member of the House of Lords. Nevertheless, due to his poor health, he withdrew entirely into private life from the year 1703 onward. Now, in rapid succession, appeared the writings to which Shaftesbury owes his fame:
*   In 1708, the *Letter Concerning Enthusiasm*.
*   In 1709, the *Essay on the Freedom of Wit and Humour* and *The Moralists*.
*   In 1710, his *Advice to an Author*.

Finally, in 1711, he republished his essays in three volumes under the title *Characteristics of Men, Manners, Opinions, Times etc.* In this collection, he included a revised version of his early work, *Inquiry Concerning Virtue or Merit*, which had been published against his will by John Toland in 1699 <note>John Toland (1670–1722) was a controversial rationalist philosopher who published Shaftesbury's draft without permission.</note>. He also added a new volume titled *Miscellaneous Reflections on the Preceding Treatises, and Other Critical Subjects*.

Only two more years were granted to him after this intense period of writing. The worsening symptoms of his asthma <note>original: "asthmatischen Leidens"</note> prompted him to seek relief in the south in July 1711. However, he died at the beginning of 1713 in Naples, barely 42 years old.

Had Shaftesbury taken a greater part in political struggles and intervened decisively in the course of state affairs—as would have been expected given his family tradition—then the account of his external life would require much greater detail. However, his talent and inclination led him to become a philosophical writer instead, and thus it is the inner <margin>Shaftesbury's philosophical focus</margin> development of his mind that is most significant.

# Life and Writings of Shaftesbury

While the development of our author claims our interest in the first instance, it is the content and tendency of his writings that must primarily occupy our attention.

Shaftesbury views it as his special task to reawaken a taste for philosophical inquiry within the circles of high society <note>original: "feinen Welt"</note>. He aims to replace trivial or frivolous conversation with more serious subjects worthy of a man. Through his own artistry of presentation, he wants to show how such questions can be handled without falling into stiff scholarship and spiritless pedantry. To tell the truth with a smile<sup>1</sup>; to use the weapons of irony and, if necessary, mockery to combat superstition, fanaticism, and the prejudices and weaknesses of both individuals and their eras; to awaken and elevate a confidence in the goodness of human nature—a confidence lost through dark religious ideas; to work toward a more natural understanding of the sphere of human duties; to preach tolerance and social feeling; and to revive a feeling for the beauties of nature and the glory of the world—these are the most important subjects and the main goals of his literary activity.

It is clear—even if an author might completely recede behind his subject matter in purely scientific investigations—that in questions of ethics, religion, and literary criticism, the writer must simultaneously reveal a piece of himself and his innermost nature. In these cases, the works must become an expression of personality. This permeation of the material with a highly personal character is especially evident in Shaftesbury. Form and content bear a unified stamp, no matter how much may have originated from foreign sources in specific details. Just as a diamond reflects the light it receives with increased brilliance and in more vivid colors, so too do the spiritual rays glow and warm at the same time, having passed through the head and heart of this enthusiastic herald of humanity.

If one considers *The Moralists*, in which Shaftesbury models the form of Platonic dialogues, to be his most perfect writing...

...has called it, there is no doubt that even his early work, *An Inquiry Concerning Virtue or Merit*, with its clear organization and rigorous intellectual progress, represents the *core of his philosophical standpoint*. His other essays group themselves around this core like free variations and expansions of the same theme; according to the author’s intention, each of the five *Treatises* is then followed by one of the *Miscellaneous Reflections* as a cheerful afterpiece <note>original: "Nachspiel"; a concluding performance or commentary that follows a more serious main work.</note>.

Natural talent and intellectual agility, a happy temperament, and an environment favorable to the development of his innate abilities combined with a careful, methodical education that guided him toward healthy and noble pursuits—these conditions worked together in perfect harmony for the grandson of *Lord Ashley* to lay the foundation for his success. His early years were mentioned above; the spirit of the most refined worldly culture <note>original: "weltmännischer Bildung"; the sophisticated education and manners of a gentleman.</note> surrounded him from youth, and the early, deep influence of classical literature is evident throughout his work. In antiquity, he found the models for his devotion to beauty—he has been called a *Platonist* with good reason—and here he found the principle that virtue consists in the harmony of natural forces. Here, too, he found moral systems that do not look to the afterlife for the value and reward of virtue.

It is easy to see that Shaftesbury thus found himself in a conflict with the Christian worldview that was difficult to bridge. Yet, what was true Christianity? Which of the historically developed denominations came closest to the spirit of its founder? For two centuries, the dogmatic bickering of the self-appointed interpreters of Christianity had led to nothing but the "religion of love" producing a harvest of hatred and persecution. Christian nations tore each other apart, and unheard-of human sacrifices were offered to religious fanaticism. These conditions could not continue if all culture was not to be jeopardized and destroyed. The best minds had turned away from the revolting spectacle offered by the bloody religious persecutions. Voices demanding mutual tolerance—where agreement was impossible—grew louder and louder. Those men who sought to reach an agreement above specific creeds by attempting to provide a philosophical basis for Christianity and faith in God gained ever-increasing influence. # Life and Writings of Shaftesbury

The religion of the English Deists <note>Deism: a belief in a creator who does not intervene in the universe; popular in the 17th and 18th centuries as an alternative to organized dogma.</note>, which brought back the belief in a benevolent Creator, a harmoniously perfect world order, and the dignity of human nature—challenging the era's obsession with the devil and the madness of witchcraft—found in Shaftesbury not its first, but certainly its most enthusiastic and influential advocate. Which German would not feel, to name but a few, the spirit of Lessing, Mendelssohn, and Herder blowing toward him here, or recognize that Schiller’s dithyrambic youth poetry was born entirely from this enthusiasm? Finally, how numerous and surprising, despite all contrasts, are the traits of inner kinship he shares with Schleiermacher!

With a certain logical necessity, Deism leads to optimism—to the assumption that the actual world is simultaneously the best of all possible worlds. It is no accident that almost simultaneously with Shaftesbury's *The Moralists*, Leibniz published his *Essay on Theodicy* (Amsterdam 1710) <note>original: "Essai de Théodicée"; a work defending the goodness of God in the face of the existence of evil.</note>. This is an interesting parallel to the discovery of calculus <note>original: "Infinitesimalrechnung"</note>, which was achieved independently by Newton and Leibniz at the exact time the development of mathematics required it. Shaftesbury himself, however—this should be emphasized in conclusion—was undoubtedly strongly influenced, and not only on this point, by Spinoza's *Ethics*, which he likely became acquainted with during his second stay in Holland. He adopted a number of the most important thoughts from this profound "natural history of human emotions" directly, even if he did not include the philosophical foundation on which Spinoza’s propositions are built into his own reflections. In this, he is like a spiritual organism of the highest vitality, which assimilates what corresponds to its nature and rejects what is foreign to it.

It is not the task of this introduction to replace the study of a history of literature and philosophy. Therefore, we refrain from tracing in detail the impact of Shaftesbury's ethical and aesthetic views on contemporary and later literature, insofar as they have not already been mentioned. Just as they found enthusiastic disciples and developers on home soil in Francis Hutcheson’s (1694–1747) *Inquiry into the Original of Our Ideas of Beauty and Virtue*, and in Henry Home and Edmund Burke, they were also carried to France, especially by Voltaire. They still live on today in our classical writers, and much that now seems self-evident to us has its roots in Shaftesbury, either directly or through the mediation of other authors.

# Life and Writings of Shaftesbury

## The Line of Thought in the *Inquiry Concerning Virtue or Merit* <note>original: "Untersuchung über Tugend oder Verdienst"; Shaftesbury's primary ethical treatise, first published in 1699.</note>

According to the general view, religion and morality are inseparable. However, daily experience teaches us that zealous religious practice can exist without genuine morality, and true morality can exist without a confession to a specific religion—indeed, even in the case of total unbelief. This leads to the task of investigating what virtue actually is, and what factual relationships exist between virtue and religion. Given the extraordinary diversity of religious convictions among mankind, it is necessary to first provide an overview of their main types so that the effect of each of these convictions on moral behavior can be examined.

In the realm of morality, we are concerned with the concepts of moral good and evil. We therefore ask first: what do we call "good" and "perfect" at all, and what do we call "bad" or "imperfect"?

We cannot apply these labels to countless things in nature because we lack insight into their inner essence and their destination. However, in the study of the living creation (much like with works of human craftsmanship), we can discover that every creature carries its own purpose within itself, and that nature compels it to pursue these inherent needs with all its strength. Accordingly, we consider a creature to be more perfect the better it is able to fulfill the purposes set for it by nature—just as we call a human tool more perfect the better it serves the specific purposes for which it was invented by man.

Yet, the individual creature is not only an end in itself; it also forms a higher unity with creatures of its own kind, with new purposes that are rooted in this community. Indeed, even the circle of creatures of the same...

It is in this way, finally, that a mind becomes a wilderness where everything is laid waste. Every fair and beautiful thing is removed, and nothing remains except what is savage and deformed. Now, if banishment from one’s country, <margin>5</margin>removal to a foreign place, or anything that feels like solitude or desertion is so heavy to endure, what must it be like to feel this inward banishment? This is a real estrangement from human interaction <note>original: "commerce"</note>, a way of being in a desert and in the most dreadful of <margin>10</margin>solitudes even while in the middle of society. What must it be like to live in this disharmony with everything—this incompatibility and opposition to the order and government of the universe?

From this, it appears that the greatest of miseries <margin>15</margin>accompanies the state that results from the loss of natural affection; and that to possess those horrific, monstrous, and unnatural tendencies <note>original: "affections"</note> is to be miserable in the highest degree.

## Conclusion.

<margin>20</margin>Thus, we have endeavored to prove what was stated at the beginning. And since in the common and well-known sense of vice and moral badness <note>original: "illness"</note>, no one can be vicious or bad except by:

1. The deficiency or weakness of natural <margin>25</margin>affections; or
2. The violence of selfish passions; or
3. Such passions as are plainly unnatural.

It must follow that, if each of these is harmful and destructive to a person <note>original: "the creature"</note>—to such an extent that <margin>30</margin>his most complete state of misery is created by them—then to be wicked or vicious is to be miserable and unhappy.

## Announcement.

The publishing house, in collaboration with the undersigned, intends to issue a series of works by English writers in the fields of philosophy, cultural history, and natural science. This initiative aims to address the increasingly felt shortage of truly intellectually enriching reading material for the senior classes of our modern-language schools.

While students at classical secondary schools <note>original German: *Gymnasiast*</note> become acquainted with the spirit of classical antiquity through a clearly defined circle of works of timeless value—such as Homer and the tragedians, Herodotus and Thucydides, Demosthenes and Plato—and while the source of our religious convictions is revealed to them in the New Testament, mediocrity and a lack of planning still prevail in the selection of French and English prose readings. This remains true despite some recent attempts at improvement. This is not the place to investigate the deeper reasons for this phenomenon; moreover, they often lie outside the school's sphere of influence. However, a few more words may be said about the scope and character of this announced collection, which aims to provide a remedy and whose first volume is now being published.

The interest in discussing philosophical, ethical, religious, and aesthetic problems is currently enjoying a welcome resurgence. If we nonetheless allow our senior students <note>original German: *Primaner*, referring to students in their final years of schooling</note> to enter life or university without having awakened an interest in the deepest questions of humanity in their receptive souls—which long for clarity and truth—it is a sin against youth. This failure is more significant than any lack of technical skill or memorized knowledge.

Therefore, this collection will primarily give a voice to those philosophers whose writings have provided classical expression to the leading ideas of their time. We have chosen to start no further back than John Locke because no work is as well-suited as Locke’s *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* to serve as an introduction to specifically English philosophy and as preparation for reading modern philosophers.

As the second volume, parts of Shaftesbury’s *Characteristics* will appear later this summer; a selection from David Hume’s philosophical writings is currently in preparation. Should the direction of this collection meet with approval, it shall be continued up to Herbert Spencer, in whose writings the entire wealth of modern scientific knowledge is constructed into a powerful system.
