...so illuminated by the light of **Nature and Grace** <note>original: "Naturæ & Gratiæ"</note> that he, not unjustly among so many thousands upon thousands of highly learned men, was and still is to be called a Prince, Hero, and Monarch of **Divine sciences** and natural riches (as much as this mortality could grasp <note>original: "quantum, hæc mortalitas capere potuit"</note>). Not only was his person banished, shamed, insulted, and blasphemed **from the chairs of the schools** <note>original: "ex cathedris scholarum," referring to university professors and academic authorities.</note>, but also all his noble, precious writings; likewise, all his disciples and followers were most cruelly persecuted and ruined.

...y moment, been so enlightened by the lights of **Nature and Grace** <note>original: "Naturæ & Gratiæ"; in Paracelsian philosophy, the "Light of Nature" refers to innate human reason and the "Light of Grace" to divine revelation.</note> that he was, and still is, rightly called a prince, hero, and **monarch** <note>original: "Monarcha"</note> of divine knowledge and natural riches (as much as this mortal state could grasp <note>original: "quantū, hæc mortalitas capere potuit"</note>) among so many thousands and thousands of highly learned men. 

But what has he earned from the world with his ingenious, numerous, and diverse writings, and those wondrous cures—performed even to the point of miracles <note>original: "ad miraculū usq;"</note>—in incurable and desperate illnesses? Only that **Lord World** <note>The personification of worldly society, often depicted in this era as fickle, ungrateful, and wicked.</note>, drowned in godlessness, ambition, envy, self-interest, and all manner of wickedness, practiced his usual "Thanks be to God" <note>original: "Deo gratias"; used here ironically to mean the world repaid him with hostility instead of true gratitude.</note> against him. He not only cast out his person, but also all his noble, precious writings from the **lecture halls of the schools** <note>original: "ex cathedris scholarū"</note>, shaming, reviling, and blaspheming them, as well as most cruelly persecuting and destroying all his disciples and followers.

# Preface.

...enough of a "man of letters" <note>original: "Kunstwiſchen"</note> (using the term in its broad sense <note>original: "latè ſumpto vocabulo"</note>) to make it known that he was specifically destined <note>original: "conſtelliret," an astrological term implying his fate was written in the stars</note> for nothing other than to slander <note>original: "traduciren"</note> honest, honorable people in the worst way with his scoffing and sarcasms <note>original: "ſcomatiſmis vñ ſarcaſmis"</note>. He spared neither the dead nor the living, and indeed, [he attacked] such highly qualified persons that—speaking with all due respect—he was not worthy to untie their shoelaces <note>A biblical allusion to John the Baptist's humility (Mark 1:7), here used to show the critic's inferiority compared to those he attacked.</note>. Yet they had to endure his leaping pen and tongue and withstand one "burr-throw" <note>original: "klettenwurff" – a metaphor for throwing insults that stick like burrs to clothing.</note> after another. This man continued this until his end, yes, into the grave and the pit—which may God forgive him and us all. 

I do not mention this here with the intent of inflicting injury <note>original: "animo ingerendi injuriam"</note> (about which I protest again and again <note>original: "de quo iterùm atq; iterùm proteſtor"</note>), but rather because I am wounded by the indignity of the matter <note>original: "indignitate rei vulneratus"</note>. I could not leave this untouched, for the sake of the pure truth and to assist the innocence of Paracelsus <note>original: "paracelſiſche unſchuld"</note>. Especially since it is known to me and generally to everyone that this dead man understood just as much about the works left behind by Paracelsus <note>original: "paracelſiſchen Relictis"</note> as a camel knows about playing the organ, or a sow about the lute <note>A common Renaissance proverb used to mock someone attempting a craft for which they have no talent or understanding.</note>. Yet he could not cease from his rage <note>original: "rabie"</note> until finally he barked himself to death against the highly honored and much-praised Brotherhood of the **Rosy Cross**. May **GOD** forgive him once more, and may He be gracious and merciful to his poor soul.

But to speak further of **Theophrastus** <note>Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim, better known as Paracelsus (1493–1541).</note>, I call you to witness—all you physicians, astronomers, surgeons, spagyrics <note>term: "spagyrici" — alchemists who use the method of "separating and recombining" to create medicines.</note>, chemists, naturalists, laboratory workers, and artists—be my witnesses, I ask you, and confess openly: Have you read his writings with judgment <note>original: "Scripta cum judicio"</note> and understanding?
