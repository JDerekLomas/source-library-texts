## Narrative and Explanatory Maps of Jiangxi

Gui E <note>original: "桂萼" (1478–1531), a high-ranking Ming Dynasty official and scholar-geographer.</note> stated: Jiangxi was anciently the territory of Yangzhou <note>Referring to one of the "Nine Provinces" of ancient China; a vast region covering the lower Yangtze and southeastern coast.</note>. It is situated at the intersection of the regions of Wu <gloss>Southern Zhili</gloss>, Chu <gloss>Huguang</gloss>, Min <gloss>Fujian</gloss>, and Yue <gloss>Guangdong</gloss>. Because the natural defenses and mountain barriers are dispersed [across these different borders], the overall strategic topography is inherently weakened. Its state of security and its strategic weight are constantly determined by the conditions of the four surrounding regions. If the borders are tranquil and the populace is in harmony, then Jiujiang <note>A vital port city on the Yangtze River.</note> alone commands the upper reaches of the river, exerting a dense and restrictive influence over the various prefectures and counties located along the river.

Near Nankang [Prefecture], bordering the great lake <gloss>Poyang Lake</gloss>, pirate ships emerge from all sides; therefore, one cannot be without military preparation. Between Nanan <gloss>Nanan Prefecture</gloss> and Gan <gloss>Ganzhou Prefecture</gloss>, there are the regions of Ting and Zhang <gloss>Tingzhou and Zhangzhou, both prefectures belonging to Fujian</gloss>, as well as Xiong and Shao <gloss>Nanxiong and Shaozhou, both prefectures belonging to Guangdong</gloss>. 

In this area, all the mountains converge. Stretching across prefectures and crossing provincial borders, the forests and valleys are lush and dense. When bandits arise, this region becomes their primary lair <note>The original Chinese term "yuan sou" literally means a deep pool for fish or a thicket for animals, used here as a metaphor for a refuge for outlaws.</note>. For this reason, the important post of Governor <term>Xunfu; a high-ranking official tasked with coordinating military and civil affairs across provincial borders</term> was established, leading troops to oversee the region.

Yuanzhou [Prefecture] is situated close to Changsha <gloss>a prefecture belonging to Huguang Province</gloss>. Because of the presence of fugitive populations and migrant households, it is quite difficult to investigate and monitor. Furthermore, in Nanchang, Jianchang, and Raozhou, members of the imperial clan <note>Referring to the many princes and relatives of the Ming imperial family who were granted estates in these fertile areas.</note> reside permanently. The common people there are exhausted by the burden of providing endless supplies and funds for them. Compared to the prefectures of Lin <gloss>Linjiang</gloss>, Ji <gloss>Ji'an</gloss>, Rui <gloss>Ruizhou</gloss>, Xin <gloss>Guangxin</gloss>, and Fu <gloss>Fuzhou</gloss>, the hardships in these areas are even more severe.

Generally speaking, the people of Jiangxi are simple, frugal, and hardworking, possessing a spirit of anxious diligence. However, since the Hongzhi era <note>The reign of the Ming Emperor Xiaozong, 1487–1505, often considered a silver age before mid-Ming social decline.</note>, taxes and labor services have gradually become more frequent and burdensome. Among the local inhabitants, many of the young and strong no longer attend to farming. They leave to seek a living in all four directions, even to the point of abandoning their wives and children. As a result, social customs and traditional rituals have deteriorated day by day, and unruly youths occasionally emerge.

In the regions between Nanan and Ganzhou, the various mountain ranges of Tingzhou, Zhangzhou, Nanxiong, and Shaozhou converge. Fuzhou is plagued by excessive litigation and frequent banditry. Since Yuanzhou borders the lake, the responsibility for suppressing waterborne bandits falls upon the Provincial Governor <term>Fuchen; the Grand Coordinator</term>, while the mountain bandits of Nanan and Ganzhou are the responsibility of the Supreme Commander <term>Duchen; a higher-ranking official overseeing regional military affairs</term>. 

Taken as a whole, the local customs of Jiangxi are characterized by diligence and thrift. Its people are hardworking <term>Kongzong; implies being constantly occupied with labor or urgent business</term> and unpretentious, valuing substance and practical effort.
