<meta>This page serves as the formal introduction (prologue) to the Decretals of Gregory IX, a foundational collection of Canon Law compiled in 1234. The text includes the "Glossa Ordinaria" (standard commentary) by Bernard of Parma, which explains the legal and philosophical framework of the work.</meta>

If the beginning of this book is to be announced as a formal instruction, one must consider its intention, its subject matter, its utility, which branch of philosophy it belongs to, its method of proceeding, and the title of the book. The intention of Lord Gregory in this present compilation was to reduce the various constitutions and decretal letters of his predecessors into a single body of work... which were previously scattered through many volumes or compilations. These older collections introduced confusion and difficulty; therefore, he created a minimal compilation to provide a unified tool for students, clearly resolving the confusion found in previous digests. The subject matter in this work consists of the decretal constitutions and letters collected under individual titles. The utility is that by reading and understanding these truths, we may learn to follow the path of right reason, which consists of justice; for justice is the constant will to render to each their due. <note>original: "iusticia... ut iustice iusticiam"</note> It is categorized under Ethics—the science of morality—as a book of laws intended to govern the behavior of men.

The method of proceeding is as follows: He divides his work into five parts. The first part treats the Holy Trinity and the Catholic Faith, as well as the sources of law and the offices of judges. The second part treats judgments (legal proceedings). The third part treats the life and honesty of the clergy and the laws regarding tithes. The fourth part joins together the laws on betrothals and the impediments to marriage. The fifth part concerns accusations and criminal offenses. <note>These five parts are traditionally remembered by the Latin mnemonic: Iudex, Iudicium, Clerus, Connubia, Crimen (Judge, Judgment, Clergy, Marriage, Crime).</note> He divides the work into five parts in likeness to the five senses of the human body, which anyone who judges must possess; otherwise, a judge is not fit to join the marital union of justice. This is also in likeness to the four elements of the human body.
