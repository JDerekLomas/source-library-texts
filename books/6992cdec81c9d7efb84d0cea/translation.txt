# Grand Fur

<note>original: "Da Qiu" (大裘). This refers to the most sacred sacrificial garment worn by the Emperor during the winter solstice and other supreme rites.</note>

When sacrificing to the Supreme Deity of the Bright Heavens <note>original: "Haotian Shangdi" (昊天上帝). The highest divinity in the traditional Chinese celestial hierarchy, representing the sky and the governing order of the universe.</note> and honoring the Five Emperors <note>original: "Wudi" (五帝). Deities associated with the five directions—North, South, East, West, and Center—and the five phases of nature.</note>, the attire worn is the **Black Lamb Fur robe**.

The ceremonial crown <term>Mian (冕): the formal, flat-topped crown of the Emperor and high officials.</term> is worn **without beaded tassels** <note>original: "Liu" (旒). This is paired with a **dark top garment** <term>Xuan yi (玄衣): a garment of a deep, dark hue (often described as "mystic" black) that symbolizes the sky before dawn.</term> and a **madder-red lower skirt** <term>Xun chang (纁裳): a skirt dyed a specific shade of light red or vermilion, representing the Earth.</term>

# The Ceremonial Robe and Crown

<note>original: "Gun Mian" (衮冕). This ensemble represents the pinnacle of Chinese ritual dress, combining a flat-topped crown with a robe embroidered with sacred symbols.</note>

When offering sacrifices to the **Former Kings** <note>original: "Xianwang" (先王). Refers to the deceased royal ancestors, honoring the lineage and the foundation of the state.</note>, the attire consists of:

The **Ceremonial Robe** <term>Gun yi (衮衣): the upper garment of the ritual set.</term> featuring **five decorative symbols** <term>Zhang (章): these are the "Twelve Symbols of Imperial Authority," though here only five are applied to the upper robe, likely including motifs like the dragon, mountains, and fire.</term> and the **Lower Skirt** <term>Chang (裳): the wrap-around lower garment.</term> featuring **four decorative symbols**.

# Veiled Hat

The **Treatise on Carriages and Clothing of the Tang Dynasty** <note>original: "唐輿服志" (Táng Yúfú Zhì)</note> states that the **Veiled Hat** <term>帷帽 (wéimào); a broad-brimmed hat featuring a hanging veil of gauze or netting</term> was designed at the end of the Sui dynasty. It originated from the *mili* <note>original: "羃<unclear>离</unclear>" (mìlí); a more ancient, full-body veil that covered the wearer from head to toe</note>, but the veil was shortened to reach only the neck. During the Song dynasty, scholars often used black gauze or a full width of green silk attached to the front of an oiled hat or a **bamboo leaf hat** <note>original: "篛笠" (ruòlì); a sturdy hat made of bamboo leaves used for protection against rain and sun</note> to block wind and dust. This was used as a garment for long-distance travel, and it seemingly originated from this practice.

# Face Veil

The **Face Veil** <term>面衣 (miànyī); a protective fabric covering for the face, often used for warmth or modesty</term> utilized purple silk for both the front and back panels. ## Cloth Headband

In ancient times, people did not wear cloth headbands; scholars instead wore formal caps. **Master Fu** <note>original: "傅子" (Fùzǐ); referring to the Jin dynasty scholar Fu Xuan</note> said: "At the end of the Han dynasty, princes and dukes mostly abandoned formal scholarly attire and considered the **Cloth Headband** <term>幅巾 (fújīn); a simple head covering made from a single width of fabric</term> to be more elegant. During the Qin dynasty, the cloth headband was actually the dress of the lowly and impoverished. It was only at the end of the Han that it began to be used as a garment for the upper classes."

## Net Cap

In ancient times, there was no such system for this garment. When our current dynasty <note>the Ming Dynasty (1368–1644)</note> first pacified the realm, it sought to change the foreign customs <note>referring to the Mongol-led Yuan dynasty's influence</note>. Consequently, they used silk to tie a net to bind the hair, naming it the **Net Cap** <term>網巾 (wǎngjīn); a mesh hairnet worn by men to keep their topknot in place under a hat</term>. 

Wise observers had a saying regarding its structure: "[The cap] removes the coldness from the center and pacifies the four directions" <note>original: "去夾中凉四方平定" (qù jiá zhōng liáng sì fāng píng dìng). This is a political pun: "removing the center cold" refers to the opening at the top of the cap but sounds like "removing the Jurchens," while "four directions pacified" refers to the four-sided frame of the cap symbolizing a unified empire.</note> 

In the *Gengxu* year of the **Hongwu era** <note>roughly 1370 AD</note>, **Emperor Taizu** <note>Zhu Yuanzhang, the founding emperor of the Ming</note> was traveling incognito and arrived at the **Temple of Divine Music** <note>original: "神樂觀" (Shénlè Guàn)</note>. There, he saw a Taoist priest tying a net cap. He summoned the priest to ask about the garment, and subsequently established it as a permanent regulation for the entire nation.

# Upper Garment

The **upper garment** <note>original: 衣 (yī)</note> is the attire worn on the upper part of the body. In ancient times, **court dress** <note>ceremonial clothing worn for imperial audiences and state rituals</note> included several varieties:

*   The **Dark Robe with Dragons** <term>玄衮 (xuángǔn); the most prestigious ceremonial robe, featuring dragon motifs on a dark background, reserved for the Emperor and high dukes</term>
*   The **Woolen Robe** <term>毳衣 (cuìyī); a robe traditionally made from animal fur or bird feathers, used in specific sacrificial rituals</term>
*   The **Fine Linen Robe** <term>絺衣 (chīyī); a light garment made from fine arrowroot or hemp fiber, used during the summer months</term>
*   The **Black Silk Robe** <term>緇衣 (zīyī); a robe made of black-dyed silk, historically associated with the daily wear of the literati and minor officials</term>
*   The **Brocade Robe** <term>錦衣 (jǐnyī); a robe made of rich, multi-colored patterned silk</term>
*   The **Deep Robe** <term>深衣 (shēnyī); a foundational scholar’s garment where the upper tunic and lower skirt are sewn together to form a single long piece, symbolizing moral integrity and "depth"</term>

The construction and cut of these various garments are, for the most part, quite similar to one another.

# Lower Garment

The **lower garment** <note>original: 裳 (sháng); a wrap-around ceremonial skirt</note> is the attire worn on the lower part of the body. # Diagram of the Non-Curtain Skirt

Generally, for this skirt, there are three panels in the front and four panels in the back. This layout symbolizes the relationship between <term>Yin and Yang — the dual cosmic forces, where odd numbers (3) represent the active Yang and even numbers (4) represent the receptive Yin</term>. Unlike the standard "Curtain Skirt" <note>original: 帷裳 (wéi cháng). A pleated ritual skirt that hangs straight like a curtain.</note>, the "Non-Curtain Skirt" <note>original: 非帷裳 (fēi wéi cháng)</note> is made by cutting the fabric diagonally and joining the pieces in an inverted fashion. The width of the hem at the bottom is double that of the waistband. It is constructed without any <term>pleats (bi ji) — the small, gathered folds used to manage fabric width</term>; instead, it features <term>tapered seams (sha feng) — diagonal seams that narrow the garment toward the waist to achieve a flared shape without gathering the material</term>.

# Diagram of the Curtain Skirt

The **Curtain Skirt** <note>original: 帷裳 (wéi cháng)</note> is a type of **formal ritual dress**. It is designed to be perfectly square and upright in its proportions. It is constructed using straight, full widths of fabric that hang down like a **curtain** or a bed-hanging; it is essentially what we would refer to today as a **waist-skirt**.

The term *bi* <note>original: 襞 (bì)</note> means to arrange or prepare the fabric, and the term *ji* <note>original: 積 (jī)</note> means to layer or stack it. These two terms together refer to what is currently known as **skirt tapering** <term>qun sha — the method of narrowing the top of a skirt to fit the waist, typically achieved by folding or pleating the fabric rather than cutting it away</term>.

<note>This entry explains the "Curtain Skirt," a fundamental piece of ancient Chinese ritual attire (Shenyi system). Unlike everyday clothing, ritual garments emphasized "squareness" to symbolize the wearer's moral rectitude.</note>

***

*   **Formal Ritual Dress** (Li Fu): Clothing worn for state or religious ceremonies.
*   **Skirt Tapering** (Qun Sha): The technical process of gathering fabric at the waist.
*   **Bi**: To arrange or fold.
*   **Cuo Ji**: To gather and layer fabric into pleats.
