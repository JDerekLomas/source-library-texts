**L**aboring Nature’s monsters <note>original: "Monstra"; in this period, the term referred to any biological anomaly or "wonder" of nature, not necessarily something frightening.</note>, having struggled until now in an unhappy womb, and finally brought to a literary birth by a certain posthumous power of a most fertile intellect, implore your immortal light (Most Serene Prince), which is more merciful than any Lucina <note>The Roman goddess of childbirth.</note>. Indeed, it is not unbecoming for a Prince of your fortune to favor monsters of this kind, born with admiration acting as a midwife. For you are accustomed to nourish within cages, as a proof of your majesty, portents of a more ferocious nature sought out from every side, and to exhibit them in theaters as a generous spectacle of your kindness; so much so that in the very stables of the wild beasts, Africa—having now almost unlearned her ferocity—serves you more mildly under a milder Sun. These monsters, however, which Nature strives to produce for you from the groaning press, were not conceived by Africa alone; for it is not only at the springs of Africa that living creatures sin with degenerate love. Rather, any parched land <note>original: "phlegæa"; likely a reference to the Phlegraean Fields, associated with volcanic activity and giants.</note> labors to produce monsters if it has a hostile Heaven. How many portents the Sea teems with in a certain lustful fertility may be guessed from Proteus <note>A sea-god capable of changing his shape.</note> alone, who is himself a manifold monster. The Air also, even if it shines quite pure, is often clouded by the breath of the Sea and the lands, and creates its own Centaurs from an aerial Juno <note>A reference to the myth of Ixion, who fathered the Centaurs by a cloud shaped like the goddess Juno.</note>. Even Fire itself, once purified, from the moment it began to be nourished among us and to be stabled within its own ashes, is turned toward almost innumerable monsters; so that mortals may rightly grieve that the pernicious fire of Prometheus <note>The Titan who stole fire from the gods for humanity.</note> could never be expiated by any waters...

The author argues that anomalies occur in all elements—earth, sea, air, and fire—due to the complexities of nature.</summary>

<meta>This page serves as the formal dedication of the "History of Monsters" to Ferdinand II de' Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany. It establishes the lineage of the work from the deceased author Ulisse Aldrovandi to its final editor, Bartolomeo Ambrosini.</meta>

Therefore, all the Monsters which the defect of the elements and the blind error of causes have produced, our Aldrovandi <note>Ulisse Aldrovandi (1522–1605), a renowned naturalist and father of modern zoology</note>—the eternal glory of the University of Bologna—conceived in this single volume with a successfully persistent zeal; a praise which perhaps no one until now has entirely attained. And indeed, a second Ulysses <note>A play on the author's first name, Ulisse, comparing him to the Greek hero Odysseus/Ulysses</note>, not unequal in skill to the son of Laertes and greater in virtue, was the first to escape so many Monsters. Yet the most learned Author could not impart that light to his own work which death envied him—a death mature in age, but most bitter to the wishes of all. Nevertheless, a surviving Love for a Patrician Citizen and primary Master lived on in his Fatherland, which did not cease to work by the light of the tomb <note>original: "ad sepulchri lucernam elucubrare," meaning to study or write late into the night by candlelight</note>, until by the study of the eminent Doctor Bartolomeo Ambrosini, he more accurately arranged the unrefined offspring and hastened toward this posthumous birth. At last, a work mature for Fame is born after the funeral of its Author; whence it seems not undeservedly able to claim immortality for itself, since it has its most bright cradle among the hidden places of the tomb.

But all Monsters, being born not without omen, portend ill luck. So be it: but we opportunely avert every evil when we consecrate them by the miracle of your favor. Namely, in your name (Most Wise Prince), in your name, I say, which is inscribed in the annals of a Most Ample Family; since it carries with it all the characters of ancestral glory—whether placed in your most upright Religion, your warlike virtue, or your studies of Peace—it can obliterate any black mark of ill omen by its innate felicity. Wisely, therefore, have I consulted the longevity of this work, when I dedicated it to the immortality of your most auspicious Name. One thing remains (MOST SERENE FERDINAND) <note>Ferdinand II de' Medici (1610–1670), Grand Duke of Tuscany</note>: that just as others observe the ravings of wandering Nature more willingly than the ruts always worn by the same wheels, you also might receive more kindly these Monsters, folded together in this volume as if in a compendium; just as we rejoice to be able to offer an unusual gift as a sign of no ordinary respect.

And from here, you will be able to exhibit new monsters to your Tuscany <note>original: "Hetruriæ"</note>; which, if it once devised the art of divination <note>term: "aruspicinam" - Haruspicy, the ancient Etruscan practice of divining the future by inspecting the entrails of sacrificed animals</note> to examine the vitals of victims and portend the deeper secrets of the fates from hidden portents, it could never grow dull in its perceptive genius; until at last, having transmitted a most sharp gaze through a tube <note>A reference to the telescope, popularized by Galileo under Medici patronage</note>, it most happily dissected and most faithfully explored even the celestial Monsters. And it is pleasing to divine what your Diviner <note>original: "Aruspica"</note> will be able to divine concerning you from this monstrous offspring: That you will be no worse than Hercules <note>original: "Alcide"</note> in fortitude, and better in fortune; since Monsters are born for you which do not indeed dread your power, but promise themselves a lovable patronage from it. That you will be, in this warlike age, a Champion of letters; when these very Monsters, forged among printed characters, turn to the great benefit of the literary world.

And truly, the studies of Peace were never to be fortified by a stronger protection than in this iron age of ours, in which Peace is attacked on all sides by the sword: nor is that to be expected more opportunely from anywhere else than from the Medici Family, with whom the Muses—often fugitives from our world—have, according to the opinion of Anaxagoras <note>An ancient Greek philosopher who suggested the existence of other worlds</note>, found other worlds where they might flee. Nor is it to be obtained more easily from anyone else than from you (Most Wise Prince). Meanwhile, deign to look upon these nascent Monsters, inscribed with your name, with the omen of a most serene brow; for from this it will be right to rejoice that I have never held anything more auspicious than Monsters. And now, as I believe myself bound by my vow to you, I place another vow before the immortal God: that you may always flourish with that happiness which befits the merits of your Virtues. Nor can I wish better things for you; for that happiness cannot be greater, since you have made these virtues the greatest. Farewell.

The text links ancient Etruscan divination with modern telescopic discovery, suggesting that the Medici patronage turns "monstrous" omens into scientific and political glory.</summary>
