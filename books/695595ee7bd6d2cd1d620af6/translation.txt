I published my hypothesis of fire and flame about eleven years ago in Observation 16, pages 103, 104, and 105 of my *Micrographia* <note>Robert Hooke’s "Micrographia," published in 1665, was the first major publication of the Royal Society and is famous for its detailed copperplate illustrations of objects viewed under a microscope.</note>. This theory has been so widely accepted that many authors have since used and supported it; nor have I yet encountered any significant objection against it.

Therefore, it will not be my task now to discuss or further explain that theory. I believe anyone who investigates it closely will find enough reason to be convinced of it. Instead, I will mention some pleasant and beneficial uses of it and suggest some mechanical inventions for supplying the fuel <note>original: "Pabulum"</note>—oil or spirit—at the same rate it is consumed by the lamp's flame, which acts as a great dissolver.

I do not intend here to show a way to make a perpetual lamp. That is a fantasy <note>original: "Chimera"</note> which my hypothesis of flame seems to disprove; for the substance to be dissolved <note>original: "dissolvend"</note> must, in time, be consumed. Rather, I will show a way how to make...

Considering this problem first led me to investigate a counterbalance for liquids or fluids, which is also very useful in hydraulics, as I will have the opportunity to demonstrate later.

I can achieve this through many inventions based on very different principles. Each and every one of these can be adjusted to supply the oil or fuel <note>original: *Pabulum*</note> of the lamp in whatever quantity, manner, and proportion is desired. I will omit all other ways of achieving this effect for now, though several of them are as significant—or even more so—than the ones I mention here. Since I promised on page 32 of my description of Helioscopes <note>An early scientific instrument for observing the sun without damaging the eyes.</note> to publish a design for a counterbalance for liquids, I will explain several ways to use these balances to fulfill every requirement regarding the manner and quantity of oil supplied to the flame.

The main purpose of the counterbalance in this investigation is to keep the surface <note>original: *Superficies*</note> of the liquid (whether oil, spirit of wine <note>An archaic term for high-proof alcohol or ethanol used as fuel.</note>, oil of *turpentine*, or similar) at the same height, regardless of the quantity in the vessel. This ensures the fuel <note>original: *Pabulum*</note> remains at an equal distance from the bottom of the flame. Once the wick or flame is placed at a convenient height or distance above the surface of the oil, it will not be left dry by the receding surface until the entire supply is consumed. However, it is just as easy to design it to supply the oil in increasing or decreasing amounts. These are conveniences that none of the lamps I have encountered so far have offered in a practical way. ...inconveniences, which make it intolerable and disused:

The first is that, though it does supply the oil to the <term>wick</term> <note>the bundle of fiber that draws up the fuel</note>, it does not do so constantly or equally, but rather by starts and surges. After the receptacle around the wick is filled, the <term>surface</term> <note>original: "Superficies"</note> of the oil continues to sink by degrees to a considerable distance below the flame before any more supply is added from the large <term>magazine</term> or <term>repository</term> <note>the main storage tank for the oil</note>. This continues until the air can break in (which it does very unequally), so that sometimes so great a quantity comes down that the receptacle is over-filled and the flame is extinguished. These surges are more unequal the larger the magazine is in proportion to the receptacle by the flame, the greater the quantity of suspended oil, the more air space there is above the oil, and the more thick or sluggish the consistency of the oil is.

The second inconvenience of *Cardan's Lamp* is that the air is apt to expand <note>original: "rarifie"</note> with heat, sometimes driving down so much oil that it overflows the receptacle and chokes the flame.

The third inconvenience is that the wick decays the flame sooner because of the sinking oil, as the level is sometimes a little higher and sometimes lower upon the wick. If the wick rises up into the hollow, dead part of the cone of the flame, the streams and residues of the oil will cake together so as to deaden the flame and greatly diminish its light and heat. However, if the wick is short and allowed to go only a very little way within the lower surface of the flame, it will not be so stopped and caked with those <term>impurities</term> <note>original: "feculencies"</note>. 

The reason for this is evident: for the flame—as I have formerly proved—is nothing but the parts of the oil thinned and raised by heat into the form of a vapor, smoke, or steam. The free air that surrounds this steam keeps it in a cylindrical form and, by its dissolving property, consumes or dissolves those parts of it that are on the outside and closest to the air. Those parts of the body of vapors <note>original: "steams"</note> that rise from the wick—which are in the middle and not in contact with the outward air—are not dissolved or turned into a shining flame by the air until they rise toward the top of the cone of the flame. There, the free air can reach and dissolve them. If these vapors gather around the wick in the center of the cone of flame, they choke, clog, and completely stifle it so that the flame will quickly go out. Anyone can easily discover that this is true if he examines the flame of a lamp or candle with the help of a piece of glass. Because of the glass's transparency, he will plainly perceive that the entire middle of the flame's cone neither shines nor burns. Only the outward surface <note>original: "Superficies"</note> that is in contact with the free and fresh <note>original: "unsatiated," meaning air that has not yet been used up by combustion</note> air burns. The middle parts can be collected in the form of soot or very fine powdered coal dust.

Take, then, a piece of glass—whether window glass, a mirror plate, or the side of a vial, it does not matter; or, what is best of all, a thin plate of selenite <note>a transparent variety of the mineral gypsum</note> or Muscovy talc <note>original: "Muscovia Talk," a type of mica often used for its heat resistance and transparency</note>. Hold it horizontally in the middle of the flame so as to cut off the top or upper part of the cone. Then, immediately—before it becomes choked with soot—look down upon it. You shall plainly see that all the middle parts of the flame and the wick have no shining power or light at all, nor are they dissolved by the air, but remain in the form of soot. Only the surface or outside of the cone burns, shines, and is consumed into and mixed with the surrounding <note>original: "ambient"</note> air.

In the same manner, if you hold the glass or selenite perpendicularly and position it so as to cut the flame through the center <note>original Latin: "per axin coni," meaning "through the axis of the cone"</note> so that the air cannot reach one side of it, you may plainly perceive that the shining part of the flame is only that which is in contact with and "preyed upon" by the free and fresh air. Where that air cannot reach freely without being blocked <note>original: "glutted and satiated"</note> on its way, neither the consumption of the oil nor the heat and light of the flame is produced, but only a sooty, choking, and stifling substance.

Let *F F* in the third figure represent the *wick*, which consists of a great number of very fine *cylinders* or hairs of *cotton* (*f f f*) twisted and laid very close together. The *oil* (having a very great affinity with them) readily works its way into and adheres between these hairs. It is then forced up to a considerable height between them (as high as an inch and a half or two inches) by the pressure of the *air*, which is much greater outside the wick than between those *cylinders* or hairs. If the *oil* is removed from the top by any means, the remaining *oil* in the *vessel* will rise to fill the empty space <note>original: supply the vacancy</note> left by the part drawn off, as is seen in filtration <term>filtration: here the author is describing capillary action, where liquid moves upward through a porous material against gravity</term>. Around the sides of this *wick*, the *oil* will certainly rise, and its *surface* will be concave, as at *G G*. This is because unless there is an affinity between the *oil* and the *wick*, the oil will not rise within it; therefore, any substance that the *oil* does not readily stick to cannot be a suitable material for that purpose.

Now, apply the flame of a lamp or candle—or any other extremely hot substance, such as a glowing piece of iron or copper—to this wick filled with oil. In doing so, the oil in the wick becomes highly heated and expands into vapors, entering the surrounding air as steam (marked <term>h h h h h</term> in the diagram) and filling the surrounding air space (<term>H H</term>). Because these vapors are highly rarefied <note>original: "rarified"; meaning thinned or made less dense</note> and consequently lighter than the surrounding air, the greater weight and pressure of the air carries them upward along the curved lines <term>h i k</term>. At first, these vapors gush out of the wick at right angles, but the pressure of the air quickly turns them into a kind of parabolic curve (<term>h i k</term>). The motion of the particles is fastest at point <term>k k</term>, which is at a certain height.

The speed of this ascent increases somewhat like the speed of descent in falling bodies. I say "somewhat" because if the rising vapors were uniformly lighter than the surrounding air, the physics would be identical; however, since their density and nature change based on circumstances, the comparison is only partial.

To continue the explanation: as these oil vapors rise, if they reach a high enough temperature, they are "preyed upon" and dissolved—or burned—by the surrounding air. This dissolution <note>The author uses "dissolution" to describe the chemical process we now call combustion.</note> has several effects: first, it produces light; second, it generates enough heat to warm the next set of vapors rushing out of the wick, preparing them to be dissolved by the air in turn. The heat from that reaction does the same for a third portion, then a fourth, and a fifth, continuing successively as long as there is oil vapor to be dissolved and plenty of fresh, unsatiated <note>original: "unsatiated"; meaning air that has not yet been "filled" or depleted by the burning process.</note> air to dissolve it. The process of combustion also generates enough heat to draw the next portion of oil up into the wick and expand it into vapor, making it ready to be further heated and dissolved. **D**uring this last great heat wave in **June**, I observed a very noticeable spot <note>original: *Macula*</note> with its immediately surrounding cloudy region <note>original: *Nubecula*, now known as a penumbra</note>, as well as some other less noticeable spots further away, passing over the disk of the Sun. This is especially noteworthy because I am informed that this extraordinary heat has not been unique to **England**, but has been widespread throughout **Europe**. Future reports will inform us of what the weather has been like in other parts of the world.

Upon a second appearance of spots on the Sun's disk at the end of **July** and the beginning of **August**—specifically on **July 29**—about six spots, both large and small, appeared in one cluster with their own cloudy regions or shadows <note>original: *Umbra's*</note>. If he sends me something I think is worth publishing, I will do the same for him and repay him in kind <note>original: "repay him in his own coyn"</note>.

Lastly, since he uses the words "We" and "Us" vaguely, I ask that he explain whether he refers to the *Royal Society* or is simply speaking of himself in the plural. If he means the former, that is untrue, as I can prove with many witnesses. If he means the latter, I do not know what he is familiar with or what has been shared or explained to him.

So, not intending to trouble myself any further with him unless he gives me a reason, I dismiss him with his—
