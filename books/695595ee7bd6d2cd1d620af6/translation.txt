I published my theory <note>original: "Hypothesis"</note> of fire and flame about eleven years ago in Observation 16, pages 103, 104, and 105 of my *Micrographia*. This theory has become so well-accepted <note>original: "so far obtained"</note> that many authors have since used and supported it, and I have not yet encountered any significant objection against it.

Therefore, it is not my purpose now to discuss or further explain that theory; I have no doubt that anyone who investigates it closely will find enough evidence to be convinced. Instead, I intend to mention some enjoyable and beneficial applications of it. The main purpose of the counterpoise in this investigation is to keep the surface <note>original: "Superficies"</note> of the liquid—whether oil, spirit of wine <note>High-proof alcohol/ethanol.</note>, oil of *turpentine*, or something similar—at the same height, regardless of how much remains in the vessel. Furthermore, it is just as easy to design it to supply the oil in increasing or decreasing amounts. Those parts of the body of vapors <note>original: "steams"</note> that rise from the wick—which are in the middle and not in contact <note>original: "contiguous"</note> with the outer air—are not dissolved or turned into a shining flame by the air until they rise toward the top of the cone of flame. There, the free air can reach and dissolve them. Otherwise, by gathering around the wick in the center of the cone of flame, they choke, clog, and completely stifle it so that the flame quickly goes out. Anyone can easily verify this by examining the flame of a lamp or candle using a piece of glass. Because of the glass's transparency, you will plainly see that the entire middle of the cone of flame neither shines nor burns. Only the outer surface <note>original: "Superficies"</note>, which is in contact with the free and unconsumed <note>original: "unsatiated"</note> air, does so. The middle parts can be collected in the form of soot or very fine powdered coal dust.

Take then a piece of glass—whether window glass, a mirror, or the side of a vial, it does not matter—or, best of all, a thin plate of <term>selenite</term> <note>original: "Selenitis"; a transparent, colorless variety of gypsum.</note> or <term>muscovy glass</term> <note>original: "Muscovia Talk"; a type of mica used for windows and lanterns before glass became cheap and common.</note>. Hold it horizontally in the middle of the flame so as to cut off the top or upper part of the cone. Then, immediately, before it becomes choked with soot, look down through it. You will plainly see that the middle parts of the flame and the wick have no shining power or light at all. They are not dissolved by the air but remain in the form of soot. Only the surface or outside of the cone burns, shines, and is consumed into and mixed with the surrounding <note>original: "ambient"</note> air.

In the same manner, if you hold the glass or selenite vertically and position it so as to cut through the flame through the axis of the cone <note>original: "per axin coni"</note>—preventing the air from reaching one side—you can clearly see that the shining part of the flame is only that which is in contact with and consumed by the free and unspent air. Where that air cannot reach freely without becoming exhausted <note>original: "glutted and satiated"</note> along its path, neither the consumption of the oil <note>original: "Oyl"</note> nor the heat and light of the flame is produced—only a sooty, choking, and stifling substance.

To make the explanation of the *phenomena* <note>original: "Phænomena"; observable facts or events.</note> observed regarding the longevity or extinguishing of a *lamp's* flame more clear and easy to understand, allow me to explain how it is produced and maintained through a *diagram* <note>original: "Scheme"</note>, *sketch*, and *description*.

Let *A A* in the second diagram <note>original: "Table"</note> represent a body of *oil*, or any other combustible fluid substance. The *surface* <note>original: "Superficies"</note> of this oil, *B B*, is *horizontal* and nearly flat. [I say nearly flat, because it is always either *concave* or *convex* to some degree, depending on several circumstances—specifically, the size <note>original: "capacity"</note> and nature of the *container* <note>original: "Vessel"</note> *E E* in which it is held. For if the *container* is small and the *oil* has a greater attraction <note>original: "congruity"; a term used in early physics to describe the attraction between molecules of different substances, similar to "wetting."</note> to it than the *air* does, the *surface* of the *oil* will become very concave, especially toward the sides of the *container* as at *C C*. But if the *container* is repelled by <note>original: "incongruous to"</note> the *oil*, the *surface* will be *convex* as at *D D*. I have explained the reasons for this long ago in another work.]

Let *F F* in the third figure represent the *wick*, which consists of a great number of very fine *cylinders* or fibers <note>original: "hairs"</note> of *cotton* *f f f* twisted and laid very closely together. The *oil*—having a very great attraction <note>original: "congruity"</note> to them—easily works its way <note>original: "insinuates itself"</note> into and between these fibers and sticks to them. It is then forced up to a considerable height (such as an inch and a half or two inches) between them by the pressure of the *air*, which is much greater outside the wick than it is between those *cylinders* or fibers. If the *oil* is removed from the top by any means, the remaining *oil* in the *container* will rise to fill the empty space <note>original: "vacancy"</note> left by the part drawn off, as is clearly seen in the process of *filtration* <note>original: "Filtration"; here referring to the way liquid travels through a porous material via capillary action.</note>. The *oil* will certainly rise along the sides of this *wick*, and its *surface* will become concave as at *G G*. This is because unless there is an attraction between the *oil* and the *wick*, the *oil* will not rise within it; therefore, any substance that *oil* does not easily stick to cannot be a suitable material for that purpose.

Now, apply the flame of a lamp or candle—or any other extremely hot substance, such as a glowing piece of iron, copper, or something similar—to this wick, which is now filled with oil. By this means, the parts of the oil in the wick will be heated significantly and expand into vapors in the nearby <note>original: "contiguous"</note> air, forming the streams <term>h h h h h</term> and filling all the surrounding <note>original: "Ambient"</note> airspace <term>H H</term>. These vapors, being highly rarefied <note>thinned out or made less dense</note> and consequently lighter than the surrounding air, are carried upward along the curved lines <term>h i k</term> by the air's greater weight and pressure.

At first, these vapors gush out of the wick at right angles, but they are quickly pushed by the air into a kind of parabolic curve <term>h i k</term>. The motion of the particles within this curve is fastest at <term>k k</term>—that is, at a certain height <note>original: "Altitude"</note>. The speed of their ascent increases in a manner somewhat similar to how heavy objects accelerate as they fall. I say "somewhat" because if the rising vapors were consistently lighter than the surrounding air, the math would be identical; however, since their density <note>original: "rarefaction"</note> and nature change depending on the circumstances, the comparison <note>original: "Analogy"</note> is only partially accurate.

To continue with the explanation: I maintain that these rising streams of oil, if heated enough, are "preyed upon" and dissolved or burned by the surrounding air. This dissolution <note>the process of the oil chemically reacting with the air, which we now call combustion</note> has several effects. First, it produces light. Second, it produces enough heat to warm the following streams of vapor rushing out of the wick, preparing them to be dissolved by the air in turn. The heat from that reaction then triggers the same process in a third stream, then a fourth, then a fifth, and so on—continuing as long as there is oil vapor to be dissolved and plenty of fresh, "unsatisfied" <note>original: "unsatiated"; meaning air that has not yet reacted with fuel and is still capable of supporting combustion</note> air to dissolve it.

**D**uring the recent period of intense heat in June, I observed a very prominent sunspot <note>original: *Macula*</note> with its immediately surrounding penumbra <note>original: *Nubecula*, literally "little cloud"</note>, as well as several other less noticeable spots at a further distance, passing across the disk of the sun. I found that the main spot was nearest the center when the heat was most intense; the heat increased as the spot approached the center and decreased as it moved away from it. 

It may, therefore, be worth observing in the future whether similar weather occurs whenever such sunspots appear. It seems not entirely unlikely that the body of the sun itself may be much hotter when such eruptions appear, as those sunspots <note>original: *Maculæ*</note> often end by turning into bright patches <note>original: *Fæculæ*</note>. I am further encouraged in this theory because I am informed that this extraordinary heat has not been limited only to England, but has been very widespread throughout Europe. What the temperature has been in other parts of the world, further reports will tell us.

Upon a second appearance of spots on the disk of the sun at the end of July and the beginning of August, the heat of the weather increased to a very high degree again. Specifically, on July 29, about six larger and smaller spots appeared in one cluster with their own penumbras or shadows <note>original: *Umbra's*</note>. The heat diminished as they moved toward the edge <note>original: *Limb*</note> of the sun and grew fainter. However, since they disappeared—namely <note>original: *viz.*</note> on August 4—it has also been exceedingly hot, even though I do not see any spots today, August 7. It is possible, therefore, that other parts of the body of the sun may be experiencing an extraordinary flare-up <note>original: *inflammation*</note>...

## POSTSCRIPT.

**T**HE Publisher of the *Transactions* <note>original: "Transactions." Referring to Henry Oldenburg (c. 1619–1677), the Secretary of the Royal Society and publisher of its journal, *Philosophical Transactions*.</note>, in the issue for *October* 1675, endeavors to cover up previous injuries done to me by piling on new ones. He does this with such anger that he has cast aside both integrity and discretion; otherwise, he would not have claimed that it was as certain that none of my watches succeeded as it was that I had made them several years ago. For how could he be sure of such a negative claim? I have not shared my inventions with him since I began to view him as someone who treats information as a commodity to be traded.

Next, when he says I made them without publishing them to the world in *print*, he is being dishonest <note>original: "prevaricates."</note>.
