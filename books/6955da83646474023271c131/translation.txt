She could hardly be pulled away. Prostrating herself before all things, she adored them; she kissed the stone of the Resurrection which the angel had moved from the mouth of the monument. The very place of the body in which the Lord lay was narrow, yet she licked it with a faithful mouth as if thirsting for sacred waters. What tears, what groans of sorrow she poured out, all Jerusalem is witness, and the Lord himself whom she entreated is witness. Then she proceeded to Bethlehem, entering into the cave of the Savior, where she saw the sacred <unclear>inn</unclear> of the Lord. And with me listening, she swore with the eyes of faith that she saw the infant wrapped in swaddling clothes, crying in the manger, the Lord being adored by the Magi, the star shining from above, the Virgin Mother, and the diligent foster-father <note>Joseph</note>. She saw the shepherds coming by night to see the Word which was made flesh, even then chanting the beginning of the Gospel of John: *In the beginning was the Word.* She saw the infants slain while Herod raged, and Joseph and Mary fleeing into Egypt. 

Hail Bethlehem, House of Bread, in which was born the Bread that descended from heaven! Hail Ephrata, most fertile region whose fruit is God! David the prophet spoke: *We will enter into his tabernacle, we will adore in the place where his feet have stood.* Behold, I, sent and passing through this distance, have been heard to kiss the manger in which the Lord cried as an infant. I have prayed in the tomb where the Virgin Mother saw the infant. "This is my rest, for the Lord has chosen it; here will I dwell." 

Furthermore, she was so overcome by humility that those who saw her, despite the fame of her name and her deeds, would not believe she was who she was, but rather a lowly servant. And though she was surrounded by a great crowd of her own people, and you <note>Eustochium, Paula's daughter</note> were with her, she remained the least among them in her own eyes. From that way of life until the day of her falling asleep <note>death</note>, she made an impossible possession of her poverty. She did not go to the public baths, even in the most burning fevers; she slept upon the hardest ground, a harsh rest for her limbs. If she ever allowed herself rest, it was only after vigils spent almost entirely in prayer day and night. She was so pleasing in her ways that she even overcame the most serious <unclear>obstacles</unclear>. 

We believe she is now with us in spirit, that she might pray for our order and favor us. The Gospel of the place said: "The faith of the Lord is troubled." By God's command, she who often wore purple, fine linen, and silk was now often cast down. Her body was afflicted, and she often wept over these things; I fear to speak of her long labors. She took up the coarsest linen and the poorest clothing, never changing them unless they were worn out. She gave her desire only to the Lord. If such a woman, who preserved her chastity in that shroud, is seen as an example for all noble matrons, it is because she conducted herself so that no one dared to speak ill of her. I confess my error: I used to argue against her excessive generosity, citing the Apostle: *Not that others should be eased and you burdened, but by an equality, that your abundance may be a supply for their want.* But she did this with such joy, wanting to give all.

The passion was dissolving, as the monk <note>Jerome himself</note> witnesses. She did not seek her own name to be increased, but like a physician, she cared for others. She did not treat her daughters as mistresses, but wrapped them in her own care. I have found many who, in times of tribute, were helped by her. Two things I can say of her: she gave to others and sought the way for herself. She labored to pour out these stones <note>metaphor for her works</note> which were established with faith and trust.

John says: "The city of the Great King is built up." On feast days, she used so little oil in her food that one could estimate from that what she thought of wine, sauces, fish, milk, honey, and eggs, which she excluded as luxuries. In these things, some think they practice abstinence if they gorge their bellies but maintain a pretense of modesty. Not so with her. There are some whisperers—a vile class of men—who always look for something to gnaw on, whose minds and brains are alienated from the truth. To them she replied: "I am made a theater to the world, to angels, and to men; we are fools for Christ's sake." If the wisdom of God is foolishness to men, she was content. 

She divided her time so that at every hour the Psalms were sung, and she humbled herself with the gentlest spirit. She restrained the wantonness of the flesh in young women with prayers and discipline. She taught that the cleanliness of the body and clothing represents the uncleanness of the soul. She thought nothing of her own comfort. When she provided all things largely to others, she exhibited a different face to herself. If she herself was sick, she did not indulge herself; she was as strict with herself as she was kind to others. 

I recall that in the month of July, in the height of a most burning fever, she nearly passed away. When the doctors suggested some relief, I asked Pope Epiphanius to urge her to drink a little wine, so that her health might be restored. She sensed the trap and smiled at him. When the Pope had left after many hours of persuasion, she was asked what she had done; she replied that she would rather die than drink wine. She was firm in her habits, nourished in these things. Even when suffering from various pains or the pressure of illness, she remained constant. She held the scriptures in her memory. Indeed, she loved the history of the Word, where she could say: "Your words are sweeter than honey to my mouth."

Niklas Khrypfs or Krebs, of Cusa or Cusanus, son of a fisherman, was born at Cues on the Mosel, educated at Deventer and at Padua, and after filling minor ecclesiastical positions in Germany, and serving with great distinction at the Basel Council, was sent by Pope Eugene IV on a special mission to Constantinople. In 1448 he was made cardinal. A good Greek scholar, he brought back from the East many important manuscripts, chiefly mathematical, the translation of which into Latin he supervised. At Padua Toscanelli <note>Paolo dal Pozzo Toscanelli, the famous astronomer and cartographer</note> influenced the direction of his studies. He was the most distinguished modern predecessor of Copernicus, and held that the earth was a star and that it moved:
> "The earth cannot be fixed, but moves like other stars" <note>original: "Terra non potest esse fixa, sed movetur ut aliae stellae"</note> (quoted by Heller, no. 5963, i, p. 216, from the Basel, 1565, ed.; cf. *On learned ignorance*, bk. 2, ch. 11, in no. 7466).
On his work in connection with the reform of the calendar (the 3rd tractate in this vol.) see Heller, *ibid.*, p. 218. He was one of the pioneers in modern mathematical physics, and his studies on measurements and specific gravity are of the first importance; see the dialogue *On static experiments* at the end of no. 7466. In this he records the famous experiment, antedating Hales <note>Stephen Hales, 18th-century plant physiologist</note> by 200 years, of weighing earth and seeds, then the resulting plants, their ashes, and the earth in which they had grown.

Spiritually a man of wide vision, Nicholas was haunted by a dream of the possible union not alone of Latin and Greek Christians but of Muslims, Jews and Gentiles. "Beneath all diversity of religious forms and ceremonies he descried one spirit, and in him is to be found the original of the saying, whose authorship has been so much disputed, 'There is therefore but one religion and worship for all who flourish in intellect' <note>original: "Una est igitur religio et cultus omnium intellectu vigentium"</note> [On the peace of faith, leaf d 1]"; and he even suggested a Parliament of Religions for the establishment of religious concord (J. A. Smith, unpublished address; cf. also Webb, no. 6526, p. 349).

There is an extensive literature on De Cusa; see the list in no. 3500, p. 10, and several works noted on a flyleaf at the end of this incunabulum <note>A book printed before 1501</note>.

For two reproductions of 16th-century pictures of supposed scenes in De Cusa's life see S. Reinach, *Repertory of paintings of the Middle Ages and the Renaissance*, vol. ii, 1907, p. 637. One is in the Church of Neustift, the other in the Augustinian Convent at Brixen, De Cusa's diocese.

### ¶ Preface.

In this volume are contained certain treatises and books of the highest contemplation and learning, published among many others by a man of distinguished memory, the most excellent and learned Nicholas of Cusa <note>original: "Nicolao de Cusa"</note>, Cardinal-Priest of the Holy Roman Church under the title of Saint Peter in Chains. Regarding the supreme praise and virtue of this man—as the formerly venerable Giovanni Andrea <note>Giovanni Andrea Bussi (1417–1475), a humanist scholar and bishop</note>, Bishop of Aleria and secretary of the Apostolic Library (a most eloquent and learned man who enjoyed his familiarity and social circle), testifies in a certain place—whoever wishes to write of him will very easily find obvious and ready material to praise. However, where such a writer should stop, or what quality he should prefer among his supreme merits or ornaments, he will never be able to judge exactly, if he is wise. For as that man reports, he was of such great goodness while he lived that a better man was never born: a most sharp enemy of all vices and a public foe of pride and ambition, an adversary to all such things; immutable in the integrity of his soul; most patient in honest labors even in declining old age; and admirable in his readiness for doing good, showing favor, and returning thanks, so that he seemed to have been born for everyone else's sake, and least of all for his own. Truly, he possessed such an abundance of all learning that whatever subject happened to arise for discussion on the spot, he would always discourse upon it with such plenty that he was judged to have studied that faculty alone. The man himself was eloquent and skilled in Latin <note>original: "latinus"</note> beyond expectation; he held in his memory all histories, not only ancient ones but those of the middle period, both old and more recent. In the mathematical disciplines, no one in his time was more learned than he. He had learned Civil Law and Pontifical <term>Canon</term> Law correctly and purely; and as he possessed an immortal and eternal memory, he could recite from memory all the sanctions and decrees of the Fathers, and the opinions of the learned besides, as if he had only just then emerged from the workshop of those studies. He was a most sharp debater of Aristotelian philosophy, but in Christian theology, he was the highest interpreter and master, and a most wise priest of the heavenly mystery. Many works of this most celebrated man’s supreme genius are extant, some of which are gathered in this volume and are designated here in order by their titles:

# A Treatise by the most Reverend father in Christ and lord: Lord Nicholas of Cusa, Cardinal-Priest of the title of Saint Peter in Chains, Bishop of Brixen, to the abbot and brothers in Tegernsee

## On the Vision of God.

I will now reveal what I previously promised to you, most beloved brothers, regarding the facility of mystical theology <note>A form of theology focused on the direct, subjective experience of God's presence rather than just rational study.</note>. For I judge you—whom I know to be led by the zeal of God—worthy for this treasure to be opened to you. It is indeed very precious and most fruitful. I pray first that the supernal Word and the almighty Speech, which alone can reveal itself, be given to me, so that I may be able to describe, according to your capacity, the wonders revealed beyond all sensible, rational, and intellectual sight. I will endeavor, in a most simple and common way, to lead you by the hand experimentally into that most sacred darkness. While you are there, sensing the presence of the inaccessible light, each of you shall try, in the way granted by God, to draw nearer, and here to forestall with a most sweet libation that supper of eternal happiness to which we are called in the Word of Life through the Gospel of the ever-blessed Christ.

### Preface

To carry you humanly toward divine things, it is necessary to do so through a certain likeness. Among human works, I have found no image more suitable to our purpose than that of the "all-seeing" figure, such that the face, through subtle pictorial art, behaves as if it were looking at everything around it. Many such excellent paintings can be found, such as that of the archer in the Nuremberg market; and in Brussels by the great painter Rogier <note>original: "rogeri maximi pictoris" - referring to the Flemish master Rogier van der Weyden</note> in that most precious panel kept in the town hall; and in Koblenz <note>original: "Confluentie"</note> in my chapel of the Veronica; and in Brixen <note>original: "Brixine"</note> in the castle, an angel holding the arms of the Church; and many others elsewhere. However, lest you fail in the practice—which requires such a sensible figure—I am sending to your Charity a small panel that I was able to obtain, containing the figure of the All-Seer, which I call the "Icon of God" <note>original: "eiconam dei"</note>. You shall fasten this in some place, say on a north wall, and you brothers shall stand around it, a short distance away. Each of you shall look at it, and each will experience that, from whatever place he looks at it, he seems to be seen by it alone. It will seem to the brother positioned in the east that the face looks eastward; to the one in the south, southward; and to the one in the west, westward.

First, therefore, you will marvel at how this can be: how it looks upon each and every one of you at the same time. For the imagination of the one standing in the east cannot grasp how the gaze of the icon is turned toward another region, such as the west or south. Then, let the brother who was in the east place himself in the west, and he will experience the gaze fixed on him in the west just as it was before in the east. And since he knows the icon is fixed and unchanged, he will marvel at the change of the unchanging gaze. And if, while keeping his eyes fixed on the icon, he walks from west to east, he will experience the gaze of the icon continuously moving with him; and if he returns from east to west, it will likewise not leave him. He will marvel that it moved immovably, nor will his imagination be able to grasp how it moves similarly with someone else meeting him with a contrary motion. And when, wishing to test this, he makes a brother cross from east to west while he himself goes from west to east, and asks the one he meets if the gaze of the icon turned continuously with him, he will hear that it moved in the opposite way just the same. He will believe him; and unless he believed, he would not grasp that this was possible. And so, by the revelation of the one reporting it, he will come to know that that face looks upon all, even those walking in contrary motions.

And while he observes how that gaze deserts no one, he sees that it takes such diligent care of each person, as if it cared for him alone who experiences himself being seen, and for no other—to such an extent that it cannot even be conceived by the one it looks upon that it could take care of another. He will see also that it takes the most diligent care of the smallest creature just as of the greatest, and of the whole universe. From such a sensible appearance as this, I propose to elevate you, most loving brothers, through a certain practice of devotion into mystical theology, by allowing three things necessary for this purpose.

### ¶ The inspection of the appearance is verified in the most perfect God. Chapter One.

In the first place, I judge it must be presupposed that nothing can appear concerning the gaze of the icon of God <note>original: "eiconē dei"</note> which is not more true in the actual gaze of God. For God, who is the very summit of all perfection and greater than can be thought, is called *Theos* <note>Greek for God</note> for this reason: because He beholds all things. Just as a painted gaze appears in an image, looking at all things and each thing simultaneously—since this belongs to the perfection of sight—this gift cannot truly belong to the Truth <note>God</note> less than it belongs to the icon or the appearance. For if one sight is sharper than another among us, and one discerns nearby things while another discerns more distant ones, and one reaches the object slowly while another reaches it quickly: there is no doubt that the Absolute Sight, from which all the sight of those who see arises, excels all sharpness, all speed, and every power of all those who see, or who could ever become seers. For if I look toward "Abstract Sight" <note>Sight considered in its pure essence, stripped of physical limitations</note> which I conceive in my mind as free from all eyes and organs, and consider how that abstract sight, in its "contracted" <note>term: contractio (contraction) — the way a universal principle is limited or "shrunk" into a specific, physical form</note> state—as when seers see through that sight—is limited to time and regions of the world, to singular objects and other such conditions; and since abstract sight is free, abstract, and absolute from these conditions, I well understand that it is fitting for Sight not to look at one object more than another. Although in "contracted" being, it follows that while sight looks at one thing, it cannot look at another, or absolutely at all things. God, however, as He is true uncontracted Sight, is not less than can be conceived by the intellect regarding abstract sight; rather, He is unproportionably more perfect. Therefore, the appearance of the gaze in the icon can approach the summit of the excellence of Absolute Sight less than our own concept can. That, therefore, which appears in that image, must be held without hesitation to exist excellently in Absolute Sight.

### ¶ Absolute Sight embraces all modes. Chapter 2.

Observe after this that sight varies in seers because of the variety of its contraction. For our sight follows the passions of the organ and the mind. At one time someone looks lovingly and happily; later, sorrowfully and angrily; now as a child, then as a man; then seriously and as an old man. Absolute Sight, however, free from all contraction, embraces all and each of these modes of seeing at once and at the same time, as the most adequate measure and truest exemplar of all gazes. For without Absolute Sight, there can be no contracted sight. It embraces in itself all modes of seeing, and all just as much as each, and remains entirely free from all variety. For in Absolute Sight, all modes of contracted seeing exist uncontractedly. For every contraction is in the Absolute, because Absolute Vision is the "contraction of contractions" <note>A paradoxical Cusan concept: the Absolute is the ultimate limit or "tightness" that defines all other things</note>. For contraction is uncontractable. Therefore, the simplest contraction coincides with the Absolute. Without the Absolute, nothing is contracted. Thus, Absolute Vision is in all sight, because through it all contracted vision exists, and without it, it could not exist at all.

### From the vision of God, providence, grace, and life are called eternal.

->Chapter 4<-

Approach now, you brother contemplator <note>The author addresses the monks of Tegernsee for whom this was written.</note>, to the icon of God, and first place yourself to the east, then to the south, and finally to the west. And because the gaze of the icon looks at you equally from every side and does not desert you wherever you go, a speculation will be excited in you; you will be provoked and will say: "Lord, now in this image of yours, I behold your providence by a certain sensible experience." For if you do not desert me, who am the vilest of all, you will never fail anyone. Thus, you are present to all and to each, just as being is present to all and to each, without which they cannot exist. For so you, the absolute Being of all, are present to all things, and your way of being is present to all other ways of being. And thus, each thing guards its own being, so that it would rather let the being of all others go to destruction than its own. For so you, Lord, look upon everything that exists, so that it cannot be conceived by anything that exists that you have any other care than that it alone should exist in the best way it can. And because all other things that exist are for this purpose alone: to serve so that that which you look upon may be at its best. You do not permit me, Lord, to conceive by any imagination that you, Lord, love anything else more than me, since your gaze does not desert me alone. And since the eye is where love is, then I experience that you love me, because your eyes are most attentively upon me, your little servant. Lord, your seeing is loving. And just as your gaze looks upon me so attentively that it never turns away from me, so too is your love. And since your love is always with me, and your love, Lord, is nothing other than you yourself who love me, hence you are always with me, Lord. You do not desert me, Lord; you guard me on every side, because you take the most diligent care of me. Your being, Lord, does not abandon my being. For I exist only insofar as you are with me. And since your seeing is your being, I therefore exist because you look upon me; and if you were to withdraw your face from me, I would no longer subsist. But I know that your gaze is that greatest goodness which cannot fail to communicate itself to every capable subject. You, therefore, will never be able to desert me as long as I am capable of receiving you. It belongs to me, therefore, to make myself continually more capable of you as much as I can. I know, however, that the capacity which provides union is nothing but likeness <note>original: "similitudo"</note>. Incapacity, however, comes from unlikeness. If, therefore, I render myself similar to your goodness in every possible way, according to the degree of likeness, I shall be capable of truth. You have given me being, Lord, and a being of such a kind that it can continually render itself more capable of your grace and goodness. And this power which I have from you, in which I hold a living image of the power of your omnipotence, is free will <note>original: "libera voluntas"</note>, through which I can either enlarge or restrict the capacity for your grace. To enlarge it, indeed, through conformity, when I strive to be good because you are good; when I strive to be just because you are just; when I strive to be merciful because you are merciful; when all my effort is turned toward you, because all your effort is turned toward me; when I look only to you most attentively and never turn away the eyes of my mind, because you embrace me with a continuous vision; when I turn my love to you alone, because you, who are charity, are turned to me alone. What is my life, O Lord, if not that embrace by which your sweetness of love so lovingly enfolds me? I love my life above all things because you are the sweetness of my life. I contemplate now, as if in a mirror, in an image, or in a riddle <note>original: "in speculo in eicona in enigmate" — a reference to 1 Corinthians 13:12 regarding our limited earthly perception of divine truth</note>, that eternal life which is nothing other than the blessed vision. In this vision, you never cease to see me most lovingly, even unto the innermost depths of my soul. And for you, "to see" is nothing other than to give life; it is to continually pour in your sweetest love. It is to set me on fire for your love by the infusion of that love, and by that fire to nourish me; by nourishing me, to kindle my desires; and by kindling them, to give me to drink from the dew of gladness. By that drinking, you plant within me the fountain of life, and by planting it, you cause it to increase and endure. You communicate your immortality and bestow the unfading glory of the celestial, highest, and greatest kingdom. You make me a sharer in that inheritance which belongs to the Son alone, and establish me as a possessor of eternal happiness, where the garden of all delights <note>original: "ortus deliciarum" — likely referring to the Garden of Eden or Paradise</note> that can be desired is found. Nothing better than this can be imagined, not only by any man or angel, but it cannot even exist in any mode of being. For it is the absolute maximum of every rational desire, than which nothing greater can be.

### **This vision of God is called providence, grace, and eternal life. Chapter V.**

Oh, how great is the abundance of your sweetness, which you have hidden for those who fear you! For it is an inexplicable treasure of the most joyful gladness. To taste your sweetness is to apprehend, through an experiential touch, the pleasantness of all delightful things in their very source. It is to reach the essence <term>ratio: the underlying logic or principle</term> of all desirable things in your wisdom. Therefore, to see the absolute essence which is the essence of all things is nothing other than to taste you, God, with the mind, since you are the very sweetness of being, of life, and of intellect. What else is your "seeing," Lord, than that you look upon me? You look with an eye that desires to be seen by me. By seeing me, you grant that you—the hidden God—may be seen by me. No one can see you except insofar as you grant that you be seen. Nor is seeing you anything other than that you see the one who sees you. I see in this image of yours <note>The author is likely looking at a specific painting or icon where the eyes seem to follow the viewer</note> that you are ready, Lord, to show your face to all who seek you. For you never close your eyes; you never turn them elsewhere. And although I may turn away from you because I turn myself entirely toward something else, you nevertheless do not change your eyes or your gaze because of this. If you do not look upon me with an eye of grace, the fault is mine, because I am divided from you by turning away and turning toward something else which I prefer to you. Yet even then, you do not turn yourself away entirely, but your mercy follows me to see if I might ever wish to return to you so that I might be capable of grace. For the reason you do not look upon me <note>i.e., with favor</note> is that I do not look upon you, but rather reject and despise you. O infinite piety! How unhappy is every sinner who abandons you, the vein of life, and seeks you not in yourself, but in that which is nothing in itself—and which would have remained nothing if you had not called it out of nothing. How foolish is he who seeks you, who are goodness itself, and while seeking you, retreats from you and turns his eyes away. Every seeker seeks nothing but the good; and everyone who seeks the good and retreats from you, retreats from that which he seeks. Every sinner, therefore, wanders from you and goes far away. But whenever one returns to you, you meet him without delay. Even before he looks back at you, you cast the eyes of mercy upon him with fatherly affection. For your "having mercy" is nothing other than your "seeing." Therefore, your mercy follows every man as long as he lives, wherever he may go, just as your gaze never deserts anyone. As long as a man lives, you do not cease to follow him, and with sweet and internal admonition, you urge him to cease from error and turn back to you so that he may live happily. You, Lord, are the companion of my pilgrimage. Wherever I go, your eyes are always upon me. To see, for you, is to move. Therefore, you move with me and never cease from movement as long as I move. If I rest, you are with me. If I ascend, you ascend; if I descend, you descend. Wherever I turn, you are there. You do not desert me in the time of tribulation. Whenever I call upon you, you are present. For to call upon you is to turn myself toward you. You cannot be missing from one who turns toward you, nor can anyone turn toward you unless you are present first. You are present before I turn toward you; for unless you were present and urged me, I would be entirely ignorant of you, and how could I turn toward one whom I did not know?
