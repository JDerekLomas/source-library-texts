# CHARLES

By Divine clemency elected Emperor of the Romans, forever August: King of Germany, of Castile, of Leon, of Aragon, of Navarre, of Naples, of Sicily, of Mallorca, of Sardinia, of the Indian Isles and the Ocean Sea, Archduke of Austria, Duke of Burgundy, of Lorraine, of Brabant, of Limburg, of Luxembourg, etc. Count of Flanders, of Artois, of Burgundy Palatine, and of Hainaut, of Holland, of Zeeland, of Ferrette, of Haguenault, of Namur, etc. Prince of Swabia, Marquis of the Holy Empire, Lord of Frisia, of Salins, of Mechelen, of the Utrecht lands, and Conqueror in Asia and in Africa.

To all those who shall see these present letters, greetings. On behalf of our dear and faithful legal advisor and historiographer, Master Henry Cornelius Agrippa, Doctor of both laws <note>Doctor of both Civil and Canon Law, a high academic distinction.</note>, Knight, it has been explained to us: That the said petitioner recently wrote and composed four books and volumes titled in Latin:
1. **On Occult Philosophy.** <note>original: "De occulta Philosophia"</note>
2. **Declamation on the Vanity of Sciences.** <note>original: "De Vanitate scientiarum Declamatio"</note>
3. **Commentaries on the Brief Art of [Ramon] Lull.** <note>original: "In artem breuem mundi Lullij commentaria"</note>
4. **And an Abbreviated Table, Orations and Letters.** <note>original: "Et Tabula abbreuiata. Orationes & Epistolae"</note>
Which books and volumes the said petitioner would willingly have printed in our lands and lordships: but he fears that some printers of our said lands, having obtained a copy of each of his volumes, might counterfeit them, which would cause him ~~great regret and damage~~ <note>The OCR shows a strike-through in the original drafting or a correction in the text.</note> if we did not provide him with a grant of grace. Humbly and most humbly petitioning that, considering what has been said, it please us to grant and accord him that he may have the said four books and volumes printed by such printer in our said lands as he sees fit, without others being allowed to print them within the term of the next six years; and to have our letters patent <note>An official public document issued by a monarch to grant a right, monopoly, or title.</note> issued to this effect. For this reason, having considered these things: And after our beloved and faithful masters George de Themseke, Provost of Cassel, and Henry de Sunderhuisen, counselors and ordinary masters of requests of our household, by our ordinance, have seen, visited, and signed the said books at length. Favorably inclining toward the petitioner and his request, we have, by good advice and deliberation of the council, granted, consented, and agreed: we grant, consent, and agree by giving him leave and license of special grace through these presents that he may and shall be able to have the said four books and volumes printed in such number and quantity and by such printer of our lands as he sees fit. And so that this printer does not lose his pains, costs, and expenses which he will need to incur for the said new printing and what depends on it, we have forbidden and do forbid by these presents all other printers and residents for the period and term of the next six years to print or have printed the said books or any of them without the consent of the said printer, upon pain of confiscation of the books and volumes they have thus printed, and beyond this, to be punished and corrected arbitrarily. Thus we give mandate to our beloved and faithful Chief Presidents and people of our Private and Great Councils, the Chancellor and people of our council in Brabant, the President and people of our chamber of council in Flanders, and all our other justices, officers, and subjects or their lieutenants whom this may concern, and to each of them in his own right and as it shall pertain to him, that concerning our present grace, grant, leave, and license during the time and in the manner stated, they allow the said petitioner together with the said printer to fully and peacefully enjoy and use it without causing or allowing any disturbance or hindrance to the contrary. For such is our pleasure; in witness of which we have caused our seal to be attached to these presents. Given in our city of Mechelen, the 14th day of January in the year of grace one thousand five hundred and twenty-nine. <note>This date likely follows the "Easter Style" of dating; in modern calendars, it would be January 1530.</note> And of our reigns, namely of the Romans the 11th, and of Castile and others the 15th.

Signed on the reverse by the Emperor in his council.

### TO THE READER.

<image-desc>Ornamental woodcut initial 'N' containing a seated figure in classical robes, possibly a scholar or a sibyl, holding a book or a tablet.</image-desc>

I DO NOT DOUBT that the title of our book, **On Occult Philosophy** <note>original: "de Occulta philosophia"</note>, or **On Magic** <note>original: "siue de Magia"</note>, will by its rarity entice many to read it. Among these, some—weakened by a mind of distorted opinions—and many more who are malicious and ungrateful toward our talent, will approach it. In their reckless ignorance, they will take the name of "magic" in its worst sense. Scarcely having seen the title, they will cry out that we are teaching forbidden arts, sowing the seeds of heresy, providing a stumbling block to pious ears and a scandal to noble minds; they will say that I am a sorcerer, a superstitious man, a person possessed by demons, because I am a <term>magus</term> <note>A term that could mean a wise man or a sorcerer; Agrippa spends this preface arguing for the former definition.</note>. 

To these people, I might respond that among learned men, the word "magus" does not signify a sorcerer, nor a superstitious man, nor one possessed by demons, but rather a wise man, a priest, and a prophet. The **Sibyls** <note>Ancient prophetesses of the Greek and Roman world whom Renaissance Christians believed had predicted the coming of Jesus.</note> were "magas" (female magi), and thus they prophesied so clearly concerning Christ. Furthermore, the **Magi** <note>The "Wise Men" from the Nativity story in the Gospel of Matthew.</note>, through the wonderful secrets of the world, recognized that Christ, the author of the world itself, had been born, and they were the first of all to come and worship Him. Thus, the very name of "magic" was accepted by philosophers, praised by theologians, and not even disliked by the Gospel itself. 

I believe, however, that these censors of such stubborn arrogance would sooner forbid themselves the Sibyls, the holy Magi, and even the Gospel itself before they would accept the name of "magic" into their good graces. They so carefully consult their own consciences that neither **Apollo**, nor all the **Muses**, nor even an **angel from heaven** could rescue me from their curse. To such people, I now give this advice: do not read our writings, do not try to understand them, and do not remember them. For they are harmful; they are poisonous. The **gate of Acheron** <note>original: "Acherontis ostium"; Acheron was a river in the Greek underworld. Agrippa is mockingly warning that the book is a gateway to hell for the closed-minded.</note> is in this book; it "speaks stones"—let them beware lest it knock out their brains.

But for you who come to read with an open mind, if you apply as much discernment of prudence as bees do when gathering honey, then read in safety. For I think you will receive no small benefit and a great deal of pleasure. If you find anything that does not please you, set it aside and do not use it; for I do not approve of those things for you, but merely report them. Yet, do not reject the rest for that reason. Even those who look into the volumes of physicians find poisons written alongside antidotes and medicines. 

I confess, moreover, that magic itself teaches many superfluous things and many portents intended for curious display; leave these aside as vain, but do not remain ignorant of their causes. However, those things which can be done for the utility of men—to avert bad outcomes, to destroy sorcery <note>original: "maleficia"</note>, to cure diseases, to drive out phantoms, and to preserve the excellence of life, honor, and fortune, without offending God or injuring religion—who would judge those things to be anything less than useful and even necessary? 

But because I have warned you that I have written many things by way of reporting rather than by way of affirming (for this seemed necessary so that we might skip over as few things as possible), and because we have followed many of the opinions of the **Platonists** and other Gentile philosophers where they suggested arguments for our method of writing: therefore, if there be any error anywhere, or if anything be said too freely, forgive my youth. For I composed this work when I was less than a young man <note>Agrippa began the first draft around 1509-1510 when he was 23; it was not published in this final form until he was in his 40s.</note>, so that I might excuse myself and say...

# EPISTLE.

...for if anyone had cleared it from the charge of impiety, or handed it down to us pure and sincere; for among the more recent writers whom I have seen—**Roger Bacon** <note>original: "Rogerium Bachonem"; a 13th-century English friar and philosopher known for his work in optics and alchemy.</note>, **Robert the Englishman** <note>original: "Robertum Anglicum"; likely referring to a 13th-century astronomer.</note>, **Peter of Abano** <note>original: "Petrum Apponum"; an Italian physician and philosopher accused of heresy and magic.</note>, **Albert the German** <note>original: "Albertum Teutonicum"; better known as Albertus Magnus, the teacher of Thomas Aquinas, to whom many magical works were falsely attributed.</note>, **Arnald of Villanova** <note>original: "Arnoldum de Villa noua"; a medieval physician and alchemist.</note>, **Anselm of Parma**, **Picatrix the Spaniard** <note>original: "Picatricem Hispanum"; referring to the author of the *Picatrix*, a famous Arabic handbook of talismanic magic.</note>, **Cecco d'Ascoli the Florentine** <note>original: "Cicchum Asculum"; an astronomer and poet burned at the stake for heresy in 1327.</note>, and many others, though writers of obscure name—when they promise to hand down <term>magic</term> <note>Agrippa seeks to redefine magic as "Occult Philosophy," a natural and divine science.</note>, they have provided nothing but certain ravings supported by no reason, or superstitions unworthy of all honest men. 

Because of this, my spirit was stirred within me; and on account of both admiration and indignation, I too wished to philosophize. I believed I would perform a not unpraiseworthy task—I who from early youth have always stood as a curious and intrepid explorer concerning the operations of wonderful effects and those full of mysteries—if I were to restore **Magic** itself, that ancient discipline of all wise men, redeemed from the errors of impiety, purified, and adorned with its own reasons, and vindicate it from the injury of those who slander it. 

Although I have long deliberated this with myself, I never until now dared to descend into this arena; however, after the conversation we shared at **Würzburg** <note>original: "Herbipoli"; the Latin name for the city of Würzburg, where Agrippa met Trithemius in 1509.</note> concerning these matters, your excellent skill and learning, and your burning exhortation, gave me boldness and spirit. Therefore, having selected opinions from philosophers of proven faith, and having purged the introduction of the wicked—who, lying with feigned science, taught that the traditions of the magi were to be sought from those reprobate books of darkness as if from the very inner shrines of miracles—I have recently composed three books on magic, condensed with brevity, and inscribed them with the less offensive title ***On Occult Philosophy***. 

These I now submit to your examination (you who possess the fullest expertise in these matters) to be corrected, judged, and evaluated, so that if anything is written in them by me that might tend toward an insult to nature, an offense to the heavenly beings, or an injury to religion, you may condemn the error. But if the scandals of impiety are dissolved and purged, may you protect the tradition of truth. And so may you be willing to deal with these books of ours, and with magic itself, so that nothing that could be beneficial is hidden, nor anything approved that could do nothing but harm; so that finally these books, approved by your examination, may be worthy to go forth into public one day under happy auspices, nor fear to undergo the judgment of posterity. 

Farewell, and be indulgent toward these bold undertakings.

### JOHANNES TRITEMIUS, ABBOT OF ST. JAMES OF 
### Würzburg <note>original: "Herbipolis"</note>, formerly of Sponheim, to his 
### Henry Cornelius Agrippa of Nettesheim, greetings and 
### love.

<image-desc>A decorative square woodcut initial 'O' depicting a tonsured cleric or scholar (representing Johannes Trithemius) seated at a desk with a book, surrounded by foliage and architectural elements.</image-desc>
With what great pleasure we received your work, most distinguished Agrippa, titled *On Occult Philosophy*, which you offered to us for examination via the bearer of these presents—neither the mortal tongue can ever express, nor the pen of a writer reveal. We are moved by the greatest admiration for your extraordinary erudition, for as a young man, you penetrate such secrets and mysteries—hidden even from many of the most learned men—and have been able to bring them to light not only beautifully and truly, but also with propriety and elegance.

Wherefore, first of all, we thank you for your goodwill toward us, and if we are ever able, we shall undoubtedly return the favor with all our strength. Your work, which none even of the most learned men will be able to praise enough, we approve. Furthermore, we advise, petition, and request you with all possible urgency to continue your studies toward higher things, just as you have begun, and not to allow such excellent powers of your mind to grow cold through idleness. May you always exercise your labor toward better things, and demonstrate the light of true wisdom—by which you are greatly and divinely illuminated—even to the ignorant. Nor let the consideration of any scoundrels <note>original: "nebulonum"</note> hold you back from your purpose, of whom it is truly said: 

> **The weary ox sets its foot down firmly.** <note>A Latin proverb suggesting that those who are dull or slow-witted are often the most stubborn and judgmental of others' brilliance.</note>

Since, in the judgment of the wise, no one can be truly learned who has sworn allegiance only to the basics of a single faculty. But the Divinity has gifted you with a mind both broad and sublime; therefore, do not imitate oxen, but birds. Nor should you think that you must linger over particular details, but confidently turn your mind toward universal things; for a person is considered more learned the fewer things they are ignorant of. Indeed, your mind, being fully capable of all things, should be reasonably occupied not with a few lowly matters, but with many and more sublime ones. 

However, we warn you to keep this one precept: communicate common things to common people, but communicate higher and secret matters only to higher and secret friends. **Give hay to the ox, but sugar only to the parrot.** <note>A metaphor for intellectual discretion: do not waste high-level philosophy on those who lack the refinement to understand it.</note> Understand my meaning, lest you be subjected to the heels of oxen, as has happened to many. Farewell, happy friend, and if there is anything in our power that can be of use to you, command it and it shall be done without delay. So that our friendship may gain strength daily, write to us often, and send some of your late-night studies <note>original: "lucubrationum"; referring to works produced by the light of a lamp.</note> at once, we pray. Again, farewell. 

From our monastery at Würzburg <note>original: "cœnobio Peapolitano"</note>. The 9th day of April, in the year 1510.

*SUCH IS THE RENOWN OF YOUR ILLUSTRIOUS*
*fame, most reverend and most illustrious Prince,*
*such is the magnitude of your virtues and the splendor of your learning,*
*your frequent exercise in the finest letters, and your grave speech joined*
*with solid prudence, your elegant readiness in speaking, your knowledge*

*you far greater than all of them because of those heroic and most illustrious*

*learned and the more loving of virtue a man is, the more he desires to*
