# The Collected Illustrations of the Three Powers

<note>Original Chinese title: *Sancai Tuhui* (三才圖會). This is the title page of a monumental, 106-volume encyclopedia published in 1609 during the Ming Dynasty. It was compiled by the scholar Wang Qi and his son Wang Siyi to serve as a comprehensive visual record of all known knowledge.</note>

<term>The Three Powers</term> <note>The term *Sancai* refers to the three essential elements of the cosmos in traditional Chinese philosophy: Heaven, Earth, and Humanity. By using this title, the authors signaled their intent to document every aspect of the natural and human worlds.</note>

<note>The physical book is a classic example of "thread-bound" binding (*xianzhuang*). It features a dark blue protective cover with a white vertical title strip pasted on the left, which was the standard presentation for scholarly works in 17th-century China.</note>

<summary>The cover and title page of the Sancai Tuhui, a seminal 1609 Chinese encyclopedia. It introduces the concept of the "Three Powers"—Heaven, Earth, and Humanity—as the organizing framework for all worldly knowledge.</summary>

### The Lu Fish

On Mount Di <note>original: 抵山 (Di shan)</note> there is a fish that is shaped like an ox. It dwells on land, has a tail like a snake, and has wings located on its shoulders <note>original: 膊 (bo)</note>. Its voice is also like the sound of an ox lowing. Its name is the Lu <note>original: 鯥 (lu)</note>. It dies in the winter and comes back to life in the summer. Eating its flesh prevents swelling diseases <note>original: 腫疾 (zhong ji); likely referring to edema, bloating, or skin tumors</note>.

### The Flying Fish

In the rivers of Mount Ren <note>original: 魜山 (Ren shan)</note> there are many flying fish. ### The Wenyao Fish

At Mount Taiqi <note>original: 泰噐山 (Tàiqì shān)</note>, the Guan Water <note>original: 觀水 (Guān shuǐ)</note> flows forth and empties into the Flowing Sands <note>original: 流沙 (Liúshā); a term used in ancient geography to refer to the desert regions of the West</note>. In these waters, there are many Wenyao fish <note>original: 文鰩魚 (Wényáo yú); literally "Patterned Flying-Carp"</note>. They possess the wings of a bird and are covered in azure patterns <note>original: 蒼文 (cāng wén)</note>. By day they swim in the West Sea, and by night they travel to the North Sea. Their flavor is sweet and sour; eating their flesh can cure madness <note>original: 狂 (kuáng); refers to delirium or insanity</note>. When they are seen, it is an omen of a bountiful harvest <note>original: 大稔 (dà rěn)</note>.

### The Human-Fish <note>original: 人魚 (rényú). While this term means "mermaid" in modern Chinese, in this classical context, it refers to a creature identified by modern scholars as the Chinese Giant Salamander</note>

From **Mount Longhou** <note>original: 龍侯山 (Lónghóu shān); a mythical peak located in the northern regions according to ancient geography</note>, the waters of the **Wangjue River** <note>original: 王決之水 (Wángjué zhī shuǐ)</note> emerge and flow eastward into the **Yellow River** <note>original: 河 (Hé); in classical texts, this character used alone almost always refers specifically to the Yellow River</note>. 

Within these waters are many **human-fish**. Their shape is like that of a **giant salamander** <note>original: 𩵵 (tí); an archaic variant of the character for the giant salamander, *Andrias davidianus*</note>, but they possess **four feet**. Their voice sounds like a **small child’s cry** <note>The giant salamander is still known in China today as the "infant fish" (*wāwāyú*) because its distress call remarkably resembles the wailing of a human baby</note>. Eating its flesh can cure **mental ailments** <note>original: 痴疾 (chī jí); a term encompassing idiocy, dementia, or severe confusion</note>.

### The Bleak <note>original: 鰷魚 (tiáoyú). This fish is commonly identified today as the Sharpbelly or White Minnow (*Hemiculter leucisculus*), a slender freshwater fish native to East Asia</note>

Its shape is **narrow and long**, appearing just like a **strip** or a slender branch <note>original: 條 (tiáo); the name of the fish is a direct reference to its linear, twig-like body</note>. For this reason, it is called the *tiao*. 

Today, in the region between the **Yangtze and Huai Rivers** <note>original: 江淮 (Jiāng-Huái); a major cultural and geographic region in eastern China</note>, it is referred to as the **Tong-fish** <note>original: 鮦魚 (tóngyú)</note>. 

Its nature is to **float** near the water's surface, similar to the **silver carp** <note>original: 鱮 (xù)</note>. It is also said that the silver carp and the Bleak are both described as being **fine-grained** <note>original: 磏 (lián); a term suggesting a smooth, white, or whetstone-like quality</note>; the essential meaning behind these various names is the same.

### The Jiaoyu <note>original: 鮫魚 (jiāoyú). In modern Chinese, this term refers to sharks. However, historical Chinese natural histories often described the *jiao* as a hybrid-like creature with a "fish body and a snake tail" or, as seen here, possessing turtle-like features</note>

The **Jiaoyu** is a sea fish. Its shape resembles a **soft-shelled turtle** <note>original: 鼈 (biē)</note>, yet it **lacks feet**. The patterns on its back are like those of a **tortoise**, though they are **coarse and rough** <note>original: 麤錯 (cūcuò). This refers to the abrasive, sandpaper-like texture of sharkskin, which was historically known as shagreen</note>. Within its skin, there are "pearls" <note>likely a reference to dermal denticles, the small, tooth-like scales that cover sharkskin</note> which can be used to **decorate sword hilts**. 

When its young are startled, they **enter the mother’s belly** <note>This is a common historical observation regarding ovoviviparous sharks, which appear to carry and protect their young internally</note>. It is said that while the dragon’s pearl is found in the chin, the snake’s pearl is in the mouth, the turtle’s pearl is in the feet, the fish’s pearl is in the eye, and the clam’s pearl is in the belly, the **Jiaoyu’s pearls are found within its skin**.

# The Wei Fish <note>original: 鮇 (wèi). While this character sometimes refers to a type of small fish like a loach, in the context of "stone fish" from Longxi, it refers to fossilized fish, likely of the *Lycoptera* genus common in northern China's sedimentary layers</note>

The **Wei** is also a kind of fish. Its **head is sharp and pointed**, and its color is **yellow and black**. Its body has **fins** <note>original: 鬛 (liè). In this context, it refers to the dorsal or pectoral fins visible in the stone</note>; it resembles a fish, yet it is **not a fish** <note>The author recognizes that these are stone objects, though they possess all the features of living creatures</note>.

In **Longxi** <note>An ancient administrative region located in modern-day Gansu Province</note>, there is a place named **Yulong** (Fish-Dragon). It produces **"stone fish"** <note>original: 石魚 (shíyú). This is one of the earliest Chinese descriptions of fossils</note>. One must **dig into the ground** to retrieve the stones; when the stones are **broken open**, the fish are found inside. 

Most of these are of the **Wei** variety, appearing **dark purple** <note>original: 紺 (gàn). A deep, violet-black or charcoal color often seen in carbonized fossils</note> as if **painted with lacquer**. The **scales and fins** are **strikingly lifelike**. If one **burns them**, they still produce a **fishy odor** <note>This reflects a historical belief that fossils retained the "spirit" or organic essence of the living creature</note>.

### The Needle-mouth Fish <note>original: 針口魚 (*zhēnkǒuyú*). This refers to the needlefish or more likely the halfbeak (family *Hemiramphidae*), characterized by its elongated lower jaw and slender body</note>

The **Needle-mouth fish** has a mouth that **resembles the tip of a needle**. There is a **red spot** located on its head. Along **both sides of its belly**, extending from the head all the way to the tail, there is a **white path** <note>Likely referring to the lateral line or the prominent silvery band common in many small forage fish</note> that shines like **the color of silver**. 

Its body is **slender** and its tail is **forked**. It reaches a length of **three or four inches** <note>A traditional Chinese inch (*cun*) is approximately 3.3 centimeters, making the fish about 10–13 cm long</note>. These fish appear in the **midst of the sea** during the **second lunar month** <note>This corresponds to early spring, typically February or March in the Gregorian calendar, which marks the seasonal appearance of these fish in coastal waters</note>.

### River Porpoise <note>original: 江豚 (*jiangtun*), commonly known today as the Yangtze finless porpoise</note>

The **River Porpoise** is the same as the **River Pig** <note>original: 江猪 (*jiangzhu*)</note>. Its **appearance is like that of a sturgeon** <note>The original text uses the character *chun* (純), which in biological contexts of this era often refers to sturgeons or large, smooth-skinned fish</note>, and it **emits a sound from within its nose**. On **top of its head is a hole** that **spouts water straight up**. It **leaps in and out of the waves**; if it is seen, it **signifies that wind is coming** <note>Sailors traditionally viewed the porpoise's activity as a warning of an approaching storm</note>. It is **without scales**, **black in color**, and has **much fat and oil**. Because there is a **union of passages within its brain** <note>A reference to the unique structure of the blowhole</note>, it is also named the **Union Fish** <note>original: 并魚 (*bingyu*)</note>. To catch them, **people take a porpoise calf and tie it up in the water**; the **mother will then come of her own accord** to be with it, allowing her to be captured. Its **young are like snakehead fry** <note>original: 鱧魚子 (*liyu zi*)</note>, gathering in **schools of tens of thousands** to **follow the mother** as she travels.

### Shrimp

Shrimp <note>original: 蝦 *xia*</note> have many antennae, are excellent swimmers, and enjoy leaping. In the "Explanation of Fish" section <note>referring to the *Shi Yu* chapter of the *Er Ya*, the oldest surviving Chinese dictionary</note>, the *hao* <note>original: 鰝</note> is defined as a "large shrimp." These emerge from the sea and can reach lengths of ten, twenty, or even thirty feet <note>original: 一二三丈 *zhang*; while a *zhang* is roughly 3.3 meters, this likely refers to large lobsters or legendary sea creatures</note>. 

Their antennae are several feet long. In the Guangdong region, there are "Red Shrimp Cups," and in ancient times, people used these long antennae to craft hairpins and walking sticks.

Today, in the Fujian region <note>original: 閩中 *Minzhong*</note>, there are "Five-Colored Shrimp" that are over a foot long and display five distinct colors. There is also the "Plum Shrimp," which appears during the plum rains <note>the early summer rainy season in East Asia</note>; they are colloquially called "Seasonal Shrimp." 

"Reed Shrimp" are green in color; legend says they are transformed from reed mats. "White Shrimp" and "Green Shrimp" are each named for their respective colors. "Mud Shrimp" are said to be transformed from rice flowers; they are mostly found in the mud of rice paddies. Another name for them is the "Seedling Shrimp." 

Furthermore, in Hanzhong, there is the "Shrimp Aunt" <note>original: 蝦姑 *xiagu*; the Mantis Shrimp</note>, which is shaped like a centipede and is also called the "Tube Shrimp."

### Crabs

The <term>crab<note>original: 蟹 (xiè)</note></term> possesses eight legs and two claws. Its shell is often marked with twelve spots of a deep carmine color. The fullness or emptiness of the meat within its belly corresponds to the phases of the moon. <note>In traditional Chinese lore, it was believed that the meat of crustaceans grew and shrank in synchronization with the lunar cycle, being at its peak during the full moon.</note> One distinguishes between male and female by the size of the umbilical flap on the underside: the male is called the <term>hen-yang<note>original: 狠蝪; literally "ruthless lizard"</note></term>, while the female is called the <term>bo-dai<note>original: 博帶; literally "wide belt," referring to the wider abdominal flap of the female crab</note></term>.

There are many different varieties of this creature. The <term>qiumou<note>original: 蝤蛑; likely the Mud Crab or Swimming Crab</note></term> is quite large, reaching over a foot in length; its two claws are so powerful that it is said it can even fight a tiger. The <term>yongjian<note>original: 擁劔; literally "Sword-Clutcher"</note></term> has one large claw and one small claw; it uses the larger one for combat and the smaller one for feeding. The <term>huxun<note>original: 虎蟳; Tiger Crab</note></term> is large with patterns resembling a tiger's stripes; it drifts along, sinking and rising with the waves.

Smaller crabs, such as the <term>pengqi<note>original: 彭蜞; a type of marsh or fiddler crab</note></term>, have white shells. When they sense the tide is about to arrive, they emerge from their burrows and raise their claws as if to welcome the water; these are called <term>zhaochao<note>original: 招潮; "Tide-Beckoner," the common name for the Fiddler Crab</note></term>. Those that follow the receding tide and walk slowly through the mud are called <term>tantu<note>original: 攤塗; "Beach-Spreader"</note></term>. Those that reside inside the shells of snails and gather food for the snail are known as <term>luonu<note>original: 蠃奴; "Snail Slave," referring to the Hermit Crab</note></term>. <note>Ancient observers often misinterpreted the hermit crab's use of a shell as a form of servitude to the original snail.</note>

There is also the <term>pengqi<note>original: 髼蜞; a variant name for a marsh crab</note></term>, also known as the <term>yiwang<note>original: 倚望; "Leaning and Watching"</note></term>. It resembles the standard marsh crab but is green in color; when it advances, it frequently raises its two claws to look out. The common folk call it the <term>wangchao<note>original: 望潮; "Tide-Watcher"</note></term>. Other varieties include the *jiepu*, the *shagou* ("sand dog"), and the *luhu* ("reed tiger"). The *jiepu* is larger than the marsh crab and is marked with black spots.

### The Mussel

The *Piya* <note>original: 埤雅; an 11th-century Chinese encyclopedia of flora and fauna compiled by Lu Dian.</note> calls it the "Bang." The *Erya* <note>original: 爾雅; the oldest surviving Chinese dictionary and encyclopedia, dating to the 3rd century BCE.</note> refers to it as the "Chan" <note>original: 𧒄 (chan); an ancient term for a large bivalve mollusk.</note>. 

The larger specimens reach a length of two to three inches. Inside its belly, there is a tiny <term>commensal crab<note>original: 蟹子 (xiezi). This refers to the pea crab (family Pinnotheridae), which is often found living inside live bivalves.</note></term> no bigger than an elm seed. These two creatures share a single body and live together in harmony. At times, the tiny crab emerges to forage for food, then returns to the safety of the shell.

The "pregnancy" of the pearl within its body corresponds to the waxing and waning of the moon—growing as the moon fills and shrinking as it fades. It is also known as the <term>Shen<note>original: 蜃; a legendary giant clam believed in Chinese mythology to exhale vapors that create mirages (hence the word for mirage: "shen-lou").</note></term>.

### Shellfish

Shellfish move by carrying their shells upon their backs, and there are many different varieties. Those found in rice paddies are somewhat large and feed only on dark mud. Those found in mountain streams are extremely small; they feed on moss and are quite clean. There are "fragrant snails" and "mother-of-pearl snails." Many foreign nations blow into conch shells as instruments; the people of Linyi <note>An ancient kingdom in central Vietnam, also known as Champa</note> blow them when going into battle. Ancient people used shellfish designs as ornaments for doors and gates, signifying the desire for the home to be as tightly and securely sealed as a shell. # Shells

Shells come from <term>Rinan<note>An ancient commandery located in what is now central Vietnam.</note></term> and the <term>Rising Sea<note>A historical name for the South China Sea.</note></term>. They are categorized as <term>armored insects<note>Traditional Chinese classification for shelled creatures like mollusks and crustaceans.</note></term>. Their flesh is like that of a tadpole, possessing both a head and a tail. Because their backs are the primary part used, they are called *Bei* <note>original: "貝" (shell). The text suggests a linguistic link to the word for "back" (背, bèi), as they sound identical.</note>. 

In ancient times, shells were used as currency, and tortoise shells were treated as treasures. When the Qin dynasty rose, they abolished shells and implemented metal coins. 

**Types of Shells:**
*   **The Purple Cowry:** Measuring a foot in length, its pattern resembles red lightning amidst black clouds. It is used to heal illnesses.
*   **The Pearl Cowry:** Possessing a plain background with red and black markings. It is used to brighten the eyesight.
*   **The Ribbon Cowry:** Featuring a green background with green patterns. It is used to dissipate "Qi blockages" <note>Internal obstructions or respiratory congestion in traditional medicine.</note>.
*   **The Rosy Cloud Cowry:** Having black patterns with yellow streaks. It is used to treat <term>prolapsed hernias<note>Referred to in the text as "tuishan," a general term for swellings in the lower abdomen or scrotum.</note></term>.

There are varieties of lesser quality than these, such as the **Goiter-beaked Shell** and the **Cicada-backed Shell**, which are used to expel heat and reduce water retention. The largest shells are as big as wheels. Shells from the South Seas can also be as small as pearls or gravel, appearing startlingly white. 

**Properties and Effects:**
Shells are "cold" in nature and sweet in taste. They can stop water-based toxins. However, ancient lore attributes specific psychological and physical effects to different varieties:
*   The **Floating Shell** makes a person speak very little.
*   The **Washing Shell** makes a person easily startled.
*   The **Sui Shell** <note>original: "雖貝"</note> causes internal tumors or masses.
*   The **Chewing Shell** causes miscarriages.
*   The **Bounty Shell** makes a person forgetful.
*   The **Dreamy Shell** makes children foolish and women lustful.
*   The **Jade Shell** induces children to steal.
*   The **Withered Shell** strengthens one's willpower.

### Mussels

Mussels <note>original: 殼菜 *kecai*, literally "shell vegetable"</note>, also known as "bland vegetables" <note>original: 淡菜 *dancai*; so named because they were often dried without additional salt</note>, are produced in the regions of Fujian and Siming <note>Modern-day Ningbo, Zhejiang province</note>. ### Goose Barnacles

The goose barnacle <note>original: 龜脚菜 *guijiaocai*, literally "turtle-foot vegetable"</note>, also known as the *shijie*, grows on rocks within the sea, clinging to the stones like oyster beds. Because its shape resembles the foot of a turtle, it was given this name; it looks like a human fingernail attached to a fleshy limb. It is also known by the names "Immortal’s Palm" and "Buddha’s Hand Cockle." In the spring and summer, it produces sprouts resembling seaweed and even features flower-like growths. The barnacles found in Siming are particularly plump and delicious. This is the same creature praised in Guo Pu’s <note>A famous scholar and poet of the Jin Dynasty (276–324 AD)</note> *River Rhapsody*, which describes how the *shijie* barnacle blooms in accordance with the seasons.

### Edible Shellfish and Their Properties

The **Clam** <note>original: 蛤蜊, *géli*</note> has a shell described as thick and round; its meat is similar to that of the *che’ao* <note>a larger species of hard clam</note>. It is effective at clearing "sand" from the system, stimulating the appetite <note>literally: "opening the stomach qi"</note>, and neutralizing the effects of alcohol poisoning. If cooked with radish, its "pillars" <note>the adductor muscles, similar to scallops</note> will detach from the shell easily.

**Blood Clams** <note>original: 蚶, *hān*</note> originate from <gloss>Siming</gloss> <note>an ancient name for the Ningbo region</note>. There are two varieties: one is cultivated by people in flooded rice paddies, and the other grows naturally in the coastal mudflats without human intervention, which is called the "Wild Blood Clam." The shells are large and dark-colored <note>literally: "sooty black"</note>. The meat is firm, and the edges of the mantle are bluish. When the shells are used in medicine, they are called **Ark-shells** <note>literally: "Tile-ridge shells"; original: 瓦壟子, *wǎlóngzǐ*</note>.

The **Earth-iron Clam** <note>original: 土鐵, *tǔtiě*</note>—also known as the "Sand-arm" or the "Sand-cloak"—has a thin, green shell with a white tail. Its flavor is excellent. Those harvested from Siming are considered the highest quality.

**Razor Clams** <note>original: 蟶, *chēng*</note> live within the sea mud. They are roughly the size of a finger and about three inches long. The meat is white and the shell is thin; the two halves of the shell remain slightly open at both ends.

**Corbicula** <note>original: 蜆, *xiǎn*</note> resemble mussels but are smaller. They are white in color with thin shells and are found in the sea. The "Yellow Corbicula" has a yellow shell and is found in the tidal mud. <gloss>They are particularly plump during the fourth lunar month.</gloss>

### The Toad

Toads give birth through their mouths. They have large bellies and black backs, and their skin is covered in many <term>warty bumps<note>Original: "feilei" (痱磊). This refers to the characteristic glandular protrusions on a toad's skin.</note></term>. Their movement is slow and deliberate.

Common folklore also suggests that the toad "cherishes its soil" <note>This refers to a strong homing instinct or a deep connection to its birthplace.</note>. Even if one is captured and taken to the distant outskirts of a city, it will return to its original home after a single night.

### <term>Toads</term>, <term>Frogs</term>, and <term>Tree Frogs</term>
<note>The original headings use the terms Xiama (蝦蟆), Zao (竈), and Min (黽). While these terms often overlap in historical Chinese, the author here attempts to categorize them as distinct species based on appearance and behavior.</note>

The **Toad** <note>original: Xiama (蝦蟆)</note> has black spots on its back and a small body. It is capable of jumping to catch all manner of insects and is a skilled singer. This is the very creature that **Emperor Hui of Jin** <note>Reigned 290–307 AD; he is famously remembered in Chinese history for his perceived lack of intelligence, reportedly asking whether the croaking frogs he heard were crying for "public or private" reasons.</note> heard while in the **Hualin Garden**. 

There is another variety that resembles the toad but has longer hind feet; it is green in color with wide, staring eyes as if in a fit of rage. This is called the **Zao frog**. There is yet another kind called the **Min**, also known as the **Gengmin**. It is excellent at leaping and bounding. Like the Gengmin, it is particularly irritable, and its croaking is loud enough to grate on the human ear. Although the common people often consider the **Toad** and the **Zao frog** to be the same creature, the **Records of the Grand Historian** and the **Book of Han** <note>The standard historical texts of ancient China.</note> record that in the autumn of the fifth year of the **Yuanding era** <note>112 BC, during the reign of Emperor Wu of Han.</note>, the Zao frogs and the Toads fought a battle against one another. Therefore, we know they are not the same creature.

### Butterflies

**Butterflies** <term>Jiadie; specifically referring to the Nymphalidae family, but used here for butterflies generally</term> have powdery wings and antennae. They are also known as *hudie* <note>The most common Chinese term for butterfly</note>, "wild moths," or "wind butterflies." There are some varieties as large as bats, which are either black or marked with blue-green spots; these are called **Phoenix Children** <term>Fengzi; likely referring to the Swallowtail butterfly</term>.

They are creatures that excel at transformation. The green caterpillars found among vegetables during the spring crawl upon the walls of houses or onto grass and trees, where they secure themselves with silk. If one observes them for a while, they develop sharp, angular protrusions <note>original: "guijiao" — this refers to the formation of the chrysalis or pupa</note>. After six or seven days, the back of the casing splits open, and the creature molts, emerging as a butterfly. 

The large butterflies scatter their eggs upon citrus trees, which then become green caterpillars. After a long period, they leave their crawling state and become large butterflies again.

The text of *Liezi* <note>A foundational Taoist text attributed to Lie Yukou, c. 4th century BCE</note> states: "The roots of the Bird’s Foot plant <note>original: "niaozu" — likely the bird's-foot trefoil</note> become grubs, and its leaves become butterflies." This is perhaps not mere fiction <note>The author is reflecting on ancient theories of spontaneous generation versus metamorphosis</note>. 

The *Supplement to Notes on Ancient and Modern Things* <note>original: "Xu Gujin Zhu" — a natural history text</note> says: "Species such as bees and butterflies have no females; they all smell with their antennae, for the antenna is indeed their nose." <note>This reflects a historical misunderstanding of insect biology, where the lack of obvious external genitalia led scholars to believe the creatures were monosexual or reproduced through "influence."</note>

### Potter Wasps

The varieties of the <term>potter wasp (蠮螉, *Yēwēng*): A type of solitary wasp known for building mud nests</term> are extremely numerous. Those with yellow bodies and slender waists are called hemp-wasps (*Xǐ-yēwēng*). Their waists are exceptionally thin. These "slender-waisted wasps" build their nests on small trees or under the eaves of houses; the nests all hang upside down.

There is also a variety with a thin waist and a black body called the earth-wasp (*Tǔ-yēwēng*). These are also known as the <term>*Guǒluǒ* (果臝): A classical name for the wasp used in ancient poetry</term>. They resemble wood-wasps but are larger and build their nests in the ground. Today, people also call them "barbarian wasps" (*Hú-yēwēng*).

The honey-wasp (*Mì-yē*) resembles other wasps but is smaller and produces honey. Their physical form is small and slightly yellow; generally, their waists and abdomens are well-proportioned, appearing similar to flies or cicadas. Twice a day, they fly out and then reconvene; this gathering is known as the "Drunken Court" (*Zuìyá*).

The **Green Dragonfly** <note>original: *qingling* (青蛉). This term generally refers to dragonflies and damselflies of the order Odonata.</note> likes to fly and gather above the surface of the water. They come in three colors: green, red, and yellow.

### The Spider

Spiders cast their webs across the four corners of eaves, shaped much like a square fishing net <note>original: 罾 (zeng), a traditional Chinese lifting net used for fishing</note>. The spider stations itself in the center; as soon as a flying insect brushes the web, the spider immediately twitches the silk with its legs, ensuring the prey cannot break free. 

The naturalist Tao Hongjing <note>A renowned 5th-century physician and alchemist known for his commentaries on Materia Medica</note> noted that there are dozens of spider species, while the *Erya* <note>The oldest extant Chinese dictionary, dating back to the 3rd century BCE</note> records seven or eight varieties. 

Today, there are "grass spiders" with five-colored bodies and long legs. Once their web is finished, they use thick silk to weave dozens of layered folds stretching toward the four corners. Some suggest this is a form of protective magic <note>original: 厭勝 (yansheng), a practice of using charms or ritual actions to suppress evil influences or ward off pests</note> intended to ward off other insects. 

It is also said that there are "earth spiders." The smaller ones are known as the *xiaoshao* <term>xiaoshao: a classical name for a long-legged spider, often associated with the harvestman or daddy long-legs</term>. Those with particularly long legs are popularly called "Little Happiness" <note>original: 喜子 (xizi)</note>. In the regions of Jingzhou and Heinei, people call them the "Mother of Happiness." If this insect descends and lands on a person's clothing, it is believed to be a lucky omen signifying that a dear guest or relative is about to arrive.

# The Praying Mantis
### Including the Egg Case

The praying mantis is known as the **Ax-Insect** <note>original: *fuchong* (斧蟲); so named because its raptorial forelegs resemble the shape of a hand-axe.</note>. <note>original: "Lougu"</note>

### The Fly

Flies are fond of rubbing their front legs together, which resembles the action of twisting a rope. For this reason, the written character for "rope" <note>original: 繩 *sheng*</note> is derived from the character for "fly" <note>original: 蠅 *sheng*—the author is noting a phonetic and visual connection between the two words</note>. They also enjoy rubbing their hind legs together, grasping their wings as if fanning themselves. 

There is a variety known as the "green bottle fly"; its head is red like fire, and its back appears as if it is carrying gold. It is a creature of filth that can "turn white into black" <note>This refers to a famous poetic metaphor where the buzzing of flies represents how slanderers can confuse right and wrong</note>. The "housefly" is smaller than the green bottle fly, and its color is a true deep azure. There is yet another type that is neither green nor azure, commonly known as the "flesh fly" <note>literally "hemp fly" *maying*, referring to its mottled grey patterns</note>. In the summer, these flies enjoy swarming around the human body; if driven away, they immediately return. Consequently, it is common to call a loathsome or annoying person a "flesh fly." 

The sound they make is produced entirely by their wings. The *Classified Compendium* <note>original: 類從 *Leicong*</note> states that flies are born from ash. If a fly should drown in water, placing it into ash will bring it back to life in just a moment. This is precisely what the *Huainanzi* <note>An influential philosophical and scientific encyclopedia from the 2nd century BCE</note> refers to when it says, "rotting ash gives birth to flies."

### Mosquitoes

Mosquitoes are flying insects that bite humans. They possess a long proboscis shaped like a needle. By nature, they detest smoke; if one burns <term>mugwort<note>original: 艾 (ai); a fragrant medicinal herb often dried and burned to repel insects</note></term> to smoke them out, they will scatter and flee. ### The Moth

The moth resembles a yellow butterfly but is smaller. There is a variety that is known for fluttering around lamps and flying at night, called the "flying moth" or the "light-seeker," which also belongs to this same category.

## The Cockroach (<term>Feilian</term> <note>In modern Chinese, this refers specifically to the cockroach, though historical texts often used it to describe various stinking beetles or shield bugs that affected crops.</note>)

The *fei* is the "plate-carrying" <note>Referring to the shield-like structure (pronotum) that covers the insect's head.</note> stinking bug; the name is also written with the character *fei* (蜚). It resembles the *guang* beetle but is lighter and smaller. It is capable of flight and lives among the grasses. During the eighth and ninth months <note>Late autumn in the lunar calendar.</note>, sensing the coming cold, many enter into human dwellings. 

The plate-carrier likes to gather upon rice plants in the early morning to feed on the rice flowers. Farmers usually rise early to pick them off and move them elsewhere. By the time the sun rises, they have all scattered. Because they eat the rice flowers and possess a foul odor that can cause <unclear>toxic</unclear> inflammation, they prevent the crops from flourishing. For this reason, the *Spring and Autumn Annals* <note>A foundational Chinese historical text recording significant events and omens.</note> specifically records the appearance of these insects.

### The Inchworm

The **inchworm** is a "bending and stretching" insect. ### Gadflies

There are several varieties of gadflies <term>meng: a general term for large, blood-sucking flies like horseflies</term>. ### Silkworms

Silkworms are creatures of <term>Yang (the solar, active, and dry principle in nature)</term>. They detest moisture; they eat [mulberry leaves] but do not drink. In appearance, their mouths resemble those of a horse, and their mottled skin patterns resemble those of a tiger.

Silkworms raised for a second brood are called <term>Original Silkworms (Yuan Can)</term>, also known as <term>Wei Silkworms</term>. According to the *Fangyan* <note>An ancient Chinese dictionary of regional dialects</note>, *Wei* means "fine" or "small." The *Records of Wu* states that in the Nanyang Commandery, silkworms produce silk eight times a year. The *Records of Linyi* states that in the Jiuzhen Commandery, silkworms mature eight times a year.

The *Records of Yongjia Commandery* states that in Yongjia, there are eight generations of silkworms:
*   The <term>Molting-Precious silkworms (Tuizhen)</term> produce silk in the third month.
*   The <term>Zhe-Tree silkworms (Zhecan)</term> <note>original: "柘蠶"; these are silkworms fed on the leaves of the Wild Mulberry or Cudrania tree</note> begin producing silk in the early fourth month.
*   The <term>Yuan silkworms</term> produce silk in the fourth month.
*   The <term>Beloved-Precious silkworms (Aizhen)</term> produce silk in the fifth month.
*   The <term>Aiji silkworms</term> produce silk at the end of the sixth month.
*   The <term>Cold-Precious silkworms (Hanzhen)</term> produce silk at the end of the seventh month.
*   The <term>Four-Emergence silkworms (Sichu)</term> produce silk at the beginning of the ninth month.
*   The <term>Cold silkworms (Hancan)</term> produce silk in the tenth month.

The commentary says that for all silkworms raised in a second cycle, the previous generations are referred to as <term>Zhen (Precious)</term>. There are also <term>Wild Silkworms</term> that are not raised by humans; they live on the mulberry trees, eat the leaves, and spin their cocoons there.

### Ants

The large ones are colloquially called **horse ants** <note>original: "馬蟻" (mǎyǐ); likely referring to large carpenter ants.</note>. In the *Lesser Calendar of the Xia* <note>An ancient agricultural text tracking seasonal changes.</note>, they are referred to in the twelfth month as **Black Stallions** <note>original: "玄駒" (xuánjū); a classical name for ants reflecting their black color and tireless movement.</note>. 

Ants possess the <term>virtues of ruler and subject</term> <note>Refers to the hierarchical social structure of the colony, which ancient Chinese scholars compared to human Confucian society.</note>. They can sense the coming of rain; when heavy rain is about to fall, the ants will seal the entrances to their mounds. The philosopher <term>Baopuzi</term> <note>Ge Hong (c. 283–343 CE), a famous Daoist alchemist.</note> stated: "Ants possess the wisdom to conquer the weak." 

Ants that have wings are called **officers** <note>original: "尉" (wèi).</note>. These are transformed from the **white ants** <note>original: "白蟻" (báiyǐ); usually referring to termites.</note> found within rotting wood. When the weather is clear and warm, they emerge in swarms to fly, though they cannot fly very high. Soon after, they shed their wings, which fall in great numbers to the ground, and then they perish.

### The Snail

The snail resembles a small, white-colored shellfish. It lives among the grasses and trees found near ponds and marshes. It has two horns upon its head; it uses these to touch its surroundings while moving, but retracts them instantly if it is startled. Both its head and its tail can be fully withdrawn and hidden within its shell.

During the peak of summer, in the heat of the midday sun, it hangs itself beneath the leaves of trees. It often climbs to great heights, but once its supply of slime is exhausted, it immediately withers and dies. 

One name for it is the "Snail-Bug" <note>original: 蝸蟲 *wōchóng*</note>. Because it possesses two horns, it is named a "Cow" <note>original: 牛 *niú*, which forms the second half of the common Chinese word for snail, *wōniú*</note>. It is also known by the names *Xiyu* <note>original: 螔蝓</note>, *Lingli* <note>original: 陵蠡</note>, the "Earth Snail" <note>original: 土蝸</note>, and the "Attached Snail" <note>original: 附蝸</note>.

There is another variety that lacks a shell but possesses horns; its body is slightly larger, and it lives in low, damp places. After a long period of rain, these creatures crawl across the walls of houses during the day, leaving behind trails of silver. It is said that these are the elders of the snails. This is what the common people call the slug <note>original: 蜒蚰 *yányóu*</note>.

### Leech

The **Leech** is an aquatic creature. In the *Erya* <note>The *Erya* is the oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, dating back to the 3rd century BCE.</note>, it is referred to by the name *ji*. People today call them <term>water leeches</term>. The larger ones, which can reach a foot in length, are called <term>horse leeches</term> or sometimes *maqi*. 

Depending on their habitat, they are distinguished as <term>stone leeches</term>, <term>grass leeches</term>, or <term>mud leeches</term>. All of these varieties are capable of attaching themselves to the bellies of humans, oxen, or horses to suck their blood. In the most severe cases, they can even burrow into the flesh to reproduce, which causes great harm. 

Another name for them is *mahuang*. There is also a variety that is thin and long which likewise enters human flesh; however, if one attempts to pull it out, it snaps easily. This type is colloquially known as the <term>lanqi</term> <note>literally, "rotting horse-leech."</note>.

### The Centipede

The *Guangya* <note>An expanded dictionary and encyclopedia compiled in the 3rd century CE</note> states that the <term>langqu<note>An archaic name for the centipede; literally "dung-beetle larva," though here specifically identifying the centipede.</note></term> is the **centipede** <note>Original: 吳公 (Wugong)</note>. By its very nature, it is capable of overcoming snakes. If it suddenly encounters a large snake, it will immediately climb upon it and devour its brain.

Some say that when a snake sees a centipede, it will coil up and lie prostrate in submission. The centipede stands before the snake and exerts a "binding" power <note>referring to *jin*, a form of ritual or hypnotic restraint common in ancient Chinese lore</note>; the snake then opens its mouth, and the centipede enters to eat its intestines, eventually boring a hole through the snake's side to exit.

The philosopher **Zhuangzi** said that the centipede finds the "belt-snake" <note>Original: *dai*, a variety of small snake</note> delicious. The centipede particularly enjoys eating its eyes.

A rhapsody <note>A form of descriptive prose-poetry known as *fu*</note> by **Ge Hong** <note>A famous 4th-century alchemist, physician, and Daoist scholar</note> says that the largest centipedes reach a length of one hundred paces, with heads as large as the body of a carriage. When butchered and sliced, their meat is as white as a gourd; people compete to buy it to make into a broth.

### The Mayfly

The **mayfly** <note>Original: 蜉蝣 (Fuyou)</note> is similar in form to the **longhorn beetle** <note>Original: 天牛 (Tianniu), literally "heavenly ox," known for its long antennae</note> but is smaller and possesses a shell. Its antennae are three to four inches long and are yellowish-black in color. Beneath the shell, it has wings and is capable of flight. If roasted and eaten, its flavor is even more delicious than that of the **cicada**.

These insects emerge all at once, covering the surface of the water; they die shortly thereafter and drift along with the current. In the regions of **Liang** and **Song** <note>Historical states in ancient China, located in the central plains and the Yellow River valley</note>, they are called the <term>qulüe<note>An archaic regional name for the mayfly recorded in ancient dialect glossaries.</note></term>. They congregate in dense thickets or rotting vegetation <note>Original: 鬱棲 (Yuqi)</note>. Because they are born in the morning and reproduce by evening—embodying the concept of "drifting" <note>Original: 浮遊 (Fuyou), a homophone for the insect's name, meaning to drift or wander</note>—they are thus called the **mayfly**.

# The Yin Larva

**Guo Pu** <note>A famous scholar and commentator (276–324 AD) known for his work on the *Erya*, the oldest surviving Chinese dictionary</note> states that the **Yin** <note>Original: 螾 (yǐn). ### The Katydid

The **Katydid** <note>Original: 莎雞 (*Shājī*). ### The Long-horned Grasshopper

The **Long-horned Grasshopper** <note>Original: 斯螽 (*Sīzhōng*). In classical texts, this refers to species within the Tettigoniidae family, often called katydids in English</note> belongs to the locust family. It is characterized by its long, green body, as well as its long antennae and long hind legs. It is capable of rubbing its legs together to produce a sound that can be heard from several dozen paces away. # The Grass-Insect and the Earth-Locust

Master Zhu <note>Referring to Zhu Xi (1130–1200 CE), the preeminent Neo-Confucian scholar whose commentaries on the Classics became the standard for centuries</note> says: The **grass-insect** belongs to the locust family; its sound is *qi* <note>Original: 音奇 (*yīn qí*). Lu Ji, in his *Commentary on the Plants and Animals in the Classic of Poetry*, states: The size and length of the **earth-locust** <note>Original: 阜螽 (*fù zhōng*)</note> are similar to a locust. Master Yan of Huagu <note>Yan Cansun, a Song dynasty scholar known for his work on the *Classic of Poetry*</note> says: The **carrying-locust** and the **fan** are both in fact the **winter katydid**.

### Crickets

Crickets resemble locusts but are smaller, pure black, and possess a glossy luster. They are also known as the **gong** <note>Original: 蛬 (*gǒng*)</note> or the **qinglie** <note>Original: 蜻蛚 (*qīngliè*)</note>. Born in the summer, they begin to chirp in the autumn; they enjoy singing from beneath soil, stones, bricks, and roof tiles. They are particularly fond of fighting; whenever one is victorious, it immediately chirps with pride. Because their sound resembles the rapid pace of weaving, the people of Youzhou <note>An ancient province covering parts of modern-day Hebei and Liaoning; the name suggests the insect's song acts as a seasonal alarm for weavers</note> call them the **Urge-the-Weaving** <note>Original: 促織 (*cùzhī*)</note>, though this is actually a misidentification.

Master Zhu <note>Zhu Xi (1130–1200 CE), the influential Neo-Confucian philosopher whose commentaries were central to the imperial examinations</note> states in his *Commentary on the Mao Poetry*: "The **long-horned grasshopper** <note>Original: 斯螽 (*sīzhōng*)</note>, the **spinner-cricket** <note>Original: 莎雞 (*shājī*)</note>, and the **cricket** are a single creature that changes according to the season, taking on different names." However, Master Hu of Xin’an <note>Referring to Hu Kuangchu, a scholar noted for his botanical and zoological corrections to classical commentaries</note> believes that these are three distinct creatures, each with its own unique name, color, and appearance. I have recorded both of their views here in this chapter to await the investigation of future naturalists <note>Original: 博物者 (*bówùzhě*), literally "those who investigate the vastness of things"</note>.

### Stem-Borers

Those that eat the heart <note>the inner pith or stem</note> of the seedling are called **stem-borers** <note>Original: 螟 (*míng*)</note>; those that eat the leaves are called **leaf-eaters** <note>Original: 蟘 (*tè*)</note>; those that eat the joints of the stalk are called **joint-thieves** <note>Original: 賊 (*zéi*); the name implies they "rob" the plant's strength at its structural points</note>; and those that eat the roots are called **root-borers** <note>Original: 蟊 (*máo*)</note>.

### Tadpoles
<note>Original: *Kedou* (科斗). The term literally refers to their resemblance to the shape of a traditional Chinese ladle or dipper.</note>

**Tadpoles** are the offspring of **toads**. At the edge of the water, toads trail their eggs in long strings, winding them around aquatic grasses like lengths of rope. As the days pass, black spots appear within these strings and gradually darken. When the spring waters rise and the adult toads croak loudly—disturbing the stillness—the tadpoles all emerge. Because of this, they are called **"Noisy Offspring"** <note>Original: *Guozi* (聒子). The text implies a folk belief that the sound of croaking triggers the hatching process.</note>.

Their heads are round and their color is black. When they first emerge, they possess a tail but have no legs. As they grow slightly larger, their legs develop and the tail eventually falls off. They are also known to **cannibalize one another**. Often, a tadpole in front will have its tail bitten and held by one following behind it. As they swim up and down together, the follower will suddenly yank back; the leader's tail then breaks off, and it dies. Today, people commonly call this behavior the **"Toad Formation"** <note>Original: *Xiama Zhen* (蝦蟇陣). This term suggests a military-like array or a "battle" of toads.</note>.

Some say that during **"long months"** of the lunar calendar (those with thirty days), their front two legs grow first, whereas during **"short months"** (those with twenty-nine days), their back two legs grow first. 

The *Notes on Things Past and Present* <note>Original: *Gu Jin Zhu* (古今注), a 4th-century work by Cui Bao that explains the origins of names for animals, plants, and everyday objects.</note> states: "One name for them is the **'Hanging Needle'**, and another is the **'Black Fish'**." This is because their body shape resembles a fish, while their tail is as thin as a needle.

### Lice (Aquatic Varieties)
<note>The character used here, *Shi* (蝨), is a variant of the previous heading, used to group aquatic insects that share a similar shape or parasitic nature with land lice.</note>

### Collected Illustrations of the Three Realms: Volume on Birds and Beasts, Book Six

**Dragon Lice** <note>original: *Longshi* (龍虱). Known today as the predacious diving beetle (*Dytiscidae*).</note>
The Dragon Louse is found in the seas of Fujian. It resembles a long-horned grasshopper but is smaller and black in color, with two wings and six legs. During the autumn months, when gale-force winds arise, they fly in from the sea and land in rice paddies, ponds, or along the seashore. Coastal people gather them and preserve them using oil and salt, considering them a rare delicacy.

**Lice** <note>original: *Shi* (蝨). A general term for parasitic insects.</note>
The louse is an insect that bites humans. The *Treatise on Nourishing Life* <note>original: *Yangsheng Lun* (養生論), a classic text on health and longevity</note> states: "Lice that dwell on the head are black." The *Huainanzi* <note>A 2nd-century BCE philosophical and scientific compendium</note> says: "When the bathwater and washing basins are prepared, the lice mourn for one another." <note>An idiom suggesting that the lice know their end is near once their host decides to bathe.</note> All living things have lice; thus, the *Huainanzi* also says: "The steaming essence of cattle and horses gives rise to nits and lice." Furthermore, the philosopher Master Zhuang <note>original: *Zhuangzi*</note> referred to the lice on a pig as *Ruxu*. <note>original: *Ruxu* (濡需), a term describing the state of being content and lingering in a narrow, dirty environment.</note>

There is another variety that is black and round, named the **Stinking Louse** <note>original: *Choushi* (臭虱), the common bedbug</note>. These are found in extreme abundance in wine shops and the inns where travelers stay.

**Fleas**
The flea is a jumping insect that bites humans. If one is slow, the insect is immediately lost; success in catching it depends on being "early" <note>original: *zao* (早), meaning fast or early</note>. For this reason, the word for flea is the same as the word for "early" or "morning." The character is derived from the "claw" radical <note>original: *zhua* (爪)</note> because they are caught using the fingernails. The *Analytical Dictionary of Characters* <note>original: *Shuowen Jiezi* (說文解字)</note> records the character as *zao* (蚤).

**Water Lice**
Commonly known as **Water Fleas**. These are lice that are born in the stagnant mud of downstream waters.
