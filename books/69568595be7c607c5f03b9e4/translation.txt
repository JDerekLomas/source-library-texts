SIR,

**H**aving often tired you with discourses on several parts of music, wherein you desired to receive satisfaction without obtaining the effect I proposed for myself, I reflected upon the saying: "No man understands that which he cannot so express as to make another understand it." I doubted whether the hypothesis I entertained was not founded upon errors which I had swallowed without any due examination. I resolved, for a trial of it, to trace my notions as near to their principles as I could and set them down in a methodical way, so that I might discover <unclear>whether</unclear> they were well deduced from one another and from <unclear>such experiments</unclear> as I had in memory. 

Having done this, I send them to you as the severest judge I know. It has not been your custom to flatter me, and now I desire your censure <note>original: "censure"; here meaning a formal judgment or critical review rather than a condemnation</note>, if I may impose it upon you to give it in writing. In doing so, you will be obliged to quote what I say truly before you object to it, and to stand by those allegations and inferences you will set down as your own; in both of these respects, I had a great disadvantage in verbal discourse. 

I shall anticipate your condemnation of the style for being too contracted and obscure; you must take the fault upon yourself when you know it does not proceed from any aversion I have to philology <note>original: "Philologie"; here referring to the love of learning, literature, and polished language</note>, but because I thought it would be labor lost to enlarge upon these points. When writing to so great a philosopher and musician as you are, I cannot fail to be understood.

# Of SOUND.

**M**y design being to explain the nature of *Music*, it will not be improper to inquire first concerning the cause of Sound. Although I cannot hope to give full satisfaction—because our senses lack the precision <note>original: "subtilty"</note> to discover the motions of the minute parts of the air upon which it depends—yet perhaps I may assign a possible cause for the production of Sound. The knowledge of this will give some ease to the wondering philosopher <note>In this context, "philosopher" refers to a student of natural science.</note>; and if I have the good fortune to show a probable cause, the curious <note>The "curious" refers to those interested in scientific inquiry.</note> will be satisfied without further inquiry, which is the only benefit they can gain from speculations of this nature.

<margin>1.</margin>
The *phenomena of sound* which I consider significant are these: 
1. It may be produced in the Torricellian vacuum <note>original: "Torricellian vacuity"; the space created at the top of a barometer, which early scientists used to test if sound could travel without air.</note>. 
2. It causes motion in solid bodies. 
3. It is diminished by the placement of solid bodies in its path. 
4. If the bodies placed in between are very thick, its passage is wholly obstructed. 
5. It seems to reach the ear in straight lines, even when the object is positioned so that it cannot travel in a direct line to the ear. 
6. When the air is still, its reach is *spherical*. 
7. When there is a wind, that *sphere* is enlarged in the direction the wind blows and diminished on the opposite side. 
8. It does not arrive at the ear instantly, but considerably slower than sight. 
9. It travels as quickly against the wind as with it, though it is not as loud nor does it travel as far.

There are many other observable appearances, and more will daily occur to the curious through experiments; however, the consideration of these qualities of *sound* and the visible actions that produce it has led me to entertain the following hypothesis.

I suppose the air we breathe to be a mixture of various minute bodies which are of different sorts and sizes, though all of them are so small as to escape our senses. The grosser <note>original: "grosser," meaning larger or more substantial</note> of them are elastic and are resisted by solid bodies, which are in no way permeable to them. The smaller parts pass through solid bodies, but not with such ease that they do not—upon a sudden and violent start—shock the parts of solid bodies that stand in their way, as well as the larger parts of the air. 

There may also be another degree of most subtle <term>Ethereal parts</term> <note>The "Aether" was a hypothesized substance thought to fill all space and allow for the transmission of light</note> with which the gaps of these and all other bodies are filled. These find freer passage everywhere and are incapable of compression; consequently, they are the medium and cause of the immediate communication of sight. 

The middle sort of these particles I consider to be the medium and cause of *sound*. At any time when the larger air is driven off from any space—leaving it to be possessed by these and other more subtle bodies—and then returns by its elasticity to its former place, these parts are then pushed out with violence as if from the center of that space, communicating their motion as far as the sound is heard.

Or, when any solid body is moved with a sudden and violent motion, these parts must be affected by it. For just as these parts are resisted enough by solid bodies to shock them, so on the contrary they must necessarily be moved by the sudden starting of solid bodies.

So it is that sound may be caused by the trembling of solid bodies without the presence of larger Air, and also by the restoration of larger Air when it has been divided with any violence.

We see a bell will sound in the <term>Torricellian space</term> <note>A vacuum created at the top of a mercury barometer, named after Evangelista Torricelli</note>. Furthermore, a sudden crack will be caused when the air is divided with any sudden force, such as:
* By the end of a whip, which has all the motion of the whip contracted in it and throws off the air by a sudden turn.
* By <term>accension</term> <note>The act of catching fire or exploding</note>, as in thunder and guns.
* Or by any impression of force carrying it where other air cannot follow as forcibly, such as when compressing air in a bladder until it breaks, or in a <term>Potgun</term> <note>A popgun or a small toy gun used to create a loud noise</note>.

I shall leave others to apply this hypothesis <note>original: "Hypothesis"</note> to the aforementioned phenomena <note>original: "Phenomena"</note> (which they may easily do) and proceed to the discourse of music, where I am, in its proper place, to show how this action that causes sound is performed by the various instruments of music.

## Of a TONE.

A tone is the repetition of cracks or pulses in equal spaces of time, occurring so quickly that the gaps or intervals between them are not perceptible to the senses.

The quicker the pulses are, the higher <note>original: "acute"</note> the tone is. Where the intervals are not equal, nor in musical proportion, the sound is not a tone but an irregular noise.

The range of music extends from those tones whose intervals are so great that the individual pulses can be distinguished by the senses, to those whose gaps are so very small that they cannot be measured against any other.

Within this range, the various tones are infinite in number, just as all space may be divided infinitely <note>original: "in infinitum"</note>. However, the tones useful in music are those within the scale, which are not very many; they are placed in the scale according to how they relate to one another.

## *How* **TONES** are *produced*, and of *assistances*
## to the **SOUND** by *Instruments*.

**W**herever an object is supported by a spring-like tension <note>original: "stands upon a Spring"</note> that vibrates in equal cycles, that object, when put into motion, will produce a **Tone**. This tone will be more *grave* <note>low-pitched</note> or *acute* <note>high-pitched</note> according to the speed of the vibrations.

Therefore, vibrating strings have a **Tone** according to their thickness or their tension; and Bells, which vibrate in shifting oval shapes <note>original: "cross ovals"</note>, produce Notes according to their size or the thickness of their sides. The same is true for all other objects whose surfaces, when displaced by force, spring back and carry beyond their original position.

It is easy to understand how every pulse from such vibrations causes **sound**. The heavy **Air** is thrown off by the violence of the motion, which continues for a moment after the vibrating body returns. Consequently, some space must be left for that subtle matter which, upon the reaction of the **Air**, radiates outward as if from a **Center**. This action, which I have proposed to be the cause of **sound**, is repeated with every vibration.

There is in any pipe a cavity of a certain gauge or size which is exposed to the outward air, though only in some parts of it; and according to the size of that cavity is the *tone* of the pipe.

The air in any cavity is capable of being compressed by outward force. If the cavity is large, the air easily yields to compression; but if it is small, it is more difficult to compress. When the compressing force ceases, the body of air that was compressed with difficulty flies out with sudden violence; that which is more easily compressed restores itself less quickly. Because of this, every cavity has a certain *tone* according to the measure of the vibrations of the air within it, which it will maintain regardless of how the force is applied to it. This is similar to how water in a shallow area or creek, when moved by the large waves of the sea, does not maintain the motion of those huge waves, but instead moves in waves proportional to its own size and depth.

Based on this, I conclude that any pipe—whether made of wood, stone, glass, or metal—has a certain and fixed *tone* according to its gauge and cavity. This is the natural *tone* which will be produced by blowing into it. If the cavity is small, the *sound* will be acute <note>original: "acute"; meaning high-pitched</note>, and it may be made even higher in pitch by apertures <note>original: "apertures"; holes or openings</note>, by which the cavity is diminished and the enclosed air is exposed to the outward air to a greater degree.

When I say the *tone* depends on the proportion of the cavity regardless of what the pipe is made of, I do not deny that if the materials of the pipe are soft and yielding, it may cause some small alteration to the *tone* compared to what it would be if the materials were of a hard and firm substance, where the impelling air would find a more brisk resistance. However, the difference is insignificant; and since the "spring" <note>The elastic property of air that allows it to bounce back after compression.</note> of the air depends mainly upon the size of the cavity, I take no notice of it.

Another kind of whistle occurs when a hollow body with a small cavity is pierced by opposite holes. A blast of air from either direction will cause a *tone*, which seems to be produced in this manner:

The air that is violently drawn or thrust through these holes is narrowed at the passage by the speed of the motion. Once inside the cavity, it is somewhat enlarged, and consequently, its force is directed so that it presses beyond the boundaries of the opposite opening. # Of SCHISMS and the Scale
## *of* MUSIC.

**T**here are *schisms* <note>original: "schismes"; here referring to the small mathematical discrepancies or intervals that occur when tuning musical scales</note> in *Music* because the *Scale* is naturally fitted to one *Key*. Although it will serve reasonably well for some other keys, it will not work for all of them.

The *Scale of Music* is not defined by any fixed quantities of whole *Notes* or half *Notes*, even though the intervals are commonly called by those names. Instead, the degrees in the *Scale of Music* are fixed by the *Ear* at those points where the pulses of the *Tones* are coincident <note>original: "coincident"; meaning the vibrations of the two notes align or "lock" together in a pleasing way</note>, without regard to the specific mathematical quantity. I shall endeavor to show how all the *Notes* enter the *Scale* through their relationships and importance. It will then be obvious why, for ease of instruction and convenience, the *Scale of musical degrees* is organized as *Musicians* currently present it.

Once the *Key Note* is established, the first *Note* in importance is the fifth above it, which must be placed perfectly. While the *Tune* remains in the *Key*, this note has the greatest emphasis; if there is any departure from the *Key*, this *Note* is the most likely to take its place. Therefore, once the *Chords* <note>original: "Chords"; here referring to the harmonic intervals or related notes</note> to the *Key Note* are set, the *Chords* related to this fifth note must be considered. Without them, there can be no variety, as this is the first step away from the *Key*.

The other *Chords* related to the *Key* are the minor and major third, the fourth, and the minor and major sixth <note>original: "third b and #... sixth b and #"; the author uses "flat" (b) for minor and "sharp" (#) for major intervals</note>.

The *Chords* related to the fifth above the *Key* are the 2nd, and the minor and major 7th, which function as the fifth and the minor and major thirds relative to that *Note*.

## Of MUSIC *consisting of several parts in concert.*

**M**usic in concert <note>original: "Conſort"; a small ensemble of instruments</note> consists of *harmony, formality*, and *conformity.*

*Harmony* is the grateful sound produced by the joining of several *tones* in a chord with one another.

I showed before that *tones* in a chord with one another will join and give one entire sound, whereas discordant *tones* will jar. I also showed that some chords were more perfect than others, according to how frequently the coincidence of their pulses <note>The vibrations or sound waves</note> occurred. It will be proper here to say something concerning the several natures of the chords in conjunction, and the qualities of combined sounds.

In all combined sounds, the bass is—according to its name—the foundation, and all the upper chords are pleasing as they relate to that. This is because the bass encompasses all the rest, and its pulses are, according to nature, bigger and stronger.

The structure of a single melody must be precise because the composer is not restricted and may follow his own imagination. However, in a **consort** <note>original: "Consort"; a small ensemble of instruments playing together</note>, the composer must consider the harmony of all the parts joined together, and therefore he is not at liberty to do as he pleases.

There are certain allowances required by the nature of the different parts: the melody of the **treble** <note>the highest part</note> must be airy and brisk, while that of the **bass** <note>original: "Base"; the lowest part</note> must be slow and robust. The inner parts are generally employed to fill the *music* with chords and have little need for elaborate melody, except when they are following a **point** <note>a musical theme or subject used in imitation</note>. But once these allowances are considered, an expert *musician* will give a sufficient share of melody and sweetness to every part—sometimes making the treble move at a grave pace and the bass rest so that the beauty of the inner parts may be clearly heard.

The third requirement of *music* in a consort is *conformity* among all the parts. Just as the structure of a single part requires that the following notes should be agreeable to those that came before, so conformity requires that each part should have a similar movement toward the notes that follow.

This movement is often so highly regarded that the immediate harmony must yield to it. For if the upcoming notes have a strong relationship to the **key** <note>the tonal center of the piece</note>, and the melodic path to them in the various parts requires using **discords** <note>dissonant or clashing notes</note>, that path should not be avoided because it creates such a strong expectation of what is to come. Instead, the discords will be permitted; their harshness is softened by the anticipation of the following chords, and the harmony of the subsequent notes is made even more welcome because of the tension of the passage.
