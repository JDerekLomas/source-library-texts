*Since, Most Illustrious and Most High Prince, Most Merciful Lord, my father Johannes Kepler, Imperial Mathematician, was already weary enough from the motion of the earthly mass <note>original: "motu molis terrestris" — a reference to Kepler's lifelong work on the physics of the Earth's movement</note>, he began to dream of Astronomy and the motion of the Lunar body; but I know not what omen this dream brought with it! To us, his children, it was indeed most mournful; although to him, the outcome of this omen was pleasant enough, indeed most longed for. For while this Dream was being written and was under the press, my Father was seized (alas!) by a heavier, indeed a fatal sleep, and his spirit flew (as we hope) above the Lunar region to the heavens, leaving us children exposed to the injuries of Mars <note>The Roman god of war, referring here to the ongoing Thirty Years' War</note> and the miseries of this world, destitute of almost every temporal aid. When the most Distinguished and Learned Man, Lord Jakob Bartsch, Doctor of Medicine and designated Professor of Mathematics in the Academy of Strasbourg, my kinsman <note>Bartsch was Kepler's son-in-law</note>, had taken up the care of this printing, he also, before the matter was finished, was seized by a fatal disease and died.*

*In the meantime, having returned to Germany from a journey which I had undertaken with a certain Austrian Baron, and having had no news of the condition of my family for two years, I wrote to them from Frankfurt to Lusatia <note>A region in eastern Germany/western Poland</note> so that they might inform me how and if they were living. And behold, my widowed stepmother came to me with four orphans, destitute of money. And indeed, they were in a most turbulent state, and in a place most inconvenient because of the high cost of food, bringing with her the incomplete copies of this Dream and imploring my help—I, who myself have need of the help and promotion of others. She likewise demands from me the completion of the copies of this Dream: but what good can I hope for from this Dream, when it was fatal to my Father and my kinsman? Nevertheless, since it is fitting for a son not to hide a name of a Parent so famous and honorable, but rather, if he cannot increase that fame by his own genius, at least to preserve it with all his might, I also could not refuse this petition; indeed, I have desired it. But a Patron for this work is still lacking. Certainly, one will hardly be found among military men, as they are now little concerned with the Astronomy of the Lunar globe, but are rather forced to look about lest they be crushed or harmed by musket balls and cannonballs <note>original: "globis sclopedariis et tormentariis" — a play on words between the celestial "globes" and the "globes" of lead and iron used in war</note>. Therefore, I could find no one more worthy than You, Most Illustrious Prince, whose patronage this little work might enjoy, as you yourself are most practiced in Mathematical study, are most foreign to the fury of war, and who cherished our Parent with your most Merciful patronage while he was still among the living. Therefore, the Orphans, relying on firm confidence, trust that You will not deny Your Patronage to them and to this little work. They humbly commend themselves and this Dream to Your Illustrious Highness through me, [praying to] God the Best and Greatest with most ardent [prayers...]*

### I. The Dream, or Lunar Astronomy

In the year 1608, while disputes were raging between the brothers Emperor Rudolph and Archduke Matthias, and their actions were commonly compared to examples taken from Bohemian history, I—stirred by the general public curiosity—turned my mind to reading Bohemian chronicles. Having come across the story of the maiden Libussa <note>A legendary 8th-century prophetess and founder of Prague</note>, who was most celebrated for her magical arts, it happened one night that after contemplating the stars and the Moon, I settled into bed and fell into a very deep sleep. In my sleep, I seemed to be reading a book brought from the trade fair, the contents of which were as follows:

> My <note>1</note> name is *Duracotus* <note>2</note>; my homeland is Iceland, which the ancients called Thule <note>3</note>. My mother...

---

<note>1</note> <margin>Notes written successively between the years 1620 and 1630.</margin> The sound of the name itself occurred to me from the recollection of similar-sounding proper names in Scottish history, a region that looks out toward the Icelandic Ocean.
<note>2</note> In our German tongue, this sounds like "frozen earth" <note>original: "Terram glacialem"</note>. In this remote island, I chose a place for myself to sleep and dream, so that I might imitate the philosophers in this genre of writing. For Cicero also crossed into Africa to dream <note>referring to the "Dream of Scipio"</note>, and Plato fashioned an "Atlantis" in that same Western Ocean from which to summon fabulous aids for military virtue; and finally Plutarch, in his small book *On the Face in the Moon*, after much discourse, wanders out into the American Ocean and describes for us a location of islands which a modern geographer would likely apply to the Azores, Greenland, and the regions of Labrador situated around Iceland. Indeed, whenever I reread that book by Plutarch, I am intensely amazed by what chance it happened that our dreams or fables coincided so accurately. For I recall with a faithful enough memory the occasions for every part of my fiction, and they were not all born from reading this book. I have in my possession a very old paper, written in your hand, most distinguished Christopher Besold; when you had conceived about twenty theses concerning the celestial appearances on the Moon from my dissertations in the year 1593, and had presented them to Master Vitus Miller, then the ordinary President of philosophical disputations, intending to debate them if he had consented. At that time, I had not yet seen Plutarch’s works. Later, I came across Lucian’s two books of *True History*, written in Greek; I chose those booklets to help me learn the language, aided by the delight of a most daring fable which nevertheless hinted at something of the nature of the whole universe, as Lucian himself warns in his introduction. He also sails out beyond the Pillars of Hercules into the Ocean, and is swept up with his ship by whirlwinds and carried to the Moon. These were my first footsteps of a journey to the Moon attempted in later times. I first obtained Plutarch’s booklet in Graz in 1595, having been alerted to it by reading Erasmus Reinhold’s *Commentary on the Theories of Purbach*; and from it, in Prague in 1604, I translated many things into the optical part of Astronomy. However, I did not choose Iceland for the hypothesis of my dream because of the islands named by Plutarch in the Icelandic Ocean. Rather, among the reasons was that at that time in Prague, Lucian’s booklet on the voyage to the Moon was for sale, translated into the German language by Rollenhagen’s son, joined with the narratives of Saint Brendan and of Saint Patrick’s Purgatory in the subterranean fires of the Icelandic mountain, Hekla. Since Plutarch also, according to the opinion of pagan theology, placed the purgatory of souls in the Moon, it pleased me, as I was about to set out for the Moon, to set sail specifically from Iceland. A greater recommendation for this island came from the narrative of Tycho Brahe, of which more below. Nor was the memory of reading the history of the Dutch wintering in the frozen Nova Zembla <note>Novaya Zemlya</note> without effect; it provided many astronomical exercises which I translated into the optical part of Astronomy in 1604.
<note>3</note> In my dwelling, which I used by the permission of Martin Bachazek, Rector of the Carolinum Academy, there hung on the wall a very old geographical map of Europe, in which the word **Fiolx** was written near the locations of Iceland. Whatever that sounded like, its harsh sound pleased me; and I added a word common for women in the old language, **Hildis**; hence *Brunhildis, Mathildis, Hildegard, Hiltrud*, and the like.

While she lived, she took great care to ensure that I did not write. For she used to say that there are many destructive haters of the arts, who, because of the dullness of their minds, misinterpret and slander what they cannot grasp. <margin>4. Because it is more likely that this refers to the son, the promoter of the arts, than if he were imagined to be writing while his mother was still alive. But I also wished to hint at this: that the offspring, **Science**, is born from **Unskilled Experience**—or, in the medical way of speaking, from **Empirical Practice**—as its mother. It is not safe for Science, as long as the mother, **Ignorance**, survives among men, to reveal the most hidden causes of things to the common crowd. Rather, one must spare the modesty of antiquity and wait for the maturity of years, so that Ignorance, as if worn out by old age, may finally die. Since the goal of my *Dream* is to build an argument for the **Motion of the Earth**—or rather, to solve the objections taken from the universal contradiction of the human race—by using the example of the Moon: I judged that this old Ignorance was already sufficiently extinct and rooted out from the memory of ingenious men. And yet, the Soul still struggles in the bond of so many limbs, joined so firmly for so many centuries; that aged mother survives in the Universities. But she lives in such a way that death should be considered a happier state for her than life.</margin>

<margin>6. This happened to me recently during my travels; though not to me alone, but to a group of several like-minded people. For when a **Theologian**, who professed the **Augsburg Confession** <note>The primary confession of faith of the Lutheran Church</note>, attacked us with great zeal and seemed to seek weapons from Scripture to oppose us, he finally became inflamed by our defenses. Raising his voice and calling all sacred things to witness, he exclaimed that this doctrine <note>the Copernican system</note> fought *against all reason*. I then finally broke my stubborn silence—for until then I had sat there as a mere listener—and said: "Surely, this is what drives the men of your faction, even the ignorant ones. For if you grasped the utility, necessity, and possibility of this doctrine within the narrow limits of your reason, you yourselves would have long ago turned aside the force of arguments sought from Scripture by seeking a convenient interpretation, as you are not infrequently accustomed to do in other matters. But now, such is the weakness of your reason that you do not see that even we possess some small portion of reason. Therefore, the doctrine does not fight against all reason; for it does not fight against the reason of **Astronomers** and **Physicists**. For what one man does not grasp, another more skilled in the subject does."</margin>

<margin>7. Every man suffers his own injury; but to **Copernicus's** work *On the Revolutions*, this is the capital injury: that those unskilled in Astronomical matters (interpreting the censorship of books not according to its intent, but wrongly) think that the work must not be read unless the **Motion of the Earth** is first removed. This is the same as saying it must not be read until it has been consumed by flames. Thinking that these people should be refuted not with arguments, but with laughter, I wrote this **Epigram**:
> To stop his lust, they could geld the Poet,
> With the testicles removed, life remained.
> Woe to you, **Pythagoras**, who are accused of abusing your Brain;
> They grant you life, but first they take your wits away.</margin>

<margin>8. I wonder if the author of that bold satire titled *Ignatius’s Conclave* <note>A satire by John Donne</note> had obtained a copy of this little work of mine; for he stings me by name even at the very beginning. For in the course of it, he brings poor Copernicus before the tribunal of **Pluto**, to which, if I am not mistaken, the entrance is through the pits of **Mount Hecla**. 3. 5. 8. 9. You, my friends, who have knowledge of my affairs and what the cause was for my recent journey to **Swabia**, especially if any of you previously obtained this manuscript, will judge that this little book has been ominous for me and mine. And I do not disagree. Truly, there is a great omen of death in a lethal wound inflicted, or in poison swallowed; and there seems to have been no less of a domestic disaster in the publication of this writing. You might believe a spark fell into dry material: that is, these words were taken up by minds that are dark within, suspecting all things to be dark. The first copy was carried from **Prague** to **Leipzig**, and from there to **Tübingen** in the year 1611 by the **Baron von Volckerstorff** and his tutors. How far is it from your belief that in the **Barber shops** (especially those where the name of my **Fiolxhildis** is an ill omen because of their occupation <note>A reference to the witchcraft trial of Kepler's mother, Katharina, who was accused of being a witch/herbalist</note>), they gossiped about this fable of mine? Certainly, from that very city and house, slandering rumors about me arose in the following years; these, taken up by hostile minds, finally flared up into a public scandal, with ignorance and superstition fanning the flames. Unless I am mistaken, you will judge that my house could have been free from six years of harassment, and I from my recent year-long journey, if I had not violated the dreamed-of precepts of this Fiolxhildis. It pleased me, therefore, to take revenge for the trouble caused by this dream by publishing the book; my adversaries shall have a different reward.</margin>

In the first years of my childhood, my mother, pulling me by the hand and sometimes lifting me onto her shoulders, used to lead me frequently <sup>12</sup> to the lower ridges of Mount Hekla, <sup>13</sup> especially around the feast of Saint John, when the Sun is visible for the full 24 hours and leaves no place for night. <sup>14</sup> Gathering certain herbs with many ceremonies and cooking them at home, <sup>15</sup> she used to make little pouches out of goat skins; these she would fill with air and carry to the nearby port to sell to the captains of ships, and in this way she sustained our livelihood.

Once, out of curiosity, I cut open a pouch that my mother was selling without knowing its contents; having spread out the herbs and the <sup>16</sup> linens which, embroidered with a needle, displayed various characters, I deprived her of this small profit. My mother, inflamed with anger, handed me over to a ship captain in place of the pouch so that she might keep the money. And he, setting sail unexpectedly the next day from the port, headed with a favorable wind <sup>17</sup> as if toward Bergen in Norway. After a few days, <sup>18</sup> with a North wind arising, he was carried between Norway and England and sought Denmark; having passed through the strait, since he had letters <sup>19</sup> from the Icelandic Bishop to be delivered to Tycho Brahe, the Dane, who lived on the Island of Hven, and because I was indeed violently ill—

---

—the story, as I believe, of that Empedocles in Diogenes Laertius, who, when he had climbed Mount Etna so that he might obtain divine honors after death, is imagined to have cast himself into the very craters and sacrificed himself alive to the flames; perhaps he was seeking the causes of the eternal fire and, having progressed with blind rashness to a place from which he could not return—the surface encrusted with ash crumbling under his footsteps—with a late repentance for his curiosity and no care for this fame, he unwillingly gave up his mourning soul. For a similar fate befell Gaius Plinius <note>Pliny the Elder</note>, who, having sailed to Pompeii for the sake of philosophizing during the disaster of the Vesuvius fire through hostile showers of ash and pumice, was suffocated by the sulfurous stench and the ashes filling his breathing passages. Thus Homer is said in the riddle of the Fishermen, and Aristotle by the ebbs and flows of the Euripus, to have thrown away their lives into the waves, according to legendary accounts. Thus many avenge their love of knowledge upon themselves with poverty, having provoked the hatred of the ignorant wealthy.

...from the tossing of the ship^20 and the unaccustomed warmth of the air, being as I was a youth of fourteen years. When the ship reached the shore^21, the captain left me with an island fisherman along with letters of recommendation; then, promising to return, he set sail.^22

When the letters were delivered, Brahe was greatly cheered and began to ask me many questions^23; because I was unskilled in the language, I did not understand him, except for a few words. Therefore, he gave the task to his Students,^24 whom he supported in great numbers, to speak with me frequently. By Brahe's generosity and a few weeks of practice, it came about^25 that I could speak Danish passably well. I was no less ready in storytelling than they were in questioning. Indeed, I marveled at many unfamiliar things, and in turn, I recounted many new things from my own country to those who wondered at them.

Finally, when the ship's captain returned to fetch me,^26 he was refused, much to my joy.

The astronomical exercises delighted me in a wonderful^27 way; for the Students and Brahe, with marvelous instruments, would gaze all night at the Moon and the stars. This reminded me of my mother, since^28 she herself was also accustomed to commune constantly with the Moon.

By this opportunity, I—a semi-barbarian from a poor background—came into the knowledge of the most divine science, which prepared the way for me toward greater things.

Indeed, after several years had passed on this island, a desire finally came upon me to see my homeland again; for I thought it would not be difficult for me, on account of the knowledge I had acquired, to rise to some position of dignity among my unrefined people. Therefore, having bid farewell to my patron and obtained permission to depart, I went to Copenhagen. There I found traveling companions who, because of my knowledge of the language and the region, gladly took me under their protection. I returned to my homeland in the fifth year after I had left.

*The first happiness of my return was that I found my mother still breathing, and still occupied with the same pursuits as before; and, being alive and well-dressed, I brought an end to her long-standing grief for the son she had lost through her own rashness. <note>original: "ornatus" - implying he returned with the status and clothing of a scholar.</note> The year was then turning toward Autumn, and those long nights of ours followed in succession; for in the month of Christ’s Nativity <note>December</note>, the Sun at Noon barely emerges and is immediately hidden again. Thus my Mother, during this vacation from her own labors, clung to me and would not leave my side wherever I went with my letters of recommendation. She began to inquire now about the Lands I had visited, and now about the Heavens—she rejoiced most vehemently that I had learned that science. # On the Effects of Narcotics and the Journey

With **narcotics and opiates**, the traveler must be lulled to sleep immediately at the start, and his limbs must be carefully arranged <note>original: "membratim explicandus"</note> so that the lower body is not carried away from the torso, nor the head from the body, but so that the violent force of the acceleration is distributed across every individual limb. <margin>In those huddled together, the parts closest to the propelling cause suffer more, being pressed by the weight of the parts resting upon them.</margin> Then a new difficulty takes hold: an immense cold and the prevention of breathing. We encounter the former through a power innate to us, and the latter by applying moistened sponges to the nostrils. Once the first part of the journey is completed, the transport becomes easier. <margin>See above, Number 57.</margin> Then we expose the bodies to the open air and withdraw our hands. At that point, they curl themselves up like spiders; we transport them with little more than a nod <note>a slight movement or signal</note>, so that finally the bodily mass inclines of its own accord toward the proposed destination. <margin>This is said only for the sake of the argument. Nature deserts me; I do not know if it is pleasing to turn to a joke in a serious matter. The allegory also grows cold. That Demon, who is called Astronomy, supplies the lack of things necessary for sustenance through an innate power of fiery fervor for observation—though he does so quite coldly.</margin> But this **momentum** <note>original: "ῥοπὴ" (rhopē) - weight, inclination, or turning of the scale</note> is of little use to us because it is too slow; therefore, as I said, we accelerate it with a nod, and we now precede the body so that it does not suffer any damage from a very hard impact upon the Moon. <margin>One should not pass over such an opportune story from Aristotle concerning the Philosophers who climbed Mount Olympus in Asia for the sake of observation.</margin>

Men are accustomed, when they wake up, to complain of an ineffable weariness in all their limbs, from which they recover quite late in order to walk. <margin>Namely, when the body has been snatched from the sphere of the Earth's magnetic power by such a distance that the magnetic power of the Moon's sphere now outweighs it.</margin>

<margin>When the magnetic powers of the Earth and the Moon are suspended by contrary attraction, it is just as if no body were enticing it anywhere. Then, therefore, the body itself, as a whole, pulls its own limbs as parts smaller than the whole.</margin>

<margin>Not entirely by a nod. Strength is still needed. For every body, by reason of its matter, possesses a certain **inertia** <term>inertia: Kepler's term for the natural resistance of matter to motion</term> toward movement, which provides the body with rest in every place where it is positioned outside of pulling forces. It is necessary for whoever is going to move that body from its place to overcome this force, or rather, this inertia.</margin>

<margin>Specifically, when the sphere of the Moon's magnetic power prevails due to proximity. For assuming that any mass of Earth, equal to the Lunar globe, pulls equally strongly: a body so located between both globes—such that the proportion of its distance from each is the same as the proportion of the bodies themselves to each other—will hang immobile, the pulls in different directions canceling each other out. For example, if it is distant from the Earth by 58 and 1/59 Earth-radii, and from the Moon by 58/59 parts of an Earth-radius. But the same body, moved just a little closer to the Moon, will now follow the pulling Moon, its force prevailing due to proximity.</margin>

For in Thesis 139, he had proved that when the Moon has departed a full 12 degrees from the Sun, it has scarcely an eightieth part of its visible diameter illuminated by the Sun. He therefore posits that all that air which extends beyond the boundary of the Moon's body is tinted by the Sun's rays, since it is permeable or transparent to them; thus, the Moon is never completely extinguished, not even in the central conjunction itself.

He confirms this doctrine with five additional experiments. First, that the ray of a darkening Sun admitted through a hole always makes the outer convex circumference of the Sun's image larger than the inner concave one—the defect, of course, being cut out or covered by the convex body of the Moon—even though the full Moon is usually larger in diameter than the Sun. He thinks, therefore, that when we measure the full Moon, we are measuring that which stands out beyond the body from the illuminated lunar air all around; but when the Moon itself covers the Sun, it does so alone, aided not at all by this airy tunic of hers, which the Sun's rays pass through without being hindered or intercepted.

This experiment, sought from the observation of a Solar Eclipse, is indeed very true; and it also moved Tycho Brahe to report the diameter of the New Moon as smaller than the diameter of the Full Moon; and Longimontanus also defends his master in *Danish Astronomy*. David Fabricius, the Frisian astronomer, was also much occupied with this "shirt" of the Moon; his opinions I examined in the *Prolegomena to the Ephemerides*. It is very true, I say, that in the image of the darkening Sun admitted through a small hole, the convex circumference is a portion of a larger circle and the concave a portion of a smaller one; but the cause brought forward by the disputant is not suitable. By the width of this semi-diameter, a bright fringe grows around the crescent image of the Sun, even at the tips of the horns; thus they become blunt. When this bright fringe is wiped away, a pure image is left, whose outer circumference is now more contracted, and the inner and concave one more relaxed. Thus, with this remedy applied, the diameter of the Moon covering the Sun is found to be consistent with the diameter of the full Moon.

I did not judge these three experiments suitable for testifying to such a far-reaching extension beyond the body of the Moon. I attributed the cause of the appearance to the nature of sight: for even at night the pupil of the eye is dilated by natural motion, and the more crowded light of a shining visible point breaks in, and widely imbues the visual spirits in the retiform tunic <note>the retina</note>: and the same happens even by day, with the eye directed toward a strong light. In this way, the picture of visible things in the retiform tunic is spoiled, the bright parts being dilated and occupying the region of the neighboring dark parts. And to this picture inside the eye in the cavity of the Retina, the outward vision of the visible thing corresponds exactly from the opposite side.

The author also acknowledges this solution in a note to Thesis 151, with my name suppressed, and he avoids it however he can, alleging that the same things happen by day. But that by which I dissolve the reasoning, although it is more evident by night, nevertheless also holds true by day.

Yet not no testimony is gathered from here, especially from the fourth and fifth experiments, in favor of lunar air. For because the rays of the Sun pass through it and render it very bright; although the edge is nonetheless otherwise fit for projecting a shadow, yet this same edge, by this its absorbed brightness, vehemently moves the sight; to which vehemence corresponds that passion and spreading tint in the retina, and therefore also the appearance of such a great bright width in the visible thing, which, although not of real width, yet by its real vigor and clarity causes that premature emergence of the Moon. I do not say the real visible width of the crescent causes an apparent width as if commensurate with itself; but the real clarity causes a false and excessive width, because of the strong tinting of the retiform tunic. See what I have argued in this manner against not dissimilar experiments of **David Fabricius**, in the **Prolegomena of my Ephemerides**.

I skipped the third proof; the Disputant introduces it in Thesis 149. The bright margin of the shining Moon is clear and pure, and without spots; in the middle, the whole Moon appears spotted: because, to be sure, the lunar air in the middle of the body is thin and shallow on the slopes, but toward the margins, it meets the sight with depth. For so in the plains of the earth, the air above our heads, although illuminated by the Sun, does not greatly move the sight, and to those looking out from a deep well it does not cover the larger stars: but the air poured around mountains far off, because it offers a passage of great depth to the sight, grows white, tints the mountains placed beyond with a blue color, nay, obscures them completely, and even in the absence of the Sun, clouds the brightest rising stars. So clouds are mostly either non-existent or scattered and transparent above our heads: toward the Horizon they are always densest, where there is even the least bit at the zenith.
