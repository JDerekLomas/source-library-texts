# Illustrated Explanations for the Preface of the Record of Trades

->Supplementary Annotations on the Classics and Commentaries<-

The construction of chariots for land travel was a task established by the Sages. By the time of the <term>Zhou people<note>Referring to the Zhou Dynasty (c. 1046–256 BCE), the era during which the standards in the Record of Trades were supposedly set.</note></term>, they valued the carriage body above all else. Although it is a single vessel, it is the one craft that requires the greatest gathering of diverse artisans. The existing commentaries and explanations on the <term>Record of Trades<note>The Kao Gong Ji (考工記).</note></term> remain somewhat superficial. Scholars since the <term>Tang dynasty<note>618–907 CE; Ruan Yuan is criticizing the long-standing academic tradition of simply repeating old notes rather than investigating the physical engineering.</note></term> have strictly adhered to these traditional annotations, yet they have rarely managed to fully synthesize and master the original text of the Classic itself.

I, <insert>Ruan</insert> Yuan, observe that while several gentlemen of our current age have already settled the various matters regarding the other crafts in the *Record of Trades*, there are still gaps concerning the specific craft of chariot making. Consequently, I have closely examined the measurements, ratios, and standards of the era. I have relied on the <term>Odes<note>The Shijing (詩經) or Classic of Poetry, which contains many descriptions of contemporary chariots.</note></term> to verify the terminology for the felloes, spokes, hub-covers, and naves; the wheel-cords and the "ears" of the carriage; and the axle-housing, the depth of the axle-mounting, the support timbers, the crossbar, the yoke, the pole, and the sideboards.

I have organized this work as follows:
First: Explanations on the construction of the wheel.
Second: Explanations on the carriage body.
Third: Explanations on the axle and undercarriage.
Fourth: Explanations on leatherwork.
Fifth: Explanations on metalwork.
Sixth: Explanations on the sequence and calculation of chariot dimensions.

My goal is to explain what was previously unclear and to make the obscured details manifest. To this end, I have created:
Diagram One: The Wheel.
Diagram Two: The Carriage Body.
Diagram Three: The Axle and Undercarriage.

Written by the student Ruan Yuan of Yizheng, in the 53rd year of the <term>Qianlong reign<note>This corresponds to the year 1788.</note></term>.

### Section 1: The Wheels

To examine a chariot, one must begin with the component that makes it move, which is called the wheel.

The term "chariot" is a collective name for the <term>felloes<note>The outer wooden rim of a wheel into which the outer ends of the spokes are inserted.</note></term> and the spokes. This is why <term>Laozi<note>The ancient philosopher and reputed author of the Tao Te Ching. The quote here (from Chapter 11) emphasizes that the utility of a carriage comes from the relationship between its parts and the "nothingness" at the center of the hub.</note></term> said, "Assemble the various parts of a carriage, yet there is no carriage [without the coordination of these parts]." Although a chariot consists of distinct divisions such as wheels and spokes, in terms of its practical utility, nothing takes precedence over the wheel.

Therefore, the <term>Record of Trades<note>The *Kao Gong Ji* (考工記), China's oldest known technical encyclopedia, which details the standards for various crafts.</note></term> states: "In any examination of a chariot, one must begin with the part that hides itself against the ground." For this reason, the investigation of a chariot starts with the wheels.

The <term>Shuowen<note>The *Shuowen Jiezi* (說文解字), an early 2nd-century dictionary that is a primary source for the original meaning of Chinese characters.</note></term> explains: "That which has spokes is called a wheel (*lun*); that which lacks spokes is called a felloe (*rou*)." Thus, "wheel" is also used as a general term for the <term>rim<note>Original: *ya* (牙).</note></term>, the <term>spokes<note>Original: *fu* (輻).</note></term>, and the <term>hub<note>Original: *gu* (轂), the central part of the wheel through which the axle passes.</note></term>.

The *Record of Trades* states: "The wheel of a <term>war chariot<note>Original: *bingche* (兵車).</note></term> is six feet and six inches in diameter. The wheel of a <term>state carriage<note>Original: *chengche* (乘車), used by high-ranking officials and for ceremonial purposes.</note></term> is six feet and six inches. The wheel of a <term>hunting chariot<note>Original: *tianche* (田車).</note></term> is six feet and three inches." I will now provide explanations based on the war chariot and the state carriage.

The <term>Record of Trades<note>Original: *Kao Gong Ji*. This is because the <term>rim<note>Original: *wang* (輞). While *ya* refers to the segments, *wang* refers to the assembled outer circular frame of the wheel.</note></term> is not made of a single piece of wood; its curvature must be achieved through <term>bending<note>Original: *rou* (揉/輮). This refers to the ancient technique of using heat and moisture to bend straight timber into arcs.</note></term>. <gloss>In the "Explication of the Trigrams" in the *Book of Changes*, the Kan hexagram is associated with "shaping and bending" for the making of bows and wheels; the *Ji Jiu Pian* primer also contains the character for "bent rim."</gloss> These segments are joined, sometimes five to form a circle, or sometimes six <gloss>though the classical texts do not explicitly state the exact number of pieces</gloss>. At the points where they "embrace" and join together, there must be "male teeth" <note>Tenons.</note> that interlock to ensure stability. Because these joints resemble teeth, the component is called a *ya* <note>The Chinese word *ya* (牙) literally means "tooth."</note>. The <term>*Shuowen*<note>The foundational Chinese dictionary *Shuowen Jiezi*.</note></term> says: "The word *ya* refers to the teeth of a male, depicting the shape of upper and lower teeth interlocking." In the context of chariot felloes, the character for *ya* is written with the wood radical <note>Changing 牙 to 枒.</note>. The definition provided is: "The meeting point of the chariot's rim." Thus, *ya* was originally the name for the joint where the rim segments meet; by extension, the entire rim came to be called *ya*. <gloss>The *Record of Trades* says: "Examine the tenons and mortises to ensure they are not uneven (*ou*)." The *Shuowen* defines *ou* as "teeth that do not meet properly," which serves as evidence for the "tooth" meaning of the felloe segments. Furthermore, the *Zuo Zhuan* states: "The cheekbones and the chariot-felloe rely on each other." The commentator Du Yu notes that the "chariot-felloe" refers to the wheel itself. These various references mutually clarify the meaning.</gloss>

Regarding the measurements for the width and thickness of the felloes, there are records in the classic text that can be consulted. The *Record of Trades* states: "Divide the height of the wheel into six parts; use one part to determine the circumference of the felloe segment." Thus, the felloe's girth is one-sixth of the wheel's height.

## Explanations of the Carriage Box, Part 2

The part of the vehicle designed to hold objects is called the **carriage box** <note>original: "Yu" (輿); specifically the body or "passenger" compartment of the carriage.</note>.

Therefore, the invention of the box followed the invention of the wheel. The "carriage box" is a collective name for the **outer frame-beams**, the **side-rails**, the **wheel-guards**, and the **sideboards**. It is incorrect to believe the term refers only to the space inside the horizontal railings and crossbars.

## Third Explanation: The Pole

### The Curved Shaft Pole

The <term>pole<note>Original: 輈 (*zhōu*). The central longitudinal beam of a chariot to which the horses are hitched.</note></term> is a curved shaft used for hitching horses. Because of its curved shape, its name shares the same sound as the word for "boat." The total length of the pole's body is over nineteen feet; no other timber component in a chariot is larger than this. Because wood that naturally fits the requirements for a pole is rare, the timber must be <term>steamed and bent<note>Original: 揉治 (*róuzhì*). A traditional woodworking technique using heat and moisture to curve straight wood.</note></term> before it will meet the proper track and the required degrees for the front upward curve and the depth of the arc. 

The "Transmissions of the Rites" section of the *Book of Rites* mentions "chariots appearing from the mountains." The *Li Dou Wei Yi* <note>An ancient apocryphal text on celestial and ritual portents.</note> says that "mountain chariots have hanging hooks." The *Xiao Jing Yuan Shen Qi* <note>An esoteric commentary on the *Classic of Filial Piety*.</note> states: "When a ruler's virtue reaches the mountains and hills, then the 'root-wood chariot' appears." These all refer to auspicious responses to a ruler’s virtue: trees in the mountains naturally grow into the shape of a "hanging hook" that perfectly fits the measurements of a chariot pole, requiring no manual bending. The reason the pole must be bent or curved is to equalize the strength of the horses when ascending or descending slopes. The section on "The Pole-Makers" notes that a straight shaft lacks this flexibility.

### Chapter 6: An Exposition on the Sequential Deduction of Chariot Dimensions

According to the **Record of Trades** <note>original: 考工記 (Kǎo Gōng Jì); a classic work on ancient Chinese technology and craftsmanship</note>, the descriptions of chariot-making are concise in wording yet comprehensive in scope. When discussing dimensions, for every primary component established, the text clearly states its measurement. Subsequently, whether the remaining information is abundant or sparse, one must analyze and deduce the rest; whether it is briefly mentioned or entirely missing, one must cross-reference to find the answers. By applying addition, subtraction, and proportional calculation, not even a hair's breadth of error remains.

Yuan <note>the author, Ruan Yuan</note> has already investigated the measurements pertaining to the general structure in the previous explanations. He now categorizes the reasons for these sequential calculations and the remaining undeduced matters, performing the mathematical steps as follows:

The *Record* states: "The height of the wheels for **war chariots** and **ceremonial chariots** <note>original: 乘車 (chéngchē); chariots used by officials or for rituals</note> is six *chi* and six *cun*." <term>chi/cun (Ancient Chinese units of measurement; during the Zhou dynasty, one *chi* was approximately 23 cm, divided into ten *cun*)</term>

Therefore, we have:
