# PREFACE.

The fact that certain bodies, after being rubbed, appear to attract other bodies, was known to the ancients. In modern times, a great variety of other phenomena have been observed, and have been found to be related to these phenomena of attraction. They have been classed under the name of *Electric* phenomena, amber <note>original: ἤλεκτρον (*elektron*)</note> having been the substance in which they were first described.

Other bodies, particularly the loadstone <note>A naturally magnetized piece of the mineral magnetite</note>, and pieces of iron and steel which have been subjected to certain processes, have also been long known to exhibit phenomena of action at a distance. These phenomena, with others related to them, were found to differ from the electric phenomena, and have been classed under the name of *Magnetic* phenomena, the loadstone <note>original: μάγνης (*magnes*)</note> being found in the Thessalian Magnesia.

These two classes of phenomena have since been found to be related to each other, and the relations between the various phenomena of both classes, so far as they are known, constitute the science of Electromagnetism.

The results of magnetic research on navigation, and the importance of a knowledge of the true direction of the compass and of the effect of the iron in a ship, are significant. But the labors of those who have endeavored to render navigation more secure by means of magnetic observations have, at the same time, greatly advanced the progress of pure science.

Consequently, his memoirs on Terrestrial Magnetism <note>original: *Intensitas vis magneticae terrestris ad mensuram absolutam revocata* (1832)</note> may be taken as models of physical research by all those who are engaged in the measurement of any of the forces in nature.

The important applications of electromagnetism to telegraphy have also reacted on pure science by giving a commercial value to accurate electrical measurements, and by affording to electricians the use of apparatus on a scale which greatly transcends that of any ordinary laboratory. The consequences of this demand for electrical knowledge, and of these experimental opportunities for acquiring it, have been already very great. These factors have stimulated the energies of advanced electricians and diffused among practical men a degree of accurate knowledge which is likely to lead to the general scientific progress of the whole engineering profession.

There are several treatises in which electrical and magnetic phenomena are described in a popular way. These, however, are not what is wanted by those who have been brought face to face with quantities to be measured, and whose minds do not rest satisfied with lecture-room experiments.

There is also a considerable mass of mathematical memoirs which are of great importance in electrical science, but they lie concealed in the bulky *Transactions* <note>The published journals of scientific organizations like the Royal Society.</note> of learned societies; they do not form a connected system; they are of very unequal merit, and they are for the most part beyond the comprehension of any but professional mathematicians.

I have therefore thought that a treatise would be useful which should have for its principal object to take up the whole subject in a methodical manner, and which should also indicate how each part of the subject is brought within the reach of methods of verification by actual measurement.

### ON THE MEASUREMENT OF QUANTITIES.

1.] Every expression of a quantity consists of two factors or components. One of these is the name of a certain known quantity of the same kind as the quantity to be expressed, which is taken as a standard of reference. The other component is the number of times the standard is to be taken in order to make up the required quantity. The standard quantity is technically called the <term>Unit</term>, and the number is called the <term>Numerical Value</term> of the quantity.

There must be as many different units as there are different kinds of quantities to be measured, but in all dynamical sciences <note>sciences dealing with matter in motion and the forces affecting it</note> it is possible to define these units in terms of the three fundamental units of <term>Length</term>, <term>Time</term>, and <term>Mass</term>. Thus, the units of area and of volume are defined respectively as the square and the cube whose sides are the unit of length.

Sometimes, however, we find several units of the same kind founded on independent considerations. Thus the gallon, or the volume of ten pounds of water, is used as a unit of capacity as well as the cubic foot. The gallon may be a convenient measure in some cases, but it is not a systematic one, since its numerical relation to the cubic foot is not a whole integer.

2.] In framing a mathematical system, we suppose the fundamental units of length, time, and mass to be given, and we derive all the secondary units from these by the simplest possible definitions.

# 6. DERIVED UNITS

->*Derived Units*<-

6. The unit of **Velocity** is the speed at which a unit of length is covered in a unit of time. Its dimensions are $[LT^{-1}]$. <note>L stands for Length, T for Time. The notation indicates length divided by time.</note>

If we adopt the units of length and time derived from the vibrations of light, then the unit of velocity is the velocity of light.

The unit of **Acceleration** is the rate of change where velocity increases by one unit in a unit of time. Its dimensions are $[LT^{-2}]$.

The unit of **Density** is the density of a substance that contains one unit of mass within one unit of volume. Its dimensions are $[ML^{-3}]$. <note>M stands for Mass.</note>

The unit of **Momentum** is the momentum of one unit of mass moving at one unit of velocity. Its dimensions are $[MLT^{-1}]$.

The unit of **Force** is the force which produces one unit of momentum in one unit of time. Its dimensions are $[MLT^{-2}]$.

This is the absolute unit of force, and this definition is implied in every equation in **Dynamics** <note>The branch of mechanics concerned with the motion of bodies under the action of forces.</note>. Nevertheless, in many textbooks where these equations are provided, a different unit of force is used: the weight of the national unit of mass. To make the equations work under that system, the national unit of mass is abandoned in favor of an artificial unit. This dynamical unit is equal to the national unit divided by the numerical value of the force of gravity at that specific location. 

In this way, both the unit of force and the unit of mass are made to depend on the value of gravity, which varies from place to place. Consequently, scientific statements involving these quantities are incomplete unless one also knows the local force of gravity where the observations were made.

The abolition of this local method of measuring force for scientific purposes is mainly due to the introduction of a general system for observing magnetic force across different countries where gravity varies. All such forces are now measured according to the strictly dynamical method deduced from our definitions; the numerical results remain the same regardless of the country where the experiments are conducted.

The unit of **Work** is the work performed by one unit of force acting through one unit of length measured in its own direction. Its dimensions are $[ML^2T^{-2}]$.

The **Energy** of a system is its capacity for performing work. It is measured by the total work the system is capable of performing by spending its entire store of energy.

### *On Physical Continuity and Discontinuity.*

7.] A quantity is said to vary continuously when, if it passes from one value to another, it assumes all the intermediate values.

We may obtain the concept of continuity from considering the continuous existence of a particle of matter in time and space. Such a particle cannot pass from one position to another without tracing a continuous line in space, and the coordinates of its position must be continuous functions of time.

In the so-called "equation of continuity," as given in treatises on Hydrodynamics <note>original: "Hydrodynamics"; the study of fluids in motion.</note>, the fact expressed is that matter cannot appear in or disappear from a specific volume of space without passing in or out through the sides of that volume.

A quantity is said to be a continuous function of its variables when, if the variables change continuously, the quantity itself changes continuously.

### *Discontinuity of a Function of more than One Variable.*

**8.]** If we suppose the values of all the variables except *x* to be constant, the discontinuity of the function will occur for particular values of *x*. These will be connected with the values of the other variables by an equation which we may write:

-> $F = \frac{F_1 + e^{n\phi} F_2}{1 + e^{n\phi}}.$ <-

As long as *n* is a finite quantity, however great, *F* will be a continuous function. However, if we make *n* infinite, *F* will be equal to *F*₂ when &phi; is positive, and equal to *F*₁ when &phi; is negative.

### *Periodic and Multiple Functions.*

9.] If $u$ is a function of $x$ such that its value is the same for $x$, $x + a$, $x + na$, and all values of $x$ differing by $a$, then $u$ is called a **periodic function** of $x$, and $a$ is called its **period**.

# 11.] VECTORS, OR DIRECTED QUANTITIES.

For the purposes of calculation, it is desirable to avoid explicitly introducing Cartesian coordinates <note>A system that specifies each point uniquely in a plane by a set of numerical coordinates.</note>. Instead, one should fix the mind at once on a point in space rather than its three coordinates, and on the magnitude and direction of a force rather than its three components. This mode of contemplating geometrical and physical quantities is more primitive and more natural than the other, although the ideas connected with it did not receive their full development until Sir William Rowan Hamilton made the next great step in dealing with space by the invention of his *Calculus of Quaternions* <note>A number system that extends complex numbers, used to represent rotations and orientations in three-dimensional space.</note>.

As the methods of René Descartes are still the most familiar to students of science, and as they are truly the most useful for the purposes of calculation, we shall express all our results in the Cartesian form. I am convinced, however, that the introduction of these ideas—as distinguished from the specific operations and methods of Quaternions—will be of great use to us in the study of all parts of our subject. This is especially true in electrodynamics, where we must deal with a number of physical quantities whose relations to each other can be expressed far more simply by a few of Hamilton’s words than by ordinary equations.

11.] One of the most important features of Hamilton’s method is the division of quantities into **Scalars** and **Vectors**.

A **Scalar** quantity can be completely defined by a single numerical value. Its numerical value does not depend in any way on the directions we choose for the coordinate axes.

A **Vector**, or **Directed quantity**, requires three numerical specifications for its definition. These may be most simply understood as referring to the directions of the coordinate axes.

Scalar quantities do not involve direction. The volume of a geometrical figure, the mass and the energy of a material body, the hydrostatic pressure at a point in a fluid, and the potential at a point in space are all examples of scalar quantities.

A vector quantity has direction as well as magnitude, and is defined such that reversing its direction reverses its mathematical sign. The displacement of a point—represented by a straight line drawn from its original to its final position—may be taken as the typical vector quantity, from which the name "Vector" <note>From the Latin *vehere*, meaning "to carry."</note> is derived.

The velocity of a body, its momentum, the force acting on it, an electric current, and the magnetization of a particle of iron are all instances of vector quantities.

### 13.] FORCES AND FLUXES.

There are certain cases in which a quantity may be measured with reference to a line as well as with reference to an area.

For example, when discussing the displacements of elastic solids, we may focus our attention either on the original and the actual position of a particle—in which case the displacement of the particle is measured by the line drawn from the first position to the second—or we may consider a small area fixed in space and determine what quantity of the solid passes across that area during the displacement.

In the same way, the velocity of a fluid may be investigated either with respect to the actual velocity of the individual particles, or with respect to the quantity of the fluid which flows through any fixed area.

However, in these cases, we need to know the density of the body as well as the displacement or velocity separately in order to apply the first method; whenever we attempt to form a <term>molecular theory</term><note>A theory that explains physical properties based on the behavior of individual molecules or particles.</note>, we must use the second method.

In the case of the flow of electricity, we do not know anything about its density or its velocity within the conductor. We only know the value of what, according to the <term>fluid theory</term><note>An earlier scientific model that treated electricity as an invisible fluid flowing through matter.</note>, would correspond to the product of the density and the velocity. Therefore, in all such cases, we must apply the more general method of measuring the **flux** across an area.

In electrical science, **electromotive force** and **magnetic force** belong to the first class, as they are defined with reference to lines. When we wish to indicate this fact, we may refer to them as **Forces**.

On the other hand, **electric and magnetic induction**, and **electric currents**, belong to the second class, as they are defined with reference to areas. When we wish to indicate this fact, we shall refer to them as **Fluxes**.

Each of these forces may be considered as producing, or tending to produce, its corresponding flux. Thus, electromotive force produces electric currents in conductors and tends to produce them in <term>dielectrics</term><note>Insulating materials that can be polarized by an electric field.</note>. It produces electric induction in dielectrics, and probably in conductors also. In the same sense, magnetic force produces magnetic induction.

13.] In some cases, the flux is simply proportional to the force and moves in the same direction, but in other cases, we can only state that the direction and magnitude of the flux are functions of the direction and magnitude of the force.

The direction and magnitude of a quantity may depend upon some action or effect which takes place entirely along a certain line, or it may depend upon something of the nature of rotation about that line as an axis. The laws of combination of directed quantities are the same whether they are longitudinal or rotational, so that there is no difference in the mathematical treatment of the two classes, but there may be physical circumstances which indicate to which class we must refer a particular phenomenon. Now, let $A$ and $P$ be placed indefinitely near to each other, but on opposite sides of a diaphragm, and let $K$ be the line-integral from $A$ to $P$.

Let $A'$ and $P'$ be two other points on opposite sides of the same diaphragm and indefinitely near to each other, and let $K'$ be the line-integral from $A'$ to $P'$. Then $K' = K$.

For if we draw the paths $AA'$ and $PP'$ so they are nearly coincident but on opposite sides of the diaphragm, the line-integrals along these lines will be equal. Suppose each is equal to $L$; then the line-integral of $A'P'$ is equal to the sum of the integrals along $A'A + AP + PP'$. This equals $- L + K + L = K$, which is the same as the integral of $AP$.

Hence, the line-integral around a closed curve which passes through one diaphragm of the system in a given direction is a constant quantity $K$. This quantity is called the **Cyclic constant** corresponding to the given cycle.

Let any closed curve be drawn within the region, and let it cut the diaphragm of the first cycle $p$ times in the positive direction and $p'$ times in the negative direction. Let $n_1 = p - p'$. Then the line-integral of the closed curve will be $n_1 K_1$.

Similarly, the line-integral of any closed curve will be:

-> $n_1 K_1 + n_2 K_2 + ... + n_K K_K$ <-

where $n_K$ represents the excess of the number of positive passages of the curve through the diaphragm of the cycle $K$ over the number of negative passages.

### *On Tubes and Lines of Flow*

If the space is divided into tubes such that the surface integral for every tube is unity, the tubes are called **Unit tubes**. The surface integral over any finite surface $S$ bounded by a closed curve $L$ is equal to the number of such tubes which pass through $S$ in the positive direction—or, what is the same thing, the number which pass through the closed curve $L$.

Hence, the surface integral of $S$ depends only on the form of its boundary $L$, and not on the shape of the surface within that boundary.

##### *Electrification by Friction.*

27.] EXPERIMENT I*. Let a piece of glass and a piece of resin <note>A solid or semi-solid organic substance, such as amber or sealing wax, often used in early electrical experiments.</note>, neither of which shows any electrical properties, be rubbed together and left with the rubbed surfaces in contact. They will still show no electrical properties. Let them be separated. They will now attract each other.

If a second piece of glass is rubbed with a second piece of resin, and if the pieces are then separated and suspended near the first pieces of glass and resin, the following may be observed:

(1) The two pieces of glass repel each other.
(2) Each piece of glass attracts each piece of resin.
(3) The two pieces of resin repel each other.

These phenomena of attraction and repulsion are called electrical phenomena, and the bodies which exhibit them are said to be electrified, or to be charged with electricity.

Bodies may be electrified in many other ways, as well as by friction.

The electrical properties of the two pieces of glass are similar to each other but opposite to those of the two pieces of resin; the glass attracts what the resin repels and repels what the resin attracts.

# ELECTRIFICATION.

It is the established practice of scientists to call vitreous electrification *positive*, and resinous electrification *negative*. No force, whether of attraction or repulsion, can be observed between an electrified body and a body that is not electrified. In any case where bodies not previously electrified are observed to be acted upon by an electrified body, it is because they have become electrified by induction <note>The process by which a charged object creates an electrical state in a nearby neutral object without physical contact.</note>.

It is impossible, therefore, that <term>electricity</term> and <term>energy</term> should be quantities of the same category, for <term>electricity</term> is only one of the factors of <term>energy</term>, the other factor being "Potential."

### *The Electric Field.*

44.] The Electric Field is the portion of space in the neighborhood of electrified bodies, considered with reference to electric phenomena. ### *Electric Potential.*

45.] If a small body carrying a small charge *e* is moved from a given point to an indefinite distance from the electrified bodies, it will experience at each point of its path a force *Re*, where *R* varies from point to point along that path. If the charge *e* could be made equal to one unit without disturbing the electrification of other bodies, we might define the potential at any point as the work done on a body charged with a unit of electricity while moving from that point to an infinite distance.

A body electrified positively tends to move from places of higher positive potential to places of lower positive (or negative) potential. Conversely, a body negatively electrified tends to move in the opposite direction.

In a conductor, the electrification is distributed exactly as if it were free to move within the conductor according to this same law. Therefore, if two parts of a conductor have different potentials, positive electricity will move from the part with the higher potential to the part with the lower potential as long as that difference exists. A conductor, therefore, cannot be in electrical equilibrium <note>A state where there is no net flow of electric charge.</note> unless every point within it has the same potential. This value is called the Potential of the Conductor.

An electrified point restricted to such a surface will have no tendency to move from one part of the surface to another, because the potential is the same at every point. An equipotential surface is, therefore, a surface of equilibrium or a "level surface" <note>Similar to how a flat floor is a level surface where a ball has no tendency to roll in any particular direction due to gravity.</note>.

The resultant force at any point on a surface is directed perpendicular <note>original: "in the direction of the normal"</note> to that surface. The magnitude of this force is such that the work performed on a unit of electricity when moving from surface *V* to surface *V'* is equal to the difference in potential, *V* - *V'*.

No two equipotential surfaces with different potentials can intersect, because a single point cannot possess more than one potential value. However, a single equipotential surface may intersect itself; this occurs at all points and lines of equilibrium <note>Points where the net electric force is zero</note>.

The surface of a conductor in electrical equilibrium is necessarily an equipotential surface. If the electrification of the conductor is positive across its entire surface, then the potential will decrease as we move away from the surface in any direction, and the conductor will be surrounded by a series of surfaces with lower potential.

However, if some regions of the conductor are electrified positively and others negatively (due to the influence of external electrified bodies), the complete equipotential surface will consist of the conductor's surface itself, along with a system of other surfaces. These other surfaces meet the conductor's surface at the lines that divide the positive regions from the negative regions. These lines will be lines of equilibrium, meaning that an electrified point placed on one of these lines will experience no force in any direction.

When the surface of a conductor is electrified positively in some parts and negatively in others, there must be another electrified body in the field besides the conductor itself. If we allow a positively electrified point to start from a positively electrified part of the surface and move constantly in the direction of the force acting upon it, the potential at that point will continually decrease. This continues until the point either reaches a negatively electrified surface with a lower potential than the first conductor, or moves away to an infinite distance. Since the potential at an infinite distance is zero, the latter case can only occur if the potential of the conductor is positive.

In the same way, a negatively electrified point moving away from a negatively electrified part of the surface must either reach a positively electrified surface or pass off to infinity; this latter case can only happen if the potential of the conductor is negative.

Therefore, if both positive and negative electrification exist on a single conductor, there must be another body in the field with a potential of the same sign as the conductor but with a greater numerical value. If a conductor of any shape is alone in the field, the electrification of every part of it will have the same sign as the potential of the conductor.

This result appears anomalous <note>original: "anomalous"; meaning inconsistent or unexpected</note> only if we expect the loss to occur by the passage of electricity through the air between the bodies.

# DIELECTRICS.

## *Specific Inductive Capacity.*

52.] Faraday gave the name "Dielectrics" to all bodies whose insulating power is high enough that, when placed between two conductors at different potentials, the electromotive force acting on them does not immediately redistribute their electricity to equalize the potential.

Faraday discovered that the capacity of an accumulator <note>original: "accumulator"; a modern capacitor</note> depends on the nature of the insulating medium between the two conductors, as well as on the dimensions and relative positions of the conductors themselves. He experimented by substituting other insulating materials for air as the dielectric of the accumulator without changing it in any other way. He found that when air and other gases were used as the insulating medium, the capacity of the accumulator remained the same. However, when shellac, sulphur, glass, and similar materials were substituted for air, the capacity increased in a ratio that was specific to each substance.

The ratio of the capacity of an accumulator made of any dielectric medium to the capacity of an accumulator of the same form and dimensions filled with air was named by Faraday the **Specific Inductive Capacity** of that dielectric medium. This value is equal to one (unity) for air and other gases at all pressures, and probably at all temperatures; it is greater than one for all other liquid or solid dielectrics that have been examined.

If the dielectric is not a good insulator, it is difficult to measure its inductive capacity because the accumulator will not hold a charge long enough for a measurement to be taken. However, it is certain that inductive capacity is a property not limited to good insulators, and it likely exists in all bodies.

# ELECTRIC GLOW

If the air, or any other surrounding <term>dielectric</term> <note>an insulating material that can sustain an electric field</note>, had an invincible insulating power, the electrical force at a sharp point would be infinite. This result would actually occur in such a case; however, the fact is that as soon as the resulting force near the point reaches a certain limit, the insulating power of the air breaks down. Consequently, the air immediately surrounding the point becomes a conductor. At a certain distance away from the point, the resulting force is no longer strong enough to break through the air's insulation; therefore, the electric current is stopped, and electricity accumulates in the air around the point.

The point is thus surrounded by particles of air charged with the same kind of electricity as itself. The effect of this charged air is to relieve the point itself from part of the enormous <term>electromotive force</term> <note>the electrical pressure or potential difference</note> it would have experienced if the solid conductor alone had been electrified. In effect, the surface of the electrified body is no longer "pointed," because the point is enveloped by a rounded mass of electrified air. This mass of air, rather than the solid conductor, may be regarded as the actual outer electrified surface.

If this portion of electrified air could be kept still, the electrified body would retain its charge—if not on its own surface, then at least in its immediate vicinity. However, because the charged particles of air are free to move under the influence of electrical force, they tend to move away from the electrified body because they carry the same kind of electricity <note>referring to the principle that like charges repel</note>. These charged particles of air tend to move off in the direction of the <term>lines of force</term> and approach surrounding bodies that are oppositely electrified. When they move away, other uncharged particles take their place around the point. Since these new particles cannot shield the point from the excessive electric tension, a new discharge occurs. These newly charged particles then move off, and the process repeats as long as the body remains electrified.

In this way, the following phenomena are produced: at and very close to the point, there is a steady **glow**. This arises from the constant discharges taking place between the point and the air immediately surrounding it.

The charged particles of air tend to move off in the same general direction, thereby producing a **current of air** from the point. This current consists of the charged particles themselves and likely other neutral particles carried along with them. By artificially assisting this air current, we can increase the glow; by blocking the formation of the current, we can prevent the glow from continuing.

The <term>electric wind</term> <note>original: "electric wind"; the stream of air moving away from a sharp electrified point</note> in the neighborhood of the point is sometimes very rapid, but it soon loses its velocity. The air, along with its charged particles, is then carried about by the general motions of the atmosphere, forming an invisible electric cloud. When these charged particles come near any conducting surface, such as a wall, they induce an <term>electrification</term> on that surface opposite to their own. They are then attracted toward the wall, but since the <term>electromotive force</term> <note>the physical intensity or "pressure" driving the electricity</note> is small, they may remain near the wall for a long time without being drawn all the way to the surface and discharged. However, the electrical forces acting between charged portions of air and other bodies are exceedingly feeble compared to the forces that produce winds arising from differences in density due to temperature. Therefore, it is very improbable that any observable part of the motion of ordinary thunderclouds arises from electrical causes.

The passage of electricity from one place to another by the motion of charged particles is called <term>Electrical Convection</term> or <term>Convective Discharge</term>.

The <term>electrical glow</term> is therefore produced by the constant passage of electricity through a small portion of air where the tension is very high. This charges the surrounding particles of air, which are continually swept away by the <term>electric wind</term>—an essential part of the phenomenon.

The glow is more easily formed in thin (rare) air than in dense air, and more easily when the point is positive than when it is negative. This and many other differences between positive and negative <term>electrification</term> must be studied by those who desire to discover something about the nature of electricity. So far, however, these differences have not been satisfactorily explained by any existing theory.

## *The Electric Spark*

57.] When the tension in the space between two conductors is significant throughout the entire gap—as is the case with two metal balls whose distance from each other is not large compared to their radii—the discharge, when it occurs, usually takes the form of a spark. Through this spark, nearly the entire electrical charge is released at once.

In this situation, once any part of the dielectric <note>original: "dielectric"; an insulating material that can sustain an electric field, such as air</note> has failed, the areas on either side of it along the path of the electric force are subjected to even greater tension. Consequently, they also fail, and the discharge tears right through the dielectric. This is much like how a small tear in the edge of a piece of paper, when pulled, causes the paper to rip all the way through, starting at the initial tear but occasionally veering toward other weak spots in the material. In the same way, the electric spark begins at the point where the electric tension first overcomes the insulation of the dielectric and proceeds from that point in an apparently irregular path, seeking out other weak points, such as dust particles floating in the air.

### 60.] STRESS IN DIELECTRICS.

Between electrified bodies, force is exerted through and by means of the medium. Just as in the familiar examples of one body acting on another through the tension of a rope or the pressure of a rod, we find that the medium must be in a state of mechanical stress.

As Faraday pointed out <note>original: *Exp. Res.*, series xi. 1297. Faraday's *Experimental Researches in Electricity*.</note>, the nature of this stress is a tension along the lines of force combined with an equal pressure in all directions at right angles to these lines. The magnitude of these stresses is proportional to the energy of the electrification—or, in other words, to the square of the resulting electromotive force multiplied by the specific inductive capacity <note>A property of a material that determines its ability to store electrical energy in an electric field; now commonly called relative permittivity.</note> of the medium.

This distribution of stress is the only one consistent with the observed mechanical action on electrified bodies, and also with the observed equilibrium of the fluid dielectric <note>An insulating material, such as air, oil, or glass, that can sustain an electric field.</note> that surrounds them. I have therefore thought it a justifiable step in scientific procedure to assume the actual existence of this state of stress and to follow that assumption to its logical consequences. 

Finding the phrase *electric tension* used in several vague senses, I have attempted to limit it to what I believe was intended by those who have used it: namely, the state of stress in the dielectric medium which causes the motion of electrified bodies and leads, when continually increased, to a disruptive discharge <note>A sudden release of electricity, such as a spark or lightning bolt, through an insulating material.</note>. Electric tension, in this sense, is a tension of exactly the same kind, and measured in the same way, as the tension of a rope. The dielectric medium, which can support a certain amount of tension and no more, may be said to have a certain strength in exactly the same sense as a rope is said to have a certain strength. Thus, for example, Thomson <note>William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, a preeminent physicist of the era.</note> has found that air at ordinary pressure and temperature can support an electric tension of 9,600 grains weight per square foot before a spark passes.

60.] From the hypothesis that electric action is not a direct action between bodies at a distance, but is exerted by means of the medium between the bodies, we have deduced that this medium must be in a state of stress. We have also determined the character of the stress and compared it with the stresses that may occur in solid bodies. Along the lines of force there is tension, and perpendicular to them there is pressure; the numerical magnitude of these forces is equal, and each is proportional to the square of the resulting force at that point. # ELECTROSTATIC PHENOMENA

...an idea of the nature of the electric polarization of the dielectric medium <note>dielectric medium: an insulating material that can be polarized by an applied electric field</note>.

An elementary portion of a body may be said to be polarized when it acquires equal and opposite properties on two opposite sides. The idea of internal polarity may be studied to the greatest advantage as exemplified in permanent magnets, and it will be explained at greater length when we come to treat of magnetism.

The electric polarization of an elementary portion of a dielectric is a forced state into which the medium is thrown by the action of electromotive force <note>electromotive force: the energy per unit charge that drives electricity, often abbreviated as EMF</note>, and which disappears when that force is removed. We may conceive it to consist in what we may call an electrical displacement, produced by the electromotive force. When the electromotive force acts on a conducting medium, it produces a current through it; but if the medium is a non-conductor or dielectric, the current cannot flow through the medium. Instead, the electricity is displaced within the medium in the direction of the electromotive force. The extent of this displacement depends on the magnitude of the electromotive force, so that if the electromotive force increases or diminishes, the electric displacement increases and diminishes in the same ratio.

The amount of the displacement is measured by the quantity of electricity which crosses a unit of area while the displacement increases from zero to its actual amount. This, therefore, is the measure of the electric polarization.

The analogy between the action of electromotive force in producing electric displacement and of ordinary mechanical force in producing the displacement of an elastic body is so obvious that I have ventured to call the ratio of the electromotive force to the corresponding electric displacement the coefficient of electric elasticity of the medium. This coefficient is different in different media, and varies inversely as the specific inductive capacity <note>specific inductive capacity: now commonly known as the relative permittivity of a material</note> of each medium.

The variations of electric displacement evidently constitute electric currents. These currents, however, can only exist during the variation of the displacement. Therefore, since the displacement cannot exceed a certain value without causing disruptive discharge <note>disruptive discharge: a sudden flow of electricity through an insulator, such as a spark or arc, occurring when the electric field exceeds the material's strength</note>, they cannot be continued indefinitely in the same direction, like the currents through conductors.

In tourmaline and other pyro-electric crystals <note>pyro-electric: crystals that generate an electric charge when heated or cooled</note>, it is probable that a state of electric polarization exists which depends upon temperature and does not require an external electromotive force to produce it. # ELECTRIC DISPLACEMENT.

If a body possessed internal electric polarization <note>original: "electric polarization"; the alignment of electric dipoles within a material</note>, the outside would gradually become charged in such a way as to neutralize the effect of the internal electrification for all points outside the body. This external surface charge <note>original: "superficial charge"</note> could not be detected by any of the ordinary tests, and could not be removed by any of the ordinary methods for discharging surface electrification. The internal polarization of the substance would therefore never be discovered unless the amount of internal polarization could be increased or diminished by some means, such as a change in temperature. In that case, the external electrification would no longer be able to neutralize the external effect of the internal polarization, and an apparent electrification would be observed, as is seen in the case of tourmaline <note>A mineral that generates an electric charge when heated or cooled, a property known as pyroelectricity</note>.

If a charge *e* is uniformly distributed over the surface of a sphere, the resultant force at any point of the medium surrounding the sphere is numerically equal to the charge *e* divided by the square of the distance from the center of the sphere. According to our theory, this resultant force is accompanied by a displacement of electricity in a direction moving outward from the sphere.

If we now draw a concentric spherical surface with a radius *r*, the total displacement, *E*, through this surface will be proportional to the resultant force multiplied by the area of the spherical surface. However, the resultant force is directly proportional to the charge *e* and inversely proportional to the square of the radius, while the area of the surface is directly proportional to the square of the radius.

Therefore, the total displacement, *E*, is proportional to the charge *e* and is independent of the radius.

To determine the ratio between the charge *e* and the quantity of electricity *E* displaced outward through the spherical surface, let us consider the work done upon the medium in the region between two concentric spherical surfaces while the displacement is increased from *E* to *E* + *δE* <note>The symbol δE represents a very small increase or "increment" of displacement</note>. If *V*₁ and *V*₂ denote the potentials at the inner and the outer of these surfaces respectively, the electromotive force by which the additional displacement is produced is *V*₁ - *V*₂, so that the work spent in increasing the displacement is (*V*₁ - *V*₂) *δE*.

If we now make the inner surface coincide with that of the electrified sphere and make the radius of the other infinite, *V*₁ becomes *V* (the potential of the sphere) and *V*₂ becomes zero. Thus, the total work done in the surrounding medium is *V δE*.

To clarify our concepts of electric displacement, let us consider an accumulator <note>original: "accumulator"; a historical term for a capacitor, a device used to store electrical energy</note> formed of two conducting plates $A$ and $B$, separated by a layer <note>original: "stratum"</note> of a dielectric $C$ <note>term: dielectric; an insulating material that can be polarized by an electric field</note>. Let $W$ be a conducting wire joining $A$ and $B$, and let us suppose that by the action of an electromotive force <note>term: electromotive force; the energy per unit charge provided by a source like a battery</note> a quantity $Q$ of positive electricity is transferred along the wire from $B$ to $A$. The positive electrification of $A$ and the negative electrification of $B$ will produce a certain electromotive force acting from $A$ towards $B$ in the dielectric layer, and this will produce an electric displacement from $A$ towards $B$ within the dielectric. The amount of this displacement, as measured by the quantity of electricity forced across an imaginary section of the dielectric dividing it into two layers, will be, according to our theory, exactly $Q$. See Articles 75, 76, 111.

It appears, therefore, that at the same time that a quantity $Q$ of electricity is being transferred along the wire by the electromotive force from $B$ towards $A$, so as to cross every section of the wire, the same quantity of electricity crosses every section of the dielectric from $A$ towards $B$ by reason of the electric displacement.

The reverse motions of electricity will take place during the discharge of the accumulator. In the wire, the discharge will be $Q$ from $A$ to $B$, and in the dielectric, the displacement will subside, and a quantity of electricity $Q$ will cross every section from $B$ towards $A$.

Every case of electrification or discharge may therefore be considered as a motion in a closed circuit, such that at every section of the circuit the same quantity of electricity crosses in the same time. This is the case not only in the voltaic circuit <note>original: "voltaic circuit"; a continuous flow of electricity, such as that from a battery</note> where it has always been recognized, but also in those cases in which electricity has been generally supposed to be accumulated in certain places.
