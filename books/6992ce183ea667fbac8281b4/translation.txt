# Chart of Cultivating Reality
<note>original: *Xiuzhen Tu* (修真圖). A title used for various Daoist diagrams and texts that describe the cultivation of the body and spirit through internal alchemy and physical exercise.</note>

**Beginning of Spring, Festival of the First Lunar Month.** 
The energetic movement is governed by the Initial Qi of the Terminal Yin <note>original: *Jueyin* (厥陰). One of the six stages of energetic flow in traditional Chinese medicine.</note>. This period corresponds to the Ministerial Fire of the Hand Lesser Yang Triple Burner <note>original: *Sanjiao* (三焦). An organ system in Chinese medicine responsible for regulating energy and fluids throughout the three main sections of the torso.</note>.

It is appropriate every day during the hours of the Rat and the Ox <note>The periods between 11:00 PM and 1:00 AM, and 1:00 AM and 3:00 AM</note> to stack the hands and press against the torso, turn the body and clasp the neck, shrugging and stretching to the left and right three to five times each. One should also knock the teeth together, perform rhythmic breathing <note>original: *tuna* (吐納), a technique involving the expulsion of "old" breath and the inhalation of "new" vital energy</note>, and swallow the saliva.

This practice treats stagnant accumulations of wind-energy, neck pain, pain behind the ears, shoulder and upper arm pain, back pain, and various ailments of the elbows and forearms.

<term>Beginning of Spring</term> (original: *Lichun* 立春): The first of the twenty-four solar terms, marking the official start of the spring season in the lunisolar calendar.

<term>Terminal Yin</term> (original: *Jueyin* 厥陰): In medical theory, this represents the transition where Yin energy reaches its peak and begins to give way to Yang.

<term>Ministerial Fire</term> (original: *Xianghuo* 相火): A vital, transformative heat within the body that supports the functions of the internal organs, as opposed to "Imperial Fire" which is associated with the Heart.

<term>Upper Arm</term> (original: *Nao* 臑): Specifically refers to the fleshy part of the upper arm or the humerus area.

# Rain Water: Middle of the First Lunar Month
**The energetic movement is governed by the Initial Qi of Reverting Yin. The period is associated with the Hand Lesser Yang Triple Burner and Ministerial Fire.** <note>original: *Jueyin* (厥陰) and *Sanjiao* (三焦). In traditional Chinese medicine, "Ministerial Fire" refers to a secondary source of heat and vitality in the body, distinct from the primary "Imperial Fire" of the Heart.</note>

> Every day during the hours of the Rat and Ox, stack the hands,
> Press the shins and clasp the neck, turning the body to the left,
> Then to the right, stretching each side three to five times,
> Click the teeth, practice rhythmic breathing, and swallow the saliva.
<note>The hours of the Rat and Ox are 11:00 PM to 3:00 AM. This exercise involves a seated twist while holding the legs and neck to stimulate the flow of energy.</note>

### Treatment for Stagnation in the Triple Burner Meridians

**Treats pathogenic toxins, dry throat, swelling and ulcers, throat obstruction, deafness, excessive sweating, pain in the outer corner of the eyes, cheek pain, and various other ailments.**

<term>Rain Water</term> (original: *Yushui* 雨水): The second of the twenty-four solar terms, usually beginning around February 18th, marking the increase in rainfall and the thawing of ice.

<term>Triple Burner</term> (original: *Sanjiao* 三焦): A unique concept in Chinese medicine referring to a "functional organ" that coordinates the movement of water and energy through the upper, middle, and lower sections of the torso.

<term>Reverting Yin</term> (original: *Jueyin* 厥陰): One of the "Six Qi" or energetic stages, representing a transition point in the body's internal balance.

<term>Outer corner of the eye</term> (original: *Ruizhi* 鋭眥): Specifically the canthus or the point where the upper and lower eyelids meet furthest from the nose.

# Awakening of Insects: Second Lunar Month Solar Term
**The energetic movement is governed by the Initial Qi of Reverting Yin. This period is associated with the Hand Yang Brightness Large Intestine and Dryness Metal.** <note>original: *Jueyin* (厥陰) and *Shou Yangming Dachang* (手陽明大腸). In traditional Chinese medicine, "Dryness Metal" refers to one of the Five Elements associated with the harvest, clarity, and the respiratory/digestive systems.</note>

Every day during the hours of the Ox and Tiger, clench the fists firmly. Turn the neck and look back towards the elbows, pulling and jerking in five major repetitions. Click the teeth thirty-six times, practice rhythmic breathing, and swallow the saliva nine times.
<note>The hours of the Ox and Tiger are 1:00 AM to 5:00 AM. "Clenching the fists firmly" (original: *wogu* 握固) is a specific Daoist hand gesture where the thumb is tucked into the palm to "seal" the vital essence. The 36 tooth clicks and 9 swallows are standard ritual numbers in longevity practices.</note>

---

### Treatment for Accumulations in the Spine, Spleen, and Stomach

**Treats pathogenic toxins that have accumulated, yellowing of the eyes, dry mouth, nasal discharge and nosebleeds, throat obstruction, and sudden loss of voice.**

**Also treats "head-wind," bleeding gums, blurred vision, sensitivity to light, loss of the sense of smell, mumps, and various sores or carbuncles.**
<note>"Head-wind" (original: *toufeng* 頭風) is a traditional term for chronic, paroxysmal headaches or migraines believed to be caused by external "wind" invading the body.</note>

<term>Awakening of Insects</term> (original: *Jingzhe* 驚蟄): The third of the twenty-four solar terms, usually beginning around March 5th. It signifies the time when spring thunder wakes hibernating insects.

<term>Hand Yang Brightness</term> (original: *Shou Yangming* 手陽明): The meridian or energetic pathway associated with the Large Intestine.

<term>Reverting Yin</term> (original: *Jueyin* 厥陰): The "Initial Qi" phase of the seasonal cycle, representing the very beginning of the wood/spring energy as it rises from the cold of winter.

<term>Nasal Discharge and Nosebleeds</term> (original: *Qiuniu* 鼽衄): A compound term for various afflictions of the nose involving either watery discharge or bleeding.

<term>Sores and Carbuncles</term> (original: *Dingchuang* 疔瘡): Specifically refers to hard, deep-rooted infections or boils that were often dangerous before modern medicine.

## Spring Equinox: Middle of the Second Lunar Month
**The energetic movement is governed by the Second Qi of Lesser Yin. This period is associated with the Hand Yang Brightness Large Intestine and Dryness Metal.**
<note>original: *Shaoyin* (少陰) and *Shou Yangming Dachang* (手陽明大腸). In traditional Chinese medicine, "Lesser Yin" refers to one of the six energetic layers, while "Dryness Metal" represents the elemental phase of the Large Intestine meridian, which is the focus of this particular exercise.</note>

Every day during the hours of the Ox and Tiger, extend your hands and turn your head, pulling and stretching to the left and right six or seven times each. Click your teeth thirty-six times, practice rhythmic breathing, and gargle and swallow your saliva nine times.
<note>The hours of the Ox and Tiger are 1:00 AM to 5:00 AM. The text specifies "six times six" (36) and "three times three" (9), which are sacred numbers in Daoist longevity practices. Gargling with saliva before swallowing is a practice known as "swallowing the jade fluid" to nourish the body's essence.</note>

---

### Therapeutic Benefits

**Treats exhaustion and <term>pathogenic toxins</term> within the <term>meridians</term> of the chest, shoulders, and back.**
<note>Pathogenic toxins (*xiedu* 邪毒) are external influences like wind or cold that disrupt the body's balance. Meridians (*jingluo* 經絡) are the channels through which vital energy, or Qi, flows.</note>

**Also treats toothaches, swelling of the neck, chills and shivering, hot swellings, deafness, and tinnitus (ringing in the ears).**

**Effective for pain behind the ears, in the shoulders, the upper arms, the elbows, the outer forearms, and the back; as well as a feeling of "fullness" of Qi, skin that is <term>rough and cracked</term>—becoming hard but remaining painless—or itching of the skin caused by "phlegm-qi."**
<term>Rough and cracked</term> (original: *cun* 皴) refers to a skin texture resembling the cracked bark of a tree or the texture strokes used in traditional landscape painting; in medicine, it indicates a deficiency of fluids or poor circulation in the skin.

<note>original: *nao* 臑. This term specifically refers to the muscles of the upper arm or the area around the humerus bone.</note>

# Pure Brightness: First Half of the Third Lunar Month
## The energetic movement is governed by the Second Qi of Lesser Yin. This period is associated with the Hand Great Yang Small Intestine and Cold Water.
<note>original: *Shaoyin* (少陰) and *Shou Taiyang Xiaochang* (手太陽小腸). In this system of traditional chronobiology, different times of year are linked to specific organ systems. "Cold Water" refers to the elemental phase (*Wu Xing*) associated with this meridian during this specific seasonal transition.</note>

Every day during the hours of the Ox and Tiger, sit upright and move your hands to the left and right as if drawing a stiff bow, seven or eight times for each side. Click your teeth, inhale the "clear" air and exhale the "turbid," and swallow your saliva three times each.
<note>The hours of the Ox and Tiger are between 1:00 AM and 5:00 AM, considered a potent time for breathing exercises. "Drawing a stiff bow" is a literal description of the <term>Daoyin</term> (stretching and guiding energy) posture shown in the illustration. "Inhaling clear and exhaling turbid" is a fundamental Daoist breathing technique to expel stale energy and take in fresh vitality.</note>

---

### Therapeutic Benefits

**Treats <term>deficient pathogens</term> and stagnations within the waist, kidneys, intestines, and stomach.**
<note>Deficient pathogens (*xu xie* 虛邪) are subtle environmental or internal imbalances that take advantage of a weakened body. Stagnations (*ji zhi* 積滯) refer to blockages in digestion or energy flow.</note>

**It also addresses heat in front of the ears, shivering from bitter cold, deafness, throat pain, and neck pain that makes it impossible to turn the head.**

**Heals sensations of the shoulders being pulled out, the upper arms feeling as if broken, and various pains in the waist, cheeks, elbows, and arms.**
<note>original: *nao zhe* (臑折), literally "upper arm break." This describes a specific type of radiating pain or weakness rather than an actual bone fracture. The exercise targets the Small Intestine meridian, which runs along the arm, shoulder, and neck to the ear.</note>

# Grain Rain: Mid-point of the Third Lunar Month
## The energetic movement is governed by the Second Qi of Lesser Yin. This period is associated with the Hand Great Yang Small Intestine and Cold Water.
<note>original: *Shaoyin* (少陰) and *Shou Taiyang Xiaochang* (手太陽小腸). As with the previous period, this exercise focuses on the Small Intestine meridian, which in traditional Chinese chronobiology is linked to the elemental phase of "Cold Water" during this specific transition toward summer.</note>

Every day during the hours of the Ox and Tiger, sit upright. Twist your hands and raise them upward to the left and right as if supporting a great weight. Move your arms to cover your chest on the left and right. Perform these movements five to seven times each. Click your teeth, perform the "expelling and admitting" breathing technique, and swallow your saliva.
<note>The hours of the Ox and Tiger are between 1:00 AM and 5:00 AM. The phrase "expelling and admitting" (<term>tuna</term> 吐納) refers to a controlled breathing practice where one exhales stale air and inhales fresh vitality. "Swallowing the saliva" is a common <term>Daoyin</term> practice believed to nourish the body's internal essence.</note>

---

### Therapeutic Benefits

**Treats masses and stagnations of the spleen and stomach, blood stasis, and yellowing of the eyes.**
<note>"Masses and stagnations" (<term>jie jia</term> 結瘕) refers to abdominal lumps or digestive blockages. Yellowing of the eyes is a traditional diagnostic sign often associated with what modern medicine calls jaundice.</note>

**It also addresses nasal congestion, nosebleeds, and swelling of the cheeks and jaw.**
<note>original: *bi qiu nü* (鼻鼽衄). These symptoms are traditionally viewed as "heat" rising through the meridians that govern the face and head.</note>

**Relieves swelling and pain on the outer and back edges of the elbows and arms, as well as heat in the palms.**
<note>These areas correspond to the physical path of the Hand Great Yang Small Intestine meridian, which runs from the hand, up the outer arm, to the shoulder and neck.</note>

# Start of Summer: Solar Term of the Fourth Lunar Month
## The energetic movement is governed by the Second Qi of Lesser Yin. This period is associated with the Hand Faint Yin Pericardium and the element of Wind-Wood.
<note>original: *Shaoyin* (少陰) and *Shou Jueyin Xinbaoluo* (手厥陰心包絡). The Pericardium is considered the "protector" of the heart in traditional medicine. The "Wind-Wood" (<term>fengmu</term> 風木) phase marks the transition of spring's wood energy into the rising heat of summer.</note>

# Every day during the hours of the Tiger and Hare, hold your breath and close your eyes. Alternately switch hands to press down and pull upward on both knees, performing this five to seven times for each side. Click your teeth, perform the "expelling and admitting" breathing technique, and swallow your saliva.
<note>The hours of the Tiger and Hare (<term>Yinmao</term> 寅卯) are between 3:00 AM and 7:00 AM. "Expelling and admitting" (<term>tuna</term> 吐納) refers to a specialized deep-breathing practice used to purge "stale" energy and inhale "fresh" vitality.</note>

**Treats stagnation and retention of Wind-Dampness,**
<note>"Wind-Dampness" (<term>fengshi</term> 風濕) is a traditional category of illness often characterized by joint pain, heaviness, or restricted movement, similar to modern rheumatic conditions.</note>

**swelling and pain in the meridians and collaterals, spasms and contracture of the elbows, swelling of the armpits, heat in the palms, uncontrollable laughter, and various other ailments.**
<note>In traditional Chinese diagnostics, "uncontrollable laughter" (<term>xixiao buxiu</term> 喜笑不休) is seen as a sign of excess heat or energy in the Heart and Pericardium systems.</note>

# Grain Buds: Mid-point of the Fourth Lunar Month
<note>original: *Xiaoman* (小滿). This solar term, beginning around May 21st, marks the time when grain seeds begin to plump but are not yet ripe.</note>

### The energetic movement is governed by the Second Qi of Lesser Yang. It is associated with the Hand Faint Yin Pericardium and the element of Wind-Wood.
<note>original: *Shaoyang* (少陽) and *Shou Jueyin Xinbaoluo* (手厥陰心包絡). While the previous period was linked to the "Lesser Yin" (Shaoyin) energy, we now transition to "Lesser Yang." The Pericardium, often called the "King's Bodyguard" in Chinese medicine, protects the heart from heat and emotional distress. "Wind-Wood" (<term>fengmu</term> 風木) energy here represents a lingering spring-like vitality that must be balanced as summer heat intensifies.</note>

Every day during the hours of the Tiger and Hare, sit in an upright position. Raise one hand as if supporting a weight upward, while the other hand presses down firmly. 
<note>The hours of the Tiger and Hare (<term>Yinmao</term> 寅卯) correspond to 3:00 AM to 7:00 AM.</note>

Perform this on the left and right sides three to five times each. Click your teeth, perform the breathing technique of "expelling the old and admitting the new," and swallow your saliva.
<note>"Expelling and admitting" (<term>tuna</term> 吐納) is a foundational Daoist breathing method for internal purification. "Clicking the teeth" (<term>kouchi</term> 叩齒) is believed to stimulate the brain and strengthen the kidneys.</note>

## For Treating Stagnant Toxins Accumulated in the Internal Organs
<note>original: *feifu yunzhi xiedu* (肺腑蘊滯邪毒). This refers to "evil" or pathogenic factors—like heat or dampness—that have become trapped within the chest and abdominal cavities.</note>

This practice addresses:
Fullness and distension in the chest and ribs; 
a feeling of apprehension and significant fluttering or palpitations in the heart; 
redness of the face and nose; 
yellowing of the eyes; 
irritability and anxiety; 
heart pain; 
heat in the palms of the hands; 
and various other related ailments.

# Grain in Beard: The Solar Term of the Fifth Lunar Month

Popular tradition holds that **Puppetry** <term>kuǐlěi: the art of performing with carved wooden figures</term> originated during the Siege of Pingcheng, when **Emperor Gaozu of Han** <note>the founder of the Han dynasty, reigned 202–195 BCE</note> was surrounded. According to this legend, the strategist **Chen Ping** <term>a brilliant advisor known for his "six wonderful plans"</term> devised a scheme to carve wooden figures into the likeness of beautiful women. He had these figures placed atop the city walls to deceive the **Consort** <note>original: 閼氏 *yānzhī*, the title for the wife of the Xiongnu Chanyu</note> of the nomadic leader **Maodun**. It is said that later generations created puppets based on this event.

However, according to the annotations in the *Records of the Han*, Annals of Emperor Gaozu (Year 7), the historian **Ying Shao** <note>a prominent scholar of the Eastern Han dynasty</note> states that Chen Ping actually commanded painters to create portraits of beautiful women to be sent to the Consort, and makes no mention of carved wooden figures.

If we look further back to the text of ***Liezi*** <note>a classic Daoist work</note>, there is a record from the time of **King Mu of Zhou** <note>reigned c. 976–922 BCE</note> concerning a master craftsman named **Yan Shi**. This artisan created a wooden man capable of singing and dancing. The King viewed a performance alongside his favorite, **Lady Sheng**. As the dance ended, the wooden figure reportedly winked its eye and used its hands to beckon the King’s attendants. 

The King became enraged, believing the "performer" was a real man flirting with his court, and intended to execute Yan Shi. In his terror, Yan Shi dismantled the figure to show that it was constructed entirely of cinnabar, ink, glue, and lacquer. This is suspected to be the true beginning of puppetry.

# Historical Origins of Football

In the *Separate Records* <note>original: 別錄 *Biélù*; compiled by the Han Dynasty scholar Liu Xiang (77–6 BCE), this was the first comprehensive catalog of the imperial library.</note> by Liu Xiang, it is said:

As for **football** <term>Cuju</term> <note>original: 蹴鞠 *Cùjū*; 'Cu' means to kick with the foot, and 'ju' refers to a ball made of leather and filled with hair.</note>, it is traditionally rumored that the **Yellow Emperor** <note>original: 黃帝 *Huángdì*; a legendary monarch and cultural hero considered the ancestor of all Chinese people.</note> created it. Other traditions suggest it originated during the **Warring States Period** <note>c. 475–221 BCE; a period of intense conflict that saw the birth of many military and philosophical traditions.</note>.

**Kickball** <note>original: 蹋鞠 *Tàjú*; a synonym for Cuju, emphasizing the "treading" or "kicking" motion.</note> is fundamentally a form of **military strategy**. It is the means by which one practices martial affairs and identifies those who possess great physical talent. These strategic and physical skills are all explained and demonstrated through the accessible form of a playful game.

The *Treatise on Curiosities* <note>original: 博物志 *Bówùzhì*; a 3rd-century encyclopedia of history, myth, and geography.</note> also notes: It was created by the Yellow Emperor.

# Illustration of the Swing
<note>original: 鞦韆圖 *Qiūqiān tú*</note>

**Visual Description and Significance:**

This woodcut illustration portrays the "Illustration of the Swing," a popular seasonal activity and a common theme in Chinese art depicting the lives of elite women. The scene is set within a private, walled garden—a sanctuary for leisure and aesthetic contemplation.

*   **The Swing:** At the center stands a towering wooden frame, reinforced with decorative banners or streamers at the top. A woman is depicted mid-swing, her flowing robes suggesting movement and grace. The swing <term>Qiūqiān <note>original: 鞦韆; historically, the term was also written as 千秋 (*qiānqiū*), meaning "a thousand autumns" or "longevity," used as a celebratory wish for the Emperor's birthday before it became associated with the play equipment.</note></term> was a central feature of the **Cold Food** and **Qingming** spring festivals.
*   **The Companions:** Four women accompany the swinger. Two appear to be assisting or waiting for their turn, while others observe, reflecting the communal and social nature of domestic garden life for women of the Ming and Qing dynasties.
*   **The Garden Landscape:** The setting is rich with symbolic flora and geology. To the right, a large **scholars' rock** <note>original: 供石 *gōngshí*; naturally weathered limestone prized for its abstract, perforated "bones."</note> provides a sense of rugged permanence. Behind the swing frame grows a **banana tree** <note>original: 芭蕉 *bājiāo*</note>, its broad leaves a favorite motif in garden art, symbolizing the ephemeral nature of life and the sound of rain.
*   **Cultural Context:** While today we see swinging as a simple playground activity, in this period it was often called "Half-Sovereign's Play" or "The Game of Immortals," as the act of soaring into the air was likened to the flight of Taoist deities.

<column-break/>

**Social Significance of the Swing:**

Historically, the swing served as a rare opportunity for women, who were often confined to the "inner quarters," to engage in vigorous physical exercise and enjoy the outdoors. During the Tang and Song dynasties, swinging became so popular during the spring that it was referred to as the "Swing Festival." In the context of this encyclopedia, it is categorized under "Human Affairs" to document the customs, tools, and social behaviors of the era.
