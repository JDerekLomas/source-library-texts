# TO THE MOST ILLUSTRIOUS
## AND MOST REVEREND LORD, LORD
## VINCENZO DIEDO
### PATRIARCA OF VENICE.

<image-desc>Large ornamental woodcut initial 'S' containing a landscape scene with foliage and a building in the background.</image-desc>

**S**enior wise men of ancient times were of the common opinion that all things, through the desire they have to return to their origin, are naturally inclined toward their own proper function and toward achieving their perfection. Wherefore, since Science <note>The author uses "Science" in the classical sense of *Scientia*, meaning organized knowledge or certain understanding.</note> is the perfection of the Intellect, and Understanding and Knowing are the proper functions of Man—through which he comes to be joined to his Creator—it follows that everyone is naturally drawn to the knowledge of things. Nor does one ever tire or weary of investigating their causes and wishing to understand the high secrets of Nature. Nor do I think that the hope of acquiring knowledge of many things alone drives him to this, but even the knowledge of a single thing; because in knowing it, he understands that he is journeying toward perfection. He judges that in surpassing all others in this, it is a thing worthy of much praise and honor. Therefore, I believe that because men love to hold the first place in any faculty, adding one thing one day and another the next, the Sciences and the Arts have grown in such a way that it is almost impossible to see from which side anything new could be added to them. 

And although it could be said that each of these has enjoyed this good fortune—perhaps because of the profit that men derive from them—nevertheless, until now it seems to me, if I am not mistaken, that Music has been little favored by fortune. For although many authors are found who have written many things on the Science and the Art <note>The "Science" refers to the mathematical theory of music, while the "Art" refers to the practical application and composition.</note>, nonetheless, a man reading them cannot acquire that knowledge which he desires; because truly they have not sufficiently touched upon, nor shown, any of those things which are of great importance. 

Wherefore I, who from my tender years have always had a natural inclination toward Music, having already consumed a good part of my life in the pursuit of its knowledge, and having noticed this state of affairs, wished to try if I could in some way bring those things pertaining to Theory and Practice back toward their perfection, to do something pleasing for all those who delight in such a faculty. And although I knew that this was too heavy a burden for me, I nevertheless thought that even if I were not to bring them to their ultimate degree of perfection, at least I might perhaps have been able to set the matter on such a path that I would be the cause of giving courage to some noble spirit to pass even further. For which reason, having proposed such a goal for myself, and having in these past years written the present INSTITUTIONS, which—

...they teach those things pertaining to one and the other of the aforementioned parts; spurred by my friends, who judged that they could be useful to students, it seemed right to me to bring them to light, dedicating them to Your Illustrious and Most Reverend Lordship. And in doing this, I was moved primarily to show, in some part, how much I remain obligated to the kindnesses shown to me by you. Furthermore, I did so because if by chance there were anyone of such a malignant spirit that—having no respect for the fact that I do this with the intention of helping others—he should be moved to blame these labors of mine, he would at least be constrained to have regard for the Illustrious name of that Lord to whom they have been dedicated. Added to this is the fact that your singular prudence, justice, religion, and kindness—qualities in you known and praised by all—have given birth in me to an incredible reverence and devotion, and I had no other way nor means to demonstrate it.

Nor can one truly have doubt concerning the singular virtues of Your Illustrious and Most Reverend Lordship, since clear testimony has been given by this most wise Senate; which, through many experiences, having known how prudent you were in the governments of the Republic, both in the city and abroad in the administrations of Verona and Udine; and finally, while you were holding office in Padua, the death of the Most Reverend Contarino <note>Gasparo Contarini (1483–1542), a Venetian noble and cardinal</note> having occurred, the Senate, judging you worthy of such honor, elected you Patriarch of Venice. And although attained honors most often tend to change the minds and habits of men, nevertheless, even though you have reached such an honored rank, the goodness of your soul has not changed or diminished in the slightest; rather, it has increased by a great deal. This can be clearly seen: for as soon as you had attained such dignity, you turned yourself first to adorning the Church, and then, with very great expense, to repairing the Palace, which was already beginning to fall into ruin. 

But just as you continually do not cease to renovate and adorn the material church, so from day to day (which is a most evident sign of religion and charity) you do not stop sustaining and relieving the spiritual one, continually offering aid to the poor—not only to those of your own city, but also to foreigners, and to those who, departing from infidelity, come to Christianity. And like a vigilant shepherd, and diligent farmer and guardian of the Vineyard of the Lord <note>A common biblical metaphor for the Church or the faithful</note>, you attend to providing that your flock be not harmed by wolves, and that from this Vineyard the bad branches be removed, or otherwise managed in such a way that they become fruitful. All these things truly bear most clear witness to the world of your rare virtues, which have moved me to dedicate these labors of mine to you, such as they may be. And if the gift is small, look at least upon the respect of my soul toward you, which is infinitely great.

->Of Your Illustrious and Most Reverend Lordship<-
->The most affectionate servant,<-
->Gioseffo Zarlino.<-
