## Tail Mansion Banner | Heart Mansion Banner
<note>These two banners represent the "Heart" and "Tail" of the Azure Dragon of the East. In traditional Chinese cosmology, the twenty-eight lunar mansions are groups of stars through which the moon passes. In an imperial procession, these flags symbolized that the Emperor was the earthly pivot around which the heavenly bodies revolved.</note>

->[Illustration: Two ceremonial banners are displayed. The banner on the right is labeled "Banner of the Heart Mansion" and shows three stars arranged in a slightly curved line. The banner on the left is labeled "Banner of the Tail Mansion" and shows nine stars arranged in a hook-like shape, resembling the tail of a dragon or scorpion. Both flags feature a distinctive flame-like serrated border, and the poles are decorated with a traditional "dragon head" fitting near the top and long flowing tassels.]<-

<term>Heart Mansion (Xin Xiu)</term> <note>original: 心宿. The fifth of the twenty-eight lunar mansions. Located at the "heart" of the Azure Dragon, its central star is Antares (Alpha Scorpii). In ancient Chinese astrology, this constellation was often associated with the Emperor himself; if the planet Mars "lingered" here, it was seen as a grave omen for the ruler.</note>

<term>Tail Mansion (Wei Xiu)</term> <note>original: 尾宿. The sixth of the twenty-eight lunar mansions, representing the tail of the Azure Dragon. It consists of nine stars forming the curve of what Westerners recognize as the tail of Scorpio. It was traditionally associated with the imperial harem and the success of the Emperor's descendants.</note>

## Banner of the Dipper Mansion | Banner of the Winnowing Basket Mansion
<note>These banners represent two of the Twenty-Eight Mansions (二十八宿), which are constellations used in traditional Chinese astronomy to mark the moon's progress through the sky. The Winnowing Basket completes the eastern quadrant, while the Dipper begins the northern quadrant.</note>

->[Illustration: Two ceremonial flags are depicted on tall poles. The flag on the left is the **Banner of the Dipper Mansion**, displaying six stars arranged in a shape similar to a ladle. The flag on the right is the **Banner of the Winnowing Basket Mansion**, displaying four stars in a trapezoidal arrangement. Both flags feature jagged, flame-like edges and are mounted on poles topped with spearheads and decorative tassels.]<-

<term>Dipper Mansion (Dou Xiu)</term> <note>original: 斗宿. The first mansion of the Black Tortoise of the North. It consists of six stars in the modern constellation of Sagittarius. It is often called the "Southern Dipper" to distinguish it from the "Big Dipper" (Northern Dipper).</note>

<term>Winnowing Basket Mansion (Ji Xiu)</term> <note>original: 箕宿. The seventh and final mansion of the Azure Dragon of the East. It consists of four stars in Sagittarius. In mythology, it is associated with the wind, as a winnowing basket is used to blow away chaff from grain.</note>

<term>Twenty-Eight Mansions</term> <note>original: 二十八宿 (Ershiba Xiu). A system of constellations that divide the ecliptic. They are grouped into four "symbols" or quadrants: the Azure Dragon (East), Black Tortoise (North), White Tiger (West), and Vermilion Bird (South).</note>

<term>Imperial Regalia</term> <note>original: 儀仗 (Yizhang). The ceremonial weapons, banners, and umbrellas carried by guards during imperial processions to signify power and cosmic order.</note>

<note>original: 危宿旗 (Wei Xiu Qi) and 虚宿旗 (Xu Xiu Qi). These are the fourth and fifth of the seven mansions of the North. In the "Lu Bu" (Imperial Procession), these banners represented the Emperor's command over time and the celestial alignment of his reign.</note>

->[Illustration: Two ceremonial banners are displayed on ornate poles. The banner on the right is the **Banner of the Emptiness Mansion**, featuring a constellation of two stars. <term>Emptiness Mansion (Xu Xiu)</term> <note>original: 虚宿. The fourth mansion of the Black Tortoise of the North. In ancient Chinese astrology, this constellation was associated with the height of winter and the "emptiness" or stillness required for the rebirth of the solar year.</note>

<term>Rooftop Mansion (Wei Xiu)</term> <note>original: 危宿. The fifth mansion of the Black Tortoise of the North. Its name, "Rooftop" or "Danger," refers to its high position in the sky, likened to the ridge-pole of a house. It was often associated with construction and the completion of official duties.</note>

# The Flower Canopy

According to the *Notes on Things Ancient and Modern* by <note>Cui Bao was a scholar of the Western Jin dynasty (265–316 AD) who compiled records of ancient rituals and objects.</note> Cui Bao, the <term>Flower Canopy<note>original: 華蓋 (Huagai). # Pheasant Fan

According to the <term>Notes on Things Ancient and Modern<note>original: 古今注 (Gu Jin Zhu). A Jin dynasty work by Cui Bao that explains the origins of various rituals, clothing, and everyday objects.</note></term> by Cui Bao, pheasant-tail fans originated during the reign of Emperor <term>Gaozong<note>Also known as Wu Ding (r. 1250–1192 BCE). Legend states that a pheasant crowed atop a sacrificial vessel, which the King interpreted as a divine sign to reform the government.</note></term> of the Shang dynasty. Following the auspicious omen of the crowing pheasant, pheasant feathers were frequently incorporated into ceremonial garments and insignia. 

Under the <term>Zhou dynasty system<note>The ritual standards established roughly between 1046–256 BCE, which served as the blueprint for later Chinese imperial protocol.</note></term>, these feathers were used for the attire of the Queen and the wives of high-ranking lords. The imperial hand-carriages were decorated with <term>ceremonial screens<note>original: 翣 (Sha). These were large, fan-like screens held by attendants or fixed to carriages to signify rank and protect the nobility.</note></term> made of gathered feathers; these screens were specifically intended to shield the occupants from wind and dust.

The <term>Six Statutes of the Tang Dynasty<note>original: 唐六典 (Tang Liudian). A massive compilation of administrative law and government structure completed in 738 CE.</note></term> states that pheasant-tail fans were used in older times, but at the beginning of the <term>Kaiyuan era<note>713–741 CE. A period of great cultural and economic prosperity under Emperor Xuanzong.</note></term>, the design was changed to embroidered peacocks to favor a more refined and economical style.

In the Song dynasty, there were three grades of these instruments: the Great Pheasant Fan, the Middle Pheasant Fan, and the Small Pheasant Fan. Their design was square at the bottom and rounded at the top, featuring a straight handle and a center made of <term>scarlet silk gauze<note>original: 緋羅 (Feiluo).</note></term>. The outer perimeter was decorated with an arrangement of pheasant feathers. In the center, a pair of peacocks was embroidered, interspersed with various flowers. Below this, a horizontal piece of black-lacquered wood joined the frame to the handle, secured with gilded bronze floral studs.

The Yuan dynasty followed this design. The current system <note>Referring to the Ming dynasty, when this encyclopedia was compiled.</note> is identical to that of the Song.

## The Fly-whisk

The <term>Fly-whisk</term> <note>original: 拂子 (fuzi)</note> belongs to the same category as the <term>Deer-tail Whisk</term> <note>original: 塵尾 (chenwei). The whisk is secured into a mount shaped like a golden dragon’s head. The handle itself is made of wood and painted with golden dragon patterns. From the end of the handle hangs a decorative knot and tassel made of purple silk.

# Spittoons and Spit-basins

According to the records, during the era of <term>Emperor Wu of Han</term> <note>r. 141–87 BCE</note>, <term>Kong Anguo</term> <note>A descendant of Confucius and a prominent scholar</note> served as a Palace Attendant. Because of his status as a Confucian scholar, he was specially granted the privilege of holding the imperial spittoon. The officials of the court regarded this as a great honor. 

The "Memorial on Miscellaneous Items" by <term>Emperor Wu of Wei</term> <note>The famous general and statesman Cao Cao (155–220 CE)</note> states that among the various items for imperial use, there was one spittoon made of pure gold and four round spittoons finished with lacquer oil. 

The "Miscellaneous Records of Jiaozhou" notes that in the fourth year of the <term>Taikang era</term> <note>283 CE</note>, King Fan Xiong of <term>Linyi</term> <note>An ancient kingdom in central Vietnam</note> presented one amethyst spittoon and two each of blue and white crystal spittoons.

In the imperial processions of the <term>Song Dynasty</term> <note>960–1279 CE</note>, there was an official dressed in purple whose role was to carry the golden spittoon, though the specific design of that vessel was not recorded. In the <term>Yuan Dynasty</term> <note>1271–1368 CE</note>, both spittoons and spit-basins were made of silver with lids and were gilded with gold.

Under current regulations <note>Referring to the Ming Dynasty (1368–1644 CE), when this text was compiled</note>, these vessels are all made of yellow gold. The <term>spittoon</term> <note>original: *tuihu* (唾壺)</note> has a small mouth and a very large belly, with a lid as wide as the belly itself. The <term>spit-basin</term> <note>original: *tuiyu* (唾盂)</note> is circular in shape, resembling a <term>fou</term> <note>An ancient earthenware wine or water jar</note>; its lid only just covers the mouth of the vessel. There is a tray positioned beneath it, and both vessels are decorated with dragon patterns.

The **Water Basin** <note>original: *shuipen* (水盆)</note> is what was known in ancient times as the *xi* <note>A ritual vessel used for washing hands or feet</note>. According to the *Old Affairs of the Eastern Palace of the Jin Dynasty* by <term>Zhang Chang</term> <note>A historian and official of the Jin Dynasty (266–420 CE)</note>: "When the Crown Prince was first appointed, he was provided with one gilded silver washing pitcher, along with a blue silk cord made of three strands, measuring two feet five inches in length."

# Incense Box

According to the *Yunhui* <note>original: *Yunhui* (韻會); a comprehensive dictionary compiled during the Yuan Dynasty</note>, a box (*hezi*) is the general name for a vessel used to contain objects. During the Han Dynasty, the scholar <term>Liu Xiang</term> <note>77–6 BCE; a famous librarian and author</note> wrote the "Ode to the Box."

The *Diary of Activity and Repose of the Yuanjia Era* <note>original: *Yuanjia Qiju Zhu* (元嘉起居注); records of the Liu Song Dynasty court, 424–453 CE</note> states: "For the morning audience, there were two gold-engraved boxes and two silver-threaded boxes." In the palace processions of the <term>Song Dynasty</term>, there were two attendants whose duty was to carry boxes containing **dragon-brain incense** <note>original: *longnao* (龍腦); high-quality borneol incense prized for its cooling scent</note>.

Under the regulations of the <term>Yuan Dynasty</term>, these boxes were made of silver with a diameter of seven inches, gilded with yellow gold, and featured silver clouds and dragons on the surface. Under **current regulations** <note>Referring to the Ming Dynasty system</note>, they are made of pure gold in a circular shape. The lid is engraved with dragon patterns, and the circumference of the base is decorated with engraved floral patterns in the shape of lotus petals.

# Incense Burner

According to the *Miscellaneous Records of the Western Capital* <note>original: *Xijing Zhaji* (西京雜記); a collection of anecdotes about the Han capital, Chang'an</note>: "A skilled craftsman in Chang’an named <term>Ding Huan</term> created the **bedding incense burner** <note>original: *woru xianglu* (卧褥香爐); a gimbaled incense burner designed to be placed inside bedding without spilling</note>. In the palace processions of the <term>Song Dynasty</term>, there were two <term>Daoist priestesses</term> <note>original: *nvguan* (女冠); literally 'female crowns,' a term for female Daoist ritualists</note> who held incense burners and incense trays. Under the <term>Yuan Dynasty</term> regulations, the burner featured a silver base with a lotus flower design set upon it.
