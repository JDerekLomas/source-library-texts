# The Forty-seventh Difficulty: Why the Face Withstands Cold

The **Forty-seventh Difficulty** <note>original: "四十七難" (sì shí qī nàn). This refers to the 47th question in the *Nan Jing* (Classic of Difficulties), a foundational medical text from the Han Dynasty that uses a Q&A format to explain complex physiological concepts.</note> states:

Why is it that the human face alone is able to **withstand the cold**? <note>original: "耐寒" (nài hán). This refers to the observation that humans can leave their faces exposed in winter while the rest of the body requires covering.</note>

The answer is: The human head is the **meeting point of all the Yang** <note>original: "諸陽之會" (zhū yáng zhī huì). In traditional medicine, "Yang" represents the active, warm, and protective energies of the body. The head is seen as the highest point where all heat-related energy channels converge.</note>. 

While all the **Yin meridians** <note>original: "諸陰脉" (zhū yīn mài). These are the energy channels associated with the internal organs and cooler, nourishing energies.</note> reach only as far as the **neck and the chest** and then turn back, only the Yang meridians all ascend to the head. This is why the face is able to withstand the cold.

# Volume Five

## The Forty-Eighth Difficulty

**The Forty-Eighth Difficulty states:** It is said that people have "three vacuities" and "three repletions." What does this mean?

**The answer is:** There is the vacuity and repletion of the **pulse**, the vacuity and repletion of the **illness**, and the vacuity and repletion of the **diagnosis** <note>original: "診" (zhěn). In this context, "diagnosis" refers specifically to the physical signs observed or felt on the patient's body during an examination, such as skin texture or localized sensations.</note>.

Regarding the vacuity and repletion of the **pulse**: a "soggy" <term>soggy: *ru* (濡), a pulse that feels soft and thin, often disappearing under pressure; it indicates a lack of vital energy (vacuity)</term> pulse constitutes vacuity; "tight" <term>tight: *jin* (緊), a pulse feeling like a taut cord, indicating cold or obstruction</term> and "firm" <term>firm: *lao* (牢), a pulse that is deep, large, and very strong</term> pulses constitute repletion.

Regarding the vacuity and repletion of the **illness**: that which is "exiting" <note>Refers to symptoms moving from the interior to the exterior, or the loss of fluids/energy.</note> constitutes vacuity; that which is "entering" <note>Refers to pathogens moving deeper into the body.</note> constitutes repletion. One who is talkative constitutes vacuity; one who is silent constitutes repletion. That which is slow <note>Referring to a relaxed or slow onset of symptoms.</note> constitutes vacuity; that which is acute <note>Referring to sudden or violent symptoms.</note> constitutes repletion.

Regarding the vacuity and repletion of the **diagnosis**: a "soggy" <note>Here referring to soft, flaccid tissue or skin that lacks tone.</note> quality constitutes vacuity; a "firm" <note>Referring to hard masses or rigid areas felt during palpation.</note> quality constitutes repletion. Itchiness constitutes vacuity; pain constitutes repletion.

If there is pain on the exterior but a pleasant feeling <note>original: "快" (kuài). Refers to a sense of ease or relief from pressure.</note> in the interior, it is **external repletion and internal vacuity**. If there is pain in the interior but a pleasant feeling on the exterior, it is **internal repletion and external vacuity**. Therefore, these are known as the states of vacuity and repletion.

# The 49th Difficulty: Distinguishing Internal and External Disease

### Distinguishing the Proper Meridian from the Five Pathogens

The 49th Difficulty asks: There are diseases originating in the **proper meridian** <term>proper meridian: *zhengjing* (正經), referring to the primary channel or organ system itself</term> and there are injuries caused by the **five pathogens** <term>five pathogens: *wuxie* (五邪), also translated as the five "evils," representing external influences or imbalances coming from other organs</term>. How can one distinguish between them?

The answer is:
*   Grief, sorrow, and brooding thoughts injure the **Heart**.
*   Physical cold and drinking cold liquids injure the **Lung**.
*   Rage and anger cause the **Qi** <note>The vital energy or breath of the body</note> to counter-flow upwards without descending; this injures the **Liver**.
*   Improprieties in food and drink or overexertion injure the **Spleen**.
*   Sitting on damp ground or straining oneself to enter water injures the **Kidney**.

These are what we call "diseases originating in the proper meridian itself."

### Defining the Five Pathogens

What are the Five Pathogens? They are:
1.  **Strike by Wind** <note>original: *zhongfeng* (中風)</note>
2.  **Injury by Summer Heat** <note>original: *shangshu* (傷暑)</note>
3.  **Food, Drink, and Overexertion** <note>original: *yinshi laojuan* (飲食勞倦)</note>
4.  **Injury by Cold** <note>original: *shanghan* (傷寒)</note>
5.  **Strike by Dampness** <note>original: *zhongshi* (中濕)</note>

These are called the Five Pathogens.

### Case Study: When the Heart is Diseased

Suppose the Heart is diseased. How can one know if it was contracted from a **Strike by Wind**?
The answer is: its color should be **red**.

Why is this said? The Liver governs **color**. 
*   When the Liver's influence enters itself, the color is green-blue.
*   When it enters the **Heart**, the color is red. 
*   When it enters the **Spleen**, the color is yellow.
*   When it enters the **Lung**, the color is white.
*   When it enters the **Kidney**, the color is black.

Because the Liver [associated with Wind] acts as the pathogen to the Heart, we know the color should be red. This disease presents with body heat, fullness and pain in the **hypochondrium** <note>The area of the upper abdomen just below the ribs</note>, and the pulse is floating, large, and **string-like** <term>string-like: *xian* (弦), a taut pulse associated with Liver disharmony</term>.

How can one know if the disease was contracted from **Injury by Summer Heat**?
The answer is: there should be an aversion to odors <note>This can also be interpreted as the presence of a specific diagnostic smell</note>. 

Why is this said? The Heart governs **odors**. 
*   When the Heart's influence enters itself, the smell is **scorched**.
*   When it enters the Spleen, the smell is **fragrant**.
*   When it enters the Liver, the smell is **rancid** <note>original: *sao* (臊), a smell like raw meat or animal musk</note>. When it enters...

### Diagnostic Signs of the Five Pathogens

The Kidney corresponds to the smell of rot, and the Lung corresponds to a raw or fishy smell. Therefore, we know that when a Heart disease is caused by **Injury by Summer Heat** <note>original: *shangshu* (傷暑)</note>, the patient will develop an aversion to foul odors. The symptoms of this condition are fever, irritability, and heart pain. The **pulse** <term>pulse: *mai* (脉), the tactile quality of the radial artery used in diagnosis</term> is floating, large, and scattered.

How do we know if a disease is caused by **Food, Drink, and Overexertion** <note>original: *yinshi laojuan* (飲食勞倦)</note>? In such cases, the patient will have a craving for bitter flavors. If the condition is one of **deficiency** <note>An empty or weakened state of vital energy</note>, the patient will have no appetite; if it is a state of **excess** <note>An overabundance or blockage of energy</note>, they will have a desire to eat. 

Why is this the case? The Spleen governs the sense of taste. When this influence enters the Liver, it manifests as a preference for sour; when it enters the Heart, it becomes bitter; when it enters the Lung, it becomes pungent; when it enters the Kidney, it becomes salty; and when it remains in its own organ, the Spleen, it is sweet. Thus, we know that when a pathogen from the Spleen enters the Heart, it causes a craving for bitter tastes. The symptoms of this disease are fever, a heavy sensation in the body, a constant desire to lie down, and limbs that are weak and cannot be moved. ### The Fiftieth Difficulty

**The Fiftieth Difficulty states:**
Illnesses may be characterized by a **Deficiency Pathogen**, an **Excess Pathogen**, a **Thieving Pathogen**, a **Minor Pathogen**, or a **Normal Pathogen**. By what means can one distinguish between them?

The answer is:
*   That which comes from "behind" is the **Deficiency Pathogen**. <note>original: *xu xie* (虚邪). In the Five Elements cycle, "behind" refers to the "mother" element that produces the current organ. For example, Wood is behind Fire.</note>
*   That which comes from "ahead" is the **Excess Pathogen**. <note>original: *shi xie* (實邪). "Ahead" refers to the "child" element produced by the organ. For example, Earth is ahead of Fire.</note>
*   That which comes from that which cannot be overcome is the **Thieving Pathogen**. <note>original: *zei xie* (賊邪). This refers to the element that naturally restrains or conquers the organ—such as Water conquering Fire.</note>
*   That which comes from that which is overcome is the **Minor Pathogen**. <note>original: *wei xie* (微邪). This refers to the element the organ normally restrains—such as Fire conquering Metal.</note>
*   An illness originating within the organ itself is the **Normal Pathogen**. <note>original: *zheng xie* (正邪).</note>

How can we explain this? Suppose, for example, the **Heart** is the diseased organ:

*   If the illness is caught from **Wind**, it is a **Deficiency Pathogen**. <note>original: *zhong feng* (中風). Wind is associated with the Liver (Wood). Since Wood produces Fire, the Liver is the "mother" of the Heart.</note>
*   If it is caught from **Summer Heat**, it is a **Normal Pathogen**. <note>original: *shang shu* (傷暑). Heat is the natural element of the Heart (Fire) itself.</note>
*   If it is caught from **Dietary Irregularities or Fatigue**, it is an **Excess Pathogen**. <note>original: *yin shi lao juan* (飲食勞倦). These issues are associated with the Spleen (Earth). Since Fire produces Earth, the Spleen is the "child" of the Heart.</note>
*   If it is caught from **Cold**, it is a **Minor Pathogen**. <note>original: *shang han* (傷寒). In this specific system, Cold is linked here to the Lung (Metal). Since the Heart (Fire) overcomes Metal, this is a minor push-back from a subordinate element.</note>
*   If it is caught from **Dampness**, it is a **Thieving Pathogen**. <note>original: *zhong shi* (中濕). Dampness is linked here to the Kidney (Water). Since Water overcomes Fire, this is an attack from a superior element, hence it "robs" or "thieves" the organ's strength.</note>

### The Logic of Heat, Cold, Wind, and Qi

**Heat** generates **Wind**, while **Cold** generates **Qi** <term>Qi (氣): in this specific pathological context, it refers to the accumulation, stagnation, or "thickening" of energy, often manifesting as respiratory congestion or fullness</term>; apply your mind with care and remember these instructions well. <gloss>This statement reiterates the nature of the **Excess Pathogen** <note>original: *shi xie* (實邪); a pathogen characterized by overabundance rather than weakness</note>.</gloss>

[The text explains:]
**Wood** <note>associated with the Liver and the East</note> belongs to the element of Wood. When Wood enters the position of **Fire** <note>the "child" of Wood in the generative cycle</note>, then within Wood there is Fire. **Metal** <note>which normally restrains Wood</note> then feels a sense of fear toward this Fire. Consequently, Metal is unable to restrain Wood, allowing Wood to become even more exuberant. Therefore, it is said that "heat generates wind."

**Cold** belongs to the element of **Water**, and **Qi** belongs to the element of **Metal**. When Metal and Water occupy the position of Metal, then within Metal there is Water. **Fire** <note>which normally restrains Metal</note> then feels a sense of fear toward this Water. In autumn and winter, the **Yang Qi** is in the lower regions and the **Human Qi** is also in the lower regions; therefore, one should "take" the energy deeply.

The Classic also says: "In spring and summer, one must always 'attain a Yin'; in autumn and winter, one must always 'attain a Yang'." What does this mean?

The answer is: In the warmth of spring and summer, one must "attain a Yin." This means that when first inserting the **needle** <note>original: 鍼, referring to the traditional acupuncture needle</note>, one sinks it down to the depth of the **Kidney and Liver** <note>these organs are associated with the deepest, most "Yin" layers of the body</note> regions. Once the practitioner "obtains the Qi" <gloss>and</gloss> holds it there, this is what is meant by "attaining Yin."

In the cold of autumn and winter, one must "attain a Yang." This means that when first inserting the needle, one keeps it "floating" <note>at a superficial depth</note> at the level of the **Heart and Lung** <note>these organs are associated with the upper, more "Yang" layers of the body</note> regions. Once the practitioner "obtains the Qi," they push it inward; this is what is meant by "attaining Yang."

This is the meaning of the phrase: "In spring and summer one must attain a Yin; in autumn and winter one must attain a Yang."

<note>This diagram serves as a diagnostic "cheat sheet." It illustrates three primary contradictions that a physician must look for:
1. **Tall vs. Short:** A tall person is expected to have a "long" pulse. If they have a "short" pulse (one that does not reach its proper positions), it is a sign of declining Qi.
2. **Robust vs. Sick:** A person who looks physically strong but has a "sickly" pulse, or a person who looks emaciated but has a startlingly "strong" pulse, indicates that the body's appearance is a false facade.
3. **Thin vs. Fat:** This refers to the relationship between body mass and pulse depth. Thin people should have more accessible pulses; fat people, deeper ones. When these patterns flip, it suggests the patient's "Zheng Qi" (upright energy) is exhausted.</note>

# The Fifty-Eighth Difficulty

**The Fifty-Eighth Difficulty asks:** How many types of <term>Cold Damage</term><note>original: 傷寒 (shanghan). While it literally means "injured by cold," in this context it serves as a general umbrella term for all acute febrile or infectious diseases.</note> are there? And do their pulse patterns change accordingly?

**The answer is:** There are five types of Cold Damage. These are **Wind Strike**, **Cold Damage** (proper), **Damp-Warmth**, **Heat Disease**, and **Warm Disease**. The sufferings associated with each are different.

*   The pulse of **Wind Strike** <note>original: 中風 (zhongfeng)</note> is <term>floating</term> and <term>slippery</term> at the Yang position, while it is <term>soft</term> and <term>weak</term> at the Yin position.
*   The pulse of **Damp-Warmth** <note>original: 濕温 (shiwen)</note> is soft and weak at the Yang position, while it is <term>small</term> and <term>urgent</term> at the Yin position.
*   The pulse of **Cold Damage** (specifically) is exuberant in both Yin and Yang positions, feeling both <term>tight</term> and <term>choppy</term>.
*   The pulse of **Heat Disease** <note>original: 熱病 (rebing)</note> is floating in both Yin and Yang positions. When felt superficially, it is slippery; when pressed deeply, it feels <term>scattered</term> and choppy.
*   The pulse of **Warm Disease** <note>original: 温病 (wenbing)</note> travels through all the <term>meridians</term><note>original: 經 (jing). These are the pathways through which Qi and Blood circulate.</note>; one cannot know which specific meridian is being moved. One must seek the pulse according to whichever meridian the disease currently resides in.

In cases of Cold Damage, there are those who recover after **sweating** but die if **purged**, and those who die after sweating but recover if purged. Why is this?

**The answer is:**
Where **Yang is deficient and Yin is exuberant**, the patient will recover after sweating; however, if they are purged <note>original: 下 (xia). This refers to the use of purgatives or laxatives to clear the bowels and "drain" the illness downward.</note>, they will die immediately. 
Where **Yang is exuberant and Yin is deficient**, the patient will die if they sweat, but they will recover if they are purged.

How do we observe the symptoms of Cold and Heat diseases? 

**The answer is:** 
In cases of **Skin Cold-Heat** <note>fever and chills localized at the surface</note>, the skin is so sensitive it cannot even come near the bedding mat. The body hair appears scorched, the nostrils are dry, and the patient cannot produce sweat. 
In cases of **Muscle Cold-Heat**, the skin and flesh are painful...
