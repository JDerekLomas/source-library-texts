# ILLUSTRIOUS QUESTIONS,
## derived from Books 1 and 2 of the Pandects,
### FIRST.

**B**oth public and private law <note>original: "JUs ÆQUÈ PUBLICUM, ATQUE PRIVATUM"</note> are derived, as stated in Law 1, from natural precepts, the custom of nations, and civil reason. See the argument in Title 1, Law 2, there: *that we should obey our Country.* <note>original: "ut Patriæ pareamus"</note> On Justice and Law, the final law in the Digest concerning Embassies. <note>The "Pandects" or "Digest" is a massive 6th-century compilation of writings by Roman jurists, organized into 50 books.</note> And just as in private law, the Civil Law—if it recedes entirely from natural reason, since it does not determine but rather invents something unknown to Nature—is not to be called Law, but Tyranny. In the same way, Public Law (and even the "reason of state" <term>ratio Status: the principle that the interests of the state may justify actions otherwise considered illegal or immoral</term> itself) must have its basis in Nature, right reason, or the honorable custom of Nations. *Johannes Gæddeus on Law 6, at the beginning, note 11, regarding the meaning of words. The Presiding Professor, in his disputation on Law 1, on Justice and Law, thesis 20,* where he cites many in support of this opinion. Those who disagree (chiefly through Law 1, section 2 at the end, on Justice and Law, and the final section of the Institutes on the same) include *Wesenbeck in the Paratitla on Justice and Law, under note 14, and on Agreements note 4; our Teacher Dr. Heinrich Bocer, class 1, disputation 2, thesis 5.* Likewise, the most noble and ingenious *Marcus Lycklama, Member, book 7, Eclogue 41, section 1.*

2. Regarding those conditionally freed <term>statuliberi: slaves who were granted their freedom in a will, provided they fulfilled a specific condition, such as paying a sum to the heir</term>, we believe they do not break the highest division of persons (proposed in Title 5 by the *Jurist Gaius in Law 3 on the status of men*), since they are still counted among slaves. *Law 9 at the beginning, Law 16, Law 25, Law 29 at the beginning on the conditionally freed; Law 1, section 4 on the Silanian Decree; Law 9 on interrogations.* Here, however, we judge that the said *Law 29 on the conditionally freed* does not conflict with *Law 14 on interrogations and Law 9 on punishments.* For we distinguish between crimes committed against the heir and those committed against a stranger. This was also the view of *Pierre Perrenon, book 1 of Animadversions, chapter 3,* long ago. Various reconciliations of these laws are reported by *Leopold Hackelman, Illustration 1, thesis 14; Dominicus Arumæus, Exercise 1, thesis 23; Hilliger on Donellus, book 2, chapter 11, at the beginning.*

3. Paternal power <term>Patria potestas: the absolute authority held by a Roman male head of household over his children and descendants</term> is a right peculiar to Roman citizens. *Law 3 of Title 6, on those who are independent or under another's legal power; the penultimate section of the Institutes on paternal power.* See *François Connan, 2 Commentaries 15, number 2; Bodin, 1 on the Republic 4, and Method of History, chapter 6; Pierre Grégoire of Toulouse, 11 Syntagma 5, number 3; Vaconius à Vacuna, 1 Declaration 8; Alberico Gentili, 4 on Marriages, chapter 1; Friedrich Tileman, disputation 3, thesis 30 and following; Andreas Gerhard, Disputations on the Institutes 2, thesis 1; Timæus Faber, disputation on the Institutes 5, thesis 2.* However, this paternal power granted to Roman citizens differs in many ways from that "Paternal Command" <note>original: "Imperio illo Paterno"</note> received among all peoples by the Law of Nature. This natural command even a Mother has; this a Father has over an emancipated son; this a son has, who himself, together with his own children, is established under the power of his own father. See *Hilliger on Donellus, 2 Commentaries 20, Letter C. Coras on the said Law 3, on those who are independent or under another's power.* That which is handed down by *Johannes Calvinus <note>Johannes Kahl (1550–1614), known as Calvinus, was a legal scholar famous for his law dictionary.</note> in the Lexicon of Law, under the phrase "Introduced by Custom,"* satisfies Law 8 at the beginning of the title (which seems primarily to stand in the way).

5. Although adoption creates kinship through both blood and law <note>original: "cognationem & agnationem"</note>; *Law 1, Section 4, on those related by blood;* and likewise increases the status of the Adopted person: *Law 13, Law 35, on adoption; Law 5, Law 6, on Senators;* it does not, however, turn a commoner into a nobleman <note>original: "ex Plebeio Patricium"</note> (to whom our modern Nobles are similar). *The final law on the status of men.* This is the case unless the matter is specifically handled before the Prince <term>Princeps: the sovereign ruler or Emperor</term>. *Based on Law 10, Section 3, on the summons to court; and the final law on adoption.* See *Jacques Cujas, consultation 56; Friedrich Tileman, disputation 3, thesis 39, volume 1; Arumæus, exercise 2, thesis 21; Timæus Faber, disputation on the Institutes 6, thesis 10; Valentinus Arithmæus, in his Second Perils, disputation 6, thesis 5.*

6. Various Questions 29; see Justus Meier, in his miscellaneous questions taken from book 1 of the Pandects, thesis 155 and following.*

<margin>Book 2, title 1.</margin>
10. Jurisdiction, accepted in a general sense, is taken to mean every form of investigation <note>original: "cognitione"</note>, pronouncement, and decree in any case, whether it be civil or criminal. *Law 1, on the office of the one to whom jurisdiction is delegated. Matth. Stephani, treatise on Jurisdiction, 1. chapter 1.* Nevertheless, if a Prince grants Jurisdiction to a Nobleman as a Fief <term>Feudum: a landed estate held on condition of feudal service, often carrying legal rights</term>, he is not considered to have granted "Pure Empire" <term>Merum Imperium: High Justice, or the absolute power of the sword, including the right to inflict capital punishment</term>, as that requires special mention. *Argument from the last law, on the office of the one to whom etc. Fachinæus, 13. chapter 16. Bronchorst, 1. Controversies, assertion 17. See our Teacher, Lord Bocer, class 5, disputation 10, thesis 64 and following.*

11. Jurisdiction, thus spoken of in general, is divided into Empire <term>Imperium: the power of legal command and state authority</term> and Simple Jurisdiction. *Goveanus, Scipio Gentilis, Lord Bocer, Paurmeister, Thomas Michael, Matth. Stephani, etc., in their treatises on Jurisdiction. Lud. Charondas, Conrad Pincier, and Georg Obrecht disagree in the treatises written on the same matter.* Empire, which generally consists in the power of commanding <note>original: "in jubendo"</note>, *Just. Meier, Illustrious Questions, decade 1, chapter 6,* is further divided into Pure and Mixed.

12. Pure Empire exists as the punishment of a more serious crime: *Fachinæus, 9. controversies, chapter 94, and book 13, chapters 12 and 22;* which does not exclude criminal Jurisdiction. *Arumæus, disputation on the principal Laws, 2. thesis 1, question 1, at the end.* Mixed Empire, however, is the power of moderate coercion <note>original: "modicæ coërcitionis"</note> and includes any civil decree or command. *Law 3, Law 4, on Jurisdiction. Law "those things" [ea quæ], 26, on Municipal Law. See Vinc. Cabot, 2. disputations, chapter 8.* Jurisdiction is the faculty of deciding in civil matters without coercion. *Fachinæus, 13, chapter 14.* Which, while distinct by its nature from Mixed Empire, is nevertheless never found without it. *Ant. Fabius, 4. conjectures, 4. Fachinæus, 13. chapter 13. Lyclama, 4. eclogue 18. Ant. Matthæus, disputations on the Pandects, 2. thesis 2. Arumæus, disputation on the Laws, 2. thesis 1, question 2.* (who seems to have grasped the matter of Jurisdiction there in a few pages, both vigorously and clearly at the same time) *Cabot, 2. disputations, chapter 9.*

13. We group the power of appointing Guardians <term>Tutores: legal protectors appointed for those, such as minors, who cannot manage their own affairs</term> under Mixed Empire. *Law 1, on Jurisdiction. Law 6, section "the granting of a Tutor" [Tutoris datio], on Guardianship, joined with the section "but from these Laws" [sed ex his Legib.], on the Atilian Tutor. See Lord Bocer, treatise on Jurisdiction, chapter 1, number 12, and chapter 3, number 11, etc. Bronchorst, 1. Controversies, 19, and others praised by Hackelmann, 1. illustrious, last thesis, at the end; and Lyclama, 4. eclogue 16, as well as book 7, Eclogue 11.*

14. **To summon to court** <note>original: "In Ius vocare"</note> is to call someone for the sake of undergoing legal proceedings before the official who presides over the jurisdiction. *Law 1 of the Digest, on summoning to court, joined with the section 'all things' in the Institutes, on the punishment of reckless litigants.* However, according to old Roman Law, this summons used to be carried out without any Apparitor <note>a court attendant or minor official</note> or any order from a Judge: the person summoned was compelled to follow immediately; if they were unwilling or reluctant, it was legal for them to be led, seized, or dragged. *Duarenus, on disputes, book 1. Tileman, as cited, dispute 5, thesis 11 and following. Treutler, dispute 4, thesis 1, volume 1.*

15. # Taken from Books 1 and 2 of the Digest.

...seen to have [their] consent, it is thought to differ from a **Pact** <note>original: "Pacto"</note> in this way: a Pact exists when someone makes a promise to another who is requesting it. *Based on law 7, section 12, on pacts.* And they distinguish this from a **Stipulation**; for a stipulation cannot be conceived unless both parties are speaking. For this reason, neither a mute person, nor a deaf person, nor an absent person can enter into a Stipulation: *Law 1, on verbal obligations.* However, a Pact can be made by a nod <note>original: "nutu"</note> or by a letter. *Vaconius à Vacuna, declaration 65; Jacobus Revardus, book 2 of variations, chapter 1; add Reinerus Bachovius, miscellaneous disputes, chapter 3 and many following.*

21. In both **named contracts** <note>specific types of contracts recognized by law, like sales or leases</note> and **unnamed contracts** <note>agreements that do not fit a specific legal category but are still binding</note>, fulfillment <note>original: "implementum"</note> is required so that a legal action may be brought. *Law 'Julianus' 13, section 'offerri' 8, on the action of purchase.* This is so, however, that in named contracts, an obligation is born immediately; but it does not produce a legal action except for the party who has fulfilled their part, at least by an offer of performance. In unnamed contracts, however, before a "cause" exists (that is, before either of the contracting parties has fulfilled their part), neither an obligation nor an action is considered to have been born. *Vacuna, declaration 67.*

22. They call it a **statutory pact** <note>original: "Pactum legitimum"</note>, not because it takes its form from the Law, but because the Law stands by a bare agreement and grants it the effectiveness of a civil obligation. *Law 6, where Duarenus comments on pacts; Revardus, book 4 of variations, chapter 1.* Furthermore, a "just pact" differs from a statutory agreement: *Goveanus, book 2 of variations, chapter 26.* Just as we think that a Statutory Pact and a Contract have distinct and divided reasoning; since the former produces actions from confirmation, while the latter produces actions by its own nature. *Law 6, on pacts; law 12 of the Code, on interest; the final law of the Code, on the promise of a dowry; Cujas, book 11 of observations, chapter 17, and book 14, observation 28; Hackelmann, illustrious disputes, dispute 2, thesis 3.*

23. The effect of **Pacts** is to produce a **natural obligation** <note>an obligation that is morally binding but may not be enforceable in civil court without specific legal remedies</note>, which the Praetor is accustomed to protect by granting an "exception" <note>a type of legal defense</note>. *Law 7, section 'but since none' 4, on pacts; law 10 of the Code, on the same. Vacuna, declarations 21 and 23.* Nor should we pass over **Wesenbecius** *in his commentary on pacts, number 9* and others, who hardly doubt that under **Canon Law** <note>the law of the Church</note>, an action arises from a pact. Certainly, *chapters 1 and 3 of the Extravagantes on pacts*, on which they rely, cannot command that. *See the Teacher, Doctor Bocer, treatise on the law of contracts, chapter 1; Fachinaeus, book 2 of controversies, chapter 100; Forcatulus, dialogue 69, number 5; Tileman, dispute 7, thesis 37 and following; Hackelmann, illustrious disputes 2, thesis 10 at the end.* Although the **customs of the Germans** and even **Wurtemberg law** <note>original: "jus Wirtembergicum"</note> may prove the opposite.

24. Against this: Duarenus on law 7; Fachinaeus, book 2 of controversies, chapter 98; see Hackelmann, illustrious disputes 2, thesis 9; Bachovius, miscellaneous, chapter 10; Bronchorst, enunciation 1, chapter 35.*

...of two <note>completing the word *duorum* from the previous page</note> parties, it is thought to differ from a **Pact** <note>original: "Pacto"</note> in this way: a Pact is when someone makes a promise to someone who asks for it. *See the argument in Law 7, section 12, "On Pacts."* They distinguish this from a **Stipulation** <note>A formal, verbal contract in Roman law involving a specific question and answer.</note> because a stipulation cannot be conceived unless both parties are speaking; therefore, neither a mute person, nor a deaf person, nor an absent person can enter into a Stipulation. *Law 1, "On verbal obligations."* In contrast, a Pact can be made by a nod of the head or through a letter. *Vaconius à Vacuna, declaration 65; Jacobus Ravardus, book 3, variety 1; add also Reiner Bachovius, miscellaneous disputations, chapter 3 and many following.*

## 21. In both **named contracts** <note>Contracts with specific titles like "sale" or "hire" that had set legal rules.</note> and **unnamed contracts**, fulfillment <note>original: "implementum"</note> is required in order for a legal action to be brought. *Law of Julian 13, section "to be offered" 8, "On the action of purchase."* However, in named contracts, the obligation is born immediately, but it does not produce a right to sue unless the person has at least offered to fulfill their part. In unnamed contracts, however, until a "cause" is underlying (that is, before either of the contracting parties has fulfilled their part), neither an obligation nor a right to sue is considered to have been born. *Vaconius, declaration 67.*

## 22. They call it a **legal pact** <note>original: "Pactum legitimum"</note> not because it takes its form from the Law, but because the Law stands by a "naked agreement" <note>An agreement that usually doesn't grant a right to sue under strict Roman law.</note> and grants it the effectiveness of a civil obligation. *Law 6, where Duarenus comments "On Pacts"; Ravardus, book 4, variety 1.* Furthermore, a "just pact" differs from a "legal convention." *Goveanus, book 2, variety 26.* We also believe that a Legal Pact and a **Contract** <note>original: "Contractu"</note> have distinct and separate logic; for the former produces actions from a secondary confirmation, while the latter does so by its own nature. *Law 6, "On Pacts"; Law 12 of the Code, "On interest"; the final Law of the Code, "On the promise of dowry"; Cujas, observation 17, book 11 and observation 28, book 14; Hackelmann, illustrious disputations 2, thesis 3.*

## 23. The effect of Pacts is to produce a **natural obligation** <note>An obligation that is morally binding but might not be enforceable by a direct lawsuit.</note>, which the Praetor <note>A Roman judicial official.</note> usually protects by granting an "exception" <note>A legal defense used by a defendant.</note>. *Law 7, section "but when none" 4, "On Pacts"; Law 10 of the Code, on the same; Vaconius, declarations 21 and 23.* We should not skip over the opinion of Wesenbeck *in his notes on Pacts, number 9* and others, who have almost no doubt that under **Canon Law** <note>The law of the Church.</note>, a right to sue arises from a simple pact. Certainly, *chapters 1 and 3 of the Extra "On Pacts,"* which they rely upon, cannot mandate that. *See our Teacher, Master Bocer, treatise on the Law of Contracts, chapter 1; Fachinaeus, controversies, book 2, chapter 100; Forcatulus, dialogue 69, number 5; Tileman, disputation 7, thesis 37 and following; Hackelmann, illustrious disputations 2, thesis 10 at the end.* Although the customs of the Germans and even the **Law of Wirtemberg** <note>Local law of the Duchy of Württemberg.</note> might prove the opposite.

## 24. A pact added immediately <note>original: "incontinenti," meaning at the time the contract is signed.</note> to a contract shapes the legal action. *Law 7, section 5, "On Pacts."* Thus, a lawsuit is granted based on the principal contract, rather than through "prescribed words" <note>original: "praescriptis verbis," a flexible type of lawsuit for cases without a standard name.</note>. *Arguments from Laws 2 and 3, "On prescribed words"; Law 4, "On the commissary clause"; Borcholt on Pacts, final chapter; Donellus, "On prescribed words," chapter 2; Connanus, commentary 5, chapter 2; Contius, book 1, disputation 17; Wilhelm Valentin Forster, "On Pacts," chapter 8, page 236; Arumaeus, disputations on the principal Laws 3, thesis 10.* Duarenus *on the said Law 7*, Fachinaeus *controversies 2, chapter 98*, Hackelmann, Bachovius, and Bronchorst argue the opposite.
