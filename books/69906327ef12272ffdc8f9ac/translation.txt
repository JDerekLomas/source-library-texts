...[those] whom we call Wise men, He has established: their care is equal to that of kings, and the divine love toward them is far greater. If anyone considers how fluid and unstable human affairs are, and how they cannot be ruled by any human skill or contained by any force, he will understand both the supreme dignity and the absolute necessity of wisdom. 

Since there were so many more powerful kings in the age of Alexander the Great and Trajan <note>Trajan (reigned 98–117 AD) was an Emperor known for the Roman Empire's greatest territorial expansion.</note>, and both before and after them, none won such immortal glory for themselves as these two did—simply because one was the disciple of Aristotle and the other of Plutarch <note>Renaissance tradition held that the philosopher Plutarch was the tutor of the Emperor Trajan.</note>. But Alexander Severus, son of Mammaea <note>Julia Mammaea was the powerful mother of the Emperor Alexander Severus (reigned 222–235 AD); he was known for his intellectual interests and tolerance.</note>, was even more glorious than those others because he was most devoted to the study of wisdom. By contrast, look at the Commoduses, Caligulas, Vitelliuses, and Caracallas—monsters of the human race, worthy of the Empire only by the virtue <term>virtue: here meaning the "virtus" or ancestral merit and power</term> of their ancestors—what unhappy ends overwhelmed them? 

Nor is the primary part of ruling a mere skill in letters; rather, it is the study of wisdom and the cultivation of justice. Tiberius and Claudius were learned men, yet they were born to be the greatest destruction of the human race. Trajan and Alexander Severus were perhaps less skilled in formal learning, yet they provided a great light to the world because they elevated good and wise men and favored scholarly pursuits. 

We must not consider what kind of fortune we currently enjoy, but what kind we are worthy of, and what kind it is proper to expect; indeed, even what kind we might be forced to endure. It is foolish not to fear adversity when we have placed our hope in untrustworthy things. God overturns the greatest kingdoms by the smallest designs; conversely, He has elevated many from a private condition to the highest peak on account of their virtue. He alone governs all things with His finger <note>original: "digito temperat"—a common metaphor for effortless divine control.</note>. 

Mortals are persuaded that everything is subject to our own strength and skill. Therefore, it is a lesser evil to hate wise and learned men than to despise them; for this latter vice is joined with the greatest ambition and folly, while the former is evidence of human weakness or a certain kind of tyranny. There is no reason why anyone should [regard] this...

Therefore, these two should be the highest pursuits for men: to be able to do as much as possible and to know as much as possible. For the sake of these, God has equipped us in manifold ways with prudence, arts, sciences, disciplines, literature, and examples. 

Yet the knowledge of future things seemed to be something even more divine, which would not only teach one to know, but would also greatly favor one's power. For this reason, He placed it at the summit of certain arts, and as a kind of ornament to them: namely, in agriculture, medicine, navigation, and military and civil governance. For what is more beautiful than to see a man like a certain sacred oracle, correctly predicting the future: whether a ship will escape shipwreck, or a sick person will survive a disease, or a candidate will obtain a public office, or if he has predicted a storm or some other event? 

But since these types of <term>divination: the art of discovering hidden knowledge or the future by supernatural or technical means</term> did not satisfy everyone, but only those who devoted their labor to such specific disciplines, divine generosity also granted many general types of foreknowledge of the future: <term>Astrology: the study of the movements and relative positions of celestial bodies interpreted as having an influence on human affairs</term>, <term>Physiognomy: the practice of assessing a person's character or personality from their outer appearance, especially the face</term>, <term>Chiromancy: the art of palm reading</term>, and <term>Metoposcopy: the art of reading a person's character or destiny from the lines on their forehead</term>. 

However, because all of these were intended only for learned men, it pleased Him to also provide a certain general method which would be common to all mortals at all times, and to subject it to us without the need for any complex art. -># EPISTLE<-

...one should judge it just as one would any other <term>natural science: a field of study concerning the physical world and its laws</term> and human art. For if it is considered superstitious to foreknow future events, and something denied to man, then why is it permitted for physicians, sailors, farmers, and prudent military commanders to foreknow such things?

If, however, it is the method itself that is condemned—the very one that alone finds approval in <note>original: "sacris literis," likely referring to the various instances of prophetic dreams in the Bible.</note> Holy Scripture—who then will be more religious for it? But it is true that one may use this kind of discipline poorly, believe in it rashly, trust it foolishly, or interpret it incorrectly. Yet what other discipline will not be dangerous for this same reason? For a physician can kill the sick, a lawyer can judge unjustly, and even a barber could slit a man's throat.

But they will say (I know) that the path to error is not so prepared and obvious for those professions as it is for wickedness; yet the path to wickedness is closed to no one. It is enough that the path is not naturally inclined toward error, as is the case in all <term>divination: the practice of seeking knowledge of the future or the unknown</term>, especially regarding dreams, which is practiced among the common folk. But they are mistaken. The path to error lies open to everyone; there are many twists and turns everywhere, and we wander in the dark. Yet there is also one easy and certain defense against the dangers of error, which, if someone scorns it, he has no cause to complain; but if he embraces it, he will not suffer.

That defense is this: that he should recognize all these things as natural, to be either guarded against or provided for, not to be cursed and lamented as if they were fixed certainties. If the roof of a house collapses, or if a dog howls, both are natural events; but how they are received by the mind is how they should be judged. Consider your own life, your habits, your deeds. Often these things are either of no consequence, or they are the signs of a warning God. 

If a plum tree grows in a house, you will expect fruit; yet fruit does not always come. For either the tree will perish before it blossoms, or it is <term>blasted: "siderat," a term for a plant being struck by disease or celestial influence</term> and will not flower; or if it blossoms, the fruit will not follow (just as with our sycamore trees); or if it does produce fruit, it will not ripen; or before it ripens, a thief will steal it away. ...fear: of which things we take away one from them, and we show that the remaining one must also be taken away. The meanings of dreams are natural and entirely free from necessity; yet they are linked to natural causes, not subject to superstition, and not to be feared due to some specific divine decree.

I have always marveled at what happened to me regarding my teaching post in <note>original: "Bononiæ," referring to the University of Bologna, where Cardano was a professor.</note> **Bologna**, which I was not even considering at the time: when by one dream I was so warned that I was not going to accept the position at all, and by another as if I were certainly going to admit it. This led me to once think that this kind of <term>divination: the practice of seeking knowledge of the future</term> was like that of a man who is warned not about the future, but only about the present, and indeed makes a **conjecture** about future things from those warnings.

But why are we forced to suspect this, when it is already clear to us that natural causes provide a conjectural knowledge of future events? Therefore, since there is nothing so good that one might not bring about their own ruin by using it poorly—whether it be wealth, power, glory, or wisdom—so there is nothing so unfavorable that we cannot use it successfully.

And just as physicians compose <term>alexipharmaca: antidotes or remedies against poison</term> from poisons, and mild medicines from harsh ones; while others, on the contrary, by mixing good things ineptly, applying them at the wrong time, or handling them imprudently, prepare great troubles for themselves and their kin; so conversely, if someone uses the divination of dreams appropriately—though it be natural, obscure, and <term>ancipiti: uncertain or two-sided</term>—he will gain no small help in directing human actions. 

But if he does so listlessly, or through negligence, or with too much curiosity, he will suffer loss rather than benefit. Therefore, the whole matter rests upon the **prudence** and skill of those who use it. 

It is dangerous to trust in conjectures. I admit it: but how many people are there who do not direct all their actions by conjecture? For this reason, it is better to trust in many things at once and with judgment, than in a few and rashly—whether they be strong or weak. Of those who have put faith in dreams, no...

...to raise them up, or to equal them with the unworthy. The seat of the blessed lies wide open; this condition of mortality is narrow. It is not within the will of mortals to carry anyone to the highest seats: the Most High alone has reserved this power for himself. To hinder, to betray, to overthrow is granted to everyone: so that it is not glorious which is common not only with other animals, but also with plants, stones, and roof tiles. Wherefore **Socrates** is quite skillfully praised by **Cicero**; but his opinion is not understood as skillfully as it is, nor is it known how gravely he related it. In each inquiry, you will consider those four things which were proposed <margin>Book 1, Ch. 14.</margin> in the Chapter on the *Comparison of the parts of the world and man* <note>original: "Comparatione partium mundi & hominis"; Cardano’s theory that the human body (the microcosm) reflects the universe (the macrocosm).</note>: and they are: Man and his parts, or what is within us; what pertains to us, such as a wife, children, and the rest; affections <note>original: "Affectiones"; here referring to emotions, states of being, or qualities.</note>; and causes. Next, consider all the qualities of the thing: for instance, if you see a **plum tree** in your courtyard and you notice it is **blooming**, and from there it produces **fruit** which you **eat** while still **sour**. First, you will look at what a "tree" signifies generally, then what a "plum tree" signifies specifically, in the Chapter on Plants. Then, in the Chapter on Actions, look for what it signifies to "gather fruit" and what it signifies to "eat." However, because "gathering fruit" might not be explicitly listed, you will learn through similarities and general rules; you should consult the section after the Chapter on Sensory things because of the "sour fruit," and the section on things derived from trees (which follows the Chapter on Plants), as well as the Chapter on Foods. Finally, look up "to bloom." This makes eight elements to consider: the tree, the plum tree, blooming, sour fruit, gathering, food, and eating <note>The Latin text lists seven items here—tree, plum, blooming, sour fruit, gathering, food, and eating—but states there are eight.</note>.

Thirdly, you will turn to the general Chapter on interpreting dreams <margin>Book 1, Ch. 11.</margin> to consider the general headings, of which there are three to be considered in all cases besides the specific exposition. There you will also be specifically taught the method of interpretation more extensively than here.

Next, you will consider the specific differences of dreams from the Second Book: whether it is an imperfect dream, a forgotten one, a repeated one, or a habitual one; and especially if it is a phantom <note>original: "idolū"; a dream image produced by the mind rather than a divine source.</note>, a recollective dream <note>original: "anamnesticon"; a dream that retrieves a lost memory.</note>, or a mixed dream, or if it is "great" or "fatal."

In the fifth place, consider the **conditions of the dreamer**: for example, I am an old man, poor, with children, a doctor, a professor, hated in my homeland, and little liked by the magistrates, burdened with great envy, yet known throughout the whole world. The same dream will signify something far different for me than it would for a young physician who is wealthy, who only practices his art, and who is more carefree.

Finally, you will go to the Fourth Book and there, among the one hundred and fifty-one examples, you will take care to find some similar to your own. If you are diligent, it will hardly happen that you do not find one or more that are at least partially similar. By their interpretation or similarity, you may arrive at the point where—as the saying goes—you **hit the nail on the head** <note>original: "rem acu tangas"; literally "to touch the thing with a needle," meaning to identify something with perfect accuracy.</note>.
