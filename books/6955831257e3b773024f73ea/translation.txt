# PREFACE.

**I**t is an extraordinary *fact* that although Ireland has, from a remote antiquity, been celebrated for its cultivation of music and admitted to be one of the parent countries of that delightful art, the present work is the first *general* collection of its national airs. Most of them are of such ancient origin that the names of their authors and the era in which they were composed are equally unknown.

The works of some of Ireland's latest composers, such as <term>Connollan</term> <note>original: "Connollan"; likely referring to Thomas or William Connellan, famous 17th-century Irish harpers.</note> and <term>Carolan</term> <note>Turlough O'Carolan (1670–1738) was a legendary blind harper and composer, often called the last of the Irish bards.</note>, have been selected before; but even for these, it remained necessary until this day to provide accurate copies. Meanwhile, the superior productions of their masters—on whom they had modeled their style and whose excellence they have not matched—are now only partially known, even in the very country where they once flourished. To rescue them from being forgotten and to open a new source of musical delight, the public is now presented with the first volume of a collection that has long been eagerly desired.

A brief account of the circumstances which led to this collection will naturally be expected.

The rapid decrease in the number of itinerant performers on the Irish harp, along with the resulting decline of that tender and expressive instrument, gave the first idea of assembling the remaining harpers dispersed over the different provinces of Ireland. A meeting of them was accordingly arranged, at a considerable expense, by the Gentlemen of Belfast on July 12, 1792, and generous premiums were distributed among them according to their respective merits.

The compiler of this volume was appointed to attend on that occasion to transcribe the various airs played by the different harpers. He was particularly cautioned against adding a single note to the old melodies, which—according to conclusions that will be drawn later—seem to have been preserved in their pure form and handed down unalloyed through a long succession of ages.

A principal motive for convening this gathering of the last remaining Irish bards was to procure, while they were still available, the most approved copies of tunes already known by performers. Another goal was to revive and perpetuate a variety of other extremely ancient tunes for which no copies existed, and which were therefore likely to soon become extinct.

This end was, in a great degree, secured by the meeting mentioned; and it has since been perfected by the editor of the present work, who made a tour through a major portion of the kingdom for the purpose of comparing the music already obtained with that in the possession of harpers in other parts, and of making such additions as would render the work complete.

# PREFACE

This work is now before the public <note>original: "tribunal"</note>, which is the natural judge of its merits. It may, however, be stated without being overconfident, that as long as public taste remains pure enough to admire melodies that speak directly to the heart, the ancient music of Ireland will be studied with increasing delight. The performer should remember that the music of a country and its language are analogous. There are unique idioms and delicate characteristics in both; to truly enter into their spirit, some time and practice are required. This is especially true for those compositions produced in a very distant period.

We may be permitted to mention a few of the reasons that lead us to believe that some portions of the following music are of great antiquity.

Most of the performers gathered at the meeting mentioned above <note>The Belfast Harp Festival of July 1792, where Bunting transcribed these melodies from the last of the traditional harpers.</note> were elderly men, and they all agreed in one opinion regarding the reputed antiquity of those airs which they called *ancient*. They smiled when questioned about the era of such compositions, saying, "They were more ancient than any to which our popular traditions extended."

It appears that the old musicians, in passing this music down to us through many centuries, treated it with the utmost reverence; they seem never to have dared to make the slightest innovation to it over time. We naturally draw this conclusion from finding that harpers collected from parts far distant from one another, and taught by different masters, always played the same tune in the same key, with the same kind of expression, and without a single variation in any essential passage, or even in any single note. The beauty and regularity with which the tunes are constructed appear surprising. This circumstance seemed even more extraordinary when it was discovered that the *most* ancient tunes were, in this respect, the most perfect, allowing for the addition of a bass line with more ease than those that were less ancient. Hence, we may conclude that their authors must necessarily have been excellent performers, well-versed in the theoretical <note>original: "scientific"</note> part of their profession, and that they originally intended for *harmony* to be included in the composition of their pieces. It is remarkable that the performers all tuned their instruments on the same principle, while being totally ignorant of the theory itself, and without being able to explain the reason for their method of tuning or for their way of playing the bass.

On an impartial review of all these circumstances, we are inclined to believe that those specimens which have survived and been passed down to us are only the remnants <note>original: "wreck"</note> of better times, the history of which is either lost or incorrectly remembered in a confused series of traditions.

Giraldus Cambrensis <note>Gerald of Wales (c. 1146 – c. 1223), an archdeacon and chronicler who accompanied Prince John to Ireland.</note>, who came over to Ireland during the reign of Henry II, gives us a striking account of the state in which he found the music of this country. This enlightened prelate—a native of Britain and probably not entirely free from the prejudices then held against the Irish—was a man well-acquainted with the fine arts in general, and with music in particular, as it was practiced at that time by the most refined nations of Europe. But such was the celebrity of Irish music a century preceding the arrival of Cambrensis <note>Giraldus Cambrensis (Gerald of Wales), a 12th-century chronicler.</note>, that the Welsh Bards, so celebrated for their knowledge in this art, condescended to seek for and receive instructions from those of Ireland. Caradoc, also a Welshman in the twelfth century, assures us—without any of that narrow-minded partiality so common with national writers—that the Irish devised all the instruments, tunes, and measures in use among the Welsh.

The Bards, according to the testimony of Strabo, Diodorus, and Ammianus Marcellinus, existed among the ruder branches of the Celtic tribes before the time of Augustus. When we consider his occupation, we cannot help but be astonished at the extraordinary powers of his mind. He occasionally attempted almost every style in music: the elegiac, the festive, the romantic, and the sacred. He excelled so much in each that we scarcely know for which of them his genius was best suited. His first composition was a romantic and mournful piece called "Bridget Cruise," addressed to a lady to whom he was tenderly attached without any hope of success. He is said to have dedicated fifteen different pieces to her, none of which are contained in this collection. The first was either originally imperfect, or the copy obtained was so corrupted that a bass part could not be adapted to it. His last tune was inscribed to his physician, Dr. Stafford. He composed the *Fairy Queen*, *Rose Dillon*, and others of his serious pieces early in life; but after establishing a reputation and becoming too fond of festive company and the bottle, he dedicated his time to the composition of his *Planxties* <term>Planxty: A lively Irish harp tune, often composed in honor of a specific person or patron.</term>, which required no intense labor or Diligence. We may form some idea of the fertility of his genius from this circumstance: one harper who attended the Belfast Meeting <note>The Belfast Harp Festival of 1792, organized to preserve the traditions of the Irish harp.</note>, and who had never seen Carolan or been taught by anyone who had copied from him, had acquired over a hundred of his tunes. The harper noted that these made up only a very small part of the actual total.

As Carolan never taught any traveling pupils except his own son (who had no musical talent), and as we have never heard that any of his pieces were written down until several years after his death—when the younger Carolan, under the patronage of Dr. Delany, edited a small volume—we need not wonder if nine-tenths of his entire work is irreparably lost.

In *Carolan's Concerto* (No. 42) and in his *Madam Cole* (No. 16), the musician will perceive clear imitations of Corelli <note>original: "Correlli"; referring to the Italian composer Arcangelo Corelli.</note>, in which the exuberant imagination of that admired composer is successfully copied. In the ancient air *Gradh gan fios* <note>original: "Gradh gan fios"</note>, or *Love in Secret* (No. 14), the listener will be charmed by one of the most pleasing melodies any country has produced; it is so old that no trace could be discovered of the century in which it was written.

The lyrics of *Coolin* existed as far back as the reign of Henry VIII—a very modern period when compared with the time in which the melody itself was composed. *Scarfuint na Gompanach* <note>original: "Scarfuint na Gompanach"</note>, or the *Parting of Friends* (No. 25), is considered very ancient. It is often played by harpers when the audience is about to leave, and popular tradition holds that it was composed while the Irish suffered under the oppression of the Danes and were forced to hide in caves and secluded places.

The tune called *Thugamar fein a Samhradh linn* <note>original: "Thugamar fein a Sambra lin"; translates to "We brought the summer with us."</note> (No. 61) is probably extremely ancient. It was sung by the group of young women who went out from Dublin to welcome the Duke of Ormond when he landed in Ireland. # PREFACE.

The extreme improbability that the ancient Irish *borrowed* their music from a country that has no national music of its own (with the exception of Wales) is quite clear. Their devoted attachment to their own music and the praise it received from other countries, combined with their ignorance of the English language and their deep-seated hatred for their invaders, were effective barriers against any such plagiarism or adoption.

The tune titled *If You Go to a Foreign Land* <note>original: "Ad ccoigreac ma bin tu"</note> (No. 1), which was obtained in County Mayo, is one we have reason to believe is the oldest still in existence. It was sung by only one person of a very advanced age; although many people were present, few even knew it by name, yet they all appeared greatly delighted by the composition.

To list all those tunes that speak to the heart and harmonize with the finest feelings of our nature would extend this preface to an unreasonable length. It should be noted, however, that several of the tunes in the following collection were not taken from the Irish harp, but from singers <note>original: "songsters"</note>; therefore, as they now stand, they are not always adapted for that instrument.

We cannot conclude without seriously urging the gentlemen in the southern parts of Ireland to follow the example of the Belfast Society <note>The Belfast Harp Society was formed in 1808 to revive the Irish harp tradition, which was then in danger of disappearing.</note> by promoting similar meetings of harpers in their own provinces. It is a debt that every man owes to his country: to search for and preserve the records of days gone by, to oppose the destructive ravages of time as much as he can, and to make permanent the fleeting works of every kind of genius. In this case, these works come from an era so remote that they frustrate our attempts to determine their exact place in history. The reverence with which our ancestors held the music of Ireland, along with every other trace of Irish antiquity, and the respect it has received for so many centuries from foreign nations, seems well-suited to inspire similar feelings in their descendants. Shall we allow these traditions to perish in our hands at the close of perhaps the last century in which even a single new ray of light can be found amidst the gloom with which time covers its earliest and often most interesting works? In paying them all due attention, we are not merely gratifying a natural feeling of national pride; we are tracing the progress of the human mind and attempting to restore a lost page in the history of humanity.
