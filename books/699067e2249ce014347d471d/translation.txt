**I**n all arts and sciences, **Practice** <term>Praxis: the practical application or exercise of a branch of learning.</term> and **Theory** <term>Theoria: the philosophical or speculative study of a subject.</term> seek a union, so that if one should abandon the other, both would deservedly fail. <meta>This page continues the preface of Book 8 of "Musurgia Universalis." On the previous page, Kircher argued that music requires both Theory and Practice; here, he reveals his motivation for creating a mechanical composition system: to prove to arrogant professional musicians that a theorist can indeed compose beautiful music, and to provide a tool for missionaries in foreign lands.</meta>

->Artis Magnæ Consoni, & Dissoni<-

...so that he whose mind was set on searching more deeply into the inner secrets of Music would find that in the intention of performance, practice would not fail Theory, nor would Theory fail practice. An added stimulus to carry out this proposal was the arrogant scolding of purely practical musicians <note>Kircher refers to professional performers and composers who lacked formal education in music theory.</note>; for they think it impossible for a theorist to compose a melody with the skill, elegance, and grace that modern Music rightly requires. Indeed, they greeted the contrary assertion with laughter and mockery. To show their scolding was false and unjust, I have applied myself seriously to this task for many years, leaving nothing untried to discover an Art by which anyone—no matter how unskilled in Music—could, in a short time and without labor, achieve what practical Composers barely achieve in many years.

Therefore, having been taught by frequent musical experience, I noted a great power of combinations hidden beneath musical intervals and the diversity of tones. I approached the principles of the entire art of musical combination <note>original: "Musurgicæ combinationis"</note> with the help of the analytic art <note>Kircher is referring to combinatorics, the branch of mathematics dealing with combinations of objects.</note>, and with truly great labor, I reduced the more excellent and necessary transformations of harmonic relations into tables. These were arranged with such secret skill that, in whatever position the Melotactic <term>Melotactic: Pertaining to the arrangement or "taxis" of melody.</term> columns were ordered, a new harmony would always emerge. Since this wonderful Melotactic combination achieved quite successful results even in simple Counterpoint <note>original: "Contrapuncto"</note>—while it was still, so to speak, crying in its first cradle—I was immediately carried away by a mental fervor. Not content with these results, I applied my mind to extracting a universal method of musical composition <note>original: "Musurgię Melotheticę"</note>. In this, I seem to have succeeded, exhibiting every harmonic beauty and grace together with the appropriate artistic words. Although these things at first seemed paradoxical <note>original: "παράδοξα"</note> and truly prodigious to musicians, they were nonetheless convinced by experiments conducted by various illustrious men, and they learned to have faith in this Art whose principles they did not yet know.

Our new musical art consists primarily in the skillful combination of Melotactic Tables; however these are arranged, a new harmony necessarily emerges. Thus, since such compositions are nearly infinite, an infinite diversity of harmonic concertos is born. Whoever, therefore, knows how to adapt our Melotactic Tables according to the rules to be prescribed by us shortly, and knows how to rightly transfer the harmonic numbers into the melotactic scale, will find himself composing any skillful songs—simple, complex, florid, diminished, syncopated, woven with artistic ligatures, and following one another in the manner of fugues—with the same ease. For just as in Algebra <unclear>Algebra</unclear>, the calculator <term>Logista: one who performs mathematical calculations by following set rules without necessarily understanding the underlying theory.</term> completes the clarification of paradoxical questions by following the course of the rules with such ease that the operator himself does not know how he has done it, and thus an unexpected effect comes forth with the calculator barely thinking; so too, for those musical composers following the course of the prescribed rules, the desired harmony will come forth, whether performed by those skilled in Music or those ignorant of it—even by children and women.

Certainly, the vows and effective pleas of our Fathers migrating to the most remote lands of the Indies <note>Kircher was a Jesuit priest; "the Indies" refers to the global missionary territories, including the Americas and East Asia.</note> did much to bring this secret to the public. Since the attraction of these peoples consists in the frequenting of musical exercises and divine praises, and printed books or composers are not always available, they believed this "Musurgia" would be of the greatest use to them. With this, they will be able to produce the desired songs not only in the Latin language, but in any proposed language—even Indian and however unpolished the idiom—as will be discussed at length later.
