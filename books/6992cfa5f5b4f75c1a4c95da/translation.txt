### Customs and Social Character

Their narrow style of clothing is the opposite of ours <note>The author contrasts the fitted Ottoman garments—likely the tunics and trousers seen during the 19th-century Tanzimat reforms—with the loose, flowing robes of the Chinese gentry</note>. Upon entering a household, they remove their shoes as a gesture of etiquette. When eating or drinking, they sit on the floor with their legs spread <note>original: "箕坐" (*jīzuò*); a traditional Chinese term for sitting in a way deemed disrespectful in China, here used to describe the West Asian custom of sitting on mats or low cushions</note> and take their food directly with their hands.

Women reside in the seclusion of the deep inner chambers. They do not meet with men, nor do they exchange greetings; the separation between the internal and external spheres is extremely strict. 

The character of the people is contradictory: they appear brave but are inherently timid; they appear truthful but are actually cruel; they appear quick but are in truth lazy; they appear solemn but are actually licentious; they appear generous but are in fact stingy <note>This series of harsh characterizations reflects the common biases found in the European sources that Wei Yuan adapted for this gazetteer</note>.

### Religion and Law

The entire nation follows **Islam** <term>Islam: "回教" (*Huíjiào*); literally "The Religion of the Hui," the standard Chinese term for Islam</term>. They regard their King [the Sultan] as the **Leader of the Faith** <term>Leader of the Faith: "教主" (*Jiàozhǔ*); here referring to the Sultan’s role as the Caliph</term>. They boast that the profound mysteries of their religion are unique and found in no other faith. 

They strictly forbid the consumption of alcohol, yet they take great pleasure in giving alms and building **guesthouses** <term>Guesthouses: "館廨" (*Guǎnxiè*); referring to caravanserais or *imarets* (public soup kitchens/inns) intended to house travelers</term>. However, they smoke opium and believe in charms, spells, and heterodox arts. For any practice that is forbidden by law, the penalty for an offender is death.

### Literature and Education

Their native literature is shallow and clumsy. When they first conquered **Greece** <note>original: "額里西" (*Élǐxī*), a transliteration of "Hellas"</note>, the local Greek literature was nearly brought to total destruction. Subsequently, there was King **Orhan** <note>original: "荷占王" (*Hézhàn Wáng*); Orhan I (r. 1324–1362), the second Ottoman ruler, known for establishing the first Ottoman administrative and educational structures</note>, who possessed a great love for literature and learning. 

He established a great library in the **Bursa** <note>original: "普魯薩" (*Pǔlǔsà*); the first major capital of the Ottoman Empire</note> region. These centers for scholars are called **Madrasas** <term>Madrasas: transcribed here as "馬特勒西圖" (*Mǎtèlēixītú*); Islamic theological colleges</term>. The Sultan granted these institutions very generous stipends, wishing for scholars to remain in study for a long period to eventually attain the official rank of **Ulama** <term>Ulama: transcribed as "玉里麻" (*Yùlǐmá*); the class of legal and religious scholars who interpreted Islamic law</term>.

# The Provinces of the Ottoman Empire in Europe

The Ottoman Empire <note>original: 都魯機國 (*Dūlǔjī Guó*); a phonetic transliteration of "Turkey"</note> consists of various provinces <note>original: 部落 (*bùluò*); while literally meaning "tribes," Wei Yuan uses this term to denote administrative regions or states</note> situated within Europe. <gloss>The Black Sea connects to the Great Ocean; its borders to the east, west, south, and north are shared with Russia and Austria.</gloss> To the northwest, it governs ten major provinces, forty-six minor provinces, two hundred and forty-eight administrative centers, and thirty-four islands of various sizes.

# Northern Turkey in the Great Western Ocean

It is said that in ancient times, the famous scholar Aristotle <term>Yàlìsīduō; the traditional Chinese transcription of the Greek philosopher</term> dedicated himself to "investigating the principles of things" <term>wùlǐ; a term used here for natural philosophy or physics</term>. However, he encountered a certain tide whose cause he could not fathom. In his frustration, he threw himself into the water and drowned. A local proverb arose from this: "Aristotle wished to grasp the tide, but the tide grasped Aristotle instead."

One region is called Corfu <note>original: 哥而府 (*Gē’ěrfǔ*)</note>, which measures six hundred *li* <term>a Chinese unit of distance; approximately 300 kilometers</term> in circumference. It produces wine, oil, and honey of exquisite quality. In every direction, one sees only groves of oranges, pomelos, and citrons; no other trees grow there. The climate is clear and mild, yet it is said that wild birds never visit this land.

Another region is called Hungary <note>original: 翁加里 (*Wēngjiālǐ*)</note>, located to the south of Poland <note>original: 波羅尼 (*Bōluóní*)</note>. Its natural products are immensely abundant; the cattle and sheep found there are sufficient to supply the needs of the entire continent of Europe <note>original: 歐羅巴 (*Ōuluóbā*)</note>. 

There are four remarkably strange waters in this land:
1. One springs forth from the ground and immediately solidifies into stone.
2. One flows freely during the winter months, but conversely turns into ice during the heat of summer.
3. If iron is thrown into another, it becomes soft like mud; when smelted again, it transforms into fine copper. <note>This likely refers to the "cementation" process observed in copper-rich mine waters, where iron is chemically replaced by copper</note>
4. The fourth has water of a deep green color; when it freezes, it becomes a green stone that never melts.

Of the frontier lands, cultivated fields occupy five-tenths of the area. Mountains, forests, rivers, lakes, and gardens for trees and vegetables each occupy one-tenth. There are approximately 100,000 farmers. The "five grains" <term>five grains: a traditional Chinese categorization for staple crops like rice, wheat, and millet, here used to describe European cereal staples.</term> produced are insufficient for the people's needs, and they must be imported from foreign countries. 

Other products include dyes, grapes, olive oil, silk, and "fine fruits" <note>likely referring to currants or citrus</note>. Those living by the sea are skilled at fishing, and the people take great pleasure in trade. In the 21st year of the **Daoguang** reign <note>1841 CE</note>, the total value of imports and exports was 43,752,200 *yuan* <note>original: 圓 (yuan). Referring to the Spanish silver dollar, the international currency of the era.</note>. Its merchant fleet consists of 3,197 vessels with 18,600 sailors, many of whom are also pirates. 

There are many harbors where ships can take shelter from the wind. The islanders are quite civilized and literate, specifically those from **Syra** <note>original: 敘刺 (Xu-ci)</note>, **Andros** <note>original: 安多 (An-duo)</note>, **Mykonos** <note>original: 米哥尼 (Mi-ge-ni)</note>, **Hydra** <note>original: 希得拉 (Xi-de-la)</note>, and **Poros** <note>original: 破羅 (Po-luo)</note>. They are described as being well-versed in letters but also quite cunning.
