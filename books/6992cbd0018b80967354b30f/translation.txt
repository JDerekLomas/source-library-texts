Of the two divisions of this work, distinguished by their own specific titles, the first aims to present atomism <note>original: "Atomistik." Now, of course, the construction of every house requires, besides the stones, also space, time, measure, rule, plan, and for all that outwardness, **Someone** for whom the whole house is built, who builds it, and who inhabits it. The building blocks do not do it alone, nor through themselves; neither the connecting nor the decomposing is their deed; they are merely that which has been decomposed; and the most general, highest, and best that belongs to the construction was lost during the decomposition of the structure and is no longer to be found in the stones; yet there is also no construction without the building blocks, and in the case of great structures, one will have to ask from what and how they built themselves or were built, until the concept itself sets the limit of the question. One can quite well concede that the concept of absolutely simple, <term>point-like</term><note>Point-like (original: *punctueller*): Having the characteristics of a mathematical point—possessing position but no extension or physical dimensions.</note>, and in the strictest sense **infinitely small beings** remains burdened with the same difficulty as the concept of an infinitely large world, insofar as the imagination can never exhaustively keep up with it. As purely extensive frameworks, space and time cannot represent or provide "content" on their own; one cannot say that space and time exist *within* something. On the contrary, everything that exists must be conceived of as existing *in* space, *in* time, or in both. Conversely, <term>simple beings<note>The author likely refers to "monads" or fundamental atoms—indivisible units that make up the fabric of reality.</note></term> can only provide content, but they do not possess extension themselves. Thus, space and time are in themselves absolutely empty, while simple beings are that which bring "fullness" into this void.

While simple beings fill space, they do not "occupy" it in a physical sense. If they truly occupied it, space would not contain them as "content" (as the logic of their relationship requires), but rather they would "cover" or overlap the space. Just as anything that covers the circumference of a circle is not *inside* it, points can exist within a circle in any number. Instead of a circle's circumference, consider space itself. Furthermore, the "fullness" of reality—which consists of a multitude of distinct, individual things—would vanish if space were absolutely "filled" or saturated. The infinite richness that simple beings bring into space is only possible because space itself remains "unoccupied."

Instead of saying that space is filled by matter, one could say from a certain (albeit limited) point of view that space remains as empty with the existence of matter as it would be without it. This is because all simple beings, taken together as points, ultimately converge into a single point that represents no physical extension.

Time is no more "filled" by the existence of matter than space is; for "being in time" is no more to be confused with "filling time" than "being in space" is with "filling space." However, a combined filling of both space and time occurs, if one wishes to phrase it so, through **motion**. Should a simple being ever come to a total rest (though absolute rest does not exist), it would, during that duration, fill time with nothing. It is not the simple being itself that fills space and time during motion, but rather the *motion* of the simple being that fills them, insofar as motion is a product of both.

However, perhaps it can be countered that acoustic and elasticity phenomena depend on the "lower" forces of <term>molecules</term> <note>here meaning larger clusters of matter</note> that are relatively far apart, while heat phenomena depend on the "higher" forces of particles that are relatively close, where a proportional approach and recession must then gain greater influence. For now, however, it will be difficult to decide with any certainty on the reality of one or the other mode of explanation.

Regarding the opposing types of electricity, one might perhaps consider that friction, like the contact of dissimilar bodies, has the effect of making the oscillations of the smallest particles of matter or <term>ether</term> <note>The "aether" was a hypothesized medium through which light and heat were thought to travel in 19th-century physics</note>—which as heat oscillations may be randomly oriented—respectively more parallel and more perpendicular to the surface of the bodies. Specifically, negative electricity might rest upon oscillations in the former sense. The different forms of positive and negative sparks and of <term>Lichtenberg figures</term> <note>Branching patterns formed by electrical discharges on the surface of insulating materials</note> would agree well with this; and it seems to me that if one wishes to think of the electrical state as depending on the oscillations of particles at all, one can hardly think of anything else. The <term>galvanic current</term> <note>A steady flow of electricity (direct current)</note> would then be an interference phenomenon of such oscillations.
