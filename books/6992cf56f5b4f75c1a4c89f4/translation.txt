## Records of the Island Nation of Japan

The *History of Ming* <note>original: "Ming Shi" (明史), the official historical record of the Ming dynasty (1368–1644)</note> states: Japan was known in ancient times as the country of <term>Wa</term> <note>original: "Wano" (倭奴). An ancient name for the Japanese people used in Chinese records.</note>. During the <term>Xianheng era</term> <note>670–674 CE</note> of the Tang dynasty, the name was changed to Japan <note>original: "Riben" (日本), literally "Sun's Origin"</note> because it is near the Eastern Sea where the sun rises. The land is surrounded by sea, with large mountains only in the northeast corner. It is divided into five provinces, seven circuits, and three islands <note>The "Goki-Shichidō" administrative system</note>, totaling one hundred and fifteen "islands" <note>likely referring to smaller administrative divisions or provinces</note> and five hundred and eighty-seven districts. Various smaller states are subordinate to it. A small state covers one hundred <term>li</term> <note>A Chinese mile, roughly 500 meters</note>, while a large one does not exceed five hundred *li*. Small households number in the thousands, and large ones do not exceed ten or twenty thousand.

The ruler’s family has used the surname "King" for generations, and the ministers also hold hereditary offices. Prior to the Song dynasty, they maintained continuous contact and offered tribute to China. Only during the reign of <term>Khubilai Khan</term> <note>Shizu of the Yuan dynasty</note> did they refuse to come, despite envoys being sent repeatedly to summon them. Consequently, the Emperor ordered <term>Fan Wenhu</term> to lead a naval force of one hundred thousand to conquer them. Upon reaching Wulong Mountain, they encountered a violent storm, and the entire army was lost; thus, there was no further contact throughout the remainder of the Yuan dynasty.

When the Ming dynasty rose, Japan took advantage of China’s military preoccupations to repeatedly raid coastal prefectures and counties. In the second year of the <term>Hongwu era</term> <note>1369 CE</note>, an envoy was sent to deliver an imperial decree and question them regarding these raids. Japan did not submit and continued their plundering as before. Although they sent tribute several times, these were rejected because they lacked the proper formal petitions. In the twentieth year <note>1387 CE</note>, the Emperor ordered Zhou Dexing, the Marquis of Jiangxia, to go to Fujian, and Tang He, the Duke of Xin, to go to Zhejiang to organize maritime defenses. Fujian was ordered to prepare one hundred sea-going vessels, and Guangdong was to hold reinforcements in readiness. At that time, <term>Hu Weiyong</term> <note>A high-ranking official and early Ming Chancellor</note> was plotting treason and sought help from Japan. [The Japanese] claimed to be arriving with tribute and presented giant candles that secretly contained gunpowder, knives, and swords. When their mission arrived at the capital, the plot of Hu Weiyong <note>original: "惟庸" (Hu Weiyong). The first Chancellor of the Ming, executed in 1380 for treason; the Emperor suspected Japan was aiding his conspiracy.</note> was exposed. Consequently, the Emperor determined to sever ties with Japan and focus exclusively on coastal defense. Later, he composed the *Ancestral Instructions* <note>The *Huang-Ming Zuxun*, a set of foundational policies for the Ming dynasty.</note> regarding fifteen "countries not to be conquered," and Japan was included among them.

At the beginning of the Yongle era <note>1402–1424 CE</note>, official communications and tribute missions first resumed. At that time, pirates from islands such as Tsushima and Iki were plundering coastal residents. When the Emperor rebuked the Japanese King, the King was ashamed and dispatched troops to capture the gangs. He bound twenty of their leaders and presented them as captives. From that point on, tribute was frequently offered, and captured sea pirates were often handed over. Their memorial stated, "The claims of <term>shishu</term> <note>original: "鼫鼠". Literally 'marmots' or 'large rats,' a classical metaphor for rebels or hidden troublemakers.</note> on our islands are truly not known to your servant; I beg for your mercy." Nevertheless, the pirate raids did not cease.

In the seventeenth year [of the Yongle era], the Regional Commander of Liaodong, Liu Jiang, dealt them a crushing defeat at Wanghaiguo. From then on, pirate raids became rare, and tribute envoys also stopped coming.

In the fourth and eighth years of the Zhengtong era <note>1439 and 1443 CE</note>, forty Japanese vessels raided Taizhou and Haining. Previously, during the Hongxi era <note>1424–1425 CE</note>, two men from Huangyan and Longyan had fled to Japan to escape their labor duties and served as guides for the raiders. Later, the raiders grew more cunning; they would alternate between carrying local products and weapons as they moved along the coast. If they saw a vulnerable opening, they would brandish their weapons and engage in unrestrained plundering. If they found no opportunity, they would display their local goods and claim to be a tribute mission. The southeastern coastal regions suffered greatly from this. 

Their tribute envoys also frequently committed murders and violated the laws. Those who came to pay tribute were all seeking the profits of trade and collaborated with corrupt local powerful families along the coast. Thus, they were simultaneously "tribute-bearers" and "pirates."

In the twenty-seventh year of the Jiajing era <note>1548 CE</note>, the Grand Coordinator <term>Zhu Wan</term> <note>A famous Ming official known for his strict enforcement of maritime bans.</note> strictly imposed internal prohibitions and executed those caught collaborating with the foreigners. Because of this, the prominent families of Zhejiang and Fujian—who had long served as internal hosts for the Japanese—lost their profits and grew to resent Zhu Wan. Furthermore, Zhu Wan repeatedly sent memorials to the imperial court, explicitly describing how these powerful families were in league with the Japanese. As a result, the people of Fujian and Zhejiang all came to despise him.

The Inspecting Censor Zhou Liang, a native of Fujian, submitted a memorial slandering Zhu Wan. He requested that the office of "Grand Coordinator" be changed to "Grand Inspector" to diminish Zhu Wan's authority. His faction at court supported him from all sides, and the request was eventually granted. Later, Zhu Wan was stripped of his rank and dismissed for the crime of "unauthorized execution" of prisoners. Zhu Wan committed suicide. For four years after this, no Grand Coordinator was appointed. The maritime ban was relaxed once more, and the ensuing chaos only intensified.

According to the ancestral system: Zhejiang established a **Superintendency of Maritime Shipping** <term>Shibo Tiju Si; the imperial office managing foreign trade</term>, managed by a eunuch official stationed in Ningbo. When the eunuch official arrived, he would standardize the prices of goods, and the authority to manage the traders rested with the high officials.

When Emperor Shizong <note>The Jiajing Emperor, reigned 1521–1567.</note> withdrew the regional eunuch commanders from across the empire and subsequently tightened the prohibitions on maritime trade, the business of trade shifted into the hands of powerful and wealthy local families. These families increasingly defaulted on the payments owed to the foreign traders. When the traders demanded their money urgently, the elites would use alarmist talk to frighten the generals and officials into sending troops to suppress them as "pirates." When the troops were about to move out, the elites would then deceive the traders with kind words, saying: "In the end, I will not fail to pay you what I owe."

The Japanese traders lost their capital and could not return home; they grew deeply resentful. Meanwhile, great villains like **Wang Zhi**, **Xu Hai**, **Chen Dong**, and **Ma Ye**—men who had long lived in hiding among the foreigners—found they could no longer operate successfully on the mainland. They all escaped to offshore islands to act as masterminds for the Japanese, enticing them to launch raids. Consequently, the great pirates of the sea adopted Japanese clothing and flags and divided their ships to plunder the mainland.

The pirate menace grew more severe by the day. Therefore, the imperial court debated reinstating the office of **Grand Coordinator** <term>Xunfu; a high-ranking provincial governor with military oversight</term>. In the seventh month of the thirty-first year [of Jiajing, 1552], the Assistant Censor-in-Chief **Wang Yu** was appointed to the post.

However, the momentum of the crisis had already become impossible to extinguish. Previously, at the start of the dynasty, **Guard and Battalion stations** <term>Weisuo; the primary military unit of the Ming army</term> were built at strategic coastal points and provided with warships. They were overseen by the Regional Military Commission, Vice-Commissioners, and other officials, and the control was thorough. But after a long period of peace, the ships were dismantled and the ranks became empty. When alarms were raised, the authorities had to recruit fishing boats to assist in patrolling and guarding.

The soldiers were not properly trained, and the boats were not built for combat. When they saw pirate ships approaching, they would immediately flee and hide at the first sign of danger. Furthermore, there was no supreme commander to lead them. Consequently, wherever the [pirates'] tattered sails pointed, nothing remained but ruins and devastation.

In the third month of the thirty-third year [1554], Wang Zhi conspired with various Japanese groups to launch a massive invasion. Hundreds of ships joined together, covering the sea as they arrived. From East and West Zhejiang to the North and South of the Yangtze River, thousands of miles of coastline sounded the alarm simultaneously. The pirates plundered the area between Lishui, Liyang, and Yixing. Government troops emerged from Lake Tai, crossed through Wujin, and reached Wuxi. They stayed at Mount Hui for a day and night before racing over one hundred and eighty *li* <note>approximately 60 miles</note> to Hushushu. There, they were surrounded by the government army, who caught up to them at Yanglin Bridge and annihilated them. In this campaign, the rebels numbered no more than six or seven thousand, yet they traveled thousands of *li*, and the number of people killed or wounded in battle reached nearly four thousand. It took over eighty days before they were finally suppressed. This occurred in the ninth month of the thirty-fourth year <note>October 1555</note>. 

Cao Bangfu, the Regional Coordinator <term>Xunfu; a high-ranking provincial governor</term> of Yingtian, reported the victory to the throne. However, Zhao Wenhua <note>a prominent official and protégé of the powerful Grand Secretary Yan Song</note> was jealous of his success. Using the excuse that the pirates were still nesting at Taozhai, Zhao Wenhua gathered a large force of troops from Zhejiang and Zhili. Together with Hu Zongxian—his personal commander—he coordinated with Cao Bangfu for a joint suppression campaign. They advanced along separate routes and camped at Zhenqiao in Songjiang. The pirates charged with their elite forces, and the government army suffered a crushing defeat. Zhao Wenhua’s spirit was broken, and the rebels grew even more audacious. 

In the tenth month, the pirates landed at Yueqing. They plundered Huangyan, Xianju, Fenghua, Yuyao, and Shangyu; the number of people killed or captured was beyond counting. It was not until they reached Sheng County that they were finally defeated, and even then, their force had consisted of fewer than two hundred men. Yet they had penetrated deep into three prefectures and lasted fifty days before being pacified. 

Prior to this, another branch of pirates had traveled from Rizhao in Shandong, plundering through East Anwei to Huai'an County, Shuyang, and Taoyuan, reaching as far as Qinghe. They were eventually delayed by rain and annihilated by government troops from Xu-Pi. Though this group consisted of only a few dozen men, they caused havoc across a thousand *li* and killed over a thousand people. Such was their ferocity. 

Following the defeat at Zhenqiao, Zhao Wenhua saw that the pirate threat remained severe. They continued to move from Zhelin to Zhoupu and remained stationed as before in their old nests at Chuansha, Jiading, and Gaoqiao. Not a day passed without some form of incursion. Nevertheless, Zhao Wenhua requested to return to the imperial court under the pretext that the "pirates had been quelled."

The pirates spoke of reaching the Goto Islands <note>original: "Five Islands," a chain in the East China Sea belonging to Japan</note> of that country. There, Wang Zhi <note>the most powerful pirate lord and merchant of the era, also known as Wang Wufeng</note> and Mao Haifeng <note>Wang Zhi’s adopted son and lieutenant</note> claimed that Japan was in the midst of internal chaos; the King and his high ministers had all died. Consequently, the various islands were no longer under a central authority. They argued that unless individual proclamations were sent to each island, there would be no way to stop the raids. 

They further claimed that the incursions from places like Satsuma were the work of "foreign islanders" who had taken to the sea, and did not reflect the true intentions of the Japanese leadership. They begged for the right to send tribute and engage in mutual trade <note>the "Tribute System" was the only legal way for foreign nations to trade with the Ming Dynasty</note>, expressing a willingness to kill other bandits to prove their loyalty. They hoped to pacify the various islands and then send the "King" back to Japan. They requested that Hu Zongxian <note>the Supreme Commander of the coastal defenses</note> relay this to the Ministry of War. 

The Ministry responded by saying that Wang Zhi and his associates were originally registered commoners of the Ming Empire. If they truly wished to show loyalty, they should immediately lay down their arms. However, the pirates made no mention of surrendering their weapons; they only sought to open trade and present tribute, behaving as if they were an independent tributary state. Their treachery was difficult to predict. Therefore, the Ministry ordered the Supreme Commander to project the majesty of the nation and strictly reinforce defenses. They sent a directive to Wang Zhi and the others, demanding they clear out the pirate nests in the Zhoushan Islands to prove their sincerity. If the coastal borders were truly cleared, rewards and honors would naturally follow. The Emperor approved this plan.

At that time, both Eastern and Western Zhejiang were suffering under the pirates <term>Wokou; literally "dwarf pirates," referring to the multi-ethnic seafaring raiders composed of Japanese and disgruntled Chinese merchants</term>. The burning and killing in Cixi were particularly horrific, with Yuyao suffering nearly as much. The areas around Zhelin, Zhapu, Wuzhen, and Zaolin in Zhejiang had all become pirate strongholds. In total, over 20,000 pirates had arrived. Hu Zongxian was ordered to urgently devise a strategy. 

In the seventh month, Hu Zongxian reported that the pirate leader Mao Haifeng had offered to surrender. Mao first defeated a pirate force at Zhoushan and then defeated them again at Libiao. He also sent his subordinates to persuade various islands to submit to the Ming court, while requesting substantial rewards for his service. The Ministry granted Hu Zongxian the authority to act as he saw fit. 

During this period, the pirate leaders Xu Hai, Chen Dong, and Ma Ye had joined forces to besiege Tongxiang. Hu Zongxian devised a plan to sow discord among them <note>a classic "divide and conquer" strategy</note>. As a result, Xu Hai captured Chen Dong and Ma Ye and handed them over as part of his surrender. The remaining pirate forces were annihilated at Zhapu. 

Shortly thereafter, government forces cornered Xu Hai at Liangzhuang, where he was executed <note>literally "gave up his head"</note>, and his remaining followers were wiped out. The various pirate bands in Jiangnan and Western Zhejiang were largely suppressed. However, the pirates in Jiangbei <note>North of the Yangtze River</note> proceeded to attack Danyang, plundered Guazhou, and burned the grain transport ships. The following spring, they struck again at Rugao and Haimen, attacked Tongzhou, plundered the Ning Village in Yangzhou, and reached Baoying. They eventually invaded the Prefecture of Huai’an and gathered at Miaowan. It took over a year before they were finally defeated. Meanwhile, the pirates in Eastern Zhejiang entrenched themselves in the Zhoushan Islands, which repeatedly served as their base of operations.

The locations attacked by the army were previously visited by Jiang Zhou, who had been sent to deliver proclamations to the various islands. Upon reaching Bungo <note>original: "Feng-hou," a province in eastern Kyushu, Japan</note>, he was detained. However, he managed to send monks to reside on islands such as Yamaguchi <note>original: "Shan-kou," a region in western Honshu</note> to deliver the proclamations demanding the prohibition and suppression of the raids. Consequently, the Governor of Yamaguchi, Minamoto no Yoshinaga <note>original: "Yuan Yizhang," the 31st head of the Ouchi clan</note>, prepared a formal dispatch and returned the people who had been plundered. This dispatch was notably affixed with the "King's Seal."

The Governor of Bungo, Minamoto no Yoshishige <note>original: "Yuan Yizhen," historically known as the powerful daimyo Otomo Sorin</note>, sent the monk Deyang and others with local products and a memorial to apologize for past offenses. He requested the issuance of trade tallies <term>Kanhe: official notched vouchers used to verify authorized tribute missions and prevent smuggling</term> to resume tribute relations, and he sent Jiang Zhou back to China. 

Earlier, Zheng Shungong, who had been dispatched by Yang Yi <note>the previous Supreme Commander</note> to scout the seas, had also reached the island of Bungo. The lord of that island likewise sent the monk Qingshou with a ship to apologize, stating that the previous incursions were entirely the work of treacherous Chinese merchants who had secretly incited the various islanders to violence. Yoshishige and his peers claimed they truly had no knowledge of these raids.

Thereupon, Hu Zongxian <note>the Supreme Commander of coastal defenses</note> submitted a memorial to the throne detailing these events. He noted that although Jiang Zhou had been on his mission for two years, he had only visited the two islands of Bungo and Yamaguchi. He observed that some of the arriving parties brought tribute but lacked the official seals and trade tallies; others possessed seals but did not use the proper title of "King." All of these instances were violations of the Imperial Court’s established protocols.

However, Hu Zongxian argued that since they had come to offer tribute and had returned the captured people, they showed a genuine fear of punishment and a desire for imperial favor. He suggested it was appropriate to treat their envoys with courtesy and instruct them to relay orders to Yoshishige and Yoshinaga. These lords were, in turn, to command the King of Japan to capture and hand over the pirate leaders who had incited the chaos, as well as the Chinese traitors. Only then would they be permitted to engage in tribute trade. The Emperor issued an edict approving this plan.

While Wang Zhi was entrenched on his sea islands, he and his partisans—Wang Ao, Ye Zongman, Xie He, Fang Wu, and Qing Xi—each commanded their own forces. They enticed and coerced the Japanese pirates <term>Wokou: literally "dwarf bandits," referring to the multi-ethnic pirate fleets that ravaged the Chinese coast</term> to establish their dominance. The Imperial Court went so far as to offer the reward of a noble title and ten thousand pieces of gold for their capture, yet they remained out of reach. 

By this time, the government forces were well-prepared for the pirates. Although the pirates remained arrogant and violent, many were exterminated. On some islands, not a single soul was left alive; these were often the former followers of Wang Zhi. 

Hu Zongxian, being from the same prefecture as Wang Zhi, had provided housing for Wang’s mother, wife, and children in Hangzhou. He sent Jiang Zhou with a letter from Wang's family to summon him. When Wang Zhi learned that his family was safe and well, he was deeply moved. Yoshishige and the others were also pleased that China might allow mutual trade. Consequently, they outfitted a massive ship and sent forty of their subordinates to accompany Wang Zhi and his associates back to China.

# Illustrated Treatise on the Maritime Kingdoms, Volume 18
## Appendix: Routes of the Southeast Ocean
Page 6

<margin>Southeast Ocean: Island Kingdoms, Part 5</margin>

According to the *Investigation of the Eastern and Western Oceans*, the shipping routes for the Southeast Ocean begin at **Mount Qizhou** and the **Qizhou Sea** <note>Seven Islands Sea, located near modern-day Hainan</note>. <gloss>The *Records of Qiongzhou* state: Located one hundred *li* east of Wenchang.</gloss> In the middle of the sea, seven peaks rise in succession. Within these islands are springs of sweet, drinkable water. When ships pass, they must offer livestock and rice porridge as a sacrifice to the sea; otherwise, they will encounter malevolent spirits. Navigating this area is extremely perilous; if a ship drifts slightly to the east, it hits the **Ten-Thousand-Mile Stone Bank** <note>original: 石塘 (Shitang); a reference to the treacherous coral reefs and shoals of the Paracel or Spratly Islands</note>. This is what the *Records of Qiongzhou* refer to as the "Stone Bank East of Wanzhou." Those who strike these reefs rarely survive.

At the Qizhou Sea, the water depth is 130 fathoms <term>original: 托 (tuo), the span of a man's outstretched arms</term>. If heading toward **Cochinchina** <note>original: 交阯東京 (Jiaozhi Dongjing), referring to the Tonkin region of northern Vietnam</note>, use the single *Shen* compass direction <note>South-Southwest</note> for five watches <term>original: 更 (geng), a unit of time and distance at sea, roughly 2.4 hours of sailing</term> to reach **Mount Limu**. <gloss>Also known as Five Finger Mountain, located four hundred *li* south of Ding’an County. The *General Gazetteer of Guangdong* states:</gloss> Mount Limu is located in Qiongzhou. Within the mountains, five peaks stand tall like human fingers. Every morning after 9:00 AM, the clouds and mist disperse, and one peak can be seen piercing the emerald sky. Between 3:00 and 7:00 PM, it is hidden by clouds once more. Use the *Geng-Mao* direction <note>East/East-Northeast</note> for fifteen watches to reach **Mount Haibao**.

**Mount Haibao**. <gloss>Use the single *Hai* and *Qian-Hai* directions <note>Northwest/North-Northwest</note>. From the Tushan Estuary, sail for five</gloss> watches to reach **Jichang Gate** <note>Cock's Crow Gate</note>, which is the sea gate of **Van Don** <note>original: 雲屯 (Yuntun)</note> in Annam. The *Unified Records of Cochinchina* state: The territory borders the sea to the east and the Kingdom of Laos to the west. Furthermore, from the Qizhou Sea, use the *Kun-Wei* direction <note>Southwest</note>... to reach the **Ancient City** in the south and **Siming Prefecture** in the north.

**Mount Tonggu**. <gloss>The *General Gazetteer of Guangdong* states: Located northeast of Wenchang by the Tonggu Sea.</gloss> The waters around Mount Tonggu are extremely deep and dangerous. Use the *Kun-Wei* direction <note>Southwest</note> for four watches to reach **Mount Duzhu**. <gloss>The mountain is located in Wanzhou.</gloss> **Mount Duzhu**.
