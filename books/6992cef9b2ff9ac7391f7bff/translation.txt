### Border Garrisons, Part 3

## Datong
<note>Datong, located in modern-day Shanxi Province, was one of the "Nine Garrisons" of the Ming Dynasty, serving as a critical defensive hub against northern nomadic incursions.</note>

The *Examination of Administrative Geography* <term>original: *Zhifang Kao*; a reference to historical geographical records used to track provincial boundaries and resources</term> states: Datong was anciently the land of Yunzhong <note>An ancient name for the region dating back to the Warring States and Han periods</note>. At the beginning of the dynasty, the territory outside the main garrison city was divided into three routes: Central, East, and West. The "Great Border" and "Second Border" <note>Strategic lines of the Great Wall system</note> were established to defend against the invaders <note>original: *lu* (虜), a derogatory historical term used to refer to northern nomadic tribes, specifically the Mongols during the Ming era</note>. 

However, since the outposts of Dongsheng and Fengzhou could no longer be held, and the populations of Yunchuan and Yulin were moved inward, the outer double-border territories became entirely occupied by the invaders' dens. Subsequently, additional fortifications were constructed at the border towns of Zhenbian and Shahuya... <note>Shahuya, or "Kill the Barbarian Pass," was a vital passage in the Great Wall near the border of modern Shanxi and Inner Mongolia.</note>

<summary>This page introduces the strategic geography of Datong, describing how the Ming Dynasty's northern defense lines shifted inward after the loss of outer frontier posts to northern nomadic tribes.</summary>
<keywords>Datong, Wubeizhi, military geography, Great Wall, Ming Dynasty defense, Yunzhong, Shahuya Pass</keywords>

In the corner of this region, additional forts such as Yungang and Yunyang were established. Looking eastward, these outposts are within sight of one another, creating a continuous line of mutual support and reinforcement. 

The strategic situation of the "Three Yuns" <note>This likely refers to the three major strategic areas within the Datong jurisdiction: Yunzhong, Yungang, and Yunyang</note> is now vastly superior to what it was in the past. This defensive line stretches from Tiancheng in the east, where it reaches the boundary of the Xuanfu Garrison and the Xiyang River; to the west, it extends to Jingping, meeting the Beilou Pass in Shanxi. The total length of the inner and outer border defenses covers more than six hundred and forty *li* <note>Approximately 320 kilometers or 200 miles</note>.

Beyond these borders, the tribes of the Prince of Conformity <term>original: *Shunyi Wang*; the title granted by the Ming court to the Mongol leader Altan Khan</term>, along with Bayan Ajai, Cheligeng, the Wushen, the Baiyao, and the Tayiji <term>A Mongol title for nobility or princes</term> of the five routes, reside and graze their herds.

[Altan Khan] loved him more than all his other grandsons. For him, he arranged a marriage with a woman of the Yao clan, the daughter of **Daishen** <note>original: *Daishen*; a Mongol noble</note>. Later, **Bahan Naji** himself betrothed a woman from the **Uushin tribe** <note>original: *Wushen bu*; a subgroup of the Mongol people</note>, the daughter of Mian-che-jin, to be his concubine. 

Soon after, **Altan Khan** <note>original: *Anda*</note> forcibly seized a different unnamed woman—who was already betrothed to the **Ordos Commander** <term>Ao-er Du-si; referring to the leader of the Ordos Mongols</term>—to take as his own concubine. When the Commander raised troops to protest this, Altan Khan became fearful. He subsequently took the woman betrothed to Bahan Naji and gave her to the Ordos Commander as compensation for the one he had stolen.

Filled with resentment over this trade, Bahan Naji turned his back on his grandfather and surrendered to the Ming Dynasty in the **Year of Gengwu** <note>1570 CE</note>. Altan Khan repented his error and begged for his grandson’s safe return. He offered his submission through formal protocols and pledged his sincerity to pay tribute. These are the origins and circumstances of how the **Tribute Market** <term>Gongshi; the legalized trade system between the Ming and Mongols</term> was established through Bahan Naji.

# Ministry of Revenue: Taxes and Corvée, Part 3

In the 3rd year of the Jiajing reign <note>1524</note>, the administration began to distinguish between **Main troops** and **Guest troops** <note>original: <term>zhu-ke</term>; "Main" troops were local permanent garrisons, while "Guest" troops were external reinforcements sent to the border</note>. The quantity of salt licenses <note>original: <term>yin</term>—government permits for the salt trade used to generate revenue</note> allocated for the Main troops was determined based on the annual **Capital Transport Silver** <note>original: <term>jingyun yin</term>—funds sent from the central government to the borders for military supplies</note> quotas. At that time, the amounts were weighed and adjusted according to the surpluses or deficits found in the civilian-military colonies <note>original: <term>tuntian</term></note> and the incoming Capital Transport Silver.

In the 7th year of the Zhengtong reign <note>1442</note>, the Capital Transport Silver system was first established with an initial amount of 269,638 *taels* <note>A <term>tael</term> or *liang* is a unit of weight for silver, roughly 37 grams</note>. In the 19th year of the Chenghua reign <note>1483</note>, the distinction between Main and Guest troops was first introduced. In the 1st year of the Jiajing reign <note>1522</note>, the government began increasing the annual quotas. Before the 16th year <note>1537</note>, the amount did not exceed 70,000 *taels*. However, after the 7th year <note>1528</note>, the administration requested and added to the budget every year, eventually reaching several times the previous amounts. In the 19th year <note>1540</note>, the specific funding quotas for Main and Guest troops were separated, and in the 45th year <note>1566</note>, the permanent administrative regulations were finally fixed. From this point forward, unless there were tax exemptions due to disasters or urgent military emergencies requiring more troops and horses, the expenditures were not permitted to exceed these established figures. Finally, in the 6th year of the Longqing reign <note>1572</note>, it was established that silver from legal fines and retained local funds—totaling 181,000 *taels*—would be used to offset the Capital Transport Silver for Guest troops. Only after these funds were applied would the remaining requested amounts be issued.

# Shanxi

The *Records of Regional Geography* <note>original: <term>Zhifang Kao</term>; a Ming dynasty geographical treatise</note> states: Among the various military defense commands, Shanxi is considered part of the interior. ## Yansui

The *Records of the Bureau of Provisions* <note>original: "职方考" (Zhifang Kao) — an official administrative geography record used during the Ming Dynasty</note> states that Yansui <note>A strategic military district in modern-day northern Shaanxi</note> was the territory of the ancient Shang Commandery. At the beginning of the current dynasty <note>The Ming Dynasty (1368–1444 CE)</note>, cities such as Dongsheng were constructed to serve as garrison outposts.

Regarding the lands beyond the [Yellow] River: since control was lost in the *Jisi* year <note>1449 CE, the year of the Tumu Crisis, which significantly weakened Ming northern defenses</note>, the Mongol leader Huo Shai <note>original: "火篩" — also known as Khorsai, a prominent chieftain who frequently raided the border</note> entered and occupied the Ordos Loop <note>term: <term>套内</term> (Taonei) — the fertile and strategic region "inside the bend" of the Yellow River</note>. It was then that discussions began regarding moving the garrison headquarters to Yulin.

It was argued that the garrison city was situated far off in the extreme frontier, leaving its flanks and rear <note>literally "shoulders and back"</note> exposed to enemy attacks. To the east of Shenmu <note>A county in Shaanxi</note>, the defenses rely upon the Yellow River to maintain a secure position.

The [Ordos] region is blocked by the [Yellow] River on three sides and bordered by the frontier to the south. From east to west, it spans nearly two thousand *li* <note>approximately 1,000 kilometers</note>, while from north to south, it covers eight or nine hundred *li*. To the north of the River lie the three Shouxiang Cities <note>Surrender-Accepting Cities, strategic outposts originally established during the Han and Tang dynasties to manage nomadic submissions</note>. To the east of these cities lies Dongsheng. Following the Jisi Crisis <note>the 1449 Tumu Crisis, a pivotal conflict where the Ming Emperor was captured by Mongol forces</note>, Dongsheng was lost to the enemy. The Mongol leader Huoshai <note>Khosai</note> subsequently raided southward; having realized the strategic value of the Ordos Loop, he entered and occupied it.

During the Jiajing reign <note>1522–1567</note>, a leader named Jinong <note>a Mongol title for a high-ranking prince</note> partitioned the land of the Ordos Loop. This area was the most fertile region near the Guanzhong passes. He divided his troops into three divisions, known as the *Ao’er Dusi* <note>the Ordos Regional Military Commission, a tribal administrative structure recognized by the Ming</note>. After Jinong died, his sons Banbuhai and Bujier Taiji succeeded him. Currently, chieftains <note>original: "酋長" (qiuzhang) — a historical term for tribal leaders</note> such as Qiejin Huangtaiji, Bushitu, Zhuangtulai, Zhuolitu, and Zaiseng lead various tribes and command large followings, frequently causing trouble for the Yansui and Ningxia regions. In the 29th year of the Wanli reign <note>1601</note>, an imperial edict was issued permitting the resumption of peace agreements, tribute, and trade as in former times.
