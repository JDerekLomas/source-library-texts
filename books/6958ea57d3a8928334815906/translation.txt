# Collected Works of Ekiken, Volume Two

## Introductory Notes

*   This volume contains ten different works totaling forty-four scrolls: *Record of Careful Thought* (6 scrolls), *Record of Great Doubts* (2 scrolls), *Collection of Self-Amusement* (7 scrolls), *Remaining Discourses on the Investigation of Things* (1 scroll), *Compilation for Self-Admonition* (1 scroll), *Essentials for Beginning Students* (3 scrolls), *Notes on the Punctuation of the Lesser Learning* (6 scrolls), *Notes on the Record of Recent Thoughts* (14 scrolls), *Japanese and Chinese Numerical Names* (1 scroll), and *Continued Japanese and Chinese Numerical Names* (3 scrolls).

*   The *Record of Careful Thought* <note>Japanese: Shinshiroku</note> was completed in the fourth year of the Shōtoku era (1714) when the author was eighty-five years old—the very year of his death <note>original: "the year of changing the mat" (ekisu), a classical euphemism for the death of a scholar</note>. It is a book expressing his personal views on philosophy, the meanings of the Classics, morality, ethics, and education. It includes the author's own preface. Appended to the end is a section titled "On Myself," in which he reflects on his life's work and scholarship during his final years.

*   The *Record of Great Doubts* <note>Japanese: Taigiroku</note> is a work expressing views held for over thirty years, questioning the theories of the Song Dynasty Neo-Confucians. It was completed in the summer of the fourth year of Shōtoku at age eighty-five, following the *Record of Careful Thought*. Written only a few months before his death, it is truly his final work. It contains the author's own preface. The commonly circulated printed editions only contain Dazai Jun's <note>Also known as Dazai Shundai (1680–1747), a prominent Edo-period scholar</note> critique and Ōno Dōmei's afterword, but lack the author's preface. As Dōmei's afterword is not worth preserving here, we have omitted it and specifically added the author's own preface.

    The author's preface is signed "Kaibara Atsunobu <note>Ekiken's formal given name</note>, an eighty-four-year-old student from Chikuzen Province," dated the spring equinox of the year of the Water Snake in the Shōtoku era (1713). This differs slightly from the *Chronological Biography* <note>Nenpu</note>, which states this book was completed after the *Record of Careful Thought*. This is likely because after writing the preface, he continued to revise and polish the text <note>original: "delete and moisten"</note>, and it was only a few months before his death that he finally declared it finished.

# Editorial Notes

Although it appears to have been completed when he was eighty-three, at the end of the seventh volume there is a signature and date marking the <term>Qingming Festival</term> <note>A traditional East Asian festival for honoring ancestors, occurring in early April</note> in the third month of the year 1714 (the year of the Wood Horse), along with the signature of the eighty-five-year-old man, Kaibara Ekken. This is likely because after the book was initially finished, he continued to delete and refine the text, remaining active in his revisions even until the year of his death <note>original: "the year of changing the straw mat," a literary euphemism for the deathbed of a scholar</note>. There is a preface by his disciple, Takeda Sadanao.

* The three works titled *Record of Serious Reflection* <note>Shinshiroku</note>, *Record of Great Doubts* <note>Taigiroku</note>, and *Collection of Self-Amusement* <note>Jigoshū</note> are his most valuable and important writings. Those who wish to understand the Master’s theories and thought in detail must read these books.

* The *Further Discourses on the Investigation of Things* <note>Kakubutsu Yowa</note> has no recorded date of completion. It is a collection of essays expressing his personal views on various subjects such as scholarship, morality, and medicine. It is included in the fourth volume of the *Kan'utei Series*.

* The *Book of Self-Warning* <note>Jikeihen</note> establishes four prohibitions regarding the arts of the mind, speech, dignity, and social interaction. It explains the preparation for self-control and reflection by citing the maxims of the ancients. Although the date of completion is missing, it was likely around 1667 or 1668, when he was thirty-eight or thirty-nine years old. It contains an afterword by his elder brother, Kaibara Sonzai.

* The *Essentials of Knowledge for Beginners* <note>Shogaku Chiyō</note> was completed in 1697 when he was sixty-eight. For the benefit of beginning students, he selected and recorded maxims from the ancients and added his own commentary. The book is divided into five sections: Learning, Self-Cultivation, Social Interaction, Management of Affairs, and Admonitions. It contains a preface by Itō Soan and a preface by the author himself.

* The *Reference for the Reading of the "Lesser Learning"* <note>Shōgaku Kudoku Bikō</note> was completed in 1669 when he was forty. It is a compilation of commentaries by past sages regarding Zhu Xi’s *Lesser Learning*, to which he added his own insights. It was a famous book that circulated widely at the time. In his later years, he further expanded and corrected it. This is the same work as the *Collected Commentaries on the "Lesser Learning"* edited by his disciple Takeda Sadanao.

* The *Reference for the "Reflections on Things at Hand"* <note>Kinshiroku Bikō</note> was completed in 1668 when he was thirty-nine. It compiles the commentaries of past sages on the *Reflections on Things at Hand* <note>A foundational Neo-Confucian anthology</note> and adds his own insights. At the time, it was praised alongside the *Reference for the Reading of the "Lesser Learning"* and was one of his most widely circulated works.
