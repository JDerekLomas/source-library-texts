## Means of Introduction to the Study of Nature.

### On the influence upon the perception of nature; the description of nature — landscape painting — the cultivation of exotic plants, concerning the physiognomic character of the plant world.

# C o s m o s .

I step out from the circle of objects into that of sensations. The reflections of external being, as grasped by the imagination in its dark activity and its manifold directions, form a kind of nature-view which constitutes a world of its own: the inner world. We wander through this world, not to <unclear>fathom the measure of knowledge concerning nature, but rather to show how the image of the outer world is reflected in the soul through the power of lively, spiritual perception, and how the impulse toward the contemplation of nature has been active at different times and in different stages of culture. We follow this formation of the imagination by considering the influences of nature upon the creative power of the mind and the emotions within the creations of poetry, landscape painting, and the cultivation of exotic plants. We show how the endeavor to wander through the interior of the world has favored the knowledge of the physical world.</unclear> <note>Humboldt is arguing that art and emotion are not distractions from science, but are actually essential drivers that encourage humans to study the physical laws of the universe.</note>

# I. Description of Nature. — The Feeling for Nature according to the Diversity
# of Eras and Ethnic Groups.

It has often been stated that the joy found in nature, while not foreign to Antiquity, was nevertheless expressed with more economy and less vivacity of feeling than in modern times. "When one recalls," says Schiller⁴ <note>Friedrich Schiller (1759–1805), a major German poet and philosopher. The work cited is his influential essay "On Naive and Sentimental Poetry" (1795).</note> in his reflections on naive and sentimental poetry, "the beautiful nature that surrounded the ancient Greeks; when one reflects on how familiarly this people could live under their fortunate sky with free nature, how much closer their way of imagining, their way of feeling, and their customs lay to simple nature, and what a faithful impression of the same their poetic works are: then the observation must seem strange that one finds among them so few traces of the sentimental interest with which we moderns can cling to scenes and characters of nature. The Greek is indeed accurate, faithful, and circumstantial to the highest degree in the description of the same, but with no more heartfelt participation than he shows in the description of a garment, a shield, or a suit of armor. # On the Feeling for Nature in Antiquity

As much truth and excellence as these individual statements contain, they can by no means be extended to the whole of Antiquity. We may also call it a limited view to always understand "Antiquity"—when it is to be contrasted with modern times—as exclusively the Hellenic <note>Greek</note> and Roman world. A deep feeling for nature expresses itself in the oldest poems of the Hebrews and the Indians: that is, among tribes of very different Semitic and Indo-Germanic descent.

We can only draw conclusions about the mindset of ancient peoples from the expressions of their feelings for nature which are articulated in the remnants of their literature; we must therefore track these expressions all the more carefully and judge them all the more cautiously, as they appear only sparingly within the grand forms of lyric and epic poetry. In Hellenic Antiquity, in the "flowering age" of humanity, we certainly find the most delicate expression of deep natural sentiment mixed into the poetic depictions of human passion or an action taken from legendary history; but the descriptive element of nature itself appears then only as an accessory, because in Greek artistic culture <term>Kunstbildung: the specific way a culture develops its aesthetic and artistic standards</term> everything moves, as it were, within the circle of humanity.

The description of nature in its form-rich diversity—nature poetry as a separate branch of literature—was entirely foreign to the Greeks. # The Human Connection to Nature

A vibrant, public life of the people drew away from a dull, fanatical immersion into the quiet workings of nature; indeed, physical phenomena were always assigned a relationship to humanity<gloss>5</gloss>, whether in the proportions of their outward form or their inner, stimulating vitality. It was almost exclusively these relationships that made the observation of nature worthy—under the thoughtful form of the simile—of being drawn into the realm of poetry as distinct, small portraits full of objective liveliness.

At Delphi, spring hymns<gloss>6</gloss> <note>original: "Frühlingspääne" (paeans), songs of triumph or healing</note> were sung, likely intended to express the joy of man after the endured hardship of winter. A descriptive representation of winter is woven into the *Works and Days*<gloss>7</gloss> of Hesiod (perhaps by the foreign hand of a later Ionian rhapsodist?). In noble simplicity, but in a sober, didactic <note>intended to teach or instruct</note> form, this poem gives instructions for farming, rules for acquisition and labor, and ethical reminders for a blameless life. It likewise rises to a more lyrical momentum only when the singer wraps the misery of the human race or the beautiful allegorical myth of Epimetheus and Pandora in an anthropomorphic <note>human-shaped</note> cloak. Also in the *Theogony* of Hesiod, which is composed of very different ancient elements, one finds several times—for example, in the enumeration of the Nereids<gloss>8</gloss> <note>sea nymphs, daughters of Nereus</note>—descriptions of the Neptunian <note>pertaining to the sea</note> realm hidden under the significant names of mythical persons. The Boeotian school of singers, and indeed the whole of ancient poetry, turn toward the phenomena of the external world in order to personify them in a human-like manner.

If, as just noted, the description of nature—whether it be the representation of the richness and luxuriance of tropical vegetation or the lively depiction of animal habits—has only recently become a distinct branch of literature, it is not because a receptivity to the beauty of nature was lacking where so much sensuality breathes. It is not as if one must miss the life-fresh expression of an observant poetic nature in the very place where the creative power of the Greeks <note>original: "Hellenen"</note> produced inimitable masterpieces in poetry and the visual arts. What we find only too sparingly in those regions of the ancient world—at least when judged by the sensibilities of our modern mindset—testifies in its absence less to a lack of receptivity than to the lack of an active need to reveal the feeling for natural beauty through words. Turned less toward the inanimate world of appearances than toward active life and the internal, spontaneous stirring of emotions, the earliest and also the noblest directions of the poetic spirit were epic and lyric. In these art forms, however, descriptions of nature can only be found as if mixed in by chance. They do not appear as separate products of the imagination. The more the influence of the ancient world faded, and the more its blossoms withered away, the more rhetoric poured itself into descriptive as well as instructive, didactic poetry. This was serious, grand, and unadorned in its oldest philosophical, semi-priestly form, such as the nature-poem of Empedocles <note>A Greek pre-Socratic philosopher (c. 494–434 BC) who wrote "On Nature" in verse</note>; through rhetoric, it gradually lost its simplicity and former dignity.

# The Poetic Description of Nature

The inspired nature-poem of Lucretius <note>Titus Lucretius Carus (c. 99–55 BCE), author of *De rerum natura* (On the Nature of Things)</note> encompasses the entire Cosmos. Related in spirit to Empedocles and Parmenides, its archaizing diction heightens the seriousness of the presentation. Here, poetry is deeply intertwined with philosophy, yet without falling into that "frostiness" of composition which—contrasting sharply with Plato's imagination-rich view of nature—was so bitterly criticized by the rhetorician Menander in his judgment on physical hymns.²² My brother <note>Wilhelm von Humboldt (1767–1835), Alexander’s elder brother and a renowned linguist and philosopher</note> has developed with great ingenuity the striking analogies and differences arising from the fusion of metaphysical abstractions with poetry in the ancient Greek didactic poems, in that of Lucretius, and in the *Bhagavad-Gita* episode from the Indian epic *Mahabharata*.²³ The Roman poet's great physical world-portrait, in its chilling atomism <note>The philosophical theory that the universe is composed of indivisible units or atoms</note> and its often wild geognostic <term>geognostic: an early term for geological, specifically regarding the structure of the earth's crust</term> dreams, contrasts with his life-affirming depiction of the human race's transition from the thickets of the forest to agriculture, to the mastery of natural forces, to the elevated culture of the mind and thus also of language, and finally to civil refinement.²⁴
