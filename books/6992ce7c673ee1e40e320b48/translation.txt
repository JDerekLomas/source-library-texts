## White Cardamom
<note>Botanical name: *Amomum kravanh*. In the Ming Dynasty, this was a highly valued spice and medicine used to treat "cold" in the stomach and to improve digestion.</note>

White Cardamom originates in the Kingdom of Kakola <note>original: "伽古羅國" (Jiāgǔluó Guó). This refers to a historical region likely located on the Malay Peninsula or the coast of Sumatra, known as a major hub for the spice trade.</note>. It is also found in Guangzhou and Yizhou <note>Modern-day Guangdong and Guangxi provinces</note>, though the local varieties are not of such high quality as those brought in by foreign ships <note>original: "蕃舶" (fānbó); referring to maritime trade vessels from Southeast Asia or the Indian Ocean.</note>.

The sprouts of the plant are similar in category to the plantain <note>original: "芭蕉" (bājiāo); specifically the Japanese fiber banana or hardy plantain.</note>, and the leaves resemble those of the Pollia plant <note>original: "杜若" (dùruò); *Pollia japonica*, known for its long, elegant leaves.</note>. The leaves grow to a length of eight or nine feet and are smooth and glossy. The plant is evergreen, remaining lush and not withering during either winter or summer. Its flowers are a pale yellow color. The seeds grow in clusters that look like grapes; they are green when raw and turn white as they ripen. They are typically harvested in the seventh month of the lunar calendar.

# Smartweed
<note>original: "蓼" (Liǎo). These are plants of the *Polygonum* or *Persicaria* genus, known for their peppery taste. In ancient China, before the introduction of chili peppers, these plants were primary sources of heat in cooking.</note>

There are many varieties of smartweed. There are Purple Smartweed, Red Smartweed, Green Smartweed, Horse Smartweed, Water Smartweed, Fragrant Smartweed, and Woody Smartweed, among others. The Purple and Red varieties have leaves that are small, narrow, and thick. The Green and Fragrant varieties have leaves that are similar to one another. The Horse and Water varieties both have leaves that are large and moist-looking with black spots upon them. <gloss>The flowers of all smartweed varieties may be red, white, purple, crimson, or black.</gloss> Woody Smartweed—also known as "Heavenly Smartweed"—is a climbing vine with leaves like those of the Cudrania tree <note>original: "柘" (Zhè), the silkworm thorn tree.</note>; its flowers are yellowish-white, and the skins of its seeds are green and smooth.

The largest variety is called "Dragon Smartweed." It is a pungent substance. When the King of Yue <note>King Goujian of Yue (reigned 496–465 BCE), who famously "slept on brushwood and tasted gall" to remind himself of the bitterness of defeat while plotting his revenge.</note> was bitterly contemplating how to avenge himself against the state of Wu, he would sleep upon smartweed. The records of Commandant Yin of the Han Dynasty include various chapters on the cultivation of mustard, mallow, smartweed, leeks, and onions. Furthermore, in the former palace of King Ding of Changsha <note>A Han Dynasty prince known for his filial piety.</note>, there was once a "Smartweed Garden." Some say that Red Smartweed can also be used to make a savory sauce.

## Pale Bamboo-leaf
<note>original: "淡竹葉" (Dànzhúyè). Known scientifically as *Lophatherum gracile*, this perennial grass is distinct from actual bamboo, though its leaves are strikingly similar in appearance.</note>

The leaves of the **Pale Bamboo-leaf** are like those of bamboo. Its flowers bloom with two petals and possess a color that is most vivid and verdant. Local country people use cotton to collect the essence of the plant, providing it for use in painting to create colors such as "Lantern Blue" and "Broken Green" <note>original: "破綠" (Pòlǜ). ## Auspicious Grass
<note>original: "吉祥草" (Jíxiángcǎo). Known scientifically as *Reineckia carnea*, this evergreen perennial is native to East Asia. In Chinese culture, it is considered a plant of good omen.</note>

Auspicious Grass is hardy and easy to grow, as it is not restricted by specific requirements for water or soil; it can even be cultivated atop stones. However, it is at its most beautiful when it has access to water. It is often used to accompany solitary scholar’s stones or spirit mushrooms <note>original: "靈芝" (Língzhī), also known as the Reishi mushroom (*Ganoderma*), a symbol of longevity</note>, as the resulting arrangement is exceedingly refined and elegant. The flowers are purple and grow in dense abundance, yet they do not bloom easily. 
<note>The phrase "does not bloom easily" refers to the traditional belief that the Auspicious Grass only flowers when the household is experiencing a period of great virtue or impending good fortune, making the bloom a celebrated event.</note>

## Spirit Mushrooms
<note>original: 芝草 (Zhīcǎo). These are legendary "immortality" fungi, typically referring to species like *Ganoderma lucidum*. They have been symbols of longevity and divine favor in China for millennia.</note>

The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary and encyclopedia, dating to the 3rd century BCE</note> states that the **spirit mushroom** blooms three times within a single year. The *Lunheng* <note>“Discourses Weighed in the Balance,” a philosophical work by the Han dynasty scholar Wang Chong</note> explains that these mushrooms grow from the earth; they emerge only when the vital energy of the soil is in a state of perfect harmony. 

Because they appear in five different colors, they are known as the “Spirit Herbs of Three Excellences.” The *Baopuzi* <note>“The Master Who Embraces Simplicity,” a central text of Daoist alchemy written by Ge Hong (283–343 CE)</note> classifies them into specific varieties, such as the **Mushroom of Three Completions** <note>original: 參成芝 (Cānchéngzhī)</note>, the **Wood Canal Mushroom** <note>original: 木渠芝 (Mùqúzhī)</note>, and the **Mushroom of Established Fruit** <note>original: 建實芝 (Jiànshízhī)</note>. 

If one is fortunate enough to obtain and consume these three types of spirit mushrooms, they will achieve **ascension to heaven in broad daylight**. 
<note>original: 白日昇天 (báirì shēngtiān). This refers to the highest level of Daoist transcendence, where the practitioner becomes an immortal and ascends to the celestial realms while still alive.</note>

The tree reaches a height of over ten feet, and its branches are lush with a purple hue. In the third month of the year, it produces white flowers with four petals. In the ninth and tenth months, it bears fruit that hangs in heavy clusters, with several dozen berries to a bunch.

The fruit is green when young and turns red when ripe. It hangs in clusters much like cherries or gardenia fruits, though its stems are shorter. ## Heavenly Fairy Vine
<note>original: 天仙藤, *Tianxian teng*. This refers to the climbing plant *Aristolochia debilis*.</note>

The Heavenly Fairy Vine grows in the mountain regions of the Yangtze and Huai River basins, as well as in eastern Zhejiang. Its flavor is bitter, its nature is warm, and it possesses a slight toxicity. In the spring, its sprouts emerge and spread out to form climbing vines. The leaves resemble those of the Kudzu plant <note>original: 葛, *Ge*</note>, though they are more rounded and smaller, and are covered with fine white hairs. This plant is evergreen and does not wither throughout the four seasons. The roots are characterized by fibrous whiskers.

The roots and sprouts are harvested during the summer months. People in the southern regions of the country utilize this plant most frequently. It is used to resolve "wind-fatigue" <note>original: 風勞, *fenglao*; a traditional diagnosis referring to exhaustion or illness caused by the combined effects of environmental "wind" and overexertion</note>. When combined with Ephedra <note>original: 麻黃, *Mahuang*</note>, it is used to treat "Cold Damage" <note>original: 傷寒, *Shanghan*; a category of acute febrile diseases</note> by inducing perspiration to break a fever. However, it must be used with caution: if taken together with Rhubarb <note>original: 大黃, *Dahuang*</note>, it can cause the "collapse of fetal Qi" <note>meaning it acts as an abortifacient or can induce miscarriage</note>.
