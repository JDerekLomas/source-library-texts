The "four last books" (47–50) were often known as the "terrible books" because they focused on crimes and punishments.</note>

### ***THESIS I.***

<margin>Book 47, Title 2, regarding Theft.</margin>
**A** person is held liable for **theft** by whose aid and counsel <note>original: "ope-consilio"</note> it was perpetrated. <note>Citing the Institutes, Book 4, Title 1, Section 11: "On obligations arising from wrongdoing."</note> Nor do I doubt that these words [aid and counsel] should be understood separately. <note>Citing various legal authorities including Gæddæus on the "Signification of Words" and the Code of Justinian.</note> Our Teacher, Doctor Harpprecht <note>Johannes Harpprecht (1560–1639), a highly influential German jurist at the University of Tübingen.</note> at the *Institutes, on obligations arising from wrongdoing, section 11*; and Arumæus, <note>Dominicus Arumæus (1579–1633), a Dutch-born jurist who helped found the study of public law in Germany.</note> *Disputations on the principal laws, 24, thesis 3.* If, however, a theft was not actually committed as a result of the counsel and persuasion of another, we say that the persuader is not held liable. <note>original: "suasorem non teneri"</note> *Digest, book 52, section 19, on theft.* Pacius disagrees. <note>referring to Giulio Pace, a famous 16th-century legal scholar.</note>

2. Since one should only dispute about what laws *ought* to be passed, but must obey those that *have* been passed: one might not unjustly wonder why it is still debated today whether the **penalty of strangulation** <note>original: "pœna strangulationis" — This refers to execution by hanging, the standard capital punishment for thieves at the time.</note> is justly imposed for theft. Indeed, there are many who think that penalty is too cruel; and among them someone wrote that such madness never flared up that they thought thieves should be punished with corporal punishment, and that such a penalty is as far removed as possible from the foundation of Justice. But Doctor Harpprecht demonstrates extensively and piously that this penalty is just, necessary, and in conformity with equity; as does Giovanni Battista Baiardi in his additions to Giulio Claro. <note>Giulio Claro (1525–1575) was a leading authority on criminal law.</note> Truly, that axiom will remain and stand perpetually: **that as crimes increase, punishments must be made more severe.** <note>original: "crescentibus delictis exasperandæ sint pœnæ"</note> For since even today there is such a crowd of thieves that no crosses, no gallows, and no tortures suffice to restrain them: how great a multitude of them would there be if that ancient monetary penalty <note>In early Roman law, theft was often treated as a civil matter requiring the payment of double or quadruple the value of the stolen item, rather than a capital crime.</note> were brought back into use? Indeed, this [capital punishment] was used among the Scythians, <note>Citing Justin, Book 2.</note> the Athenians, <note>Citing Plutarch's Life of Solon.</note> the Tartars, the Muscovites, the West Indians, <note>Citing Jean Bodin's "Six Books of the Commonwealth."</note> and even the Romans. <note>Citing Paolo Manuzio on Roman Laws.</note>

3. A debtor who steals back a pledge <term>pignus | a physical object given as security for a debt</term> from his creditor is not excused, even if he offers to pay what he owes. *Johannes Vaudus, 1. various questions 35.* For a penal action <note>A legal action intended to punish the wrongdoer.</note> has nothing in common with an action for the recovery of property <note>A legal action intended simply to get the stolen item back.</note>, and the latter does not cancel the former. *Law on common property, Digest, on the partnership.* Hence, if a thief wishes to return the stolen item—and even if he has already returned it—he is no more released from the penal action; rather, once the thing is returned, he remains just as liable for the theft as he was before the return. *Law "it does not profit," Digest, on property taken by force; Law "because it remains," Digest, on the corruption of a slave.* Nor can anyone cease to be guilty through mere repentance. *Law "who is a fugitive," at the beginning, on the edict of the aediles; Law 1, section "if a deposited thing," Digest, on deposits.*

<margin>On Property Taken by Force <note>original: "Vi Bono Rapt." referring to Book 47, Title 8 of the Digest.</note></margin>
4. Mynsinger <note>Joachim Mynsinger von Frundeck (1514–1588), a prominent German jurist.</note>, at *Section 1 of the Institutes on property taken by force*, contends that a robber—even if he is a "manifest" thief <note>original: "manifestus fur." In Roman law, a thief caught in the act or with the goods.</note> (*Law 80, section "when a robber," Digest, on property taken by force*)—is not held to the specific penalty for manifest theft. Zasius and the other Doctors of Law <note>original: "Dd.," a common abbreviation for "Doctores," the established legal authorities.</note> prove this at the *section on wrongs, Institutes, on actions*. However, Cujas <note>Jacques Cujas (1522–1590), a French legal humanist who revolutionized the study of Roman Law.</note> says (*6. Observations 2*) that although this praetorian edict <note>An edict issued by the Roman Praetor, the official responsible for the administration of justice.</note> does not distinguish between manifest and non-manifest robbery regarding the penalty proposed by the edict itself, yet with respect to the double and fourfold penalties <note>original: "dupli & quadrupli." Roman law often required thieves to pay back twice or four times the value of the stolen object.</note> which are otherwise unique to theft, there is indeed a difference between manifest and non-manifest robbery. Thus, a non-manifest robber is punished with a double penalty, while a manifest one is punished with a fourfold penalty. *Law 1, Digest, on property taken by force; Law 80, section "when a robber," Digest, on theft.* And indeed, it would be absurd if the condition of a robber (who is a more wicked kind of thief) were better than that of a simple thief. *See Arumæus, said disputation 24, thesis 9.*

<margin>On Injuries.</margin>
5. There is no doubt that one condemned for injuries is marked with infamy <term>infamia | a loss of legal and social standing, which restricted a person's ability to testify or hold office</term>. *Law 1, Digest, on those marked with infamy.* However, the Doctors of Law fight among themselves as to whether infamy follows one condemned for a slight or moderate injury. Julius Clarus affirms it (*Book 5, Opinions, section "injury," number 8*), as do Angelus (*on the law "infamous," Digest, on public judgments*), Soarez (*in the treasury of received opinions, word "injuries," number 79*), and Andreas Gaill (*2. Observations 102, number 1*). On the other hand, Prospero Farinacci (*Book 3, Criminal Practice, question 105, inspection 2, number 66*), Giovanni Battista Baiardi (*in additions to Julius Clarus, said section "injury," number 51*), and our Teacher, Doctor Harpprecht (*at section 10, Institutes, on Injuries*) deny it. And this latter opinion is confirmed by the *Law "but if of one," section "certain things," 3, Digest, on injuries* and the *chapter "since you," outside of the decretals on sentences and res judicata*. For equity does not allow a punishment more severe than the crime to be imposed on a defendant.

6. An **action for injuries** <term>actio injuriarum | a legal suit for personal insult, defamation, or physical assault</term> is extinguished by the passage of one year: *Code, on injuries, law 5.* That this is only true for a praetorian action <note>An action based on the authority of the Praetor's edict</note> and not for an action under the **Cornelian Law** <term>lex Cornelia | a specific Roman statute (c. 81 BCE) dealing with severe cases of injury such as hitting or home invasion</term> is as true as anything can be. *At the beginning of the Institutes, on perpetual and temporary actions; Julius Clarus, book 5, opinions, section "injury," number 9, and there Bajardus, number 51,* despite whatever the most learned Busius argues to the contrary in *Digest, on injuries, law 5 (Cornelian Law).* Actions descending from statutes are perpetual. Why then should the Cornelian action for injuries not be so? What stands in the way? What is the reason for the difference? The judgments of the **Imperial Chamber Court** <note>The highest judicial body of the Holy Roman Empire</note> also prove our opinion. *Mynsinger, Century 1, observation 84.*

7. Is this Cornelian action for injuries, which descends from the law, a **public prosecution**? I say no, along with my teacher Lord Harpprecht in *Institutes, on injuries, section 7 on punishment, number 11 and following, through the express text in Digest, on procurators, law 42, section 1.* Cujas (*Book 5 of Paul's opinions, title 4, section "mixed law"*) and Borcholt (*Institutes, on injuries, section "but also the Cornelian law"*) affirm that it is.

8. The **truth of a taunt** <note>original: "Convicii veritas"</note> renders the person who taunted immune from condemnation: *Digest, on injuries, law 18.* But our Doctors of Law limit this as follows: only if it is in the interest of the Republic for that accused crime to be uncovered; such as if treason, murder, or similar crimes are charged against someone. *Julius Clarus, book 5, opinions, section "injury," number 15; P. Busius on the said Law 18 of the Digest, on injuries.* Truly, Covarruvias (*Book 1, various resolutions, chapter 1, number 6*) teaches that no injury is excused unless the crime was charged with the intent that it be made manifest, and not for the sake of insult; thus, all **lust for injuring** <note>original: "libido injuriandi"</note> must be absent. I believe this opinion of Covarruvias to be most true. For the laws punish the itch and the lust for taunting: and yet this lust is present even in one who charges a crime that is beneficial to uncover. Nor do I allow it to be argued back to me that the utility which redounds to the Republic from this injury exempts the injurer from punishment. For that would be the same as if I were to argue: **Judas** is not worthy of eternal torments, since through his betrayal the redemption of the human race was promoted. Absurd! A conclusion is not valid when drawn from accidental causes.

9. A person suing for injuries cannot be met with the objection that the action was destroyed by **Retorsion** <term>retorsio | the act of "returning" an insult or injury in kind</term> if that person recognizes Retorsion as illegitimate. *See Lord Harpprecht, Institutes, on injuries, section 12, number 78 and many following.* For everyone sees these things are contradictory: that Retorsion is null, and yet it destroys an action.

11. **A judgment for swindling** <term>stellionatus | a catch-all category of fraud in Roman law, named after the 'stellio' or spotted lizard, implying a shifty, deceptive character</term> **is not considered "infamous."** <note>original: "famosum" – a legal status where a person loses their good reputation and certain civil rights after a conviction.</note> *Law 2 here.* Law 13 (*Digest, on those who are marked with infamy*) seems to contradict this. I distinguish between a case where the crime of swindling arises from a "famous" cause and one that does not; following *Duaren, book 1 of disputations, chapter 43; Cujas, 1st Response of Papinian, page 15, and 10th Observations, number 26; Wesenbeck in the paratitla <note>A summary of legal titles</note> on this section; Hilliger, chapter 18, chapter 17, letter R; and most recently Bachov at the Disputations of Treutler, 8, thesis 6, at the end.* Lord Bocer considers this matter differently in his *Unique Dissertation joined to the treatise on the crime of Majesty; Antonius Faber, 4th Conjectures, 2;* and others who are cited *above in the disputation on books 3 and 4 of the Digest, thesis 5.*

<margin>Moving boundary markers.</margin>
12. **Those who move boundary markers** <term>terminorum motores | people who illegally shift the stones or markers defining property lines</term> **are not punished by fine.** *Law 1 of this title.* Law 3 at the beginning of the same title seems to conflict. However, in that instance, the "extraordinary action" regarding a moved boundary was not being attempted, but rather a different "public action" based on the Law of Gaius Caesar. *Paci, Century 7, "Apparent Contradictions," question 59.*

<margin>Book 48 of the Digest, title 1: On Public Trials.</margin>
13. **Which crimes and trials are public, and which are private,** is not easy to explain given the great diversity of opinions. **Public trials,** says *Julius Clarus, book 5, Opinions, section 1, at the beginning,* are those where the right to accuse is granted to anyone from the general public. *Bajardus, in his additions, number 1,* criticizes Clarus there. He argues the logic does not hold: just because "anyone from the public can accuse someone of such a crime," it does not automatically follow that it is "public." For example, anyone from the public can accuse a "suspected guardian" <note>A tutor or guardian suspected of mismanagement</note>, yet that trial is not a public one. Therefore, following *Law 1 of the Digest on public trials,* I define the matter with *Arumaeus, disputation 25, thesis 1,* and others: **A public crime** is one that is included under the laws of public trials and can be reported and prosecuted by any member of the public.

As far as I know, it is not explicitly stated by name in any law, yet I do not doubt that it is possible. My argument is this: If a woman can accuse someone for the death of her relatives, she is much more able to bring the murder of her husband to court. This is an obvious consequence! <margin>On the Custody and Presentation of Defendants.</margin>
17. Under **Civil Law**, the punishment of life imprisonment cannot be inflicted on a free man as a penalty for a crime. *Law "mandatis," on punishments.* **Ulpian** <note>One of the most influential Roman jurists, active in the early 3rd century AD.</note> provides the reason in *Law "aut damnum," section "solent," Digest, on the same topic:* that prison should be used for the detention of men, not for their punishment. It is, however, different under **Canon Law** <note>The body of laws and regulations made by ecclesiastical authority (the Church).</note>. *Chapter "ut commissi," at the verse "and of those," on heretics, in the 6th book of the Decretals; and chapter "quamvis," on punishments, in the same. Andreas Gail, 2nd Observations, 26, number 7; and book 1, on public peace, chapter 1, number 23. Master Schwarzenthal, book 3, on procedure, chapter 13. Damhouder, in the practice of criminal matters, chapter 16, number 3 and following.*

18. Those who break out of prison, even if they are eventually found innocent of the original charge, are to be punished. *Law "in eos" 13, under this title; Law 1, Digest, on prison-breakers.* However, if a person is unjustly captured and detained in prison, and cannot otherwise escape an unjust condemnation which—led by probable conjectures—he fears is imminent (perhaps due to false witnesses, or from the wicked and corrupt mind of a judge), he may deceive the guards with trickery and stratagem without any stain of sin. Even if damage threatens the guards because of his flight, he may take the opportunity to either break the prison or snap his chains and thus escape. This is discussed at length by *Covarruvias, 1st Various Resolutions, 2, number 7; Decianus, book 2, criminal practice, chapter 13, number 4.*

<margin>On the Julian Law of Majesty [Treason].</margin>
19. The Canonists proclaim with full cheeks <note>original: "plenis declamitant buccis" – a Latin idiom meaning to speak loudly, boastfully, or with great emphasis.</note> that the **Pope** is the head of the heavenly and earthly kingdoms. *Alexander Carerius, On the Power of the Pontiff, book 2, chapter 3, number 5.* Even the spirits of the air, the earth, and the underworld—says **Lipsius** <note>Justus Lipsius (1547–1606), a famous Flemish humanist.</note> in his *Our Lady of Halle*—revere and tremble, and acknowledge that heavenly power on earth even if they do so unwillingly and reluctantly. From this, they even assert that the **crime of Treason** <term>Majestatis | the crime of "lese-majesty" or treason against a sovereign power</term> can be committed against the Pope by our own **Most Sacred Emperor**. *Gygas, treatise on the crime of Majesty, book 1, rubric: how and by whom the crime of injured Majesty is committed, question 2; Tiberius Decianus, criminal treatise, book 7, chapter 8, number 1.* Against these views, my Master **Bocer** rightly judges. *Treatise on the crime of Majesty, chapter 1, number 30 and following.* Indeed, if the Pope were a fit witness in his own case, the matter would be entirely free of all doubt.

# Disputation on the last 4 books of the Digest.

...that the Emperor could incur the crime of Majesty <term>crimen laesae maiestatis | high treason; literally "injured majesty," the highest crime against a sovereign or state</term> against the Pope. For the Pope could construct this syllogism, which he would surely call "Achillean" <note>original: "Achilleum"; an allusion to Achilles, meaning an argument considered unassailable or the strongest possible point</note> and invincible: Every subject who rises up against his Lord and Prince is guilty of the crime of Majesty. That is most true! But the Emperor is a subject of the Pope. This too is "most certain," at least according to the opinion of our Lord the Pope. *Chapter 1, title on custom, in the Extravagantes.* <note>The "Extravagantes" were collections of canon law that circulated outside the primary Decree of Gratian</note> But I am both ashamed and annoyed to respond to the impudent assertion of this minor premise: I say only this, that by this very act the Pope most evidently betrays his Antichristian nature. For Christ fled from those who wished to create him King; the Pontiff himself, unasked, impudently arrogates dominion over everyone to himself. All books are full, history is full, even all the monuments of the Papists themselves are full of the frauds and wicked arts by which these Roman Prelates withdrew themselves from obedience to the Emperors and gained dominion over them. To review these here would be entirely contrary to the brevity of our current purpose; this has already been done by *Simon Schardius in his unique treatise, and by Mornay in "The Mystery of Iniquity."* Surely that spirited response of Philip, King of the French, to Pope Boniface <note>Refers to Philip IV ("the Fair") and his famous conflict with Pope Boniface VIII around 1300</note> will please all sensible men: "Let your Greatest Foolishness know that in temporal matters we are subject to no one." *François Hotman, Book of Feudal Disputations, chapter 9.*

20. Jerome Gygas, in *The Crime of Majesty, book 3, rubric on rebels, question 21*, and Tiberius Decianus, in *Treatise on Crimes, book 7, chapter 5, number 19*, both assert without distinction that a federated people <note>allies or states joined by a treaty</note> can be found guilty of the crime of Majesty if they plot anything against the Emperor. There are those who distinguish between an equal treaty and one in which one party is bound to respect the Majesty of the other. *Law 7, section 1, Digest, on captives and the restoration of rights.* I maintain that no treaty (properly so called) proves a person is a subject, *following Law 7, section 1, on captives*, and therefore I assert that the crime of majesty cannot be committed by a federated ally. *Clementine Constitutions, "Pastoralis," section "rursus," on sentences and res judicata. See also Bodin, On the Republic, book 1, chapter 6.*

21. An elegant question of great importance is whether one who hatches plots against a Duke, Count, etc., who is immediately subject to the Empire, or creates a danger to his life, falls under the crime of Majesty? This is affirmed by *Julius Clarus, "Sentences," section on the crime of Majesty, number 3; Laudensis in his treatise on the crime of Majesty, question 1; and Gygas, rubric on the quality and from whom, etc., question 29.* However, it is denied by *Marcantonio Peregrini, "On the Rights of the Fisc," book 4, chapter 5, number 24; Wesenbeck in his commentary on the Digest at this title, number 7; and Fachin, "Controversies," book 9, chapter 32.* This latter opinion is confirmed by some based on political principles. There are three types of <term>fatalia: legal deadlines (literally 'fated times') after which a right of action is lost</term> for an appeal: for interposing, for introducing, and for pursuing it. Today, the time for interposing an appeal is ten days. *Novel 23, chapter 1.* For introducing it, the time is sometimes two months, but never three or six. *Laws 1 and 2 of the Code on the time of appeals; and there Cujas in the summaries.* In the Imperial Chamber Court <note>The highest judicial body of the Holy Roman Empire</note>, it is regularly six months. *Ordinance of the Imperial Chamber Court, part 2, title 30, section "it would be, however."* Often, however, the judge from whom the appeal is made is accustomed to set the time for the appellant to introduce the challenge. <margin>Whether through another, etc.</margin>
§ 57. A <term>procurator: a legal representative or agent</term> appointed for a single case who is defeated is indeed compelled to appeal from a definitive judgment <note>A final ruling that decides the main issue of the case</note>: *Law "unwilling" of the Code on procurators; canon "not unjustly" 14 of the Decretals on procurators;* however, he is not bound to pursue the appeal. *Law "if the plaintiff" of the Code on this subject; the said canon "not unjustly" of the Decretals; the Lord Presiding Officer, chapter 5, section 8; Suarez, Treasury of Received Opinions, letter P, number 301.* For the cause of the appeal is distinct from the main case, and no one should be forced against their will to manage a particular business. *Busius dissents on law "to the legate" 62 of the Digest on procurators.* But a procurator is required to pursue a challenge from an interlocutory ruling <note>A temporary or intermediate ruling made before the final decision is reached</note>, since he is understood to have received a mandate for all matters arising from the case. *Gail, book 1, observation 139, number 11.*

§ 58. However, our text here (because it is part of the chapter on the law of the treasury) must be understood in terms of political and civil equity. <margin>On Unilateral Promises <note>original: "De Pollicitat."</note></margin>
67. A <term>Pollicitatio: a unilateral promise</term> is a promise made by the offeror alone (Law 3, under this title). It is twofold: of the "law of nations" and of civil law. That of the <term>Jus gentium: law of nations</term> is one which, lacking confirmation by the law, produces no civil obligation (Section "on a bare promise," title 12, regarding the rights of the treasury; Book 5 of the *Sentences of Paul*). A "legitimate" or "civil" promise is said to be that which is confirmed by some law and produces an effective obligation, and consequently also a legal action (argued from Law 6 of the Digest, regarding pacts). This is either a vow or a specific promise. A <term>Votum: a vow</term> is a promise made to God (Law 2, section 1, under this title). A "specific promise" is one made to someone other than God, such as to a <term>Nosocomium: a hospital</term>, a <term>Xenodochium: a hostel for travelers</term>, or a <term>Ptochium: an almshouse</term> (Law 15 of the Code, regarding the Most Holy Churches; also Laws 1, 7, 8, etc., under this title). The legal action that applies to such a promise is a <term>Condictio ex lege: a formal claim based on a specific statute</term> (Single law of the Digest, regarding claims from the law; *Lord Bocer, class 2, disputation 1, thesis 40 and following*).

68. Furthermore, an absent person is not bound by a promise. *Bronchorst, Miscellaneous Assertions 2, number 79; Hilliger's commentary on Donellus, book 12, chapter 5, letter B.*
