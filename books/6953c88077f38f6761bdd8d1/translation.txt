Eliphas Levi Zahed is a pseudonym <note>A pen name</note> which was adopted in his occult writings by Alphonse Louis Constant; it is said to be the Hebrew equivalent of that name. The author of *The Doctrine and Ritual of High Magic* <note>original: "Dogme et Rituel de la Haute Magie"</note> was born into humble circumstances about the year 1810, as the son of a shoemaker. Giving evidence of unusual intelligence at an early age, his parish priest took a kindly interest in the obscure boy and secured him a place at the seminary of Saint Sulpice. There, he was educated free of charge with the intention of him entering the priesthood. He seems to have completed his studies at that seminary in a manner that met the expectations held for him. In addition to Greek and Latin, he is believed to have acquired a considerable knowledge of Hebrew, though it would be a mistake to assume that any of his published works demonstrate exceptional linguistic mastery. 

He entered his clerical novitiate <note>A period of training and preparation before taking vows</note>, took minor orders, and in due course became a deacon, which bound him by a vow of perpetual celibacy. Shortly after taking this step, he was suddenly expelled from Saint Sulpice for holding opinions contrary to the teachings of the Roman Catholic Church. The existing accounts of this expulsion are vague and include unlikely details—for example, that he was sent by his church superiors to perform duties in rural areas where he preached with great eloquence, yet remained doctrinally unsound. However, I believe there is no precedent for deacons preaching in the Latin Church <note>The Roman Catholic Church</note>. Until the biography that has been promised in France for some years finally appears, we have few available materials for a life of the "Abbé" <note>A title for a French priest</note> Constant. The path to the priesthood was closed to him—and what he did, or how he managed to support himself, remains unknown. By the year 1839, he had formed several literary friendships, including one with Alphonse Esquiros, the now-forgotten author of a fantastic romance titled *The Magician*. <margin>Alphonse Esquiros was a French writer and politician who shared Constant's early interest in radicalism and the occult.</margin> Esquiros introduced him to Ganneau, a frantic prophet of that era who had adopted women’s clothing and lived in a garret. There, Ganneau preached a type of political "illuminism" <note>original: "illuminism"; a movement claiming special enlightenment on religious or political matters</note> which was apparently concerned with the restoration of "the true legitimacy." In fact, Ganneau claimed to be a second incarnation of Louis XVII—"returned to earth to fulfill a work of regeneration." <note>Louis XVII was the son of Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette; he died in prison during the French Revolution, but many pretenders later claimed to be him.</note>

Constant and Esquiros, who had originally visited Ganneau to mock him, were instead swept away by his eloquence and became his disciples. Some element of socialism must have merged with the visionary's illuminism, and this seems to have influenced Constant’s thinking. It eventually took the form of a book or pamphlet titled *The Gospel of Liberty*. A fleeting importance was attached to this work—quite foolishly—when the authorities imprisoned the author for a term of six months. There is some reason to believe that Esquiros assisted in the writing and also shared in the legal penalty.

*The Doctrine of High Magic* was followed in 1856 by its companion volume, *The Ritual* <note>original: "Rituel"</note>; both are translated here into English for the first time. These were followed in rapid succession by *The History of Magic* <note>original: "Histoire de la Magie"</note> (1860); *The Key to the Great Mysteries* <note>original: "La Clef des Grands Mystères"</note> (1861); a second edition of the *Doctrine and Ritual* (1862), to which a long and irrelevant introduction was unfortunately added; *Fables and Symbols* <note>original: "Fables et Symboles"</note> (1864); *The Sorcerer of Meudon* <note>original: "Le Sorcier de Meudon"</note>, a beautiful pastoral story marked with a Kabbalistic stamp <note>original: "cachet cabalistique"</note>; and *The Science of Spirits* <note>original: "La Science des Esprits"</note> (1865). The two last works incorporate the substance of the pamphlets he published in 1846 and 1847.

The unstable financial situation of Constant’s younger days was, in one sense, only slightly improved in his old age. His books did not achieve a large circulation, but they secured him admirers and pupils, from whom he received payment.

In return for personal or written courses of instruction, he was commonly to be found *at home* <note>original: "chez lui"</note> in a species of magical vestment. This may be pardoned in a French magus <note>a magician or practitioner of the Western mystery tradition</note>, and his only available portrait—placed at the beginning of this volume—represents him in that attire. He outlived the Franco-German War, and as he had exchanged Socialism for a sort of transcendentalized Imperialism, his political faith must have been as much tried by the events which followed the siege of Paris as was his patriotic enthusiasm by the military reverses which culminated at Sedan. His contradictory life closed in 1875 amidst the last offices of the church which had almost expelled him from her bosom. He left many manuscripts behind him, which are still in the process of being published. Additionally, innumerable letters to his pupils—Baron Spedalieri alone possesses nine volumes—have been happily preserved in most cases, and are in some respects more valuable than his formal treatises.

No modern interpreter of occult science can bear any comparison with Éliphas Lévi. Among ancient interpreters, though many stand higher in authority, all yield to him in living interest; he is essentially the spirit of modern thought forcing an answer for the present times from the old oracles. Hence, while there are greater names, there is no influence so great—no fascination in occult literature exceeds that of the French magus. Other authors are left to specialists and the typical serious students to whom all dull and unreadable masterpieces are dedicated, whether directly or not. However, Lévi is read and appreciated much as we read and appreciate new and delightful verse which, through some whim of the poet, is dressed in the style of Chaucer. 

Indeed, the writings of Éliphas Lévi stand, in relation to the grand old lineage of initiation, in relatively the same position as the *Earthly Paradise* of Mr. William Morris stands to the *Canterbury Tales*. There is the recurrence to the old concepts, and there is the adoption of the old style, but there is in each case a new spirit. INRI <note>original: "Inri"; the inscription on the Cross, used in alchemy to mean "By fire nature is renewed whole"</note> and the Tetragrammaton <note>original: "Tetragrammaton"; the four-letter Hebrew name of God, YHWH</note>, which are the symbolic garments of the occult philosopher, are like the "cloth of Bruges and barrels of Guienne wine, Florence gold cloth, and Ypres linens" mentioned by the poet <note>A reference to William Morris’s "The Earthly Paradise"</note>. In both cases, we are looking at the year 1850 and following, wearing a mask of high fantasy. Moreover, "the idle singer of an empty day" <note>A self-description by William Morris</note> is a fair parallel to "the poor and obscure scholar who has recovered the lever of Archimedes" <note>Lévi’s description of himself</note>. The comparison is intentionally strange, but it holds up nonetheless and even allows for further development. Just as Mr. Morris in a sense undermined the *reason for existence* <note>original: "raison d'être"</note> of his poetry—and, in direct contradiction to his own mournful question, has endeavored to "set the crooked straight" by turning to Socialism—so Éliphas Lévi surrendered the rod of miracles. He undermined his own Doctrine of Magic by creating a one-sided and insincere agreement with orthodox religion, eventually dying in the arms of "my venerable masters in theology." These were the descendants—and decaying ones at that—of the "idiotic theologians of the Middle Ages." But Lévi is, as Morris was, a man of enough talent to make a paradoxical defense of a position that remains impossible to hold.

Students of Éliphas Lévi will be familiar with the qualifications and secretive retractions by which the somewhat uncompromising stance of "initiated superiority" found in the *Doctrine and Ritual* had its true meaning stripped away by the magician's <note>original: "magus"</note> later works. I have dealt with this point thoroughly in another place,* and there is no need to cover the same ground a second time. Instead, I propose to point out as briefly as possible some new considerations that will help us understand why there were serious discrepancies between the *Doctrine and Ritual of Transcendent Magic* and the volumes that followed. In the first place, the earlier books were written more specifically from the perspective of an initiate, and in the specialized language of initiation. They obviously contain much that would be pure foolishness to interpret literally, and—

What Éliphas Lévi wrote at a later period is not so much a contradiction of his earlier instruction—though it is that as well—as it is the qualifications placed by a modern transcendentalist <note>A philosopher who believes in a spiritual reality beyond sensory experience</note> on the technical exaggerations of the secret sciences. 
<margin>44</margin> For proof, we need look no further than the introduction to *The Doctrine of Magic*, and to the Hebrew manuscript cited there regarding the powers and privileges of the magus <note>original: *magus*; a magician or practitioner of the occult</note>. In this context, a literal interpretation would be insanity; these claims conceal a secret meaning and are deceptive in their literal sense. They are what Éliphas Lévi himself calls "hyperbolic" <note>Exaggerated for effect</note>, adding: "If the wise man does not materially and actually perform these things, he accomplishes others which are much greater and more admirable" (p. 223). 

But this consideration is not enough on its own to account for the issues involved. It does not explain, for example, why Éliphas Lévi—who consistently teaches in the *Doctrine and Ritual* that the dogmas of so-called revealed religion are merely nursery tales for children—should later have insisted that they be accepted in the sense of the orthodox Church by mature men of science. It becomes necessary here to touch upon a matter which, by its very nature, clearly does not allow for a complete explanation.

The precise period of study that produced the *Doctrine and Ritual of Transcendent Magic* as its first literary result is not indicated with any certainty in the author's life. Furthermore, I do not regard Éliphas Lévi as being naturally capable of profound or extensive book study. While he is intensely suggestive, he simultaneously shows little evidence of depth. He is splendid at making generalizations but lacks accuracy in detail; it would be difficult to find a worse guide regarding matters of fact. His *History of Magic* is a perfect example: as a philosophical survey it is admirable, and there is nothing in occult literature to match its literary excellence, but it swarms with historical inaccuracies. It is in every respect a polished performance, but in no way a scholarly one. Nor do I think that the writer concerned himself much with any actual reading of the...

It does not, therefore, seem unreasonable to affirm that if Lévi had been left to himself, he would not have gotten far in occult science. This is because his French energy <note>original: Gallic vivacity</note> would have been dulled too quickly by the exhaustion of pure research. Therefore, this constitutes the importance of the *Doctrine and Ritual*: undoubtedly disguised, it is still the voice of initiation. It does not matter from which school it comes, for in this regard, nothing can be spoken plainly. I can only ask for the patience of my readers—deferring their judgment—based on my honorable assurance that I am not speaking without cause. Éliphas Lévi had only partly ascended the levels of that initiation when he published the *Doctrine and Ritual*, and its publication blocked his further progress. Just as he was expelled by Saint Sulpice <note>A Catholic seminary in Paris where Lévi studied for the priesthood</note> for exercising his own judgment in matters of religious belief, he was also expelled by his occult leaders for being too indiscreet in revealing secret mysteries.
