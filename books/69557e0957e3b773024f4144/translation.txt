Having set aside the pseudonymous writer on <term>square or measured music</term> <note>original: "musica quadrata seu mensurata"; this refers to early notation where the shape of the note defined its duration</note> found among the works of the Venerable Bede (Volume 1, page 372), we rightly place Master FRANCO of Paris at the forefront of this final volume of ancient writers on song and music. He is the true pioneer of our modern music, which we call <term>figured music</term> <note>original: "musica figurata"; music using complex rhythms and distinct note shapes</note>. Whether Johannes Spadarius, in the defense of his teacher Bartolomé Ramos, names this man or another Franco as a German from Cologne, I do not know, as I do not have a copy of his book. Gesner, in his *Library*, makes him English, while Bontempi claims he was Italian. Father Giovanni Battista Martini, in the first volume of his *History of Music* (page 169), judges that our Franco likely wrote in the 11th century. Certainly, John of Salisbury, who flourished toward the end of the 12th century, complains in the sixth chapter of the first book of his *Polycraticus* about the "cutting" of notes and phrases in the music of his time. Such divisions are exactly what we find in the <term>measurable music</term> <note>original: "musica mensurabilis"</note> of Franco. This style might be called a fragment of the most ancient rhythmic music, but one that is greatly varied. That older style confined itself to the skillful division of long and short syllables—a matter well-known and common to everyone. Indeed, as Quintilian testifies in the fourth chapter of the ninth book of his *Institutes*, even schoolboys know that a long syllable lasts two beats and a short one lasts one.

FRANCO <note>Franco of Cologne, a 13th-century music theorist</note> is indeed more sparing in his division of musical time, as is MARCHETUS *of Padua*. In addition to Marchetus's *Lucidarium of Plain Music*, we also publish in this volume his *Orchard of Measured Music*, in which he frequently brings our aforementioned FRANCO onto the stage. These follow the *Science of the Art of Music* by ELIAS SALOMON, from which we have reproduced an image at the beginning of this volume—a figure of four voices singing (just as he explains the matter himself on page 59), with the *table of Master Nicholas of Lucca* <note>original: "Nicolai de Luduno"</note> attached. In this table, one may perceive a type of tailed notes <note>notes with stems</note> that goes further than what we noted in the work *On Song and Music* (Volume II, page 124) regarding JOHN *de Muris*. This John is counted among the most learned masters of mathematical science by BULÆUS in the *History of the University of Paris* (Volume IV, page 963), along with JOHN *de Ligneriis*, with whom he is said to have been very intimate. I remember seeing certain manuscript works of this kind by him in the Imperial Library at *Vienna*.

However, the esteemed BULÆUS seems to identify this JOHN as the same person as JULIAN *de Muris*, whom he later notes (in the same place, page 937) was elected Rector of the University in the month of *December*, 1350. We, however, find him consistently called JOHN in his various works or the digests of his works that we are publishing—excepting his *Mirror of Music* <note>Speculum musicae</note>. That work, preserved at *Paris*, must be his most complete and grandest labor; J. J. ROUSSEAU boasts in his *Dictionary of Music* (page 318) that he read almost the whole of it: *I had the courage* <note>original: "J'ais... eu le courage"</note> *to read it almost entirely, in order to verify the invention attributed to this author.* Rousseau is mistaken, however, when he says that the notes of figured music <note>polyphonic notation</note> received their final addition then, around the year 1330, as already...

we have observed concerning our **JOHN** <note>Johannes de Muris, a prominent 14th-century music theorist</note>. **IMBERT** *of France* follows him precisely in his rules on measurable music <note>original: "menſurabili muſica," referring to rhythmic notation</note>, proceeding no further than what I have already noted in the previously cited passage regarding **JOHN** *de Muris*, of whom **IMBERT** was a scribe and abridger; for this reason, he has been omitted by us here.

Nevertheless, by the **14th** century, music possessed so many and such great vices, which **JOHN XXII** sharply criticized in the famous decree *Learned Ignorance* <note>original: "docta ignorantia," a papal decree from 1324/25 aimed at curbing complex polyphony in church</note> (Book III, Title I, *On the life and integrity of the Clergy*, single chapter). Nor, indeed, was it used indiscriminately in that same **14th** century, even in royal and Imperial chapels. What **VOLCMAR** relates in **OEFELIUS’S** *Writers of Bavarian Affairs* (Vol. II, p. 539) concerning **WENCESLAUS**, King of *Bohemia* and son of **OTTOCAR**,—that he was accustomed to hearing three masses daily, which he commanded to be solemnly sung before him "tonally"—can be understood as referring to choral chant <note>original: "cantu chorali," or plainsong</note>. It is a bit more, however, which is read in **JOHN** *of Winterthur* regarding Emperor **HENRY VII**: that he daily had the mass sung before him musically, or *chorally*, "tonally with notation."

In *van de* **HARDT’S** *Acts of the Council of Constance* (Vol. VI, p. 1160), among the petitions of the English theologian Richard **ULLERSTON** regarding the reformation of the Church Militant, Article **XII** is directed against the prerogative of singers and officials residing in the courts of lords. # P R E F A C E.

*...they are more devout, yet by constantly insisting upon singing, they do not experience as much devotion in the loud shouting as they do in moderate silence; thus, they first recite the canonical hours without musical notation. They would not do this if more devotion were regularly increased within them through singing. And below: we have said these things regarding singers for this reason, because we see, as if with the evidence of our own eyes, that the higher prelates and even secular lords seem to have satisfied themselves and others in all respects if each of them possesses a "globe" <note>original: "globum"; likely referring to a rounded group or choir</note> of elite singers who make a noise for the sake of the world: for whom all the benefices they are able to procure are not sufficient.*

---

Next, we publish the *Introduction to Music* by **Johannes KECK**, a monk of *Tegernsee* <note>A famous Benedictine abbey in Bavaria</note> of the Order of Saint Benedict in *Bavaria*, who is also celebrated for his other writings. This work, composed around those very times—namely the middle of the 15th century—was written specifically for plainchant <term>plainchant: a body of chants used in the liturgies of the Western Church, traditionally unaccompanied</term>. In the manuscript, only a drawing of a pneumatic organ <note>An organ powered by air from bellows</note> is provided as a preface; this can already be seen in the work on song and music, where one may marvel at the simplicity of this instrument and its musical scale of pipes, which did not exceed twenty-one even well into the 15th century. At that time, **JOHANNES TINCTORIUS**, in his *Proportions of Music* (cited in Father **MARTINI**, loc. cit. p. 169), says that in his day the faculty of our music had received such an admirable increase that it seems to be a *new art* <note>original: "ars nova"; referring to the development of polyphony and new rhythmic notation</note>. What the state of music was at the end of the 15th century is shown by the *Musical Instruction* of **ADAM** of *Fulda*, which we are publishing, and finally by the *Constitutions of the Pontifical Chapel*, in which figured music <term>figured music: polyphonic music with complex rhythms, as opposed to plainchant</term> certainly has a place, while any musical instruments, and even organs, are banished.

It highlights the technological simplicity of the organ during this period and the emergence of the "Ars Nova" or new art of music.</summary>
