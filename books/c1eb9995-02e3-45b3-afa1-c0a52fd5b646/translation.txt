### Chapter One.
### Of the Point.

<margin>1</margin>
The point resembles unity <note>the number one</note> in Arithmetic. For just as Unity is not a number, but is the beginning and principle of all numbers: so too the point is the beginning of all measurement, and of all bodily dimension, having in itself neither length, nor width, nor depth.

### Of the Line.

<margin>2</margin>
A line is similar and proportional to the number two. For at the very least two points are necessary to produce and draw a line from one to the other. As it appears by the line, AB. The line holds a single dimension: for it is only long, without width, and without depth.

### Of the Plane, otherwise called the Surface.

<margin>3</margin>
A plane, otherwise called a Surface, resembles by just proportion the number three: for at the least three points are necessary to enclose and shut a plane. For even the smallest field of land, whatever it may be, requires three boundaries to close it: as it appears in the triangle A B C. The plane is long and wide, without depth. When one measures a field of land, one looks only at the length and width of the said field, without considering any depth. For as one says in Latin: *Cuius est solum, huius est caelum* <note>original: "Cuius est solum, huius est cælum" — "Whoever owns the soil, theirs is the sky"</note>.

### On the three dimensions & measurements.

<margin>5</margin>In the likeness and imitation of the most high and most holy divine Trinity, there are in all the science of mathematics only three measurements and physical dimensions: length, width, and depth. The **point** is entirely exempt from these three dimensions; the **line** is only long; the **plane** is long and wide; and the **solid**, as the most perfect of all, is long, wide, and deep.

### On the kinds of lines.

<margin>7</margin>The **line** has two kinds, for there is the straight line and the curved line <note>original: "ligne oblique"</note>. The **straight line** is produced from one point to another with the help of a wooden or brass ruler <note>original: "reiglet"</note>, such as line A B. For without a physical instrument to steady the hand, it could only be produced with great difficulty. The **curved line** is produced by means of the compass, by which the hand gains the steadiness to make the turn, such as the curved line C D E. The ruler and the compass are the two most necessary instruments of Geometry, without which no Geometer could make, invent, or prove any great thing. The ruler serves for all straight lines and angular figures; the compass serves the circle and all circular and spherical figures.

**52. To describe a regular hexagon equal in area to two, three, or several others.**

Find the potential line, or line of power <note>original: "ligne potentielle ou de puissance"; this refers to a line whose square is equal to the sum of the squares of the sides of the given hexagons, based on the Pythagorean theorem.</note> relative to the sides of all the given hexagons, and on this line construct the desired hexagon. **53. To describe a hexagon that has a specific proportion to another.**

Let the given hexagon be **A**, and let the required proportion be as line **B** is to line **C**. Find the mean proportional between **B** and **C**, and call it **D**. Then, just as **B** is to **D**, set the side of hexagon **A** to a fourth term, which will be the side of the sought hexagon.

**54. To describe a hexagon equal in area to a given circle.**

First, find a square equal to the circle through quadrature <note>Squaring the circle: the classical geometric challenge of constructing a square with the same area as a circle.</note>. From this square, find the side of a hexagon equal to it (following the 46th proposition of the first book), which will be the side of the desired hexagon.

**55. The area of a regular hexagon is equal to a rectangle formed by half of its circumference and the perpendicular line drawn from the center to one of its sides.**

This is a property common to all regular figures. It was demonstrated for the hexagon in the first chapter, and it is proven the same way for all others by dividing them into triangles of the same height as the perpendicular, where the sum of their bases equals the circumference of the figure.

**56. To measure the area of a regular hexagon.**

Multiply half the circumference by the perpendicular, or the entire circumference by half of the perpendicular; the product will be the area of the hexagon. For example, if the side is 14, the circumference will be 84, and half will be 42. If this is multiplied by the perpendicular (which is approximately 12 1/8), the product gives the area, which is 509 1/4. 

One can also measure it by dividing it into two trapezoids along the diameter, or into six equal triangles.

**57. To find the proportion of a hexagon to the square of its side.**

Since the side is to double the perpendicular as 1 is to 1.732 (expressed as a surd number $\sqrt{3}$) <note>A "surd number" is an irrational number, typically a square root.</note>, and the area is the product of half the circumference (which is three sides) by the perpendicular; it follows that the area of the hexagon is to the square of its side as 2.598 is to 1. This is because three times half of 1.732 (which is 0.866) equals 2.598.

In whole numbers, the area of the hexagon is to the square of its side as 1039 is to 400, or approximately 26 to 10.

### ON THE SQUARING OF THE CIRCLE.

### Fourth Chapter.

Many in times past have spoken of the squaring of the circle <term>squaring of the circle: the ancient mathematical challenge of constructing a square with the exact same area as a given circle</term>, and have taken great pains to find it: which they have not done. Archimedes of Syracuse and Euclid of Megara <note>original: "Euclides Megarensis." Note: Renaissance writers often confused the mathematician Euclid of Alexandria with the philosopher Euclid of Megara</note> spent time on it and profited little. Aristotle wrote about it, saying it could be found, and was not yet found: by which he incited many to do so. But they did not know how to find or invent it. A geometer named Bradwardine <note>Thomas Bradwardine (c. 1300–1349), an English mathematician and Archbishop</note> made a little treatise on it, believing he had invented it well. But there is a great fault and visible abuse in his proposition. So much so that by his quadrature, it would be necessary for the arc to be equal to its chord: which is impossible. For everyone knows that the arc is longer than its chord, however small it may be. 

A little before our time, the most Reverend Cardinal named Nicholas of Cusa <note>a 15th-century German philosopher and mathematician</note> found it well and put it in writing in his book, although to do this he used and proceeded by certain means strange to geometers. For he used infinite dimensions, which a geometer does not know and would never confess to be possible. Nonetheless, his invention is good and approved, as much by reason as by experience. Likewise, we have taken pains to find it by another means, and have not been frustrated in our labor. For while we were once on the little bridge <note>the Petit Pont</note> of Paris, watching the wheels of a chariot turning on the pavement: a visible and easy opportunity came to me to achieve my intention. It is well known that when a wheel has made one full turn on the flat pavement, the straight line on which it has made a full turn is equal to the circumference of the said wheel. Therefore, nothing more remained but to find the certain points of contact of the quadrants of the wheel, and of the half, and of the entire wheel on the pavement: so that by this means one could find a straight line equal to the parts of the circumference, and also to the whole circumference. Without which means, the squaring of the circle could not be found. When I returned home, with the help of the compass and the ruler, I found on a bronze table what I was easily searching for: as we will declare hereafter at greater length.

The second movement of the Planets is their proper and special movement, each in its own heaven turning against the first from West to East, and in various times. As the Moon, in 28 days: the Sun, in one year: and the others, according to the definition of Astrology, almost in one year, or in 28, or in 30 years.

The third movement of the said Planets (except the Sun) is by their epicycle <term>epicycle: in pre-Copernican astronomy, a small circle whose center moves along the circumference of a larger circle (the deferent), used to explain the complex, sometimes backward-looking paths of planets</term>, within which they have a singular movement from East to West: and this not around the earth, but in the thickness of their own heaven, containing within itself the orb of the epicycle, within which the orb of the Planet moves eccentrically to the earth.

The Sun alone is exempt from such movement. For it has no epicycle. And it moves more simply than the others in its simple heaven.
