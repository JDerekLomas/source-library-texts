# Interpretation of the Gospel

**T**he Gospel is a teaching of the good works of love and mercy, in which the Lord faithfully warns all those who are His disciples and wish to enter heaven against the evil works of unrighteousness. He teaches the good works of righteousness, wherein is briefly contained what is fitting for a Christian: namely, that he be beneficent, loving, and merciful toward everyone—not only toward friends, but also toward enemies—yet especially toward fellow believers. As the Lord also admonishes before the beginning of the Gospel, we should love our enemies, do good to those who hate us, bless those who curse us, and pray for those who insult us, etc. All of this is concluded in the maxim: "Be merciful, just as your Father is merciful," who is kind to the good and the evil.

# Exposition of the Gospel
<meta>catchword: Seems-</meta>

<margin>A "speck" <note>original: "spilter"</note> means to stumble; a "beam" <note>original: "balcke"</note> means to commit great failings.</margin>
...you blind [one], how can you see then what your neighbor lacks, indeed even what you yourself lack? "Eye" here refers to the intention of the heart and mind, as well as the conscience. "Beam" refers to the larger, coarser sin, just as the "speck" refers to the smaller or light sin. For example: one who carries hatred and envy in his heart, and yet wishes to sharply punish or judge his brother or another person for having a hasty spirit and becoming quickly angry—even though that person is also quickly reconciled—is the one who wants to take the speck out of his brother’s eye, while paying no attention at all to the beam in his own eye.

<margin>Wrath.</margin>
<margin>1 John 3.</margin>
Sudden, fleeting wrath is a speck or a bit of dust; but when wrath takes root and grows old, then it becomes a beam. Anger or sudden emotion clouds the <term>eye of the mind</term> <note>original: "auge des gemüts"; a common early modern term for the faculty of spiritual or moral perception</note>, but hatred and envy completely blind a person, as it says in the Epistle of John: Whoever hates his brother is in the darkness and walks in the darkness, and does not know where he is going, for the darkness has blinded him.

<margin>Chrysostom: Hatred is to be repelled with the greatest strength <note>original Latin: "Odium summis uiribus est repellendum"</note>.</margin>
Hatred is a long-lasting, aged wrath and displeasure, where a root of bitterness has grown in the heart, so that one barely grants the other person their very life. John, in his Epistle, makes no distinction between hatred and murder before God's judgment, where he says: Everyone who hates his brother is a murderer. Therefore, no one can take or pull a speck from the eye of his brother while he carries a beam in his own eye; no one can punish another on account of sudden wrath if he hides hatred and envy in his own heart and does not first improve himself.

# On the 4th Sunday after Trinity
<meta>catchword: Summary</meta>

...the more we should endeavor to cast off our own inner hypocrite whom we carry in our bosom—the sooner the better—before the day of the Lord and in His grace. For <term>hypocrisy</term> <note>original: "gleißnerey"; derived from the word for "shining," implying a false or deceptive outward luster.</note> is deeply, deeply rooted in the flesh inherited from Adam; it can only be rightly recognized, even within ourselves, in the light of Jesus Christ. How punishable a vice hypocrisy or deceit is, is also shown to us in the book of Job, where he says:

> No hypocrite shall come before the face of God <note>original: "Es wirt kein heüchler für das angesicht Gotes komen"; referencing Job 13:16.</note>.

And again:

> The hypocrites and deceivers provoke the wrath of God <note>original: "Die heüchler vn̄ betrieger reizē den zorn Gottes"; referencing Job 36:13.</note>.

So, there are primarily two points to be noted here. The first is that whoever wishes to investigate or punish the life of another must necessarily have a pure eye of the soul. The second is that the severity of the punishment toward a brother should outwardly manifest in such a way that the heat of love is not internally spoiled or extinguished. Therefore, Paul also writes to the Galatians:

> Dear brothers, if a man is perhaps overtaken by a fault, instruct him with a humble spirit, you who are spiritual; and look to yourself, that you also are not tempted. Galatians 6.

May our dear Lord Jesus Christ first take away the beam of envy and hatred from our eyes—and all malice, all deceit, all <term>backbiting</term> <note>original: "affterreden"; the act of speaking ill of others behind their backs.</note> and hypocrisy—and give us His grace and the Spirit of <term>gentleness</term> <note>original: "senfftmütigkeit"; a mild-mannered and patient disposition.</note>. Then we may pull the <term>splinter</term> <note>original: "spilter"</note> or <term>speck of dust</term> <note>original: "steublen"</note> out of our brother’s eye—that is, to correct him fruitfully—and serve our neighbor with works of love, indeed with our whole life, and be an example of improvement to everyone, Amen.
