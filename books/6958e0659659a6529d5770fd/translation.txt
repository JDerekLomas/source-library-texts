### TO THE MOST ILLUSTRIOUS AND VIRTUOUS GENTLEMAN
# BACCIO VALORIO
# PATRICIAN
## OF FLORENCE

*Secret Councilor to the Most Serene Ferdinand, Grand Duke of Tuscany.* <note>Ferdinand I de' Medici (1549–1609), a major patron of the sciences and arts.</note>

*Andreas Caesalpinus sends fondest greetings.* <note>original: "S. P. D." or Salutem Plurimam Dicit. Caesalpinus was a renowned physician, philosopher, and botanist.</note>

It will perhaps seem, most illustrious Baccio, that a curiosity for investigating foreign things brought from the most distant regions of the East and West Indies <note>At this time, "Indies" referred generally to the Americas (West) and South/Southeast Asia (East).</note> should be considered a fault, since divine providence provides what is necessary for human nature in infinite lands almost everywhere. Yet an inexhaustible greed for knowing and possessing does not rest content with frugality; it keeps men forever occupied in seeking out foreign things and the most exquisite examples of every matter. However, this is seen as permissible for Physicians, so that they may abound in remedies for the sake of health, which is dearer than all else. 

Furthermore, those who are most highly skilled in the Medical art do not lack for foreign and more curiously sought-after "noble" medicines; rather, those things which are found among us—however common they may be—can dispel every sickness (if it be not incurable) when applied at the right time, as Cornelius Celsus <note>A Roman encyclopedist known for his medical address 'De Medicina'.</note> bears witness. Truly, the incurable are not helped even by those things which are most noble, whether they excel by the sweetness of their scent, such as Balsam and Cinnamon, or by their entire substance, such as Bezoar <note>term: Bezaar (Bezoar). A mass trapped in the gastrointestinal system, then highly prized as a universal antidote to poison.</note> or Theriac <note>term: Theriaca (Theriac). A famous medical concoction, often containing opium and viper flesh, used as a panacea against all diseases and poisons.</note>...

It does not sink into the water, but spreads out over the entire surface like a most subtle veil, invisible at first. It possesses a most sweet scent, not medicinal or pungent, and it tastes of pepper. With age, it thickens and becomes more yellow.

Rightly, therefore, I thought these additions should be offered to you; once published under your authority, they may gain more certain credit among others, and you may understand me to be very much bound to you by this small gift.

Farewell. Rome. The 19th day of January, 1603.

The plant bears a likeness to the Strawberry Tree <note>original: "Arbutus"</note>, or to young Hazelnut trees <note>original: "Auellanæ"</note> growing from numerous roots, of which one thicker root is fixed deep within the earth, and next to it are many thinner ones. From a single turf emerge six or seven shoots four cubits <note>A cubit is roughly 18 inches; these shoots are about 6 feet tall.</note> in height, at which time they have attained a thickness suitable for the pipes <note>The "fistulæ" or quills of cinnamon bark.</note> to be separated from the wood. But the small branches which are near the root wither away, as usually happens to those that grow in dense forests. It bears leaves similar in shape to Laurel, with a flat surrounding line, and a certain rib running through the middle, with two others at the side meeting at the top in an acute angle; at the base, however, they form an oval shape. The space between the ribs is marked as if with filaments, like a spider's web. 

The stalks are covered with a very thin skin of varying color according to the diversity of nature, age, position, and part. For near the root, the thicker stalks are clothed in a white skin; near the peaks, a black color appears; in the middle, it is variegated. Whence it often happens that in the same turf all the differences of colors are seen, from which the ancients created various species of Cinnamon. In some, that color appears which Galen <note>A famous Greek physician and philosopher in the Roman Empire.</note> compares to milk sprinkled over black with blue. Some are seen with a wine-like color inclining toward ash-gray, as Dioscorides <note>A Greek physician and botanist, author of "De Materia Medica".</note> relates. Beneath this is contained the bark, which is cleaned to make the pipes. Thinner pipes are obtained from Ceylon <note>original: "Zeilana"</note> than from Malabar. 

Beneath that bark is the wood, the material of which is light and fibrous; in the middle it contains a reddish pith like that of the Laurel, though not as thick. The taste of the wood is insipid; if, however, it is chewed for a long time, it offers some flavor of Cinnamon <note>original: "Canellæ"</note>, but it is scarcely perceived. Between the leaf-stalk and the branches from which they emerge, there comes out a white, round, and thin stalk, which opens at the top into several small branches. On the tips of these sit little flowers, which while they are closed, imitate the clusters of the olive in shape and color. These open into six small leaves forming a sort of cup, with threads rising from the bottom and ending in a broad and round surface. But as soon as the flowers open, they are withered by the sun; their odor is not pleasant, but heavy for the brain, and the taste is astringent...

# On Indian Lycium, and the method of extracting juice from woods among the Indians. Chapter II.

The same Philip <note>Likely referring to the physician Filipe Corte-Real or a similar contemporary source</note> writes these things concerning Indian Lycium: The tree is called *Cadira* <note>original: "Cadira"; likely the Khair tree or *Acacia catechu*</note> by the Indians, from whose wood they extract a juice which they call *Catu* <note>original: "Catu"; known today as Catechu</note>. It grows spontaneously throughout the entire maritime region of India, but is more abundant in Cambay <note>A region in Gujarat, India</note>. Its roots are quite thick; the trunk is like that of a Plum or Almond tree, in which the branches are arranged unevenly both in height and in circumference. From the branches come thorns as in a Bramble, and the leaves are a bright green, narrow and small, like those of a Fir tree, forming an acute angle on the twigs rather than a flat surface. Upon these grow certain small, round, reddish things which occupy the entire width of the leaves, harsh to the taste. The bark of the tree is rough and cracked, with a reddish color on the inside, beneath which is another thin bark of a yellowish color. The outer wood is also yellowish, but the pith <note>The innermost part of the trunk</note> is a very dense reddish color, which, if cut crosswise, shatters like *lignum sanctum* <note>original: "lignum sanctum"; literally "holy wood," usually referring to Guaiacum, known for its extreme hardness</note>, and is so heavy that it sinks in water. The odor of the fresh tree is heavy. The flavor of the pith is known from the juice which is extracted, for it is bitterish, but more so astringent; these flavors are not perceived in the wood itself. Garcia de Orta and Cristóvão <note>Referring to Garcia de Orta and Cristóvão da Costa, famous 16th-century physicians and botanists who studied Indian medicine</note> assert this to be the Indian Lycium of Dioscorides <note>A famous Greek physician and pharmacologist of the 1st century AD</note>, as we can confirm from the descriptions. The method of extracting this kind of juice is as follows: The pith of the tree is taken and split into thin pieces, then ground upon a marble slab such as painters use. This powder is boiled in sufficient water for 24 hours, then strained. The decoction is boiled again until it thickens beyond the consistency of honey, but less than that of wax, so that lozenges <note>term: paftilli (lozenges) - small medicinal cakes or tablets</note> can be formed, which when dried are black on the outside and reddish within. They are proven to be pure if, when taken into the mouth in the size of half a hazelnut, they melt entirely with no grittiness under the teeth. ## *That various sects have arisen from error in first principles.*

### *Chapter I.*

**A**lthough all sects have many followers who agree on the same points, they nevertheless disagree with one another due to some error regarding first principles, from which they have fallen into vain opinions. For a small error in the beginning becomes the greatest at the end <note>A famous Aristotelian maxim: "parvus error in principio magnus est in fine."</note>. As in Philosophy, by the consensus of all, substance is a certain principle. For all other things are either said of substance or exist within substance. However, concerning the parts of substance—whether Matter or Form be prior—there is no agreement among all. For those who said that matter is prior because it bears the Form, fell into error, thinking that the principles of bodies—namely lines, surfaces, and points or numbers—are the principles of substance, as did the *Pythagoreans* <note>Followers of Pythagoras who believed that number was the essence of all things.</note>. *Aristotle*, on the contrary, contended that Form is prior, because act is prior to potency. By this reasoning, he found that the principle of all things is a simple act, and that this alone is substance through itself, and that for the sake of which all things exist, and the best. Matter, however, is not substance through itself, <margin>*</margin> but for the sake of another. Yet within the *Peripatetic* <term>Peripatetic: The school of philosophy founded by Aristotle, named for his habit of walking while teaching.</term> way, other sects emerged due to ignorance of the substance which is called Form. Whence they were ignorant of the forms of the elements, saying they were hidden, and they hesitated especially concerning the nature of the soul's intellect; from which they fell into many absurdities, not having well perceived the Philosopher's <note>Aristotle was often referred to simply as "The Philosopher" in academic texts.</note> opinion on substance, which we have explained as much as possible according to the mind of the *Greek* interpreters. He who does not hold to this path frequently wanders from the truth. Moreover, the things better known to us are singulars, for those are the things perceived by the senses.

Therefore, it must be separate from all matter and entirely incapable of being acted upon <note>original: "impassibile," meaning it cannot be changed or affected by external forces</note>, but acts only to produce nutrition in plants and the generation of their own kind; in animals, it also produces sensation, so that without any teacher, newborn chicks are drawn to the food familiar to them. In man, it also produces intelligence, so that he can resolve all things into their first principles. For insofar as it is that upon which the being of all things depends, it is capable of all things. Insofar as it is intelligent, it knows all things. Insofar as it is the highest good, it is desired by all. We see that Aristotle reached this point via the natural path, although he spoke quite meagerly concerning the Heavenly bodies, because they are most remote from our senses. But where the senses failed, he used Analogy. For he said that those things which are in Heaven have the same proportion to lower things. For just as sensible light brings all colors into action because it is visible in itself, so the intellect in action makes all intelligible things actually understood, just as light does for colors placed in darkness. For colors are not actually colors in the dark, but only potentially so, because they can receive light within a bounded transparent medium, which is the essence of color. Furthermore, among the elements, the sphere of fire—which is called Ether <note>original: "Aether"; here referring to the elemental fire below the moon</note>—holds a proportion in the celestial realms with the highest heaven. This highest heaven was called Ether by the ancients because it is "always running" <note>A traditional etymology deriving 'Aether' from the Greek 'aei' (always) and 'thein' (to run)</note>, and it is where the abstract substances <note>referring to immaterial intelligences or spirits</note> dwell. When this heat is received into these lower realms, it makes seeds fertile; it is not fire, nor does it take its origin from fire, but is a spirit <note>original: "spiritus"</note> corresponding in proportion to the element of the stars, in which every soul seems to participate. Moreover, every soul derives its principle from heaven, because it possesses the quality of "that for the sake of which" <note>The Aristotelian "final cause" or purpose</note> and of the better, which is suited to the highest end.

From these points it is also clear that in heaven there is a certain more solid part corresponding to the earth, by whose motion friction occurs, creating heat in the lower body. There is another spiritual part corresponding to air and fire, but more noble, which constitutes the *prime mover* <term>primum mobile: the outermost moving sphere in the geocentric model which carries all other spheres with it</term>. By its most rapid motion, all the lower spheres are moved toward the west as if by a containing force, so that this motion is communicated even to the spheres of fire and air, and in some part even down to the earth. We have gathered this from the change observed by the ancients in certain fixed stars over a very long and unequal period of time; this cannot be attributed to the higher spheres if what Aristotle said regarding the nature of Place is true. Furthermore, one must consider the motion of the *prime mover* to be far swifter than that of the Firmament <note>The sphere of the fixed stars</note> by reason of distance and thickness; so that perhaps one day of the Firmament is equal to a thousand years of the *prime mover*.
