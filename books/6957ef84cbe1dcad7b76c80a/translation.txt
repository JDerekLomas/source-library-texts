**FURTHERMORE**, there is no doubt that far greater progress will be made in this business if you would command your citizens—who, by God's favor, are now so successfully undertaking voyages to India, Ethiopia <note>In this period, 'Ethiopia' often referred generally to Sub-Saharan Africa</note>, and America—to instruct whatever pharmacists <term>pharmacopœis: preparers of medicines</term> and surgeons <term>cheirurgis: medical practitioners who performed manual procedures</term> they assign to the care of those sailing abroad. # To the man of exceptional fame and merit
## CHARLES DE L'ÉCLUSE
### OF ARRAS. <note>Arras, or Atrebatum in Latin, was Clusius's birthplace in the Spanish Netherlands.</note>

<image-desc>A large decorative drop cap "Q" containing an illustration of a crane or stork standing in a marshy landscape, surrounded by ornate scrolls and floral motifs.</image-desc>
What madness drives men to penetrate unknown recesses? And to track down new nations across the immense spaces of the sea—not without crime—and to disturb those at peace with war? The rage for ruling, and the infuriated desire for wealth, with which they goad mortal hearts with grim cares, have left no unspeakable crime unattempted. Would it not be better to have remained at one's ancestral hearth, and to have cherished peaceful quiet on one's native soil, than to harass the human race with foul ruins? 

But if a love of visiting long tracts of the earth is felt—since our mind, created from heavenly seed, is unable to stand still in one place, but grows stronger with sharper movement and imitates the rapid orbits of Olympus—we are able, following the example of the much-experienced Ulysses <note>The Greek hero Odysseus, famous for his long travels and wisdom.</note>, to traverse diverse places and foreign kingdoms. This is done, of course, so that there may be a care to learn the various customs of men, or to unfold the causes of the nature of things. 

From this, just as a tree flourishes gradually over a hidden age, that prudence <term>prudentia: practical wisdom or foresight</term>, the ruler of human life, is born. The heavenly powers have bestowed no greater gift than this upon the earth. Prudence tempers cities and rules peoples with counsel, just as the spirit reigns in the body, and those breaths which we have drawn from a divine seed. 

For this purpose, O glory and renowned light of the medical race, you have zealously traveled through regions situated in another world, lest your care leave anything anywhere unexamined from which an un-praised pleasure might come to noble minds, or a sweet relief to sick bodies. Happy in your labors, you who, by mixing the sweet with the useful <note>A reference to Horace’s "Ars Poetica": "He who mixes the useful with the pleasant wins every vote."</note>, carry every point, while from a rich store you bring forth the fertile wealth of the friendly earth—wealth which she pours out upon the ground, tamed by kind cultivation, not that which she hides away, secret in her innermost bosom, to withdraw cruel weapons from the vices of men. ->So that perennial nourishment is not lacking for wretched diseases.<-
Nor is this enough; the profane race of Iapetus <note>original: "Iapeti genus"; a reference to Prometheus or humanity in general, often used to denote human audacity or rebellion against divine limits</note> probes the hidden places of the earth, and descends to the lowest spirits of the dead, living and seeing, so that there might be a source from which one can perish through slow torture by night and day. But a better judgment has imbued your great-hearted breast, and has stirred your mind with a noble love to scrutinize with genius the secrets of powerful nature, the eternal laws, and the covenants ascribed to things.

It was your desire to know every kind of herb, and the nature given to trees, their various habits, the spirits of places <note>original: "geniosque locorum"; the unique natural character or atmosphere of specific geographic areas</note>, and the homelands divided among the crops (for not every land nourishes all things), the forms of shrubs, and their powers in healing. You did not trust in doubtful rumor, but chose to survey them with your own eyes, sparing no expense.

With you as our guide, it is granted to us to know the manifold faces of birds, and to see the unusual appearances and countenances of wild beasts, but without danger. Your labor sets forth many monsters of the sea <note>original: "monstra natantum"</note> to be looked upon, yet all terror is absent; no infamous rocks or monstrous whales <note>original: "immania cete"</note> threaten the wretched with fearful shipwrecks. Here are no songs of Sirens, nor is the ground white with the bones of those deprived of honor, nor do fields, desolate of their tillers, mourn in a sight lamentable to behold. The reader may traverse these safe waters in safety.

Among these cares, the best part of your life passed for you while your youth was blooming with beautiful flowers. Now too, your spirit remains the same, although envious old age has broken the strength of your body and diminished your vigor; your zeal persists and flourishes just the same, and your praised ambition maintains its course: to be of use to future ages, and to halt the flight of years with your victorious pages.

Well done, old man of great spirit! It is fitting for a leader to meet death while at his station and rank. With such a wing, the vivid force of the soul flees the earth, and fiery virtue seeks the heavens.

->Dominicus Baudius<-

# FRÉDÉRIC JAMOT
## PHYSICIAN

->To the Most Illustrious Man<-
->CHARLES DE L'ÉCLUSE, Greetings.<-

I send to you, most illustrious man, a poem in <term>two languages</term> <note>original: "δίγλωττον" (diglotton); referring to the Latin and Greek versions of the verse</note>, conceived in my mind during a night of sleeplessness (see with what grand words I adorn my gift!). It was afterwards, while awake, arranged in order and written down: to stand as a witness to all men, not only of your excellent virtue and learning, but also of my perpetual respect and well-disposed will toward you. But I fear lest, through the fault of my own talent, I have somewhat diminished your praises, which I ought to have increased; for that youthful eagerness by which poetic studies are nourished has cooled within me, and my vein of inspiration is now almost as exhausted as my life itself. I earnestly ask you, however, to accept this, of whatever quality it may be, with that same sincerity of mind with which it is given to you by me. I would wish you to dutifully return greetings in my name to that most distinguished man, most filled with every praise of excellent virtue and learning, <term>Joseph Scaliger</term> <note>A famous contemporary scholar and close friend of Clusius</note>. Farewell, and please forward these letters to our friend <term>Vulcanius</term> <note>Bonaventura Vulcanius, a professor of Greek at Leiden</note>. From our estate at Anesinianum, the Calends of March <note>March 1st</note>, in the year 1606.

->An Epic Poem on the <term>Exotic Things</term> of the Most Wise Man<-
->CHARLES DE L'ÉCLUSE.<-

**T**he Earth, mother of all, brings forth for much-weeping mortals both wickedness and ten thousand forms of disease; yet she also brings forth a release, and a firm defense against pain. From the earth spring forth baneful creeping things, the destructive harms of venomous beasts, and the natural poisons in deadly roots, which overcome men when they come into contact with them. Yet from the earth also come limb-healing herbs, to which the all-nourishing mother has granted powers to ward off grief, acting against both stinging creatures and poisonous drinks. This is the gift of the God who wards off evil, to increase the livelihood of weak mortals in His mercy; so that all might have a soothing defense against distress, and might learn a delay of hateful death—at least as many as have a stout heart and a noble spirit to track down the forms of plants and all their benefits.
