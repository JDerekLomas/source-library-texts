Thomas: "Truly <note>original: "Amēn"</note>, I say to you: This sin is more hateful <note>original: "heinous"</note> than all sins and all injustices." 

However, there is no sign that the writers of the *Pistis Sophia* found such a practice to be widespread. On the contrary, it seems to have been a rare occurrence for them—in fact, it was the most horrible thing they had ever heard of. If we are to rely on Epiphanius <note>Epiphanius of Salamis (c. 310–403 AD) was a bishop and historian famous for his "Panarion," a massive work attacking various heresies.</note> here, it is useless to look for the Gnostics of the *Pistis Sophia* in that kind of environment. 

But Epiphanius generally lacks a reputation for accuracy, and it is very difficult to believe in such widespread and loathsome wickedness. In any case, he was writing at a later date. Liechtenhan’s hypothesis, that a certain body of literature was rewritten—on one hand to serve libertine <note>original: "libertinist," referring to those who believed spiritual knowledge freed them from traditional moral laws</note> tendencies, and on the other to support ascetic <note>Characterized by severe self-discipline and abstention from all forms of indulgence.</note> ones—is more or less accepted by Harnack. However, it seems to me to be too easy a generalization to address the specific problem we face. 

In his youth, Epiphanius had unfortunate experiences with members of a libertine sect in Egypt. The moral shock this caused seems to have warped his judgment as a historian in this section of his work. It led him to collect every bit of evidence regarding obscenity he could find, as well as every gross scandal he heard of, and to generalize freely from them.

This perspective may be seen in the following observations, which introduce us to the third surviving, but as-yet unpublished, collection of Coptic Gnostic works.

<margin>The Berlin Codex.</margin>
On July 16, 1896, Schmidt <note>Carl Schmidt (1868–1938), a prominent German scholar of Coptic and Gnosticism.</note> surprised and delighted scholars of Gnosticism by reporting, at a meeting of the Royal Prussian Academy of Sciences, on the contents of a precious Coptic Gnostic Codex. This manuscript had been acquired in January of that same year by Dr. Reinhardt in Cairo from an antiquities dealer from Akhmīm, and it is now kept safely in the Berlin Egyptian Museum <note>original: "Sitzungsberichte d. k. p. Akad. d. Wissensch. zu Berlin" (Reports of the Proceedings of the Royal Prussian Academy of Sciences in Berlin), volume xxxvi</note>. This announcement and a more detailed study of one of the treatises by Schmidt in 1907 give us all the information we currently possess concerning this very important Codex. In 1900, I summarized Schmidt's first report in the first edition of my book, *Fragments of a Faith Forgotten* (pages 579–592). The Codex consists mainly of three original Greek Gnostic works in Coptic translation: (1) *The Gospel of Mary*; (2) *The Apocryphon of John* <note>term: Apocryphon means "Secret Book."</note>; and (3) *The Wisdom of Jesus Christ*. At the end, there is an extract from *The Acts of Peter*, which is also Gnostic in origin, describing an episode of the Apostle’s miraculous healings.
