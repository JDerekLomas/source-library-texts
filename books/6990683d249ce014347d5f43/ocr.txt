<header>PREFACE</header>
<page-num>IX</page-num>

The Monadology of Leibniz in an English form has long been available and familiar to students of philosophy in the excellent translations of Professor R. Latta and Professor G. M. Duncan. There is no difficulty in understanding it so far as the diction is concerned; it is the thought which is difficult. It requires interpreting, not only in relation to the scientific concepts of an age when mathematics and mechanics were highly advanced, and physics, chemistry and biology still in their infancy, if indeed they could be said to have come to birth, but also in relation to contemporary science and philosophy.

The Monadology is pure metaphysics. It belongs not to any particular time but to all time. Yet it enjoys a peculiar privilege in the history and development of modern philosophy by reason of the profound influence it has had in determining the direction of later thought, notably that of Kant and Hegel, and it cannot be neglected by anyone who is seeking to participate in the philosophical thinking of the present time.

The theory of Monads is only a small part of the intellectual work of Leibniz. For an account of his whole life and philosophy I may refer the reader to my *Leibniz* (Ernest Benn, London ; Little Brown, Boston ; 1929).

The Monadology was not written for publication. Leibniz, when he wrote it, had published only one work of philosophy in book form, *La Théodicée*, and the references in the Monadology are almost exclusively to this work.

# THE STORY OF THE MONADOLOGY

The work of Leibniz which we name the *Monadology* is a short treatise in French found among his papers without any title. It was written in 1714 about two years before his death. It purports to set forth in ninety short and carefully connected paragraphs a complete and succinct account of the philosophy which he was accustomed to describe as his "system." Another similar work, also found among his papers, bearing the title *Principes de la Nature et de la Grace fondés en raison*, consisting of eighteen paragraphs, is written with the same purpose of summarizing the philosophy. It seems probable that this was an earlier attempt, set aside as unsatisfactory, and afterwards worked up into *La Monadologie*. We know from the correspondence that Prince Eugene of Savoy, a student and admirer of the philosopher, having read Leibniz's *La Théodicée*, published in 1710, requested him to write a condensed statement of his philosophical principles, and Leibniz did so and sent it to the Prince. ## THE RELATION OF THE MONADOLOGY TO THE THEODICY

The monadology is a purely metaphysical treatise in the special meaning which Leibniz attaches to the term metaphysics, namely, a science of pure reason. In Leibniz’s view there is a faculty of pure reason (*raison pure et nue*) and by it we are enabled to know reality as it is in itself, reality independent of sensible experience and independent therefore of the religious belief or faith which depends on the evidence of sensible experience, such as miracles, the fulfilment of prophecy, and, generally, what goes by the name of Christian evidence. The principle which guides and supports this pure reason is the logical law of non-contradiction, and the truth it attains is necessary truth, the denial of which involves absurdity. In sensible experience on the other hand we are always dealing with contingent truth. Any fact of experience may be affirmed or denied without involving self-contradiction. The principle which guides experience in the search for truth is the law of sufficient reason. The Monadology is designed to give form and content to a science of pure reason.

## BAYLE’S DICTIONARY AND THE ARTICLE RORARIUS

Pierre Bayle (1647-1706) is known chiefly as the author of the Dictionary which bears his name. In many striking respects he resembles Leibniz. Both writers were adherents of the reformed religion, and both were especially interested in and deeply concerned with the particular points at issue between the Catholic Church and the Reformers, and keenly anxious to discover a *via media* which might effect a reconciliation. Bayle was the son of a minister of the Reformed Church and to the distress of his family had been converted in his student days to Catholicism. The conversion had endured however only seventeen months and he had then returned to the communion of the Reformed Church. He was particularly obnoxious to the Catholics on this account. In 1675 he was appointed Professor of Philosophy in the Protestant Academy of Sedan, but in 1681 the Academy was suppressed by Louis XIV, and he avoided prosecution by removing to Holland, where later he accepted an appointment as Professor in the University of Rotterdam. ## THE METAPHYSICAL PROBLEM OF THE MONADOLOGY

It was possible in the seventeenth century to be indifferent to religion and open-minded in theology. It was possible to be a rationalist in regard to the mysteries of life and death, to be unresponsive to hopes and fears concerning the unseen world, and to be impassive and critical towards the popular realistic representations of heaven and hell. It was impossible, however, for any philosopher to take an untheological, or as we should describe it today in positive terms, a naturalistic or scientific view of man’s nature and origin. Our modern scientific aspect of nature is the outcome of a long historical and intellectual development. It was as clear to Leibniz and his contemporaries as it is to us today that the matter of our body is continuous with the inert matter of the universe, and that the form which the vital functioning of our organism assumes, repeats a type of general functioning common to all species of living creatures. Yet it was impossible for them to conceive that the human mind, with its rational individualized activities, could have come into

### V

# A GENERAL SUMMARY OF THE THEORY OF MONADS

There are two logical principles which underlie all reasoning. These are the principle of non-contradiction which we employ in the search for mathematical and metaphysical truths and the principle of sufficient reason which we employ in all truths concerning matters of fact.

We perceive the universe of objects but we do not perceive the units of which the universe is composed. Yet we know that units must exist because the universe we perceive, and the objects we perceive as belonging to it, are composites, and if there are composites there must be units. These units are the monads, they may be defined as simple substances.

The monads are not material atoms, for atoms are outside one another and so must have parts by which they adjoin one another. The monads are absolutely without parts. A monad has no extension, no shape, no position, no movement. The monads are distinguished from one another by the quality or character which differentiates each from every other.

**1.** The Monad, the subject of this discourse, is nothing else than a simple substance which enters into composites. Simple means that it is without parts.

The concept of substance dominates the philosophy of the seventeenth century. The notion of substance arises in the common sense idea that there must be some kind of existence occupying space to which all the variety of the perceptual world is ultimately reducible and out of which in the succession of time it is generated. Early Greek philosophy arose in the quest for the *prima materia* of the world : Is it water, air, fire, or something even more ethereal, such stuff as thoughts are made of ?

Substance is the idea of the existence which gives unity and continuity to the world which is the object of experience and to the mind which is the subject of experience. What kind of thing could substance be ? The problem shaped itself in varying ways, but two opposite principles, in one aspect contradictory, in another aspect complementary, tended to assert themselves. <header>SPACE AND TIME</header>

The contrast in the section we are considering is between the conceptions of units differing only by their absolute position and relative disposition, and units which are indivisible individuals differing qualitatively and absolutely, not quantitatively. Since the monads are inextended the composite universe cannot be spatial, and since the monads are active the universe cannot be inert. The universe, therefore, does not depend on the fundamental conception of a mechanically determined order of coexistent inert masses and a mechanically determined order of successive states. But if space and time are not postulated as an absolute framework, can we give any definition to our idea of a really objective physical world ? The answer is that the difficulty is not in the conception, this is both metaphysically and mathematically presentable to the understanding, the difficulty is in the imagination, for we are precluded the use of sense imagery. Space and time are not abolished but they are subordinated. The perceived universe, the universe mirrored in each monad, will then be the sense-intuited aspect of an intelligible reality. Space and time will belong to this intuited aspect of the universe. Space is then for each monad the order of coexistences of its perceptions, time the order of successions.

It is not possible to imagine the universe without a spatio-temporal setting. Yet we can find abundant illustrations of real activities in which the necessary mental imagery involves a spatio-temporal setting, but in which this mental imagery can be recognized as no more than a supporting scaffold, and space and time themselves not fundamental conditions on which the reality depends. The living body is such a reality.

There was a widely held hypothesis which had its origin in Greek mythology and took form in various fanciful images in the Middle Ages that the earth and generally the physical world is an animated organic body. In this section and the three which follow we see that Leibniz’s doctrine has nothing in common with that idea. His theory is that the appearance which matter presents to us, however minutely we divide it and isolate parts for analysis, always turns out to be a mass effect. The real thing is living activity. Even this however does not exhaust the idea. Not only is there negatively no dead limit of the universe, no inert unit of its physical basis, but also positively there is no limit at which a subjective activity would cease to function by reason of its inability to find the where-withal to clothe its <term>entelechy</term> or soul with an organic body. Theology supports him with its affirmation of infinity in God and it is doubtless from this concept that his doctrine is derived. What he sees, however, is that the positive infinity of God, expressed in omniscience and omnipotence, postulates a correlative objective infinity. What is affirmed with such clearness in these sections is that just as there is no limit of divisibility in a physical reality conceived geometrically, so there is no limit of individuality in a reality conceived vitalistically. There is no point at which life ceases to be individualized and becomes undifferentiated activity.

### I. LEIBNIZ’S METAPHYSICS AND NEWTON’S PHYSICS

Throughout the whole course of modern philosophy we find two divergent directions in which the attempt is made to interpret our knowledge of the physical universe. Broadly speaking, we may designate the first as the way of realism, the second as the way of idealism. They lead to alternative and antithetical conceptions of the nature of the existence of the physical world.

### 2. WHAT IS VITAL IN THE MONADOLOGY

The fundamental principle of the monadology is that the real is essentially active, that the active is the living, and therefore that only living activities are true units.

The monadology sets forth a logically consistent theory of the composition or constitution of a universe in which every constituent is an active subject of experience living its own life and in living its own life subserving the universal life, the life of the universe.

The monadology rejects as self-contradiction and inconsistency the idea that dead, inert, material atoms or mass-units are or can be constituents of a universe. There is nothing dead, nothing purely inert in the universe. To be absolutely dead is not to be. The Democritean concept of the atoms and the void is rejected because mathematically the atom is not a unit, but divisible to infinity, and physically it is not simple. The reals are monads, simple substances, self-centred subjects of experience, whose activity consists in perception, and whose perceptions are the apprehension of the whole universe, limited in the degree of clearness or obscurity, because the monads are created and therefore finite, and qualified by their individual standpoints.

# THE IDEALISM OF THE MONADOLOGY

The Theory of Monads rests on the frank recognition of two facts. The first is that the knowledge which any individual subject of experience possesses is solipsistic. The second is that the relation between one mind and another is ideal and there is no real contact. The first fact was expressed by Leibniz in the dictum: “The monads have no windows by means of which anything can enter in or pass out”. (*Monadology*, 7). The second was expressed by saying that monads exercise only an ideal influence on one another (*Monadology*, 51). It follows therefore that monads cannot interact and that they can not intercommunicate. The crucial problem in monadology therefore is : how can there be intercourse or intercommunication of ideas between monads if knowledge is solipsistic and there is no interaction?

# LEIBNIZ AND SPINOZA

Spinoza died in 1677, Leibniz in 1716, a difference of thirty-nine years. The dates are important, for Leibniz's philosophical writings, those in which his system appears complete and not as a tentative sketch, were composed in the last fifteen years of his life, that is, more than twenty years after Spinoza's death and the posthumous publication of the *Ethics*.

In 1676, the year before Spinoza's death, Leibniz travelling home to Hanover from Paris by way of London and Amsterdam, paid a visit to Spinoza, was shewn by him the unpublished manuscript of the *Ethics* and carried away with him some extracts from it. Out of this simple and quite ordinary incident a monstrous fabrication of calumny has been constructed. The detractors of Leibniz suggest that the main metaphysical ideas of his philosophy were borrowed from Spinoza without acknowledgment and that the injustice was concealed by Leibniz's feigned indignation at the blasphemous conclusions laid to Spinoza's charge, a charge from which Leibniz was honourably bound to exonerate him.
