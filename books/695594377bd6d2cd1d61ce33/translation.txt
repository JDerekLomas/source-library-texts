# P R E F A C E

---

<margin>α</margin> There will perhaps be those to whom—after the very recent labors of Clavier, Siebelis, and Bekker—the plan of publishing Pausanias <note>Pausanias was a 2nd-century AD Greek traveler and geographer</note> anew may seem plainly useless or certainly unnecessary. Nor is there any reason to deny that, to those judging this matter from a distance, such a suspicion has a certain appearance of truth. <margin>כ</margin> But those who come closer, who thoroughly examine the very extensive commentaries on Pausanias, and who turn their attention to the thinness of the critical apparatus <note>The "critical apparatus" is the set of notes at the bottom of a page detailing different readings found in various manuscripts</note> brought to light so far and the lack of resources, will easily persuade themselves of the following: although our Guide <note>original: "Periegetae," referring to Pausanias as the author of a "Periegesis" or travel guide</note> has indeed been excellently served by very learned men, and his speech has been restored to its native integrity in many places, a critical edition reconstructed according to the authority of the manuscripts and furnished with a more copious critical apparatus is still lacking. To make this matter clearer, and so that what has been achieved so far and what remains to be done may be more easily seen, it seemed appropriate to provide a notice of the previous editions of Pausanias.

The first edition <note>original: "editio princeps"</note> of Pausanias was published under the care of Marcus Musurus from the house of Aldus and his father-in-law Andreas in the month of July, 1516, at Venice, in folio format. Since all other editions (if you except only Bekker’s) have flowed from this one, it is worth the effort to examine its character more accurately. Aldus Manutius indeed stated in his preface that "even if these commentaries were not polished to perfection, [he had printed them from] manuscripts..."

-> ### PREFACE <-

<note>original: PRAEFATIO</note>

...nevertheless, he <note>referring to Aldus Manutius</note> claims that he published the more correct manuscripts that were in circulation, doing so with the advice and assistance of Marcus Musurus <note>A Greek scholar and editor for the Aldine Press</note>. However, in an age that was not very concerned with such matters, he neglected to describe those manuscripts with any great care. This most deserving man mentions several manuscripts; but if we examine the edition more accurately and compare it with the manuscripts currently available, we can conclude without ambiguity that it was printed—quite carelessly—from a single manuscript, and one of inferior quality. This is clearly evident from our critical apparatus <note>The list of textual variants and manuscript sources used by an editor</note>; for the Aldine edition hardly ever departs from the reading of the second Vienna manuscript <note>original: codicis Vindobonensis alterius</note>. 

Now the question arises: from which manuscript was the Venetian edition printed? From a Venetian manuscript, of course, they say; so, for example, says Morelli in his *Library of Greek and Latin Manuscripts*, page 282. Matthias Roever, in a letter to d'Orville (see Benjamin Peter van Wesele Scholten in the *Catalogue of M. Roever's Books*, Volume I, page 50), discussing his own manuscript, adds these remarks: "The parchment manuscript seems to me to have been copied from that same original from which Aldus took his edition; Aldus (or rather, Marcus Musurus) changed and supplied many things by his own wit, and thus we read certain passages of Pausanias that have been corrupted by the fault of the 'corrector'." 

Three centuries ago, there could undoubtedly have been several manuscripts of Pausanias in Venice. However, we dare to affirm with total confidence that neither was the Aldine edition printed from the manuscript now called the "Venetian," nor was Roever's book copied from the one used for the Venetian edition. As we have already noted, the Aldine edition comes closest to the readings of the Vienna (b) and Naples manuscripts.

***

We readily concede that the edition was printed carelessly and is defiled by many typographical errors <sup>a)</sup>; however, we ourselves have not found the gaps <note>original: lacunas</note> which Clavier thought he detected, supposedly caused solely by the editor's laziness <sup>b)</sup>. Of much greater importance is what Roever mentions in the passage cited above (alongside many others): that Aldus, or rather Musurus, changed and supplemented many things using his own ingenuity—

***

<sup>a)</sup> Many errors of this kind have been passed down even into the most recent editions. For example, at Book I, chapter 25, section 8, through a hypothetical error, the Aldine edition has <term>φαγήν</term> <note>phagēn; eating</note>; subsequent editors changed this to <term>σφαγήν</term> <note>sphagēn; slaughter</note>; Clavier and Bekker restored the true reading from the manuscripts: <term>φυγήν</term> <note>phygēn; flight/exile</note>.

<sup>b)</sup> <note>original: "Il y manque même quelques passages..."</note> "It even lacks some passages which are in all the manuscripts. One finds examples of this in Book VIII, chapter 41, and Book X, chapter 8." How recklessly these claims were made will be evident from our commentary.

# PREFACE
<page-num>V</page-num>

...so that we read certain passages of Pausanias that have been corrupted by the fault of the corrector. How true this accusation is will be best proven by examples; therefore, it seemed appropriate to review certain passages in which we clearly detect the hand of a corrector—and an unsuccessful one at that.

**V, 6, 3.** In his manuscript, Musurus <note>Marcus Musurus (c. 1470–1517), a Greek scholar and editor of the Aldine Press</note> found what the second Leiden and the Vienna manuscripts also show: *“through the place, for the most part, by sand”* <note>original Greek: διὰ χωρίου τὰ πλείονα ὑπὸ ψάμμιου</note>. A remedy was close at hand, but the scribe poorly restored the passage as *“covered by sand”* <note>original Greek: ὑπὸ ψάμμου κεκαλυμμένου</note>, a corruption that spread through our editions until the time of Bekker <note>August Immanuel Bekker (1785–1871), a German philologist</note>.

**VIII, 43, 2.** In all editions we read: *“and a part of the Romans of the city in our time, which was inhabited by Evander and the Arcadians who followed him, received the name Pallantium”* <note>original Greek: καὶ 'Ρωμαίων μέρος τῆς καθ' ἡμᾶς πόλεως, ὅπερ ᾠκεῖτο ὑπὸ τοῦ Εὐάνδρου καὶ 'Αρκάδων τῶν συνακολουθησάντων, ὄνομα ἔσχε Παλλάντιον</note>. In this case, the word *“which”* <note>original Greek: ὅπερ</note>, which is absent from all manuscripts, seems to have been inserted by Musurus, and not without skill. Therefore, having previously deleted this word, we had restored *“and it was inhabited by”* <note>original Greek: ᾠκεῖτο δὲ ὑπὸ</note> from the Angelicus manuscript. Now, having examined the first Leiden manuscript, we have written *“of the city which was inhabited”* <note>original Greek: πόλεως ὃ ᾠκεῖτο</note>.

**IX, 5, 14.** *“And it befell Thersander to die at the hands of Telephus”* <note>original Greek: καὶ τὸν Θέρσανδρον κατέλαβεν ἀποθανεῖν ὑπὸ Τηλέφου</note>. All subsequent editions have received this from the Aldine edition; but the word *“to die”* <note>original Greek: ἀποθανεῖν</note> is found in no manuscript. A deeper error seems to lie beneath.

**IX, 13, 5.** Along with the Aldine, the editions offer: *“And Skedasos (for when he went to Lacedaemon no justice was given to him), thus returning to Leuctra, killed himself”* <note>original Greek: καὶ ὁ Σκέδασος (ἐς Λακεδαίμονα γὰρ ἐλθόντι οὐδεμία ἐγένετο αὐτῷ δίκη) οὕτως ἐς τὰ Λεῦκτρα ἐπανήκων αὑτὸν διεργάζεται</note>. But the word *“for”* <note>original Greek: γὰρ</note> is absent from all manuscripts (we should not conclude from Bekker’s silence that this particle is read in the third Paris manuscript) and is undoubtedly due to Musurus’s correction. Therefore, having deleted the spurious word, we had previously written from conjecture: *“And Skedasos, since no justice was given to him when he went to Lacedaemon, thus...”* <note>original Greek: καὶ ὁ Σκέδασος ὡς ἐς Λακεδαίμονα ἐλθόντι οὐδεμία ἐγένετο αὐτῷ δίκη οὕτως cet.</note>, which we later saw excellently confirmed by the first Leiden manuscript.

**IX, 16, 6.** The editions read: *“It is the custom for them to open the temple once every year on fixed days”* <note>original Greek: ἐνιαυτοῦ δὲ ἅπαξ ἑκάστου τὸ ἱερὸν ἀνοιγνύναι σφίσιν ἔθος ἐν ἡμέραις τακταῖς</note>. Musurus added the word *“custom”* <note>original Greek: ἔθος</note> on his own authority. Therefore, having struck out this noun, we changed *“for them”* <note>original Greek: σφίσιν</note> into *“they say”* <note>original Greek: φασίν</note> as an easier remedy.

**IX, 20, 4.** *“The more venerable account concerning him says that the women of the Tanagraeans, having first been initiated into the rites of Dionysus, went down to the sea for the sake of purifications”* <note>original Greek: ὁ μὲν δὴ σεμνότερος ἐς αὐτὸν λόγος τὰς γυναῖκάς φησι τὰς Ταναγραίων πρώτας Διονύσου τὰ ὄργια μυηθείσας ἐπὶ θάλασσαν καταβῆναι καθαρσίων εἵνεκα</note>. The manuscripts vary in this place, but there is no trace of the word *“having been initiated”* <note>original Greek: μυηθείσας</note> in any of them. Since this word, inserted by Musurus, did not even seem suitable enough, it occurred to us to read: *“he says the women of the Tanagraeans went down to the sea for the rites of Dionysus.”* Now the first Leiden manuscript offers a better reading: *“they say the women”* (read: *“he says the women”*)...
