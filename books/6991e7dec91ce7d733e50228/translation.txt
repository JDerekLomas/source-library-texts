## A DISCOURSE upon Coins, by *Signor Bernardo Davanzati*, a *Gentleman of Florence*; being publicly spoken in the *Academy* there, in the year <note>original: "Anno"</note> 1588.

1. **T**HE sun and internal heat separate—as if by distillation—the best juices and substances within the earth. These are filtered <note>original: "percolated"</note> into appropriate veins and mines; there they are congealed, solidified, and ripened until, in time, they are made into *metals*. The most rare and perfect of these are *gold* and *silver*, which resemble the two great celestial bodies <note>original: "Luminaries"; referring to the sun and the moon.</note> of the world in their splendor and color. Neither fire nor rust will consume them. They are not subject to destruction by moths, worms, or decay, nor do they wear down <note>original: "waste"</note> much through use. They may be drawn into wire or beaten into leaves of incredible fineness, and they possess something divine—at least, certain *Indian* peoples <note>In the late 16th century, "Indian" typically referred to the Indigenous peoples of the Americas, where much of Europe's gold was being sourced.</note> think so, for they fast when they are digging for *gold* and forbid themselves the company of women and all other pleasures out of an ancient superstition.
