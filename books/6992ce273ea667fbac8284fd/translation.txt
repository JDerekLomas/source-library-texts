The first day of the lunar month is called the **New Moon** <term>New Moon (Shuo): the beginning of the lunar month when the moon is between the Earth and Sun</term>; the fifteenth day is called the **Full Moon** <term>Full Moon (Wang): when the moon is on the opposite side of the Earth from the Sun</term>. 

The day exactly halfway between the New Moon and the Full Moon is the **First Quarter**. The day exactly halfway between the Full Moon and the Dark Moon is the **Last Quarter**. 

The Sun belongs to the **Yang** <note>the active, solar, or "masculine" principle in Chinese cosmology</note>, while the Moon belongs to the **Yin** <note>the passive, lunar, or "feminine" principle</note>. The Yin is constantly being diminished or increased by the Yang. 

From the beginning of the month, the Moon travels to the right, gradually moving away from the Sun, and its light begins to grow. By the seventh or eighth day, half of the light is visible, shaped like a **bowstring** <note>original: 弦, *xian*; the Chinese term for quarter moons refers to the string of a bow</note>; therefore, it is called the **First Quarter**. 

By the fifteenth day, the Moon is at its furthest point from the Sun, allowing it to receive its full light. The Sun and Moon "gaze" at each other from opposite sides; this is called the **Full Moon**. 

After the middle of the month, the Moon gradually approaches the left side of the Sun, and its light begins to fade. By the twenty-second or twenty-third day, only half of the light remains, again resembling a bowstring; thus, it is called the **Last Quarter**. 

By the thirtieth day, the Sun and Moon meet in **conjunction**. The light of the Moon is completely consumed by the Sun; this is called the **Dark Moon** <term>Dark Moon (Hui): the final day of the lunar cycle</term>.

## The Direction of the Dipper and Celestial Motion

The Head <term>Kui: the four stars forming the bowl of the Big Dipper</term> is used at dusk; the Handle <gloss>Shao</gloss> is used at midnight; and the Balance <gloss>Heng: the central stars of the Dipper</gloss> is used at dawn to determine the seasonal direction.

The Handle <note>original: *Shao*</note> is what establishes the direction. The Dipper serves as the Emperor's Chariot, revolving in the central heavens to look down upon and govern the four seas. ## The Origins of the Laws of the Pitch-Pipes
<note>The "Pitch-pipes" (Lülü) were a series of twelve bamboo tubes used to standardize musical tuning and, by extension, the measurements of the entire empire, including the calendar.</note>

According to the "Treatise on Pitch-pipes and the Calendar" in the *Book of Han*: The Yellow Emperor <note>The legendary ancestor of the Chinese people</note> dispatched his music official, Ling Lun, to the crags of Mount Kunlun, west of the region of Great Xia <note>Bactria</note>. There, in the Valley of Xie, Ling Lun took bamboo and cut a length between two nodes. He blew into it, establishing the "Yellow Bell" note <note>original: "Huangzhong"; the fundamental tonic note of Chinese music, associated with the Winter Solstice</note>. He then crafted twelve tubes to mimic the songs of the phoenixes. The male phoenix sang six notes, and the female phoenix also sang six. The six masculine notes became the *律* <note>original: "Lü"; masculine pitch-pipes or "Laws"</note>, and the six feminine notes became the *呂* <note>original: "Lü" (different character); feminine companion-pipes</note>. Together, these are known as the Twelve Pitch-pipes, which are used to correspond to the twelve months of the year.

The measurement of the pitch-pipes is determined by the numerical proportions of the bells <note>Specifically the fundamental *Huangzhong* bell</note>, not merely by the size of millet grains. 

Regarding the millet standard: ninety grains of **black millet** <note>Original: *jǔshǔ*. A specific variety of millet used as the standard for measurement due to its consistent size.</note> laid crosswise equal the length of the **Yellow Bell** <term>Huangzhong: The foundational pitch-pipe from which all other measures are derived</term>. The width of one grain is defined as one **fen** <term>A "minute" or the smallest unit of length, roughly 1/10th of an inch</term>. Ten *fen* make an **inch** (*cun*); ten inches make a **foot** (*chi*); ten feet make a **rod** (*zhang*); and ten rods make a **lead** (*yin*). 

As for the measures of capacity: these are the **yue**, **he**, **sheng**, **dou**, and **hu**. The method uses the *yue* as a base to contain water to calibrate weight and volume. Ten *sheng* make one *dou*. 

In the diagram of the **hu** <term>A bushel, the largest capacity measure</term>, the vessel is depicted with two handles (or "ears") at the top and bottom. To the left is the *dou* measure; to the right are the *he* and the *yue*. 

The system is established as follows: below the *he*, the **balance beam** <note>The *heng*</note> begins the measurement of weights equivalent to ten thousand bells. One *yue* contains **one thousand two hundred grains of black millet**. 

Twenty-four **zhu** <term>The smallest unit of weight in this system</term> make one **liang** <term>A Chinese ounce or tael</term>. Sixteen *liang* make one **jin** <term>A Chinese pound or catty</term>, which consists of three hundred and eighty-four *zhu*. Thirty *jin* make one **jun**, a number corresponding to the three months of a season. Four *jun* make one **shi** <term>A "stone" or picul, the largest unit of weight</term>, which symbolizes the four seasons and the twelve months of the year.

# The Origin of the Seasonal Terms

Regarding the matter of the calendar, it is said that **Fu Xi** <note>A legendary cultural hero and sovereign of ancient China, credited with creating the hexagrams of the *I Ching*.</note> established the Eight Solar Nodes <term>Ba Jie: the two solstices, two equinoxes, and the four "beginnings" of the seasons</term> to correspond to the changing lines of the trigrams. The *Treatise on Harmonics and Calendars* from the *Book of Jin* states that Emperor Wu divided these eight nodes to initiate agricultural labor. The scholar **Dong Ba** argued that the "latent" period <note>Original: *Fu*. This refers to the "Dog Days" of summer.</note> refers to a time of hiding and withdrawal, creating the symbol of the "Three Geng" <note>The three stages of the hottest part of summer, calculated using the 'Geng' or seventh Heavenly Stem.</note> to mark the twenty-four solar terms. The commentary on the *Monthly Ordinances* by **Fan** <note>Likely referring to the Jin scholar Fan Ning.</note> states that the **Duke of Zhou** <note>A foundational figure of the Zhou Dynasty, revered for establishing Chinese ritual and administrative systems.</note> created the seasonal regulations, defining the twenty-four solar terms and the seventy-two pentads <term>Hou: five-day divisions that describe specific natural phenomena</term>. Thus, the system was initiated by Fu Xi and finalized by the Duke of Zhou.

---

### The Method of Intercalation

The solar path circles the heavens in three hundred and sixty-six days; one full rotation of the sundial <term>Gui: an ancient instrument that measures the sun's shadow</term> constitutes a year. While the solar year consists of three hundred and sixty-six days, the standard lunar year is reckoned as only three hundred and sixty days, leaving a remainder of six days. Furthermore, the six "short months" <note>Months in the lunar calendar that have 29 days instead of 30.</note> within a year create another six-day deficit. Thus, a year accumulates a total discrepancy of twelve days. Over three years, this surplus reaches thirty-six days; therefore, an intercalary month <term>Runyue: an extra "leap" month added to the lunar calendar to keep it synchronized with the solar seasons</term> is established to correct the timing. With six days still remaining from that calculation, after another two years, twenty-four more days accumulate, which—when combined with the previous six—allows for the placement of another intercalary month.

In the tenth year of the <gloss>Datong era of the Liang</gloss>, they again adjusted the system to use 180 marks, increasing or decreasing the count as needed. From the Wei, Qi, and Sui dynasties through the <gloss>beginning of the current dynasty</gloss> <note>Likely referring to the Tang or Song dynasty, depending on the manuscript's era.</note>, the duration of day and night was set at 100 marks, strictly following the ancient regulations. 

The method for measuring this involves four reservoirs: first, the **Night Celestial Pool**; second, the **Day Celestial Pool**; third, the **Leveling Jar**; and fourth, the **Water Sea**. The weight of the water is calculated as one catty and eight ounces, totaling forty-eight units. One indicator rod corresponds to one solar term <term>Qi: the twenty-four divisions of the solar year</term>. The entire year is represented by a total of 2,160,000 minutes, all of which are carved onto the rods. By opening the aperture to allow the water to flow down, the central floating indicator rises to mark the heights of the twenty-four solar terms. 

Generally, each solar term differs by two and a half minutes. The day is at its shortest during the Winter Solstice and reaches equilibrium during the Spring Equinox. <gloss>Start of Spring</gloss> <note>original: "Lìchūn," the first of the 24 solar terms, usually falling around February 4th</note> This is the beginning of the twenty-four solar terms. The *Book of Rites* in the "Monthly Ordinances" says: "Bring out the clay ox to show the farmers whether the season will be early or late." The commentary states: "If the Start of Spring falls before the full moon of the twelfth month, the staff held by the clay figure <note>referring to the 'Goumang' or Spirit of Spring that accompanies the ox</note> is positioned toward the front to signal an early farming season. The *Commentary on the Monthly Ordinances* says: "The twelfth month is associated with the earthly branch *Chou*. ### New Year’s Day

The first day of the month is known as **New Year’s Day** <note>original: "Yuánrì"</note>. The *Records of the Grand Historian* states that the Xia dynasty began the year with the month of the Tiger <note>the 1st lunar month</note>, the Shang dynasty began with the month of the Ox <note>the 12th month</note>, and the Zhou dynasty began with the month of the Rat <note>the 11th month</note>. The *Comprehensive Canon* <note>original: "Tōngdiǎn," an institutional history of China</note> notes that Emperor Gao of the Han dynasty designated the tenth month as the start of the year after the pacification of the Qin state. It was during this time that the rituals for the court officials to offer New Year greetings were established. Later, Emperor Wu of Han returned to the Xia dynasty’s calendar, setting the start of the year back to the first day of the month of the Tiger. Because the First Emperor of Qin was born in the first month and named **Zheng**, the name of the month was thereafter avoided as a taboo and the pronunciation of "the first year" <gloss>pronounced in the level tone</gloss> was altered.

The *Apocryphal Book of Rites: Monthly Ordinances* says: On New Year’s Day, one should perform sacrifices to the ancestors and the deceased father. Once the wine has been offered and the spirits have descended, the family members—both high and low in rank—line up before the ancestral shrine. Each person partakes of **Pepper and Cypress Wine**, serving it in order from the youngest to the oldest. 

The *Record of the Seasons* says: **Tusu** is actually the name of a grass hut. In ancient times, a man lived in such a hut and, every New Year’s Eve, he would give the local villagers a packet of medicine to be steeped in a well. On New Year’s Day, they would draw the water, place it in wine vessels, and the whole family would drink it to avoid falling ill. People today have the formula but no longer recognize the origin of the name, simply calling it "Tusu wine."

The *Classic of Mountains and Seas* says: At the northeast gate of Mount Dushuo in the Eastern Sea lies the **Ghost Gate**, through which ten thousand ghosts pass in and out. There are two spirits, one named **Shenshu** and the other **Yulü**, who are tasked with overseeing and leading the host of ghosts; they capture any who harm humans. Consequently, the Yellow Emperor established a ritual based on this image: he erected peach-wood boards on doors and painted the likenesses of Shenshu and Yulü to ward off malevolent spirits. Peach wood is something that all ghosts fear.

The *Record of Local Customs* says: On New Year’s Day, one eats the **Five Pungent Vegetables** to refine the physical form. <gloss>The commentary states:</gloss> These five pungent vegetables are meant to assist in the vitality of the five internal organs. These five consist of: large garlic, small garlic, chives, rape, and coriander.

## Human Day

The seventh day of the first month is known as **Human Day** <note>original: "Rénrì"</note>. The *Book of Divination* <note>original: "Zhànshū"</note> by Dongfang Shuo <note>a famous scholar and advisor to Emperor Wu of Han</note> states: the first day is the day of the chicken, the second the dog, the third the pig, the fourth the sheep, the fifth the ox, the sixth the horse, the seventh the human, and the eighth the grain. If the day is clear, the creature it governs will thrive; if it is overcast, there will be calamity. Dong Xun <note>a Jin dynasty scholar</note> says that on the first day, one raises chickens at the gate, and on the seventh, one carves human figures to place above the door; this is truly the reason for it. The *Record of the Seasons in Jing and Chu* <note>a 6th-century work on festive customs</note> states: on Human Day, people use seven types of vegetables to make a pottage and cut colorful silks into "flower victories" <note>original: "huāshèng," a type of ornamental hair accessory</note> to give to one another; some also carve thin gold leaf into human figures or auspicious symbolic patterns.

## The Lantern Festival

"Yuan" refers to the fifteenth day of the first month, known as the **Lantern Festival** <note>original: "Shàngyuán," literally the "Upper Prime"</note>. The *Treatise on Music* in the *Records of the Grand Historian* says that the Han imperial house performed sacrifices to the Great One <note>original: "Tàiyī," the supreme celestial deity</note> starting at dusk and lasting until dawn. A commentary notes that the modern custom of viewing lanterns on the night of the full moon in the first month is a continuation of this tradition. In the *Miscellaneous Records of Emperor Minghuang* by Zheng Chuhui of the Tang dynasty, it is recorded that while the Emperor was at the Eastern Capital <note>Luoyang</note> on the night of the full moon, he moved his retinue to the Shangyang Palace. He ordered the setting of blazing torches that burned continuously through the day and night and constructed a lantern tower of thirty bays made of silk and colorful fabrics. It stood one hundred and fifty feet high and was draped with pearls and jade; when a light breeze arrived, they would chime together melodiously. The lanterns were shaped like dragons, phoenixes, tigers, and leopards. <insert>Middle and Harmony</insert> The first day of the second month is the **Zhonghe Festival** <note>The Festival of Middle and Harmony</note>. "Middle" <note>original: "zhōng"</note> refers to the midpoint of the "Three Yang" <note>the three months of Spring</note>; "Harmony" <note>original: "hé"</note> refers to the blending of benevolent vital energy <note>qì</note>. The state establishes festivals according to the seasons, providing the necessary items and performing the proper rituals. The *Record of Seasonal Rites in the Capital* <note>original: "Niǎnjià Xià Suìshíjì"</note> states: During the reign of Emperor Dezong <note>779–805 AD</note>, the official Li Mi requested that the first day of the second month be designated as the Zhonghe Festival. Because of this, the Emperor bestowed ceremonial rulers upon high officials and imperial relatives, a gesture known as "Measurement and Proportion" <note>original: "cáidù," symbolizing the regulation of the empire</note>. Among the common people, seeds of various grains, melons, and fruits are placed in blue silk bags and given as gifts, a practice called "Offering the Seeds of Life" <note>original: "xiànshēngzǐ"</note>. In the villages and neighborhoods, spring wine is brewed to offer sacrifices to Gou Mang <note>the ancient deity of the East and the season of Spring</note>. Officials present agricultural treatises to the Emperor to demonstrate their commitment to the "Fundamental Occupation" <note>farming</note>. Following this, the Emperor issued an official decree to codify these customs.

The Earth God Festival <note>original: "She," the ritual honoring the god of the soil</note> has no fixed date. The *Book of Rites* <note>original: "Liji," a core Confucian text on ritual propriety</note> in its "Monthly Ordinances" chapter states: "Select an auspicious first day to command the people to perform sacrifices to the Earth and Grain." The commentary clarifies: "The 'first day' refers to a 'Wu' day" <note>the 'Wu' day is the fifth day in the ten-day cycle of the traditional Chinese calendar</note>. Nowadays, the Spring Sacrifice usually falls within the second month, and the Autumn Sacrifice usually falls within the eighth month. From the "Beginning of Spring" solar term, the fifth "Wu" day is designated as the Spring Sacrifice; from the "Beginning of Autumn," the fifth "Wu" day is the Autumn Sacrifice. If a "Wu" day falls exactly on the start of the "Beginning of Spring" or "Beginning of Autumn," that day is not counted in the tally.

The "Meaning of Sacrifices" chapter of the *Book of Rites* says: "The son of the Gonggong clan, named Goulong, served as the Sovereign of the Soil. Because he was able to level the waters and land, he was sacrificed to as the God of the Earth." The *Apocrypha of the Classic of Filial Piety* <note>original: "Xiaojing Wei," a mystical commentary on the Classic of Filial Piety</note> says: "The Earth God is the master of the land. Because the land is vast and cannot be sacrificed to in its entirety, mounds of earth are raised as altars to repay the land's merit."

The Spring Sacrifice is a prayer for the growth of the grain; thus, the "Clearing the Grass" section of the *Classic of Poetry* says: "In spring, the ritual plowing is performed to pray to the Gods of Soil and Grain." The Autumn Sacrifice is an offering of thanks for the ripening of the grain; thus, the "Sharp Plowshares" section of the *Classic of Poetry* says: "In autumn, gratitude is offered to the Gods of Soil and Grain." The *Record of the Seasons of Jing and Chu* <note>a 6th-century work on seasonal customs</note> states: "Neighbors gather together to form a <term>Zhi: a community fellowship or religious association</term> for the festival. They offer livestock and wine at a shrine beneath a tree, first sacrificing to the god and then sharing the blessed sacrificial meat." Today, prefectures and counties also perform sacrifices to the Gods of Soil and Grain at raised altars.
