### The author’s defense of the Hammer of Witches.

**S**ince, among the calamities of this failing age—which, alas, we do not merely read about but experience everywhere—the ancient rising sun <note>original: "vetus oriens"; likely referring to Lucifer or the fallen state of the East</note>, dissolved by the ruin of its own fall, does not cease to infect the Church with various contagions of heresy. This Church was made fruitful by the new rising sun, the man Christ Jesus, through the sprinkling of his own blood. Yet, the enemy strives especially at this time, when the evening of the world declines toward its setting and the malice of men increases; he knows in great wrath (as Job and the Book of Revelation testify) that he has but a short time. For this reason, he has caused a certain unusual heretical depravity to grow in the Lord’s field: I speak of the heresy of witches <note>original: "maleficarum"</note>, named after the primary group in which it is known to thrive. While this heresy plots with innumerable assaults, one thing is seen in every instance: it is terrible to think upon, utterly abominable to the accused, and hateful to all the Christian faithful. It is fulfilled through works. For by a pact with hell and a league with death, they subject themselves to a most foul servitude to fulfill their filthiness. Furthermore, there are those daily afflictions which—God permitting and the power of demons concurring—are inflicted by them upon men, cattle, and the fruits of the earth. Among these evils, we inquisitors, **James Sprenger**, together with my dearest colleague deputed by the Apostolic See for the extermination of such a pestilential heresy—though we are the least among the professors of divine eloquence serving under the Order of Preachers <note>The Dominicans</note>—have judged it worthy to submit our pious shoulders to this work above all other remedies. We do this with a pious and mournful affection, weighing what remedy or solace might be administered as a salutary antidote for those deserving it. We trust in the honey-flowing bounty of Him who gives to all abundantly, and who, taking a coal from the altar with tongs, touches and cleanses the lips of the imperfect to lead all things to the desired end. Clearly, since in the works of men nothing is made so useful or lawful that some ruin cannot be brought upon it, and since our small wits do not reach the sharpness of truth unless they have been thoroughly rubbed by the file of another’s depravity, we confidently approach the struggle against anyone who thinks we should be rebuked for the novelty of this work. Let such a person know that this very work is at once both new and old, brief and yet extensive. It is certainly old in its subject matter and authority, but new in its compilation and the gathering of these things together. It is brief because of the compression of many authors into a short space; it is nonetheless long because of the immense multitude of the subject matter and the inscrutable malice of witches. Nor do we say this to presumptuously detract from the writings of other authors or to boastfully and vainly extol our own work, since few or almost no things have been added from our own genius. **W**herefore, let it be judged not as our work, but rather the work of those from whose sayings almost every single part is woven. **F**or this reason, we have not undertaken to compose poems or to extend sublime theories, but proceeding in the manner of excerptors, **W**e approach this task for the honor of the highest Trinity and individual Unity. We divide it into three principal parts: the origin, the progress, and the end, naming the treatise **The Hammer of Witches**. We leave the collection of the work to our colleague, but the execution of it to those upon whom a most harsh judgment looms, because they are seen to be appointed by God for the punishment of the wicked and the praise of the good. To Him be all honor and glory forever and ever. **Amen.**

# On the Bull of Innocent VIII

### Regarding the Bull of Pope Innocent VIII <note>Innocent VIII (reigned 1484–1492) issued the bull *Summis desiderantes affectibus*, which granted formal ecclesiastical authority to inquisitors to hunt witches.</note> recently issued against the heresy of sorceresses, forty-eight questions are to be discussed: three main points are to be explained. **First**, the origin. **Second**, the progress. **Third**, the final remedy. The origin regarding their multiplication; the progress regarding the execution of their works; the final remedy regarding the extermination of that heresy.

**The first part** concerns three things that concur to produce a sorcerous effect: namely, the demon, the sorcerer <note>original: *maleficus*; though the text later focuses on women, the Latin here uses the masculine/general form for a practitioner of harmful magic</note>, and divine permission. It contains eighteen questions in total, four of which concern the power of the demon, and the rest concern their works.

**The first** is the introduction to the entire work: **Whether** asserting that sorceresses exist is considered so Catholic <note>In this context, "Catholic" means "universally accepted Christian truth."</note> that to stubbornly defend the opposite is altogether heretical.

**The second:** Whether it is Catholic to assert that for a sorcerous effect to occur, a demon must always concur with the sorcerer, or whether one can produce such an effect without the aid of the other.

**The third:** Whether it is Catholic to assert that effects of this kind are procured by *incubus* and *succubus* demons <note>Demons believed to take male and female forms to have sexual intercourse with humans.</note> in such a way that even true human beings are procreated by such demons for the multiplication and origin of sorcerers.

**The fourth question:** Whether it is Catholic to assert that the acts of *incubus* and *succubus* demons are appropriate only to the lowest spirits.

**The fifth:** Whether it can in any way be considered Catholic that the origin and multiplication of sorcerous works proceed from the influence of celestial bodies <note>i.e., astrology</note> without the aid of demons; or from separate substances like the movers of the celestial spheres; or even from the malice of men concurring with voices and words through some power of the stars.

**The sixth** regarding sorcerers concurring with demons: **Why** women are found to be more infected by this heresy than men. The nature of the women who are involved above others is explained through the five following questions.

**The seventh:** Whether sorceresses, by the power of demons, are able to incite the minds of men to inordinate hatred or love; and on the method of presenting this matter in sermons to the people.

**The eighth:** Whether they can impede the generative power or the venereal act <note>Sexual intercourse</note> and cast spells upon it, with an incidental question: why that act is sometimes impeded in respect to one person and not in respect to another.

**The ninth:** Whether they are accustomed to take away the male members by a prestigious illusion <note>A "prestige" was a sensory deception or hallucination caused by a demon.</note> as if they were torn away from the bodies, with certain other annexed difficulties.

**The tenth:** Whether they can transform humans into bestial forms, with another incidental difficulty.

**The eleventh:** Concerning sorcerous midwives who kill the offspring conceived in the womb or outside it in various ways.

**The twelfth** regarding divine permission, which must concur with the demon and the sorceress: **Whether** commending divine permission in these works of sorceresses is so Catholic that to argue the opposite—namely, to find fault with it—is altogether heretical.

**The thirteenth**, also incidental, regarding two divine permissions: concerning the fall of the devil and of our first parents <note>Adam and Eve</note>, from which all works of sorcerers are justly permitted.

**The fourteenth:** Whether, notwithstanding the foregoing, the sins of sorcerers are more grievous than the sins of the evil angels and our first parents. This entire matter is suitable for preaching, with a declaration that they deserve the heaviest punishments even in the present life, beyond all the shameful people of the world.

**The fifteenth:** Whether on account of the sins of sorcerers, the innocent are often bewitched.

**The sixteenth:** Whether the heresy of sorceresses exceeds all other species of superstition.

**The seventeenth** is explanatory of the fourteenth, comparing the gravity of the crime in sorcerers to any sins of demons.

**The eighteenth** against five arguments of laymen: that God would not permit such great power to the devil and sorcerers; and in this matter, the end is joined to its beginning, as this last question is annexed to the first.

**The second part** of the work contains sixteen chapters under two questions, one of which is placed at the beginning and the other at the end. The first is on preservative remedies; the other on remedies for removing sorcery. The intermediate chapters treat the method of proceeding used by sorcerers in inflicting injuries.

## The First Question in Order

Whether the assertion that witches exist is so Catholic a truth that to obstinately defend its opposite is altogether heretical. And it is argued that it is not Catholic to assert anything concerning them, according to the Canon Law <note>original: "xxvi. q. v. epi." referring to the Canon Episcopi, a 10th-century decree</note>: "Whoever believes that any creature can be made, or changed for the better or worse, or transformed into another species or likeness by anyone other than the Creator of all things, is worse than a pagan and an infidel." Since such things are reported to be done by witches, to assert them is therefore not Catholic, but heretical. Furthermore, no effect of witchcraft exists in the world. This is proven: because if it did exist, it would be by the operation of demons. But to assert that demons can effect or hinder bodily transformations does not seem Catholic, because if so, they could make the whole world tremble. Furthermore, every bodily alteration—for instance, regarding the causing of sickness or health—is reduced to local motion. This is clear from the eighth book of the *Physics* <note>Aristotle's Physics</note>, which states that the first motion is that of the heavens. But demons cannot vary the motion of the heavens. Dionysius <note>Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite</note> says in his letter to Polycarp that this belongs to God alone. Therefore, it seems they can cause no transformation, at least no true one, in bodies; and it is necessary to reduce such transformations to some hidden cause. Furthermore, just as the work of God is stronger than the work of the devil, so is His creation. But witchcraft, if it existed in the world, would certainly be a work of the devil against the creation of God. Therefore, just as it is unlawful to assert that the superstitious work of the devil exceeds the work of God, so it is unlawful to believe that the creatures and works of God in men and beasts can be corrupted by the works of the devil. Furthermore, that which is subject to bodily power does not have the power of impressing itself upon bodies. But demons are subject to the powers of the stars, which is clear from the fact that certain enchanters observe specific constellations for invoking demons. Therefore, they do not have the power of impressing anything upon bodies, and thus witches have even less. Likewise,

demons do not work except through art. But art cannot give true form. Hence in the book *On Minerals* <note>attributed to Albertus Magnus</note> it is said: "Let the authors of alchemy know that species cannot be transmuted." Therefore, demons working through art cannot induce true qualities of health or sickness. But if these are real, they have some other hidden cause without the help of demons and witches. But to the contrary, in the Decrees <note>Canon Law</note> it says: "If by sorcery and witchcraft, not infrequently by the hidden just judgment of God permitting it and the devil preparing it, etc." It speaks of the obstacle of witchcraft regarding the marriage act, where three things concur: namely, the witchcraft, the devil, and the permission of God. Furthermore, the stronger can act upon that which is less strong. But the power of a demon is stronger than any bodily power. <margin><unclear>Faded title: On the power of demons and the reality of witchcraft</unclear></margin>

Some believe that demons do not exist except in the imagination of the common people, so that the terrors a person creates for themselves out of their own imagination are attributed to a demon. **And** they also believe that from a powerful imagination, certain figures appear in the senses such as a person thinks about, and they believe that demons or even witches are seen then. But true faith repudiates these ideas, through which we believe that angels fell from heaven and that demons exist. Therefore, we also confess that by the subtlety of their nature, they can do many things that we cannot. **And** those who induce them to perform such acts are called witches <note>original: "malefici" - practitioners of harmful magic</note>. Because, indeed, infidelity in a baptized person is called heresy, such people are rebuked for heresy. **Two** other errors exist among those who do not deny demons and their natural power, but disagree among themselves regarding the effect of witchcraft and the witch herself. One side concedes that the witch actually cooperates toward an effect, yet not a real one but a fantastic <note>imaginary or illusory</note> one. The other side, on the contrary, concedes a real effect upon the victim but thinks the witch only cooperates in a fantastic manner. **The foundation** of this error they have taken from two passages of the Canon <note>referring to the Canon Episcopi, a medieval legal text</note> found in Question 26, Case 5. **Where** first they rebuke women who believe they ride at night with Diana or Herodiana <note>Diana was the Roman goddess of the hunt; Herodiana refers to a legendary figure often associated with Herodias, linked in folklore to nocturnal processions</note>. Let the Canon be inspected there. And because such things often happen only fantastically and in the imagination, those in error judge that it happens this way regarding all other effects. **Second**, **Because** it is contained there that whoever believes or asserts that any creature can be made, or changed for the better or worse, or transformed into another species or likeness by anyone other than God the creator of all, is an infidel and worse than a pagan. **Hence**, because of what is said there—**or changed for the worse**—they say that the effect is not real in the bewitched person but only fantastic. **But** that these errors savor of heresy and fight against the sound understanding of the Canon is shown first by divine law, as well as ecclesiastical and civil law. This is shown in general, and finally in particular by expressing the words of the Canon, although this will be deduced more clearly in the following question.

<column-break/>

**For** divine law in many places commands that witches are not only to be avoided but also to be killed. It would not impose such punishments if they did not truly concur with demons toward real effects and injuries. **For** bodily death is not inflicted without a bodily and grave sin, although it is different with the death of the soul, which can arise from a fantastic illusion or even temptation. **This** is the opinion of Saint Thomas <note>Thomas Aquinas, the influential 13th-century theologian</note> in the second book, distinction 7, in the question: **Whether** it is evil to use the help of demons. **For** Deuteronomy 18 commands all witches and enchanters to be killed. **Leviticus** 19 also says: **The soul** that turns aside to magicians and soothsayers and commits fornication with them, I will set my face against that soul and kill it from the midst of my people. **And** again in chapter 20: **A man** or woman in whom there is a prophetic <note>original: "phitonicus" - relating to a Pythian or divining spirit</note> or divining spirit shall die; they shall overwhelm them with stones. **And** they are called "pythons" in whom the demon works wonderful effects. **Furthermore**, it was because of this sin that Ahaziah died in his sickness (2 Kings 1) and Saul (1 Chronicles 10). **Finally**, let the commentators on the divine words—whatever they have handed down in their writings on the second book, distinctions 7 and 8, concerning the power of demons and their magic arts—let the writings of any doctor on the second book of the *Sentences* be inspected. And it will be found, with no one disagreeing, that magicians and witches, by the power of demons with God's permission, can produce wonderful effects that are not fantastic. **I remain silent** about various other places in which Saint Thomas treats these works extensively, as in the *Summa contra Gentiles*, book 3, chapters 1 and 2; in the first part, question 114, article 4; **And** in the second part of the second part, questions 88 and 89. **Finally**, let the commentators and glossators be inspected regarding the magicians of Pharaoh in **Exodus** 7. **Let** the sayings of Augustine in *The City of God*, book 18, chapter 18, be inspected, **And** in the second book of *On Christian Doctrine*. **Similarly** with other doctors, to contradict all of whom is utterly absurd; nor can one be excused from the vice of heresy.
