# Jujube

The Jujube is classified as a "superior" grade herb in the *Classic of Herbal Medicine* <note>original: "本經" (*Běnjīng*), referring to the *Shénnóng Běncǎo Jīng*, the foundational text of Chinese pharmacology which categorizes non-toxic, life-prolonging substances as "superior".</note>. The *Erya* <note>The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, dating back to the late 1st millennium BCE, which contains important early botanical definitions.</note> lists several distinct varieties in great detail. When the fruit is dried, it is known as the "Great Jujube" <note>original: "大棗" (*dàzǎo*)</note> and is incorporated into medicinal formulas. Furthermore, the kernel within the pit <term>Seed Kernel <gloss>the medicinal embryo found inside the hard stone of the fruit</gloss></term>, the heartwood, the leaves, the roots, and the bark of the tree all have specific therapeutic applications <term>Main Indications <gloss>the primary symptoms or conditions a specific part of the plant is intended to treat</gloss></term>.

# Grape

<note>This page is dominated by a refined woodcut illustration characteristic of Ming and Qing dynasty encyclopedias. It depicts a heavy cluster of ripening grapes hanging beside a large, five-lobed leaf with intricate vein work. In traditional Chinese thought, the grape was often associated with fertility and abundance due to its many seeds and clumping growth.</note>

Grapes are classified under the "Fruit Category" in this volume. They were famously brought back to China by the Han dynasty explorer Zhang Qian from the western regions of Dayuan <note>An ancient state in the Ferghana Valley of Central Asia, famous for its "heavenly horses" and viticulture.</note>. 

The plant is a woody vine. The leaves have three to five lobes with serrated edges. In the summer, it bears small, yellow-green flowers that develop into clusters of berries. There are several varieties mentioned in historical texts: some are purple, some are green, and some are long like "mare’s teats" <term>Mare’s Teat Grape <gloss>a descriptive name for the *mǎnǎizhī* variety, a long, oval grape known for its sweetness</gloss></term>. 

Beyond their use as a luxury fruit and for making wine, grapes are highly valued in the medicinal tradition:
*   **The Fruit:** Used to strengthen the bones and tendons, harmonize the stomach, and nourish the "Qi" <term>Qi <gloss>the vital life force or energy that flows through the body in traditional Chinese medicine</gloss></term>.
*   **The Root:** Often boiled in a decoction to treat swelling or to alleviate "dampness" in the joints.

## Grape

The grape <note>original: "葡萄" (*pútáo*)</note> is listed in the *Classic* <note>original: "本經" (*Běnjīng*), referring to the *Divine Farmer's Materia Medica*, China's earliest pharmacological text.</note> as a **superior grade** <term>Superior Grade <gloss>a category of medicinal substances that are non-toxic and intended to promote longevity and vitality</gloss></term>. 

There are two varieties: the round and the long. They are extremely abundant in the Northwest <note>Historically referring to the arid regions and the Silk Road corridors where viticulture flourished.</note>. They are also found occasionally in the Jiangnan region <note>The lush, humid area south of the lower reaches of the Yangtze River.</note>; however, the fruits there are mostly round and purple in color, and their flavor is also inferior <note>The author notes that the damp climate of the south produces less flavorful fruit compared to the sun-drenched, dry climate of the Northwest.</note>.

## Wild Grape (Appendix) <note>original: "蘡薁" (*yīngyù*); refers to wild species of grapes such as *Vitis bryoniifolia*.</note>

The Wild Grape is indeed the common "wild grape." The Farmer of Yulou <note>The pen name of the author, Wu Qijun (1789–1847).</note> says: In the Jiangnan region <note>The prosperous area south of the lower Yangtze River.</note>, cultivated grapes are scarce, while the Wild Grape is extremely common and cheap. However, if one has never eaten the "Mare’s Teat" <term>Mare's Teat (maru): a prized, elongated variety of *Vitis vinifera* introduced from Central Asia</term> grapes from the Western Regions, how could they know that beyond the wild species, there are such extraordinary varieties?

The ancient scholar Tao Yinju <note>Tao Hongjing (456–536 AD), a renowned Daoist physician and naturalist.</note> once suggested that the "grape" mentioned in ancient texts was likely just the Wild Grape. This was simply because he had never seen the superior fruits of the western gardens, which are so effective at quenching thirst and dispelling stagnant heat.

Nowadays, northern varieties are gradually being transplanted to the south. Sometimes they are even delivered by "flying couriers" <note>A reference to high-speed horse relays used to deliver fresh produce to the elite.</note>; unlike the lychee, the grape's color and fragrance do not spoil easily during travel. The wealthy and noble wait for the arrival of the west wind <note>The autumn breeze, signaling the grape harvest season.</note> to feast upon them. Meanwhile, those wild berries—small as peas and purple-black in color—are left for the young cowherds and the birds to peck and nibble at.

The grapes produced in Yunnan are as large as dates; they cannot be dried into raisins, so they are sold in distant lands. Since that region borders Tibet, the quality of the grapes there is naturally excellent. There is also a variety called the **Stone Grape** <note>original: "石蒲萄" (*shípútáo*)</note> that grows on rocky cliffs; it is said to have the medicinal property of being able to draw out the eruptions of smallpox. <note>In traditional medicine, "bringing out" a rash or pox was sometimes seen as a way to vent toxins from the body.</note>

# Mandarin Orange

The Mandarin and the Pomelo were both listed in the "Superior Grade" of the <term>Classic of Materia Medica (Shennong Bencao Jing): The foundational text of Chinese herbal medicine, which categorized the most beneficial and non-toxic substances as "Superior."</term> The *Supplementary Records* <note>original: "Mingyi Bielu," a 3rd-century expansion of the Classic of Materia Medica</note> and various other commentaries all combined the Mandarin and the Pomelo into a single category. 

However, the *Augmented Materia Medica* <note>original: "Bencao Yanyi," a Song Dynasty work known for its critical botanical corrections</note> suggests that the inclusion of the word "Pomelo" in those earlier listings was a clerical error. 

Upon investigation, Mandarin Orange Peel <note>Known in medicine as "Chenpi," the aged rind used to regulate "qi" or internal energy</note> is used very extensively. The *Classic of Materia Medica* also states: "Another name [for the plant] is Mandarin Orange Peel." Kou's explanation <note>Referring to the physician Kou Zongshi, who argued for the distinct medicinal roles of different citrus varieties</note> is the most accurate. 

Therefore, I have now entered the Mandarin Orange under the heading of the *Classic of Materia Medica*, while the Pomelo has been separated into its own distinct entry, which is appended hereafter.

### Pomelo <gloss>Appendix</gloss>

According to the *Erya* <term>Erya: The oldest surviving Chinese dictionary and encyclopedia, dating to the 3rd century BCE</term>, the Pomelo is referred to by the names *Jia* or *Tiao*. It was the scholar Rihuazi <note>A specialist in medicinal substances (materia medica) active during the 10th century</note> who first formally documented its functions and primary medicinal uses. It is used to aid digestion and neutralize the toxic effects of alcohol. It cures bad breath in those who have been drinking and clears "foul vapors" <note>original: "E qi," referring to harmful humors or digestive stagnation</note> from the stomach and intestines. It is also a remedy for pregnant women suffering from a loss of appetite or a persistent bland taste in the mouth.

These fruits are extremely abundant in the southern regions. Those with red pulp sacs <note>original: "Hong nang," referring to the internal juice vesicles or "flesh" of the fruit</note> are considered the highest quality. 

The scholar Li Shizhen <note>The most famous pharmacologist of the Ming Dynasty, author of the *Compendium of Materia Medica*</note> categorized the *Zhuluan* <note>original: "Zhuluan," likely the Sour Orange or Citron</note> and the *Mitong* <note>original: "Mitong," literally "Honey-tube," a sweet variety</note> as a single species, but this classification is not quite accurate. 

Furthermore, the *Erya* commentary on the names *Ji* and *Jia* describes the Pomelo as being as large as a bowl. The *Zhengyi* <note>A title for "Rectified Meanings," a standard scholarly commentary</note> states that what Fan Chengda <note>A famous Song Dynasty geographer and poet known for his descriptions of southern China</note> called the "Stinking Pomelo of Guangnan" is as large as a melon and possesses an exceptionally thick rind. 

I note that this is the same fruit known in the Fujian region as the *Paozi* <note>original: "Paozi," literally "bubbling fruit," perhaps referring to its thick, airy rind</note>. Its flavor is intensely sour, though some specimens are edible. They are most commonly used as decorative offerings on altars. These and the red-fleshed Pomelo are of one general category, but represent two distinct varieties.

# Red Pomelo Peel <gloss>Juhong</gloss>

Red Pomelo Peel <gloss>Juhong</gloss> is produced in Huazhou, Guangdong. The fruit is as large as a standard pomelo and its flesh is sweet. The peel is prepared by scraping it to create the medicinal "Red Peel." Those produced within the city walls of Huazhou are considered the highest quality, but genuine specimens are extremely difficult to obtain. Common lore suggests that Huazhou produces "sliding stone" <term>Talc: A soft mineral; the text suggests the soil's mineral content influences the plant's medicinal efficacy</term> and that these trees grow among the rocks. Because of this, the peel has extraordinary power to resolve phlegm. 

Counterfeit versions are common, often made from ordinary pomelo peels processed in Huazhou to mimic the authentic product. Ancient scholars maintained that "Aged Peel" <gloss>Chenpi</gloss> must specifically come from the Mandarin orange; they noted that while bitter orange <note>original: 橙 (Cheng)</note> might be acceptable, the common pomelo is entirely different in both nature and flavor. However, the specific variety produced in Huazhou is shaped quite differently from a standard Mandarin orange.

That old tree withered and died long ago, but new growth has emerged from its roots...

# Gorgon Nut <note>Commonly known as the Fox Nut or *Euryale ferox*.</note>

The Gorgon Nut is listed as a "superior grade" substance in the *Classic of Materia Medica* <note>original: *Bencao Jing*</note>. Its tender stems can be used as a vegetable. It is known by many names: the *Lou*, the *Jiyong* <note>original: 雞壅; literally "chicken-obstruction"</note>, the "Wild Goose Head," the "Bird Head," and the "Wild Goose Peck." It is truly one plant with several names. 

The tender parts of the stem are called *Louchen*. The leaves are significantly large and possess a distinctive wrinkled, puckered texture that resembles the surface of boiling water; these are called "Gorgon disks" <note>original: *Qianpan*</note>. The plant produces a bud that blossoms with a pointed tip resembling a bird’s beak, known as the "Gorgon beak" <note>original: *Qianzui*</note>.

A poet of the Tang Dynasty once wrote: 
*Small pouches of purple silk, tightly crinkled and bright,*
*A handful of pearls hidden within the belly of a water-spider.*
<note>original: "紫羅小囊光緊蹙，一掬珍珠藏蛸腹"</note>

These lines describe its fruit. The seeds can be boiled into porridge, ground into flour, or simply chewed. Even the root has a flavor similar to taro; when boiled and eaten, the entire plant is so fragrant that nothing is left to waste.

The Honorable Ouyang Xiu <note>original: Ouyang Wenzhong Gong; a famous Song Dynasty statesman and poet</note> wrote in a poem:
*Gardeners compete to harvest the new buds,*
*Like opening a clam to find pearls from the seabed.*
*In the capital, all things are prized for their freshness,*
*And their price is so high they are compared to gems.*

He further wrote:
*I remember my younger days spent among the rivers and lakes,*
*Singing loudly from a wild boat amidst the water chestnuts and lilies.*
*The fragrance was fresh, the flavor complete, picked with my own hands;*
*Clean as jade, polished by the sand, soft and beautiful.*

Though his body was near the "Imperial Gates" <term>Wei Que <note>The monumental gates of the palace, representing a career in high government office.</note></term>, his heart roamed the rivers and lakes. "Living in the capital is no easy feat" <note>original: *Changan ju buyi*; a famous idiom referring to the high cost and difficulty of city life</note>—in this regard, the past and the present are just like "badgers from the same mound" <note>original: *Yi qiu zhi he*; an idiom meaning they are no different from one another</note>. Such was the spirit of his poetry.

The peach is categorized as a "lower-grade" substance in the *Classic of the Materia Medica* <note>original: "Benjing" (Shennong Bencao Jing). There are numerous varieties of peaches. Generally, they are divided into those grown for their fruit and those grown for their blossoms. The various parts of the tree are vital to the traditional pharmacopoeia. ## Hawthorn

<term>Hawthorn (Shānzhā)</term>: The fruit of the *Crataegus* species, known for its sour taste and its ability to improve digestion.

The Hawthorn was first formally recorded in the *Tang Bencao* <note>original: 唐本草, the *Newly Revised Materia Medica* of the 7th century, the world's first official pharmacopoeia</note> under the name "Red Claw Fruit" <note>original: 赤爪子, *chìzhuǎ zi*</note>. The Ming dynasty physician Li Shizhen believed that the ancient trees referred to as the *niu*, *qing*, and *mei* in the *Erya* <note>the oldest surviving Chinese dictionary, dating back to the 3rd century BCE</note> are actually this same plant.

In the northern regions, the larger varieties have an excellent flavor and are processed into sweets and cakes <note>shānzhā gāo, a traditional tart jelly still popular in China today</note>, while the smaller wild varieties are used exclusively for medicinal purposes. The *Qimin Yaoshu* <note>original: 齊民要術, *Essential Arts for the People*, a 6th-century agricultural treatise</note> quotes the *Guangzhi* <note>an ancient gazetteer</note> stating that the *niu* tree <note>hawthorn</note> is easy to cultivate, and many people plant it extensively to be harvested for firewood.

It is also used to fertilize fields. Guo Pu’s commentary on the *Classic of Mountains and Seas* <note>original: 山海經, *Shanhaijing*</note> also notes that the wood of the *niu* can be burned to produce ash for manuring the soil. This tree typically grows alongside mountain oaks like the *hu* <note>*Quercus dentata*</note> and the *xu* <note>*Quercus serrata*</note>. As a common mountain plant, its fallen fruit is gathered for food, and its sturdy wood is relied upon for both fuel and as a habitat for birds.

Guo Pu’s commentary on the *Erya* only mentions that the fruit is edible but does not yet classify the tree formally as a "fruit tree." However, its application as a medicine has become far more prevalent and significant in recent centuries.
