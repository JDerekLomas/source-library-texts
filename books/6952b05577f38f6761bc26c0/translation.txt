## STANZA I

<margin>The Third Book</margin>
**The** primary order established by divine Wisdom in all created things is that every inferior nature should be directly governed by a superior one. As long as the inferior nature obeys the superior, it is protected from all harm and guided without any hindrance toward its destined happiness <note>original: "determinate felicity"</note>. However, if through an excessive love for its own liberty and a desire to choose a lawless <note>original: "licentious"</note> life over a beneficial one, it rebels against its superior nature, it suffers two major disadvantages. 

First, like a ship abandoned by its pilot, it strikes sometimes one rock and sometimes another, with no hope of reaching the harbor. Second, it loses the command it held over the natures beneath it, just as it deprived its own superior of authority. 

Irrational nature <note>animals and inanimate objects</note> is ruled by others, as its own imperfections make it unfit to rule anything. God, through his indescribable <note>original: "ineffable"</note> excellence, provides for everything and requires no guidance from anyone else. Between these two extremes—God and brutes <note>animals</note>—are angels and rational souls <note>human beings</note>, who both govern others and are governed themselves. 

The highest hierarchy of angels, directly enlightened by God, shines light upon the level below them. The lowest level—called "daemons" <note>original: "Daemons"; in this context referring to intermediary spirits or guardians, not modern "demons"</note> by the Platonists and *Shemarim* <note>Hebrew: שמרים, meaning "guards" or "watchers"</note> by the Hebrews—are placed over us as guardians, just as we are placed over irrational creatures. ## STANZA IV

### The properties of Celestial Love are revealed in this stanza.

->“—in new fetters caught—”<-

The Soul, being oppressed by the Body, finds her desire for <term>Intellectual Beauty</term> <note>Intellectual Beauty refers to the divine, non-physical beauty of truth and God, accessible only through the mind.</note> asleep; but, once awakened by Love, she is led by the <term>sensible Beauty</term> <note>Sensible beauty refers to physical beauty perceived through the five senses.</note> of the Body at last to their Fountain, God.

->“—which glow<-
->Dying, yet glowing greater grow.”<-

Movement and activity are the signs of life, while their absence is the sign of death. In the person who applies himself to the intellectual part of existence, the rational and the sensory faculties fail. Through the Rational, he is a Human; through the Intellectual, he communicates with Angels. As a Human he dies, only to be revived as an Angel. Thus, the Heart dies in the flames of <term>Intellectual Love</term>, yet it is not consumed; rather, by this death, it “grows greater” and receives a new and more sublime life. See in <term>Plato</term> the stories of <term>Alcestis</term> <note>In Greek mythology, Alcestis sacrificed her life for her husband and was eventually brought back from the dead.</note> and <term>Orpheus</term>.

# STANZA V

This stanza is a description of <term>sensible beauty</term> <note>original: "sensible Beauty"; beauty that is perceived through the physical senses, such as sight, as opposed to the intellect.</note>.

> “The elder in the Sun’s glass reads
> Her face, through the confused screen
> Of a dark shade obscurely seen.”

Sensible light is the action and power of the physical and spiritual light of <term>intelligible beauty</term> <note>original: "Intelligible Beauty"; beauty that is understood by the mind or intellect rather than seen by the eyes.</note>. When <term>Ideas</term> <note>In Platonic philosophy, these are the eternal, perfect forms or patterns of all things.</note> descended into the unformed <term>Angelic Mind</term>, they were like colors and shapes in the night. Just as someone who sees a beautiful object by moonlight desires to see and enjoy it more fully in the day, the <term>Mind</term> perceives <term>ideal beauty</term> within itself only dimly. Because of the "night" of its own imperfection, this beauty appears faint and opaque (which our author calls “the screen of a dark shade”). 

The Mind, therefore, turns toward the eternal Sun (like the <term>Moon</term> does) to perfect her <term>beauty</term> through him. By addressing herself to him, she becomes <term>intelligible light</term>; this clarifies the beauty of <term>Celestial Venus</term> <note>original: "Celestial Venus"; a personification of divine or intellectual love and beauty, distinct from earthly or physical lust.</note> and renders it visible to the eye of the <term>First Mind</term> <note>original: "first Minde"; likely referring to the Supreme Intellect or God.</note>.

In <term>sensible beauty</term>, we consider two things. First, there is the object in itself, which is the same at midnight as it is at noon. Secondly, there is the light, which is in a way the <term>Soul</term> of that beauty. The <term>author</term> supposes that just as the first part of sensible beauty (physical, <term>corporeal forms</term>) proceeds from the first part of <term>intellectual beauty</term> (<term>ideal forms</term>), so too does sensible light flow from the "intelligible light" that descends upon the <term>Ideas</term>.

<margin>Introduction</margin>
Plato and was about to apply himself to the interpretation of Plotinus <note>Plotinus was a major Greek-speaking philosopher of the 3rd century and the founder of Neoplatonism</note>, that Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, then seventeen years old, came to Florence. At a social gathering, held perhaps in the Medicean palace, he fell into discussion with a Florentine citizen ten years older than himself, Girolamo di Paolo Benivieni, and formed with him one of the most famous friendships in the annals of literature.<sup>8</sup>

Born in 1463, Giovanni Pico was the youngest son of a powerful Lombard feudatory <note>A person holding land under the feudal system</note> of the Empire, Gian Francesco Pico, Count of Mirandola and Concordia; his mother, Giulia Boiardo, was an aunt of the poet count of Scandiano, Matteo Maria Boiardo. His elder brother, Galeotto, who ruled the family estates and who was married to a princess of the house of Este, was a fierce soldier. Savonarola <note>Girolamo Savonarola, the Dominican friar and later revolutionary leader in Florence</note> exhorted Galeotto to repentance in vain; Galeotto excited the wonder of his contemporaries by defying a papal excommunication for sixteen years until his death. 

Giovanni Pico’s extraordinary beauty and romantic character won him the hearts of Lorenzo de’ Medici and the intellectual society of Florence; and his strange and varied learning aroused the greatest admiration among all. To Poliziano <note>Angelo Poliziano, a leading humanist scholar and poet</note> he was “the light of all learning” <note>original: *omnium doctrinarum lux*</note>; to Machiavelli, he was a “man who was almost divine” <note>original: *uomo quasichè divino*</note>; while Savonarola describes him as being “among the rarest miracles of nature for the sharpness of his mind and the loftiness of his learning” <note>original: *inter perrara naturae miracula perspicacitate ingenii et doctrinae sublimitate*</note>.

<margin>Introduction</margin>
"...because I want to speak." <note>original Italian: "perch' io voglio dire."</note> A century and a half later, this poem seemed to the young Lorenzo de' Medici to be "a very wonderful canzone <term>a song-like Italian lyric poem</term> in which this gracious poet subtly described every quality, virtue, and accident <note>In medieval philosophy, an 'accident' refers to a quality or characteristic of a thing that is not essential to its nature.</note> of love." But to us today, it is a somewhat dull composition. It lacks the mystical enthusiasm that gives a lyrical spark to "Love always returns to the noble heart" <note>original Italian: "Al cor gentil ripara sempre amore"</note> by Cavalcanti’s lesser-known namesake and older contemporary, Guido Guinicelli of Bologna. 

The analysis itself only emphasizes how tedious the stanzas are. Guido Cavalcanti began the series of discussions on the philosophy of love which would come to fascinate the people of the Renaissance so deeply. However, the poem and the commentary we are dealing with now are on a higher intellectual level. This is because between the time of Cavalcanti and Girolamo Benivieni—and between the writer known as "pseudo-Egidio" and Pico della Mirandola—the revival of Platonism and Neo-Platonism had occurred in Italy.

Neither Guido Cavalcanti nor his commentator mentions Plato or his doctrines. Yet, not many years before the poem was written, Albertus Magnus <note>A 13th-century German scholar and philosopher who sought to reconcile Christian faith with Aristotelian logic.</note> had declared that both Plato and Aristotle were necessary for the perfect philosopher: "A person does not reach perfection in philosophy except through the knowledge of the two philosophies of Aristotle and Plato." <gloss>2</gloss> <note>original Latin: "Non perficitur homo in philosophia nisi ex scientia duarum philosophiarum Aristotelis et Platonis."</note> Dante cites Plato fairly often, but he knew nothing of him at first hand...

# THE FIRST BOOK
## I

It is a principle of the Platonists that every created thing has a threefold being: causal, formal, and participated. 

In the sun, there is no heat, for heat is merely an elementary quality and not of a celestial nature; yet, the sun is the cause and fountain of all heat. Fire is hot by nature and by its own proper form. Wood is not hot of itself, yet it is capable of receiving that quality from fire. Thus, heat has its causal being in the sun, its formal being in the fire, and its participated being in the fuel.

The most noble and perfect of these is the causal being. Therefore, Platonists assert that all perfections are in God after this manner of being: that nothing is contained *within* God, but all things proceed *from* Him; that Intellect is not in Him, but that He is the original spring of every Intellect. 

Such is the meaning of Plotinus <note>Plotinus (c. 204–270 AD) was the founder of Neoplatonism. He argued that the ultimate source of all existence, "the One," is beyond all descriptions and categories.</note> when he affirms, "God neither understands nor knows"—that is to say, not in a "formal" way <note>original: "after a formal way." In this philosophical context, "formal" means possessing a quality as a specific, defined attribute. To say God does not "know" formally means His wisdom is so far beyond human knowledge that the word "knowledge" cannot properly define it.</note>. As Dionysius the Areopagite <note>A Christian theologian and philosopher of the late 5th to early 6th century whose writings merged Neoplatonic philosophy with Christian mysticism.</note> wrote: "God is neither an intellectual nor an intelligent nature, but is unspeakably exalted above all intellect and knowledge."

# The First Book, Part II

Platonists divide all created beings <note>original: "Creatures"; in this context, the term refers to everything created by God, including inanimate objects and spirits, not just animals.</note> into three levels. The first level includes everything physical and visible, such as the heavens, the elements, and everything composed of them. The final level consists of the invisible and non-physical, which are completely free from bodies; these are properly called "Intellectual" <note>In Platonic philosophy, "Intellectual" refers to pure spirit or mind that exists entirely apart from the physical world.</note> (or by theologians, "Angelic") natures.

Between these two is a middle nature. Although it is non-physical, invisible, and immortal, it moves physical bodies because it is assigned to that task. This is called the rational soul. It is lower than the angels but higher than physical bodies; it is subject to the angels and rules over bodies. Above all of these is God himself, the author and source of every created thing. In God, divinity has a "causal being" <note>meaning God is the ultimate cause or origin of divinity.</note>. Proceeding from God to the angels, divinity has a "formal being" <note>The essence of divinity as it exists in a defined, structured form within spirits.</note>, and from there it is passed down to the rational soul by sharing in the angels' brilliance <note>original: "lustre"</note>. Below the rational soul, no nature can claim to be divine.

### III

The first of these three natures cannot be multiplied. Since he is only one—the principle and cause of all other divinity—this has been clearly proven by Platonists, Peripatetics <note>original: "Peripateticks"; followers of Aristotle's school of philosophy</note>, and our own theologians <note>original: "Divines"</note>.

Regarding the second nature—namely, the angelic and intellectual—Platonists disagree. Some thinkers (such as Proclus, Hermias, Syrianus, and many others) place a great number of creatures between God and the rational soul. They call some of these "Intelligible" <note>original: Νόετα (noeta); things perceived by the mind</note> and others "Intellectual" <note>original: νόερα (noera); things capable of thinking</note>. Plato sometimes uses these terms interchangeably, as he does in his dialogue, the *Phaedo*.

Plotinus, Porphyry, and generally the most refined Platonists assign only one creature between God and the Soul of the World. They call this creature the "Son of God" because he was produced directly by Him. The first opinion aligns most closely with Dionysius the Areopagite <note>A Christian theologian and philosopher of the late 5th to early 6th century</note> and Christian theologians, who assert that the number of angels is, in a sense, infinite. The second opinion is more philosophical and better suits the views of Aristotle and Plato. Since my only purpose is to explain their meaning, I will turn away from the first path (even though it is the only correct one) to follow the second.

## V

All intelligent beings <note>original: "understanding agents"</note> have within themselves the form of that which they intend to create. For example, an architect has in his mind a model of the building he undertakes; he strives to imitate this mental image exactly as his pattern. Followers of Plato (<term>Platonists</term> <note>Philosophers who followed the teachings of Plato, emphasizing that abstract ideas are more real than physical objects.</note>) call this pattern the <term>Idea</term> or <term>Exemplar</term> <note>A perfect model or original pattern.</note>. They describe this mental way of being as <term>Ideal</term> or <term>Intelligible</term> <note>Existing only in the intellect or thought.</note>, and the physical version as <term>Material</term> and <term>Sensible</term> <note>Perceptible by the physical senses like sight and touch.</note>.

In those materials, he expresses the form he has conceived in his mind. The poet <term>Dante</term> <note>Dante Alighieri (c. 1265–1321), the Italian author of the Divine Comedy.</note> alludes to this concept when he writes:

> “— No one can shape any work

This is because, within that one creation, he produced the "Ideas" and forms of everything in their most perfect state—that is, the <term>Ideal</term> state. # VI

After the pattern of that Mind <note>original: "Minde"; in Platonic philosophy, this refers to the *Nous* or Divine Intellect</note>, they affirm this sensible world <term>sensible world: the physical universe that can be perceived by the senses</term> was made. # X

Love (says *Plato*) was fathered upon *Penia* <note>original: "Penia" – the personification of poverty or want.</note> by *Porus* <note>original: "Porus" – the personification of resourcefulness or plenty.</note> (the son of *Metis* <note>original: "Metis" – the personification of wisdom or counsel.</note>) in *Jupiter's Orchard*. He was conceived while *Porus* was drunk on *Nectar*, during the gathering of the *Gods* to celebrate the birth of *Venus*. 

*Nature* is inherently unformed; when it receives form from God, it becomes the *Angelic Mind*. This form consists of the *Ideas*, which are the primary *Beauty*. In their descent from their divine *Fountain* <note>The divine source of all existence.</note>, these *Ideas* mix with a different nature and thus become imperfect. The first *Mind*, because its inherent opacity <note>original: "opacousness" – the quality of blocking light; here, the mind's lack of divine clarity.</note> obscures their brilliance, desires that *Beauty* which the *Ideas* have lost; this desire is *Love*. 

This *Love* is born when *Porus* (the abundance of *Ideas*) mixes with *Penia* (the poverty of that unformed nature we called *Jupiter*), in whose *Garden* the *Ideas* are planted. Adorned with these *Ideas*, the first *Mind* was named *Paradise* by the *Ancients*. *Zoroaster* invited us to this contemplative life and eternal happiness when he said, "Seek, seek *Paradise*." 

Our theologians associate this with the *Empyrean Heaven* <note>original: "Coelum Empyraeum" – the highest part of heaven, believed to be composed of pure light or fire and the dwelling place of God.</note>, the home of blessed *Souls*. By closing the eyes of his body, she opened those of his mind, through which he saw both the present and the future. The ghost of *Achilles*, who inspired *Homer* with all his intellectual meditations in poetry, deprived him of his physical <note>original: "corporeal"</note> sight.

Although heavenly love lives eternally in the intellect of every soul, only a few people make use of it. These are those who, setting aside the cares of the body, can say with *Saint Paul*: "Whether I was in the body or out of the body, I do not know." <note>A reference to the biblical passage 2 Corinthians 12:2, describing a mystical experience of heaven.</note> A person occasionally reaches this state, but remains there only a short time, as we see in moments of religious ecstasy <note>original: "Extasies"</note>.

## XXII

Thus, in our soul (which is naturally indifferent to either physical <term>original: sensible</term> or intellectual beauty), there may be three types of love: one in the intellect, which is angelic; the second, human; and the third, sensual. The latter two are concerned with the same object: physical beauty <term>original: corporeal beauty</term>. Sensual love fixes its focus entirely on the physical object, while human love separates beauty from matter. 

Most of humanity goes no further than these two types. However, those whose minds are refined by philosophy—knowing that physical beauty is merely an image of another, more perfect beauty—leave it behind and desire to see celestial beauty. They already have a taste of this celestial beauty in their memory; and if they persist in this mental elevation, they finally achieve it. They recover that which was within them from the beginning, though they were not aware of it because they were distracted by other objects.
