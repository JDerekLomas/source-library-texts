Cosimo found the instrument he needed in Marsilio Ficino, the son of a physician from Figline in the Valdarno <note>A valley of the Arno river in Tuscany, Italy</note>. Cosimo told him to abandon his father’s profession and focus on healing men’s minds rather than their bodies. In 1463, he commissioned Ficino to produce a complete Latin translation of Plato’s dialogues, giving him a farm near the Medici <note>original: "Medicean"</note> villa at Careggi and a house in Florence itself, so that he could work in ease and comfort. The translation took about fourteen years and was finished in 1477; however, when Cosimo lay on his deathbed in 1464, the work was sufficiently advanced for Marsilio to comfort his last hours by reading his version of the *Philebus* <note>A dialogue by Plato regarding the nature of pleasure and the "highest good"</note>.

“Even until his last day,” Marsilio wrote to Lorenzo de’ Medici, “when he departed from this world of shadows to enter the light, he devoted himself to the pursuit of knowledge. For when we had read together Plato’s book on the origin of the Universe and the Supreme Good, he—as you who were present well know—soon after left this life, as though he were now truly going to possess the fullness of that Good which he had tasted during our conversation.”^5

# THE FIRST BOOK
## I

It is a principle of the Platonists that every created thing has a threefold existence <note>original: "being"</note>: causal, formal, and participated. In the sun, there is no heat itself, because heat is merely an elementary quality and not of a celestial nature; yet the sun is the cause and fountain of all heat. Fire is hot by nature and by its own proper form. Wood is not hot by itself, yet it is capable of receiving that quality from fire. Thus, heat has its **causal** existence in the sun, its **formal** existence in the fire, and its **participated** existence in the fuel <term>participated: the state of a thing that does not possess a quality by nature, but "takes part" in it by being connected to a source</term>. 

The most noble and perfect of these is the causal. Therefore, Platonists assert that all perfections <note>original: "excellencies"</note> exist in God in this manner: that nothing created is *in* God, but all things come *from* him. Intellect is not in him, but he is the original spring of every intellect. 

This is what Plotinus <note>Plotinus (c. 204–270 AD) was a major Greek-speaking philosopher of the ancient world and the founder of Neoplatonism</note> meant when he affirmed, "God neither understands nor knows"—that is to say, not in a "formal" or limited way. As Dionysius the Areopagite <note>Dionysius Areopagita: a Christian theologian and philosopher whose writings blended Neoplatonism with Christian theology</note> wrote: "God is neither an intellectual nor an intelligent nature, but is unspeakably exalted above all intellect and knowledge."

## II

Platonists categorize created things <note>original: "Creatures"</note> into three ranks <note>original: "degrees"</note>. The first rank includes those things that are physical <note>original: "corporeal"</note> and visible, such as the heavens, the elements, and everything made from them. 

The highest rank <note>original: "The last"</note> consists of the invisible and non-physical beings that are completely free from bodies. These are properly called "Intellectual Natures," or what theologians <note>original: "Divines"</note> call "Angelical Natures." 

Between these two is a middle nature. Although it is non-physical, invisible, and immortal, it still moves physical bodies because that is its assigned duty <note>original: "office"</note>. This is called the rational soul. It is lower than the angels but higher than physical bodies; it is subject to the angels and serves as the ruler <note>original: "regent"</note> of bodies. 

Above all of these is God himself, the author and source <note>original: "principle"</note> of every created thing. In God, divinity exists as a "causal being." When it flows from him to the angels, it takes on a "formal being." From there, it is passed down into the rational soul by sharing in the angels' light <note>original: "participation of their lustre"</note>. Below the soul, no nature can claim the title of divine.

### III

That the first of these three natures <note>original: "Natures"</note> cannot be multiplied—as he is but one, the source <note>original: "principle"</note> and cause of all other divinity—is clearly proven by Platonists, Peripatetics <note>original: "Peripateticks"; followers of Aristotle's philosophy</note>, and our theologians <note>original: "Divines"</note>. 

Regarding the second rank, namely the angelic and intellectual nature, Platonists disagree. Some (such as Proclus, Hermias, Syrianus, and many others) place a great number of created beings <note>original: "creatures"</note> between God and the rational soul. They call some of these "intelligible" <note>original: "Νόετα" (Noeta)</note> and others "intellectual" <note>original: "νόερα" (Noera)</note>; Plato sometimes confuses <note>original: "confoundeth"</note> these terms, as seen in his dialogue, *Phaedo*. 

Plotinus, Porphyry <note>original: "Porphyrius"</note>, and generally the most refined Platonists, assign only one created being between God and the Soul of the World. They call this being the "Son of God" because it was produced immediately by him. 

The first opinion—that there are many such beings—aligns most with Dionysius the Areopagite <note>original: "Dionysius Areopagita"</note> and Christian theologians, who assert that the number of angels is in a sense infinite. The second opinion is more philosophical, best suiting the views of Aristotle and Plato. Since our only purpose here is to express their perspective, we will set aside the first path (even though it is the only right one) to follow the latter.

### IV

Therefore, following the opinion of Plotinus—which is confirmed not only by the best Platonists but even by Aristotle and all the Arabian philosophers, especially Avicenna—we affirm that God from eternity produced a created being <note>original: "creature"</note> of an incorporeal and intellectual nature. This being is as perfect as it is possible for a created being to be, and beyond it, God produced nothing else directly. 

This is because the effect of the most perfect cause must itself be most perfect. Furthermore, the "most perfect" can only be a single thing; if there were two or more such beings, it would be impossible for one not to be more or less perfect than the rest. If they were truly equal in every way, they would not be two separate beings, but the same one. 

I prefer this reasoning for our opinion over the argument provided by Avicenna <note>original: "Avicen"</note>, which is based on the principle that "from a single cause, acting as one, only a single effect can proceed." 

We conclude, therefore, that no created being except for this "first mind" <note>original: "minde"</note> proceeds immediately from God. For all other effects that come from this mind, and all other secondary causes, God is only the mediate efficient cause <note>meaning God is the ultimate source, but He acts through an intermediary rather than directly</note>. 

Plato, Hermes <note>referring to Hermes Trismegistus, the legendary Hellenistic figure</note>, and Zoroaster call this being the "Daughter of God," the "Mind," "Wisdom," or "Divine Reason." Some even interpret it as the "Word" <note>original: "the Word"; referring to the concept of the *Logos*</note>. However, they do not mean the same thing as the "Son of God" described by our theologians <note>original: "Divines"</note>. In Christian theology, the Son of God is not a created being, but is of one essence and co-equal with the Creator.

## V

All intelligent agents have within themselves the form of whatever they intend to create. For example, an architect has a mental image of the building he takes on, which he strives to imitate exactly as his pattern. The <term>Platonists</term> <note>followers of the philosophy of Plato</note> call this mental pattern the <term>Idea</term> or <term>Exemplar</term> <note>original: "Exemplar"; a model or pattern to be copied</note>. They believe this mental version is more perfect than the physical object made after it. They describe this type of existence as <term>Ideal</term> or <term>Intelligible</term> <note>existing in the intellect or thought</note>, while the physical version is <term>Material</term> and <term>Sensible</term> <note>perceivable by the senses</note>.

This is because within that one being, He produced the Ideas and forms of everything, and He did so in their most perfect state—the <term>Ideal</term>. For this reason, they call this Mind the <term>Intelligible World</term> <note>a realm of pure thought that serves as the blueprint for the physical universe</note>.

**Plato** asserts that "the Creator of the World made the world soul, and all other rational souls, in one cup and from the same elements; the universal soul being most perfect, and ours the least." # IV

Just as desire generally follows knowledge, so different types of knowing are linked to different desiring powers. We distinguish knowledge into three levels: <term>Sense</term>, <term>Reason</term>, and <term>Intellect</term>. These are accompanied by three desiring virtues: <term>Appetite</term>, <term>Election</term> <note>original: "Election" — here meaning the power of choice or free will.</note>, and <term>Will</term>. 

<term>Appetite</term> exists in animals <note>original: "Bruits" — irrational beasts.</note>; <term>Election</term> exists in humans; and <term>Will</term> exists in Angels. Physical <term>Sense</term> knows only physical <note>original: "corporeal"</note> things, and the <term>Appetite</term> only desires such things. The angelic <term>Intellect</term> is entirely focused on the contemplation of spiritual concepts. Angels do not lean toward material things unless those things are stripped of matter and spiritualized; their <term>Will</term> is fed only by eternal <note>original: "intemporal" — existing outside of time.</note> spiritual goodness. 

Rational nature <note>The human condition, defined by the ability to reason.</note> is the middle ground between these two extremes. It sometimes descends to <term>Sense</term> and is sometimes raised up to the <term>Intellect</term>. Through its own <term>Election</term> (choice), it complies with whichever of these desires it pleases. 

Thus, it appears that physical objects are desired either by the sensual <term>Appetite</term> or by the <term>Election</term> of <term>Reason</term> when it leans toward the senses. Conversely, spiritual <note>original: "Incorporeal"</note> objects are desired by the angelic <term>Will</term> or by the <term>Election</term> of <term>Reason</term> when it is elevated to a high intellectual level.

Beauty in general is a "harmony resulting from several things coming together in proportion to form a third entity." Because of this blending <note>original: "temperament"</note> and mixture of various natures that agree in the composition of a single being, every creature is beautiful <note>original: "Fair"</note>. This type of beauty begins only after Him, arising from "contrariety" (the opposition of elements), without which there is no composition. It is the union of opposites: a "friendly enmity" and a "disagreeing concord." 

This is why the philosopher Empedocles <note>A Greek philosopher (c. 490–430 BCE) who taught that the universe is composed of elements joined by Love and separated by Strife.</note> made discord and concord the fundamental principles of all things. By "discord," he understood the variety of the natures that are combined; by "concord," he meant their union. He added that in God alone there is no discord, because He is not the union of several natures, but a pure, uncompounded unity. 

In these compositions, the union must necessarily outweigh the conflict; otherwise, the structure <note>original: "Fabrick"</note> would fall apart. For this reason, in the stories told by poets, Venus (the goddess of love and beauty) loves Mars (the god of war). This beauty cannot exist without opposition; she restrains and moderates him, and this blending calms the conflict between these opposites. Similarly, in astrology, Venus is placed next to Mars to check his destructive influence, just as Jupiter is placed next to Saturn to reduce his harmful nature <note>original: "malignancy"</note>. If Mars were always subject to Venus (meaning if the opposition of principles always yielded to their proper balance), nothing would ever be destroyed.

# VI

This is **Beauty** in the broadest sense, the same as **Harmony**. For this reason, God is said to have created the World with a musical and harmonious balance <note>original: "temperament"</note>. But **Harmony**, strictly speaking, implies a melodious agreement of voices, while **Beauty**, in a restricted sense <note>original: "acception"</note>, refers to a proportional agreement in visible things, just as **Harmony** does in audible ones.

The desire for this **Beauty** is **Love**, which arises from only one faculty of knowledge: sight. This led Plotinus <note>A major Greek-speaking philosopher (c. 204–270 CE) and the founder of Neoplatonism.</note> (Enneads, Book 3, Chapter 5, Section 3) to derive the word *eros* <note>original: "ἔρως"</note>, meaning **Love**, from *horasis* <note>original: "ὅρασις"</note>, meaning **Sight**. 

Here the Platonist may object: if **Love** is only for visible things, how can it be applied to Ideas, which are invisible natures? We answer that **Sight** is twofold: physical <note>original: "corporeal"</note> and spiritual. The first is that of the senses; the other is the <term>Intellectual faculty</term>—the power of reasoning and understanding—by which we share a common nature with the Angels. The Platonists call this "sight," while physical sight is merely an image of it. As Aristotle said, "The intellect is to the soul what sight is to the body." For this reason, Minerva (the goddess of Wisdom) is called *glaukopis* <note>original: "γλαυκῶπις"</note> or "bright-eyed" by Homer. With this spiritual sight, Moses, Saint Paul, and other Saints beheld the face of God. This is what theologians <note>original: "Divines"</note> call <term>Intellectual, intuitive cognition</term>—a direct, non-physical understanding—and the <term>Beatific vision</term>, which is the ultimate reward of the righteous in the afterlife.

# XVII

[Our author] grounds his argument on the idea that into the <term>vehicle</term> <note>original: "Vehiculum"; in Neoplatonic philosophy, a "chariot" or subtle body that carries the soul</note> of the soul—which is gifted by the soul with the power to shape the body—a particular "shaping power" <note>original: "formative virtue"</note> is infused from her star. This power is distinct according to that star; thus, the appearance of one person is <term>Saturnine</term> <note>influenced by Saturn; often meaning grave or serious</note>, while another is <term>Jovial</term> <note>influenced by Jupiter; meaning cheerful or majestic</note>, and so on. In their looks, we read the nature of their souls.

But because "lower matter" <note>the physical substance of the body</note> is not always submissive to the "stamp" <note>the soul’s shaping influence</note>, the power of the soul is not always equally expressed in the visible image <note>original: "Effigies"</note>. For this reason, it happens that two people of the same nature can look different: the matter of which one consists is less disposed to receive that figure than the other. What is complete in one is imperfect in the other. Our author concludes that when the shapes of two bodies are formed by the power of the same star, this similarity <note>original: "Conformity"</note> creates love.

->“From the Sun’s most sublime abode.”<-

The <term>Tropic of Cancer</term>: the gateway through which souls descend to earth according to the <term>Platonists</term>, while they ascend back to heaven through <term>Capricorn</term>. <term>Cancer</term> is the <term>House of the Moon</term>, who rules over the vital parts of the body; <term>Capricorn</term> belongs to <term>Saturn</term>, who presides over <term>contemplation</term> <note>deep spiritual or intellectual reflection</note>.

->Is thus fed by a pleasing error.”<-

Frequently, if not always, the lover believes that the person he loves is more beautiful than they actually are; he views them through the image his own soul has formed.
